# ALGORITHMIC PROCESSES OF SOCIAL ALERTNESS AND SOCIAL TRANSMISSION: HOW BOTS DISSEMINATE INFORMATION ON TWITTER<sup>1</sup>

Carolina Alves de Lima Salge MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30606 U.S.A. {calsalge@uga.edu}

Elena Karahanna MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30606 U.S.A. {ekarah@uga.edu}

Jason Bennett Thatcher MIS Department, Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {jason.thatcher@temple.edu}

Despite increased empirical attention, theory on bots and how they act to disseminate information on social media remains poorly understood. Our study leverages the conduit brokerage perspective and the findings of a multiple case study to develop a novel framework of algorithmic conduit brokerage for understanding information dissemination by bots and the design choices that may influence their actions. Algorithmic conduit brokerage encompasses two intertwined processes. The first process, algorithmic social alertness, relies on bot activity to curate and reconfigure information. Algorithmic social alertness is significant because it involves action triggers that dictate the kinds of information being searched, discovered, and retrieved by bots. The second process, algorithmic social transmission, relies on bot activity to embellish and distribute the information curated. Algorithmic social transmission is important because it can broaden the reach of information disseminated by bots through increased discoverability and directed targeting. The two algorithmic conduit brokerage processes we offer are unique to bots and distinct from the original conceptualization of conduit brokerage, which is rooted in human activity. First, since bots lack the human ability of sensemaking and are instead fueled by automation and action triggers rather than by emotions, algorithmic conduit brokerage is more invariant and reliable than human conduit brokerage. Second, automation increases the speed and scale of information curation and transfer, making algorithmic conduit brokerage not only more consistent but also faster and more extensive. Third, algorithmic conduit brokerage includes a set of new concepts (e.g., action triggers and rapid scaling) that are specific to bots and therefore not applicable to human conduit brokerage.

Keywords: Bots, information dissemination, information transfer, algorithmic social alertness, algorithmic social transmission, algorithmic conduit brokerage, action triggers, reconfiguration, embellishment, rapid scaling, social media

## Introduction

Bots—automated accounts in online social networks (Morstatter et al. 2016)—have become pervasive, as evidenced by the increased attention they have received in academia (Boichak et al. 2018; Ferrara et al. 2016; Salge and Karahanna 2018; Shao et al. 2018) and the popular press (Abramowitz 2017; Frank 2018). Contributing to the increased attention is the fact that bots are being used to spread low credibility information on social media (Shao et al. 2018), call volunteers of social activism to action (Savage et al. 2016), and manipulate political processes around the world (Bessi and Ferrara 2016; Howard et al. 2018). In response, a few empirical studies on how bots diffuse information have developed (e.g., Gorodnichenko et al. 2018; Shao et al. 2018).

These empirical studies show that bots spread information very fast (Gorodnichenko et al. 2018) by posting, sharing, and replying to actors on Twitter and by targeting, in their messages, influential actors or actors with high followership (Shao et al. 2018; Stella et al. 2018). Additional studies show that bots having high followership are likely to disseminate information widely (Boichak et al. 2018) and that information diffusion by bots is manifested via complex contagion (Mønsted et al. 2017). Three observations emerge from this nascent literature. First, there is scant consideration of bot design characteristics. Design is at the core of bot development since it describes how the technology actually works (Gorwa and Guilbeault 2018), and is important from an information systems (IS) perspective. Second, existing bot models (e.g., Boichak et al. 2018; Mønsted et al. 2017) are grounded in the structure of social networks, positing that, with a facilitating network structure in place (e.g., high followership), information can be widely diffused, with significantly less emphasis on a providing a processual view of diffusion, which incorporates the actions and behaviors of social network actors. Third, although research studies describe strategic actions used by bots (e.g., targeting of influential actors), they only provide descriptive accounts of such strategies, lacking a theoretical account of how bots disseminate information.

As a result, attention to how bots act to diffuse information, and especially theoretically grounded accounts, is scant. This lack of bot theory is problematic because it prevents the organization of knowledge. Without a description and explanation for how bots act to diffuse information, scholars can be easily dazzled by the complexity of the empirical world in which bots are situated in, and less focused on how to impose conceptual order on such world (see Bacharach 1989; Hall and Lindzey 1957). Further, the absence of bot theory suppresses the accumulation of bot knowledge. In other words, without a bot diffusion framework that helps organize what is already known, it becomes difficult to identify what is actually new and what needs to be known in bot research. Finally, such a framework highlights the design choices that enable bots to diffuse information and stimulates future research on these.

Conduit brokerage, a processual view concerned with actions by which actors facilitate the transfer of information between parties (Obstfeld 2005, 2017; Obstfeld et al. 2014), offers a useful theoretical lens for studying information diffusion by bots. The behavior of bots, as prior research exemplifies (see Boichak et al. 2018), is well aligned with a conduit process since bots are coded to pass information from one user to another. Moreover, conduit brokerage is only realized in action (i.e., when bots actually transfer information between parties) and cannot be inferred by the degree of opportunities to broker (e.g., the betweenness centrality scores of bots). While recognizing that network structure defines the context in which brokering activities occur, exploring conduit brokerage, understood as a set of bot actions rather than the pattern of ties in bots’ social networks, complements existing views in the bot literature, and may help shed light on how bots diffuse information through their brokering actions.

Our study draws on conduit brokerage and the findings of a multiple case study to describe and explain how bots disseminate information on Twitter. We track how eight Twitter bots acted to transfer information over a period of six days. We include observational data of bot profiles, and rich data on bot activity triggered by the activity of thousands of users on Twitter and the activity of other actors outside of Twitter as well. The Twitter bots that we study are static<sup>2</sup> (i.e., defined by rules with no learning, no adaptation) instead of dynamic (i.e., powered by machine learning, with adaptation), and since diffusion can take a variety of meanings, we note that our view of diffusion aligns well with dissemination. In other words, we are interested in studying bots taking action to disseminate information, regardless of whether the information they disseminate is received or not by other actors. This view represents an important first step for better understanding the impact of bots on the spread of information more widely. Hence, the question we answer in this paper is: How do bots act to disseminate information on Twitter?

We provide five contributions to research on which we elaborate in our “Discussion” section. A primary contribution is an emergent theoretical framework of algorithmic conduit brokerage that includes two intertwined processes for how bots disseminate information. The first process, which we term algorithmic social alertness, captures how bots curate and reconfigure information, whereas the second process, termed algorithmic social transmission, captures the bot actions to embellish and distribute information. Algorithmic social alertness is significant because it involves action triggers that dictate the kinds of information being searched, discovered, and retrieved by bots, whereas algorithmic social transmission is important because it can broaden the reach of information disseminated by bots through increased discoverability and directed targeting. When integrated into our algorithmic conduit brokerage emergent framework, the processes of social alertness and social transmission encompass and formalize new concepts mentioned only in passing by Obstfeld and colleagues (reconfiguration and embellishment) as well as new concepts that are unique to bots (e.g., action triggers), illustrating the semantic relationships among these. In so doing, the framework provides a theoretical foundation for organizing previous research findings, becoming the theoretical scaffolding for stimulating future research on bots in a systematic manner.

A second contribution is drawing attention to the process of algorithmic social alertness as a critical step in how bots disseminate information. Yet, despite its importance, previous research has ignored this process, examining instead bot actions pertinent to the distribution of information (i.e., focusing almost exclusively on algorithmic social transmission). A third contribution is the new concept of rapid scaling. Rapid scaling is a process by which the curation and reconfiguration of information or the embellishment and distribution of it (or both) increases significantly at a particular point in time. Simply put, rapid scaling is germane because it is part of what makes bots unhuman. A fourth contribution is the description of two prototypical behaviors that bots enact to disseminate information, which we label amplification and boundary spanning. These behaviors are important because they encompass different action triggers and contrasting information sources for bots. A fifth contribution is the theorizing of algorithmic conduit brokerage,<sup>3</sup> which is unique to bots since it depicts a fairly reliable and invariant process, absent of human-like sensemaking, and immune to physical fatigue and emotional feelings of anger, excitement, or sadness, which are all known to influence human judgment and behavior.

## Bots and Information Dissemination

Research on bots and information dissemination is scarce. Most of the few existing studies are in political contexts, with all of the data coming from Twitter. For example, Boichak et al. (2018) identify two types of Twitter bots attempting to influence the spread of information during the 2016 U.S. presidential elections: information aggregators, characterized as bots with high tweet rate and high followership, and false amplifiers, characterized as bots with high tweet rate and low followership (p. 21). Only information aggregators augmented dissemination suggesting that bots acting in similar ways (high tweet rate) but having different network structure (low followership versus high followership) have different impacts on the dyadic movement of information.

In another research study of Twitter and U.S. presidential elections, bots played a disproportionate role in sharing lowcredibility information by posting links to articles, retweeting other accounts, and replying to other users in the very early spreading moments, before an article went viral (Shao et al. 2018). When distributing low-credibility information, bots also repeatedly targeted influential users through Twitter mentions. This sort of influential targeting is observed by a third paper, which examines the structural and emotional roles played by Twitter bots broadcasting information during the Catalan referendum (Stella et al. 2018). While the Catalan bots targeted influential users on both sides of the political spectrum, they bombarded only one group with violent information, increasing their exposure to negative and inflammatory narratives, and exacerbating political conflict on Twitter.

Information dissemination on Twitter and the role of bots in shaping public opinion is further examined during the 2016 EU Referendum (Brexit). Findings suggest that bot reactions to new messages are mostly complete within one to two hours, and that stronger interactions across bots having similar political beliefs are consistent with social media echo chambers (Gorodnichenko et al. 2018). The degree of bot influence is shown to depend on whether bots spread information consistent with the prior information spread by humans. It is argued that the aggressive use of bots, in combination with the fragmented social media environment and the role of sentiment, can contribute to vote outcomes. Finally, in a controlled Twitter experiment, researchers analyzed whether the transmission of hashtags by a network of disseminating bots mimics simple contagion, (i.e., induced by a single exposure to information) or complex contagion (i.e., induced by multiple exposures to information). Results show that complex contagion more accurately captures social transmission by bots (Mønsted et al. 2017).

Altogether, the literature describes how the structure of social networks and the strategic actions of bots affect social transmission on Twitter. It also indicates that bot dissemination is rapid and potentially impactful to human activity outside of Twitter. While insightful, current approaches are focused on the semantic content, network structure, and descriptive activities of bots to distribute information, but are largely silent on how bots curate information. They are also distant from a processual perspective informed by bot action and design characteristics. These studies identify certain patterns of bot content, structure, and behavior, but lack a theoretical account of how bots act to disseminate information. The objective of our paper is to address this gap.

## Conduit Brokerage

Conduit brokerage occurs when a focal actor passes information from actor A to actor C. Obstfeld et al. (2014, p. 141- 144) note:

In conduit brokerage…the third party relays information from one alter to the second alter without attempting to change the relationship between the alters. The broker mediates rather than moderates the relationship....Conduit brokerage is consistent with the knowledge advantage associated with structural holes (Burt 1992), but structural holes are not a requirement as we can readily imagine situations where conduit brokerage is a common occurrence in structures where alters are already tied in some way.

At its root, conduit brokerage is an information transfer phenomenon where brokers take actions to pass information between parties (Obstfeld 2005, 2017; Obstfeld et al. 2014). These actions require brokers to move a piece of information (e.g., article) termed token (which for clarity we call information) from actor A to actor C. The source of information can be a person or another entity such as an organization and a platform (Obstfeld 2017), or even a bot. While Obstfeld is perhaps the leading articulator of conduit brokerage, the perspective has been recently applied across a wide range of contexts, including individual performance (Soda et al. 2018), mediated sensemaking (Strike and Rerup 2015), dialogical diffusion (Romanos 2016), and innovation (Grosser et al. 2018). In these studies, humans (e.g., employees and coworkers; organizational board members, most trusted advisors, and family members; and activists) are usually the sources, brokers, and receivers of information.

Regardless of the context to which it is applied, framing conduit brokerage simply as information transfer obscures the complexities involved in moving information between parties (Obstfeld 2017). Such framing neglects characteristics of the broker, the information itself, and the sources/receivers of information. This sort of neglect is problematic since, in order to transfer information between actors, the broker must identify or even embellish the information discovered in a particular place, move the information to a different location, and do so successfully. Obstfeld (2017) states that some people may serendipitously stumble upon information and pass it along while others may intentionally search for information in a particular place with the goal of bringing it back to another. Whether information is passed on by chance or by a deliberate effort, brokers must be socially alert for information and poised to take action. In other words, they must curate environments to determine which information will be of interest to their audience and therefore should be transferred, and how that transfer should occur.

When brokers curate information they interpret such information in different ways, and consequently, differ in the ways they translate and ferry the information to others (Obstfeld 2017). The movement of information, therefore, includes some level of interpretation and translation which in itself may involve some degree of reconfiguration and embellishment. While Obstfeld discusses reconfiguration and embellishment in passing (see pages 32, 73, 99 and 158 of Obstfeld 2017), we provide formal definitions of such concepts given their importance to the bot dissemination context. We view reconfiguration as the arrangement of curated information in a different shape or form (e.g., a curated audio being transcribed to text,<sup>4</sup> or a curated table being turned into a chart). Reconfiguration does not involve the insertion of new information but is instead limited to adaptation. Such adaptation may be required given platform rules (e.g., to comply with character limits) or may be deliberate toward an objective (e.g., reducing cognitive processing, drawing user attention). In contrast, we view embellishment as the addition or insertion of new information to what was curated and perhaps reconfigured (e.g., adding a comment to a curated audio transcribed to text). The objective of embellishment is to draw attention to what was curated by, for example, making the information more appealing (and, thus, more likely to be shared), or more noticeable and discoverable by platform users.

Since bots are not human, developers must deliberately design them to be socially alert for information and poised to take action. That is, they have to program bots to curate and distribute information from one location to another. Reconfiguration and embellishment are optional design choices. These choices are important though and likely to influence the processing and further movement of information. Thus, to fully understand how bots disseminate information on Twitter using an algorithmic conduit brokerage lens, we must understand bot actions with respect to reconfiguration and embellishment, as well as those of curation and distribution, and consequently the nuances of algorithmic social alertness and algorithmic social transmission.

## Research Context

The 2013 protests in Brazil, known as the 2013 Confederations Cup riots or the 20 cents movement, were political, with demonstrations and marches throughout Brazil and the world. An increase of R\$0.20 (US\$0.09) in transportation fares on June 2 led to a revolt and provided the basis of a range of social media protests and street riots, which continued for days. The protests were among the largest in Brazil’s history, with over two million activists. Such public actions of dissatisfaction generated opportunities for supporting other issues where corruption was at the foreground and were used as motivation by thousands of Brazilians around the world.

On June 15, 2013, the FIFA Confederations Cup commenced in Brazil as a pilot event for the 2014 FIFA World Cup. At the time, activists were using Twitter to protest the R\$0.20 increase in transportation fares, but the worldwide popularity of the FIFA event provided a unique opportunity for protesters to also use Twitter to voice other broader issues, such as Brazil’s systemic history of political corruption and the 2014 FIFA World Cup. In just a few days, some hashtags of the protests, like the hashtag #ChangeBrazil, were trending on Twitter in Brazil and worldwide (Pereira 2017). This rapid growth of the protests on Twitter set the stage for information dissemination by bots which is the focus of this paper.

## Data Preprocessing

To understand how bots act to disseminate information, we gathered protest activity posted on Twitter. Twitter is a 321 million-user social network (Statista 2019) with a very active bot presence. Data collection on Twitter was conducted in two phases. In Phase I, we used Radian6<sup>5</sup> to gather messages with three popular protest hashtags from Twitter’s search API: #ChangeBrazil, #ChangeBrasil, and #MudaBrasil.<sup>6</sup> A total of 710,697 messages were collected in Phase I. All messages were disseminated on Twitter during the Confederations Cup, and over a period of six days from June 16 to June 21.<sup>7</sup>

Before the data from Phase I were analyzed, a lengthy process of data transformation was conducted. We first converted the data into multiple social networks. All graphs are directed and unweighted. Each actor is a Twitter user sharing a message about the protests. We added an edge between two actors if one of them replied-to/retweeted the other in a message or if one of them mentioned the other in a reply/retweet/ tweet message. Tweets that were not replies and did not contain mentions were not modeled, and all ties were flattened in that a connection between A and B indicates at least one interaction. Since the activity of bots may fluctuate over time, we segmented the social networks by day. After transforming the Phase I data to arrive at 18 network graphs (6 each [one for every day] for replies, retweets, and mentions) with 142,728 distinct actors and 231,548 unique ties, we reexamined the graph dataset to detect bots, using the funnel process (Salge and Karahanna 2018). The funnel process is a multi-method approach that combines social network analysis, machine learning, and human coding to detect bots in a manner that efficiently leverages the strengths of each method and overcomes limitations of each approach (see Salge and Karahanna 2018).

Bot Detection. Analysis through the funnel process began with social network analysis, which provided a subset of 5,861 active actors,<sup>8</sup> was followed by machine learning (ML), which detected 829 bots,<sup>9</sup> and ended with human coding, which confirmed the presence of 8 bots out of the 829<sup>10</sup> detected. Since Type I error, or the proportion of humans misclassified as bots by ML, was high, we became concerned about Type II error as well, or the proportion of bots misclassified as humans by ML. After checking the validity of a random sample of 100 actors who were active in the protests and classified as humans by ML, the coders found zero bots (inter-coder reliability = 0.99), suggesting that ML is fairly accurate in detecting non-bot actors.

While it is entirely possible for coders to have erroneously reversed the ML classification of some bots, this misclassification likely only happened for humanlike bots. More importantly, however, the coders prevented a ML misclassification of cyborgs, which refer to either bot-assisted humans or human-assisted bots (Chu et al. 2010, p. 21), as bots. A misclassification of bots as humans or as cyborgs does not bias the findings of this research. This type of error does not invalidate our conclusions of bot conduit behavior since we are certain that we have actually analyzed bots. A ML misclassification of humans or cyborgs as bots, however, is problematic and would threaten the validity of research focusing on a theory pertinent to bots.

## Sample and Analyses

After identifying the eight active bots in Brazil’s 2013 protests, we returned to Radian6 to collect additional data on these bots (Phase II data collection). We scraped all messages that each bot disseminated during the protests from June 16 to June 21 (and not only those that included the three protest hashtags collected in Phase I). This resulted in a total of 17,399 messages collected in Phase II.

Reverse Engineering. The first step in our analysis involved identifying the action trigger of bots, that is, what caused bots to act to disseminate a message. To do so, we employed a reverse engineering procedure to the Phase II data.<sup>11</sup> We followed a systematic approach to readying the data for this. First, all the information transferred by bots was examined with a graphic visualization of the total number of messages shared, grouped by day and type. This figure was useful for identifying behavioral patterns, and also provided insights on the platform features bots used to distribute information (e.g., Twitter’s retweet feature was frequently used by the bots in our sample). After creating the visual, we cleaned the data by changing every word to lowercase, and by removing all punctuation except hashtags and mentions. We also changed the data encoding to ASCII to eliminate accents, removed stop words in English and Portuguese, created a document matrix for the corpus of text, and calculated the frequency of terms.

Visualizing the frequency of terms provided us with a starting point for reproducing messages and helped us identify the bots’ action triggers. For example, the frequency table of the WorldCupRetweet bot showed the hashtag #WorldCup as the most recurrent term (appearing in 1,202 messages). This led us to reproduce, via a filtering process that used this hashtag, WorldCupRetweet’s messages which used this term. We then repeated the process using the second most frequent term. Once we reproduced all of its messages, we discovered that the trigger of WorldCupRetweet’s actions was based on two hashtags. Specifically, this bot was triggered to retweet tweets containing either the #WorldCup or #FIFAWorldCup hashtags. Table 3 shows the results of the reverse engineering procedure for all bots.

Since we detected bots in Phase I by analyzing messages with the #ChangeBrazil, #ChangeBrasil, and #MudaBrasil hashtags, we expected the action triggers of the identified bots to be associated with the protests in Brazil. But, as the World CupRetweet example shows, this was mostly not the case. In our sample, only one bot acted to disseminate information about the Brazil protests (#verasqueumfilhoteunaofogealuta, #todosunidosporumbrasilmelhor, and #forafeliciano were protest-specific hashtags (action triggers) retweeted by the Toni\_JoiaRara bot<sup>12</sup>). In spite of that, every bot forwarded protest information on to their followers. These findings suggest that bots can become active actors in social contexts about which they are not designed to disseminate information. Such a counterintuitive outcome is caused by two things. First, the design of the bots themselves, which involves autonomous retweets of tweets with particular terms ( i.e., hashtags, keywords, and mentions). Second, the actions of other actors on Twitter, which (knowingly or unknowingly) include bot terms in tweets they initiate on a different topic. Qualitative Analysis. After discovering the action trigger of bots, we used qualitative techniques to further analyze the Phase II data. Informed by the processual view of conduit brokerage, the role of automation, and a more detailed attention to the behavior of bots, we were able to identify two distinct types of prototypical behavior enacted by bots and elaborate the algorithmic processes of social alertness and social transmission that together provide the theoretical foundation for how bots disseminate information on Twitter.

## Findings

The two types of prototypical bot behaviors that we identified in the data are amplification and boundary spanning. While every bot in our sample was an amplifier, only two (BlanketRT and FreeportIL) were also boundary spanners. In what comes next, each type of prototypical bot behavior is characterized by its algorithmic processes of social alertness and social transmission. By focusing on these processes, we also describe and explain the triggers of bot actions, the sources and receivers of the information being disseminated by bots, the information itself, and the Twitter features utilized by bots. This approach accounts for the complexities involved in bots transferring information between actors, allowing us to develop a theoretical framework of algorithmic conduit brokerage.

## Prototypical Bot Behavior of Amplification

Figure 2 depicts the algorithmic conduit brokerage process of amplification. This behavior involves bot retweeting actions where tweets disseminated by users on Twitter are passed on to users following the bot and perhaps other non-followers (additional users) primarily because such tweets were appended with a particular term (e.g., hashtag) targeted by the bot.

Algorithmic Social Alertness. Tweets posted by other Twitter users that contained hashtags, keywords, and mentions included in bot scripts were the information bots searched for and discovered. The pieces of content (i.e., particular hashtags and keywords and specific user mentions) embedded in the tweets curated by these bots were qualitatively different from one another, and most importantly, were the trigger of their actions. Some bots searched and discovered tweets with hashtags on global and local protests (#Revolution and #VerasQueUmFilhoTeuNaoFogeALuta), sporting events (#WorldCup), activist groups (#Anonymous), and Hollywood celebrities (#BritneySpears), while others found tweets with particular keywords like nouns and verbs (Blanket and Share), nouns in different languages (Brazil and Brasil), and some even targeted themselves (@FreeportIL and @InstantRT). In this way, they collectively distributed information on a variety of topics and events.

Algorithmic Social Transmission. These bots distributed the tweets they curated to the larger Twittersphere by enacting Twitter’s retweet feature (link between A and C mediated by Bot in Figure 2). They automatically invoked the retweet feature when the conditional statements in their scripts returned true—that is, when the bot hashtag, keyword, or mention was discovered in a tweet posted by any of the thousands of Twitter users. ## Prototypical Bot Behavior of Boundary Spanning

Boundary spanning was the second type of prototypical bot behavior identified in the data (see Figure 3). Here, information dissemination occurs through bot tweeting actions where articles, videos, pictures, and phrases created outside of Twitter (e.g., on YouTube) are first curated from a platform, and sometimes reconfigured (to meet Twitter rules), and then embellished (with hashtags, keywords, and mentions) and distributed (via tweets) to users following the bot on Twitter and other non-bot followers. Such articles, videos, pictures, and phrases were disseminated by the bot because they came from a platform (e.g., LiveLeak<sup>15</sup>) linked to the bot account on Twitter.

Algorithmic Social Alertness. The information curated by bots enacting boundary spanning were the articles, videos, pictures, and phrases from external platforms. Before transferring information, these bots (or the platforms connected to the bots themselves) reconfigured what they curated. For example, the articles and videos from the LiveLeak platform were repeatedly arranged to only include headlines and links, presumably to meet Twitter’s character limits.

## Discussion

Our findings of a multiple case study of bot use on Twitter direct researchers’ attention to how bots disseminate information and pays specific attention to the different algorithmic social processes governing the activity of bots. It suggests that information dissemination by bots is often realized through ongoing social actions that emerge recurrently, even unexpectedly, in the everyday activity of actors sharing information online. The theoretical premise is that bot actions are generated through a cyber-social process, where the everyday activity of actors inside and outside of Twitter activate the bots’ scripts that act to transfer information between parties.

## Algorithmic Conduit Brokerage Theoretical Framework

We contribute to research on bots and information dissemination. A key contribution is an emergent theoretical framework that describes and explains how bots act to disseminate information on social platforms (see Figure 4). Prior research focuses on describing strategic actions used by bots for spreading information widely (Gorodnichenko et al. 2018; Shao et al. 2018; Stella et al. 2018), or providing theoretical accounts grounded on network structure (Boichak et al. 2018; Mønsted et al. 2017). We contribute by complementing these studies with an algorithmic conduit brokerage framework that (1) provides a more complete set of algorithmic social processes for which to study information dissemination by bots, (2) highlights important bot design features, and (3) adds insights into scaling capabilities. In what comes next, we elaborate on the implications of this framework, which begins with algorithmic social alertness, a particularly important process neglected by previous research.

Algorithmic Social Alertness. As our findings show, the action trigger of bots is a key design feature here. The action trigger is what makes bots socially alert for which kinds of information to curate and reconfigure as well as from what sources. Given the importance of action triggers to the social alertness process, future research can examine the effects of different choices of triggers (hashtags versus keywords versus mentions) to different information curation outcomes (e.g., volume, breadth, and relevance). Future research can also explore the characteristics of these action triggers (e.g., using general keyword terms such as “share” versus specific hashtag terms such as #WorldCup) that are consequential to different outcomes.

We also show that information curated internally from Twitter users (content trigger) was not reconfigured yet the information that came from the outside (platform trigger) was. This finding suggests that reconfiguration is enacted out of necessity and is associated with information location (internal versus external) and choice of action-trigger (content versus platform). Yet, we suggest the insight that reconfiguration can also be enacted from a strategic standpoint. For instance, bots can be designed to reduce cognitive effort by arranging information to foreground essential material (signaling principle) and background superfluous material (coherence principle) (see Knaflic 2015; Mautone and Mayer 2001; Mayer 2008; Tufte 2001). reconfiguration is to make sure that bots are designed to foster generative processing by adapting short headlines to fit a conversational style tailored to the user (e.g., your health may be at stake), rather than a formal style sent to all users (e.g., the health of all people may be at stake) (personalization principle). A conversational style can prime a sense of social presence (Moreno and Mayer 2004), which may cause users to pay attention and consume the information disseminated by the bot.

In addition to enhancing information processing and presentation, reconfiguration can also be used as a deliberate design choice to impact the subsequent movement of information. We speculate that bots adapting information to make it look more useful (e.g., by selecting quotes or presenting photos that emphasize the practical value of an article) and more visible or accessible (e.g., through the use of URL links that connect back to the external platform) are more likely to enhance these components in their messages, making them more likely to spread and become popular (Berger 2013). Leveraging the six ingredients of contagiousness (social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value, and stories; see Berger 2013) may be fruitful in deriving reconfiguration guidelines and predicting the effectiveness of different designs on users’ consumption and sharing of bot disseminated information. Given the novelty of the bot topic and the lack of research on algorithmic social alertness, we still know very little about what kinds of reconfiguration choices are more suitable than others, and what kinds of reconfiguration choices are actually detrimental for information processing and presentation, as well as for information sharing. Thus, more research on bot reconfiguration design is needed.

Brokerage research has indicated that sensemaking takes place within curation activity (Obstfeld 2017). In other words, brokers notice, bracket, and label what they find to determine which pieces of information are of interest and should therefore be moved from one place to another. Our findings show that static bots curate information without engaging in full circle sensemaking. While they initialize the sensemaking process by searching and finding information with specific terms or from a certain platform, static bots fail to evaluate what they find as a whole (e.g., in amplification, they only scan tweets for particular hashtags, mentions, or keywords, ignoring everything else embedded in the discovered tweets). As a result, they curate everything that they find, engaging in primitive sensemaking. This lack of human-like sensemaking on the part of the bots can result in what appears to be random action. The data further show that bots which are triggered by content (amplifiers) rather than an outside platform (boundary spanners) are more likely to disseminate information unrelated to a particular issue or theme. WorldCupRetweet is an example of that. The bot retweeted tweets about Brazil’s protests just because these tweets were embedded with the hashtags #FIFAWorldCup and #WorldCup.

Bots like WorldCupRetweet, which engage in amplification, have little control over how to discriminate among information from multiple sources. Savvy actors, attuned to this lack of control and in order to increase discoverability of their own information may intentionally append hashtags, keywords, and/or mentions that trigger bot action. This results in the bot disseminating information that substantially differs from what it had been designed to disseminate.<sup>18</sup> Based on the algorithmic process of social alertness we describe, one can infer that a content-triggered bot lacking the ability to make sense of information is very vulnerable to user manipulation.

Because human-like sensemaking is important for discriminating which kinds of information to curate and reconfigure, developing bots that are capable of making sense of information is another promising direction for future research. Identifying design choices—maybe in the form of rule-based filters, such as a list of blacklisted words implemented in the design of static bots, or through the use of reinforcement learning to teach human values to dynamic bots (see Riedl and Harrison, 2015)—can help safeguard or regulate amplifier bots from user abuse. Further, given recent trends in deep learning research (see Goodfellow et al. 2016; LeCun et al.

2015), we suspect that dynamic bots will be more effective than static bots at this type of sensemaking. Yet, whether human-like sensemaking is preferred over a more primitive rule-based filtering approach, especially for curating information of interest while also excluding unwanted text such as curse words, is a matter of context and objective as well as of error sensitivity (i.e., errors of inclusion of unwanted information and errors of exclusion of relevant information) and a fruitful avenue for future research.

The lack of information control is not a problem for boundary spanning, since, as our findings show, these bots are triggered by external platforms. The information they pass on to others is very much controlled (zero randomness), presumably by the platform owners themselves. Yet a platform trigger has its limitations as well. Unfortunately, it is possible for platform owners to feed bots with biased (or one-sided) information, subjecting them to echo chambers. Indeed, this sort of bias has already been documented in prior studies (see Gorod nichenko et al. 2018). One way to avoid bot echo chambers is to design bots to curate information from multiple platforms with different points of views, or to develop bots like FreeportIL, which was connected to LiveLeak, a platform that already does that. Of course, creating bot echo chambers (or spreading misinformation and disinformation) may be a desired goal which can be achieved by coding bots to disseminate information from platforms that match this objective. Additional studies that investigate and report such action possibilities can provide significant contributions to research in bot misinformation and are thus very much needed.

Algorithmic Social Transmission. After bots curate and reconfigure information, they enact the algorithmic process of social transmission. Here, bots embellish and distribute what was curated and reconfigured. Again, while our findings show that bots only embellish information in boundary spanning, we suggest that it is entirely possible and actually desirable for bots to embellish information in amplification as well. Embellishment is important because, as previous studies show, it allows all bots to reach a wider audience (i.e., more receivers) through enhanced discoverability and directed targeting (Shao et al. 2018; Stella et al. 2018).

Here, we discuss three types of embellishment extrapolated from the findings. Hashtag hijacking (see VanDam and Tan 2016), or the embellishment of information with popular hashtags (what we refer to as hijacked embellishment in Table 8), enables bots to boost the discoverability of information since users of social media follow popular hashtags<sup>19</sup> and utilize popular hashtags to categorize their own messages and more easily search for relevant topics and events.<sup>20</sup> This kind of embellishment was present in the data. Both FreeportIL and BlanketRT hijacked several hashtags (e.g., #News for FreeportIL and #NYC for BlanketRT). Similarly, the embellishment of information with popular keywords (what we call lexical embellishment) and popular hashtags further increases the discoverability of information transmitted by bots by connecting the information to timely events (e.g., LiveLeak #News #ChangeBrazil), global trends (e.g., LiveLeak #News #ClimateChange), and ongoing macro (and micro) conversations (e.g., LiveLeak #News #MondayMotivation) among other topics and events, causing the information to appear more attractive by seeming more relevant and more diffusible. As was shown in the data, only FreeportIL engaged in this type of embellishment.

Both hijacked and lexical types of embellishment attend to the trigger ingredient of contagiousness proposed by Berger (2013) in that they increase the probability of sharing by relating the information disseminated by the bot to popular ideas and topics in the social platform. Clearly and similarly to reconfiguration, the six ingredients of contagiousness are germane for embellishment. We suspect that bots designed to add high arousal information (such as hashtags and keywords or even full sentences invoking feelings of awe, excitement, amusement, anger, or anxiety) into the tweets or retweets they disseminate will be capable of spreading their messages more widely than those who do not, ceteris paribus (Berger and Milkman 2012). Although prior research has documented the use of high negative arousal information from bots during the Catalan referendum (Stella et al. 2018), it is unclear whether such information spread faster and further than its counterpart. As a result, we need more bot embellishment research that furthers understanding of dissemination and contagion.

Embellishing information with mentions (or targeted embellishment) is another subtle, but significant, change that augments dissemination by enabling bots to directly target influential users (e.g., celebrities) and actively broker the same piece of information between more actors. BlanketRT targeted three different users in one of the messages it repeatedly disseminated. Yet, we do not know if these users were actual celebrities or not. Regardless of the celebrity status of these users, it is important to note that the focus of targeted embellishment is not on the information itself, and how to make a tweet more emotional or practically useful so that it becomes more diffusible. Rather, the focus is on exploiting the social network of the targeted users, and in making the tweet more diffusible by increasing its exposure in the social platform. As a result, theories about the structure of social networks (Aral and Van Alstyne 2011; Burt 1982; Granovetter 1973) can be useful for deciding who the bot should target and why.

While one objective of different types of embellishment can be discoverability and the further movement of information, another can be endorsement. Of particular interest is the embellishment of information for social media campaigns embracing calls for celebrity endorsement and participation. Celebrity endorsement, or the conferring of celebrity attention, was present in the data with the hijacking of the #BritneySpears hashtag from instant\_RT. What is less known, and perhaps more difficult to accomplish, is how to design a bot like instant\_RT to attract attention to itself and recruit celebrities like Britney Spears to join and support certain campaigns as does a master-of-ceremonies (see Miranda et al. 2015). Hijacked, lexical, and targeted embellishments (e.g., #BritneySpears, Britney Spears, and @britneyspears) seem to be one way for bots to attract attention. Whether such strategies work or not, and which types of embellishment are more effective under different conditions and for different outcomes, is an empirical question worthy of investigation. Theories of attention (e.g., Broadbent 1958; Moray 1959; Treisman 1964), if further refined to the bot context, can help to inform hypotheses of embellishment and should therefore be considered as a potential lens for these studies.

The algorithmic processes of social alertness and social transmission are unique to bots. As illustrated in the data, they depict two reliable processes that never deviate from the rules. When put together into the algorithmic conduit brokerage framework, such processes offer a consistent vocabulary via which to more cohesively integrate and organize the findings of prior research, making it easier to identify new opportunities for future work (see Table 9). As mentioned earlier, prior research shows that bots post links to articles, retweet others, and even target influential users while sharing information on Twitter (Shao et al. 2018; Stella et al. 2018). Understood from the framework in Figure 4, these results altogether illustrate the algorithmic processes of social alertness (links through reconfiguration) and social transmission (targeted embellishment) with the goal to enhance information processing and presentation as well as discoverability and reach. Finally, the evidence of fast spread of information seen in previous studies (e.g., Gorodnichenko et al. 2018) epitomizes rapid scaling, which we discuss next.

## Rapid Scaling: The Epitome of Bot Behavior

Another contribution of this paper is a new concept, rapid scaling. Prior research has indicated that bots spread information very fast when they post, share, reply, and target influential users on social media (Gorodnichenko et al. 2018; Shao et al. 2018; Stella et al. 2018). Our insight is that bot dissemination is very scalable because of automation, and in many occasions, unhuman-like because of rapid scaling (think of TeamRevoltNow, and how it retweeted 34 tweets in the span of a minute).

Since bots are good at mimicking human behavior (e.g., the chatbot “Rose” winning the Loebner Prize in 2014 and 2015), the concept of automation alone is not useful, nor precise enough, for differentiating human activity from bot activity. Rapid scaling, however, further epitomizes what is unique about bot activity in places like Twitter because humans just cannot, even if they aspired to, consistently retweet more than 30 tweets in the span of a minute. Thus, because automation enables rapid scaling, and rapid scaling is part of what makes bots unhuman, we suggest that rapid scaling is not limited to algorithmic social transmission, as evidenced by our data (see the thick line in Figures 2 and 3), but is also applicable to algorithmic social alertness, including curating activities such as searching, discovering, and reconfiguring<sup>21</sup> large corpora of information (potentially thousands of social media messages or articles, videos, and phrases) for all instances of triggers (see thick lines in Figure 4). For bots (unlike humans), performing repetitive tasks, analyzing large amount of unstructured data, and handling routine conditional checks is familiar, and simply part of what they do best.

It is important to note that rapid scaling is not necessarily associated with network effects such as the diffusion of certain messages across Twitter, but rather with the actions of bots designed to disseminate information in social platforms. As such, its outcome (whether of curation and reconfiguration or embellishment and distribution) is always high volume and not necessarily high reach, although both are certainly possible (e.g., a bot curating and distributing thousands of messages in the span of minutes which are then quickly spread by thousands of users).

In sum, while rapid scaling enables us to identify what is unique about bot behavior, the algorithmic conduit brokerage framework allows us to disentangle bot actions of algorithmic social alertness that focus on decisions of curating and reconfiguring information from those of algorithmic social transmission that focus on decisions on how to distribute and embellish what was curated and reconfigured and tease apart their effects and the interplay of the two. This separation of processes is not only important for research, as discussed earlier, but also germane for issues of privacy and ethics. Although some bots may curate personal information in ethical ways, they may also distribute what they curate in unethical ways. Conversely, a bot may be designed to curate and potentially misuse personal information from a certain user without their knowledge and consent, and never actually distribute such information to others. Knowing which process is the source of unethicalness is crucial because it can help developers and regulators to stop it altogether and even prevent it from happening in the future. Researchers have already begun to study bot ethics (see Salge and Berente 2017), but more work is clearly needed.

## Conduit Brokerage Versus Algorithmic Conduit Brokerage

A final contribution is the contextualization of conduit brokerage to algorithms and bots. We contextualize Obstfeld’s notion of conduit brokerage to algorithmic conduit brokerage with the objective to understand how bots disseminate information on Twitter. In doing so we contribute to the original theory in two ways. First, we expand the domain of the theory to nonhuman actors by contextualizing constructs to bots, adding constructs unique to bots (action triggers), and demonstrating logical consistency for these concepts in relation to the framework we offer. Second, we provide clear and precise definitions for under-defined concepts, mentioned in passing by Obstfeld and his colleagues (reconfiguration and embellishment). Table D1 in Appendix D provides additional information about our contributions to conduit brokerage.

It is also clear that algorithmic conduit brokerage is distinct from the conduit brokerage perspective. A notable difference is one of action consistency. Because algorithms lack the human ability of sensemaking and are instead fueled by automation and action triggers rather than emotions, algorithmic conduit brokerage is more reliable than conduit brokerage. Further, automation increases the speed and scale of information transfer, making algorithmic conduit brokerage not only more consistent but also faster and more extensive. ## Scope Conditions

The particular types of prototypical bot behaviors identified in this research—amplification and boundary spanning—are obviously specific to eight cases (bots) within one social platform (Twitter). However, the emergent theoretical framework of two intertwined algorithmic processes outlined here— social alertness and social transmission—are generalizable and offered as stimulus for future research. As indicated earlier, this research is limited by the lack of dynamic bots. Studies that allow observation of dynamic bots would clearly extend algorithmic conduit brokerage by identifying addi tional curating activities bots use to disseminate information. Our framework was developed with respect to Twitter bots. However, we believe that the notion of algorithmic conduit brokerage is likely applicable to other types of interactions for information transfer on social platforms. Thus, future studies should examine how different network dynamics and platform features influence the algorithmic social processes used by bots.

While Twitter was relatively favorable for disseminating information, one can suppose, for example, that in a bidirectionally organized or more rigidly controlled social platform the sorts of information transfers enacted by these bots would be different. Consider Reddit as an example, where content is shared by users through a hierarchy of topic-centric communities, also known as subreddits. Users join the topiccentric communities they are interested in, and converse with others in these communities, who are also presumably interested in the same topic. Unlike Twitter, where very different topics can be curated accidentally and disseminated by a bot to the entire Twittersphere, posts in the subreddits are typically confined to the specific community and not disseminated to the entire Reddit-sphere. Although it is certainly possible for bots to curate information from a particular subreddit and distribute it to another (boundary spanning within the social platform), it is very unlikely for these bots to curate and distribute unrelated information about a topic from within that topic’s subreddit (perhaps undesired amplification), which is surely possible on Twitter (e.g., the WorldCupRetweet bot disseminated information on a variety of topics unrelated to the FIFA World Cup). In sum, because social media platforms are inherently different from one another, in terms of structure and user interaction, it is not always reasonable to a priori presume that bot activity in one particular platform mimics bot activity in another.

## Conclusion

While bots have become pervasive, our understanding of how they disseminate information is still limited. The few existing studies have generated important insights, yet they do not account for bot design characteristics, are descriptive in nature, are grounded on the structure of social networks, or are focused on information distribution, neglecting information curation. We hope that the algorithmic conduit brokerage framework that we have developed (and its constituent processes of social alertness and social transmission) stimulates future research on bots especially on the impact of bot design choices and is generative of research on bot design principles.

## An Exposition of Each of the Eight Bots

Intrigued by some of the outcomes of bot action (e.g., the fact that bots can become active actors in social contexts they are not triggered to disseminate information about), we decided to further explore the data to enrich our understanding of the bots themselves. We started our exploration in alphabetical order with Anon\_RT. It became clear to us early in the process of reverse engineering that Anon\_RT acted to distribute information about Anonymous, a network of hacktivists opposing Internet censorship and control. The bot broadcast (via retweets) four different hashtags: #anonymous (N = 584), #yal (N = 285)—a hashtag for youranonlive, #yan (N = 78)—an abbreviation for youranonnews, and #opisrael (N = 17)—a hashtag used to describe cyber-attacks against Israel coordinated by anons. Through text mining, we found that Anon\_RT was configured on RoundTeam and that the company automatically tweeted from Anon\_RT’s account (N = 34). These tweets were identical, and included the following text: “contributed tweets for @anonjak are delivered by @roundteam http://t.co/bquqioldta.” By reading the messages distributed by Anon\_RT, we further discovered that the bot heavily engaged in social activism, participating in politica protests in places like Brazil (20 cents), Syria (chemical weapons), Indonesia (fuel hike), and Turkey (Gezi Park) to name a few. Some actors were also asking for Anon\_RT’s help in these protests. For example, on June 18 at 4:59 p.m., dinhotech wrote: “@anon\_rt ajuda com rtweet? : muito bom o video : http://t.co/Ytb9yKZhUj #foradilma #todosunidosporumbrasilmelhor #vamosprotestar #anonymous.” In this message, dinhotech is protesting against Brazil’s former president Dilma Rousseff, claiming the video linked in his message is very good, and asking for Anon\_RT to help spread the content to others with a retweet. Finally, most of the messages distributed by Anon\_RT were in English (89%) with the remaining (11%) being in Portuguese.<sup>22</sup>

BlanketRT was the next bot that we analyzed. This bot was triggered to disseminate information with the keywords “share” (N = 2,756) and “blanket” (N = 41), also via retweets. Most of the blanket messages were referenced to #BlanketRT (N = 39), a hashtag of the bot’s Twitte handle. In addition, a large portion of the messages we collected about BlanketRT (N = 993) were not generated by the bot. One was a reply (“@blanketrt thanks to share our like, share & win action! did you take part in it? we wish you good luck ;) #share #win #like #facebook” from beyounica on June 17 at 12:21 p.m.) while the others were mentions (N = 11), and hashtags (N = 981). We also discovered that BlanketRT repeatedly distributed two messages about music (“[mixtape] no name nephew http://t.co/zgkp8d1rdi @indytapes @djhoodmixtape @nephew901 #soundcloud #reverbnation #share #rt #wshh #life” and “king d royalty http://t.co/p0q9qgb6do ... #atlanta #atl #miami #like #retweet #share #djs #dj #london #paris #nyc #ny #dc #va #pa #newyork”), in addition to distributing the same two other messages over and over again. The first one describes the importance of showers in people’s lives: “showers are amazing. they make you feel nice and clean, make you sound like a professional singer, and help you make all of life's decision!” while the second associates twitter functionalities with specific technology devices “#rt for #iphone, #fav for #android, #followme for #desktop.” Together, the two tweets were disseminated 189 times (95 and 94 respectively) in a period of six days (about 15 times in a particular day).

Like Anon\_RT, a great majority of the information disseminated by BlanketRT was in English (95%), with the rest being mostly Indonesian (about 4.7%). It turns out that many of the Indonesian messages retweeted by BlanketRT were coming from SATU (an organization aimed to “contribute to improving the quality of the Indonesian people through integrated initiative, creativity, and work to provide added value for the progress of the Indonesian people”<sup>23</sup>) and camera360, a camera application for iPhone and Android. Camera360 allows users to share photos directly from its application onto social media sites like Twitter. When the sharing of the photo on Twitter comes from the application, the hashtag #share gets added to the tweet with the “from camera360” words (i.e., #share from camera360). Here, #share was clearly what triggered the bot retweets. An example message is “permen paling enaakkkk...!!! beliin yg bnyk dong :3 #share from camera360# http://t.co/ygkvlullss,” which, according to google, translates to “the most amazing candy ... !!! buy a lot please: 3 #share from camera360 # http://t.co/ygkvlullss.” This particular message was tweeted by ivannaaaaaay and retweeted by BlanketRT on June 21 at 6:09 a.m. In the case of SATU, the tweets were coming from cyberkatrox, an Indonesian blogger posting links to news from SATU, and adding several hashtags to these tweets, including #share. An example message retweeted by BlanketRT from this specific user is “http://t.co/gehkx61brt #satu\_indonesia #kaskus #digg #retwee #lintasinfo #news #advertisement #indonesia #share #instagram #socialmedia # race.” In sum, BlanketRT acted to disseminate messages with “share” and “blanket” along with information about music, showers, and social media in general

The third bot we examined was BrasilRetwittes. As its Twitter’s screen name suggests, the bot distributed information about Brazil in that it retweeted messages with Brasil (N = 1,989) and Brazil (N = 713). Like Anon\_RT, BrasilRetwittes was designed on RoundTeam. Ninety tweets were automatically published by the company on the bot’s account. These tweets followed the same format as the ones disseminated in Anon\_RT’s account (e.g., “contributed tweets for @brasilretwittes are delivered by @roundteam http://t.co/jgbq8rggeb”). Moreover, BrasilRetwittes was mentioned 12 times and replied-to in 13 messages. Because the terms triggering the actions of this bot were somewhat related to the Brazil protests (clearly, Brazil was the place in which these protests occurred), we found that many of the messages retweeted by BrasilRetwittes were indeed about the Brazil protests (even though many of them were not embedded with the hashtags (#changebrazil, #changebrasil, and #mudabrasil) that we used to collect data about the protests in Phase I; recall that the data in Phase I are not representative of all of the protest data on Twitter). An example is the following tweet “excellent curation of last night's protests in brazil by @globalvoices @gvlusofonia team (in portuguese) http://t.co/fysebt…” from martaruco, which was retweeted by BrasilRetwittes on June 21 at 7:01 a.m. only because it had the term “Brazil” in it. Finally, because both Brazil with a “z” and Brasil with an “s” were the terms triggering this bot’s actions, we found that BrasilRetwittes distributed information in both English and Portuguese, with most of the messages (about 68%) being in Portuguese.

FreeportIL was the next actor we further explored. Initially, we found a total of 21 messages not generated by the bot—they were all mentioning @FreeportIL. Four of these though were actually retweeted by FreeportIL. We also noticed, by reviewing the bot’s Twitter activity, that most of its tweets were news coming from a British video sharing website called liveleaks.<sup>24</sup> Because FreeportIL repeatedly distributed information (mostly in English, 99%) from the same source in a seemingly standardized manner, we developed a hunch that the bot acted to disseminate liveleak news. We judged the validity of our hunch in two probes. First, we counted how many messages generated by FreeportIL embedded a link. If our suspicion was robust we had to at least observe a connection from the bot’s information to the video sharing website. Indeed, we found that all tweets had links. But, were they all associated with liveleaks? To answer this question, we fetched the redirects of all links. Our results show that 562 out of the 569 links came from liveleaks while the remaining 7 came from enterprisewaywire, an “online video curation platform” allowing enterprises to “aggregate video content and integrate it with their video production efforts.”<sup>25</sup> Because nearly 99 percent of FreeportIL’s messages were news feeds from liveleaks, we concluded that the bot acted to transfer information from the British website to Twitter, and specifically towards the bot followers. Similar to Anon\_RT, we found that FreeportIL also disseminated information about other protests (e.g., Turkey, Syria, G8, etc.). For example, on June 18 at 7:10 a.m., the bot tweeted: “Turkey Protests 2013 - Soul Battles http://t.co/4uh2awy919 LiveLeak #News.”

The fifth case we selected for further analysis was Instant\_RT. This bot is interesting because some of its design properties are provided in its profile description (see Table 1). For example, its biography alludes to the idea that every message posted by a subscriber with the hashtag #instantrt is likely to be distributed by the bot. Yet, our analysis show that most of its generated content does not include #instantrt. Instead, they are retweets (in English, for the most part, i.e., 97%) which contain the mention @instant\_rt (N = 4) and the hashtag #britneyspears (N = 1,205), a reference to Britney Spears, an American singer, dancer, and actress. We also discovered that, similar to Anon\_RT and BrasilRetwittes, Instant\_RT was also deployed by RoundTeam. Forty-one of its messages were automatically shared by the service company (in the same exact format as they were for Anon\_RT and BrasilRetwittes). The remaining piece of content was a reply (“@instant\_RT What is your favorite song of Britney Spears?”) from MariaMinina2 on June 16 at 4:59 p.m. In short, we found that Instant\_RT is a bot acting to distribute information about Britney Spears.

TeamRevoltNow is our sixth case. In reading TeamRevoltNow’s messages, we quickly learned that similar to Anon\_RT, this bot heavily engaged in social activism, sharing information about a variety of political revolutions worldwide. This is not surprising given the bot’s profile description (see Table 1). What is surprising, however, is that by retweeting messages with the hashtag #revolution (3,775 out of 3,965 which is about 95%), TeamRevoltNow also distributed messages in topics like music and television. For example, the bot retweeted 82 messages from Brazen Providence, a hip-hop/alternative band from New York claiming to be the revolution in rock and rap.<sup>26</sup> The message repeatedly shared by TeamRevoltNow was about Bled Dry, a music video produced by the band and launched on March 19 of 2013: “5.5 #millionviews join the #revolution watch #providence - #bleddry on #youtube #rockraprevolution, thanks4support! http://t.co/svxsyeztck.” Moreover, by reading the messages from TeamRevoltNow, we discovered that the bot was disseminating information about a television show called revolution which aired its first and second seasons in 2012, 2013, and 2014. For example, on June 19 of 2013 at 1:54 a.m. (nearly two weeks after the last episode of season 1 was aired), TeamRevoltNow retweeted the following tweet from ryanolivieri\_: “i've never seen or heard of a tv show that killed off half of its main cast in the first season as #revolution so stupid.” We also found a total of 5 replies and 65 mentions for this bot. Most of the messages from TeamRevoltNow were in English (93%) while the remaining were in Portuguese, French, Indonesian, and Turkish. Finally, TeamRevoltNow was another bot designed by RoundTeam—the company published 93 messages on the bot’s behalf.

Toni\_JoiaRara is yet another bot configured on RoundTeam. The company automatically generated 59 tweets for Toni\_JoiaRara. This bot was specifically acting to distribute information about Brazil’s 2013 protests. Its account shared messages mostly in Portuguese (89%) and with protest-specific hashtags such as #verasqueumfilhoteunaofogealuta (N = 1,447)—a phrase from Brazil’s national anthem that says “you will see that no son of yours will run from a fight” (i.e., the fight here is the protest); #todosunidosporumbrasilmelhor (N = 214) which translates to “everyone united for a better Brazil”; #forafeliciano (N = 145)—a reference against congressman Marco Feliciano who proposed the gay cure bill during the protests; #semviolencia (N = 2) which means “no violence”; and @toni\_joiarara (N = 6), a mention to the bot itself. In addition, Toni\_JoiaRara shared messages mentioning #lacerda (N = 6), last name of a Brazilian actor, Thiago Lacerda, who played Toni in the telenovela Joia Rara which was aired by Brazil’s TV network Globo in 2013. In the telenovela, Toni was an Italian activist that fought and protested for the working class. Finally, direnaklim demonstrated solidarity with the protests in a reply to the bot on June 19 (“@toni\_joiarara solidarity from #brazil & #turkey http://t.co/ot06cuvzwi”).

The last bot we examined was WorldCupRetweet. Like BrasilRetwittes, this actor’s screen name exposes its design properties. Specifically, we found that WorldCupRetweet acted to disseminate information about FIFA’s World Cup by retweeting, messages with the hashtags #worldcup (N = 1,202) and #fifaworldcup (N = 69). We also found a total of three tweets that were not generated by the bot. In the first one rleaguefan notices the actor’s bot nature. Rleaguefan posted “bahaha. this stupid robot retweeted me about the naming of the women's #rugbyleague #worldcup squad @worldcupretweet http://t.co/2wy79ycaq8” on June 20 at 9:30 a.m. In the second tweet, we discovered that WorldCupRetweet was also designed on RoundTeam. Againstthemosq shared, on the night of June 21, the following message: “rt @worldcupretweet contributed tweets for @worldcupretweet are delivered by @roundteam http://t.co/3ova9h8an0.” Finally, in the third message, realsamthompson asks WorldCupRetweet for a vote in the night of June 20: “@worldcupretweet world cup can you vote for me https://t.co/44njovabz8 go leader boards type sam thompson click vote.” Since the hashtags triggering the action of WorldCupRetweet were in English, most of the information distributed by the bot was also in English (96%). ## How Boundary Spanner Bots Transfer Information

As is shown in Figure C1, FreeportIL distributed information on Twitter about an article entitled “Criminally prosecute Judge Hagai Brenner!” (“Article Headline in Tweet”) which it pulled or received from LiveLeak since the link in FreeportIL’s tweet (http://t.co/9yRaDXcOrPv) redirects users back to LiveLeak, and specifically to a page with additional information on this same article (https://www.liveleak.com/view?i=33e\_1371368488&utm\_source=dlvr.it&utm\_medium=twitter). Upon further investigation, we observed that the page for the “Criminally prosecute Judge Hagai Brenner!” article on LiveLeak also has a link of its own (http://inproperinla.blogspot.co.il/2013/06/13-06-16-toward-first-anniversary-of.html). After clicking on this second link, we found that the article tweeted by FreeportIL from LiveLeak was actually initially posted on the In Pro Per In L.A. blog<sup>27</sup> (“Article Body”) by Joseph Zernik, also known as the Human Rights Alert (NGO)<sup>28</sup> user (“Article Author”), on June 16 of 2013 at 12:40am (“Article Date” and “Article Time”). While we do not have data on the logic used by LiveLeak to aggregate information from platforms like In Pro Per In L.A., we observe (in the data that we do have) a clear sequence of actions illustrating how bots like FreeportIL transfer information across boundaries – from externa platforms (e.g., from In Pro Per In L.A. blog to LiveLeak or just directly from LiveLeak) to Twitter (e.g,, toward FreeportIL’s followers).
