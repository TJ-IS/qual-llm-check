# Mobile App Startups and New Entry Threats: The Role of Performance Signals

Franck Soh, $^{1}$ Varun Grover $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Neeley School of Business, Texas Christian University, Fort Worth, Texas, USA, f.l.sohnoume@tcu.edu $^{2}$ Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, Arkansas, USA, vgrover@uark.edu

## Abstract

Mobile platforms offer significant entrepreneurial opportunities for the creation of mobile app startups. Investors who are backing these startups have a vested interest in seeing their mobile apps succeed. However, the market for mobile apps is very competitive, partly due to the threat of new entrants. This study examines how mobile app startups can proactively mitigate new entry threats to ensure their survival. We propose a research model that examines the effect of performance signals on new entry threats. We distinguish between substantive (nonrhetorical) signals provided by investors and consumers and rhetorical signals provided by the mobile app startups themselves on social media platforms. We suggest that while mobile app startups have less control over substantive signals such as maturity stage and level of consumer attention, they should send rhetorical signals over social media in the “right” way to reduce new entry threats. We posit that these signals include utilitarian and hedonic cues, and their effectiveness depends on substantive signals. Our model was tested using a rich longitudinal data set of over 10,000 startup mobile apps. The findings mostly support our hypotheses. We discuss the research contributions and practical implications of how mobile app startups can effectively use social media to diminish new entry threats.

Keywords: New Entry Threats, Signals, Startups, Consumer Attention, Utilitarian, Hedonic

## 1 Introduction

Mobile platforms, through two-sided network effects, provide an attractive framework for complementors to engage in app innovation. Also, mobile platform providers can directly stimulate innovation creation through actions such as platform openness initiatives through the release of new APIs (e.g., ARKIT, and COREML) (Kankanhalli et al., 2015) and the entry of the platform owner in complementary markets (Foerderer et al., 2018; Wen & Zhu, 2019). Because of this, mobile platforms are characterized by a high rate of new entry. For example, the Google Play Store and the App Store received a daily average of 3,623, and 1,380 new apps, respectively, from March to May 2020 (42matters, 2020). Such high new entry poses significant threats to incumbent complementors. These threats are prospective, as new entrants $^{1}$ materialize in the future and hinder performance (Brav et al., 2005; Goolsbee & Syverson, 2008; Pan et al., 2019). They create a highly volatile and competitive market, wherein incumbent complementors face pressure to sustain their competitive advantage (see Wiggins & Ruefli 2005).

We define mobile app startups as newly established businesses wherein the business model is built around mobile apps as the main products (e.g., Snap Inc.). Unlike amateurs (i.e., developers $^{2}$ who create mobile apps on an unpaid basis), $^{3}$ investor-funded mobile app startups (mobile app startups henceforth) have pressure from investors to increase financial performance. Besides investors, the viability of mobile app startups is important for the economy due to the associated revenue growth, innovation (high R&D investments), job creation, and high wages (Wu & Atkinson, 2017). For example, employment in such technology-based startups increased by 20% from 2007 to 2016. Since only about 25% of venture-backed startups succeed financially (Seth, 2018), new entry threats are particularly important. It is especially important to understand the factors that might mitigate the exposure of mobile app startups to new entry threats.

Existing research on new entry tends to emphasize how incumbents respond to new entry threats. Pan et al. (2019) indicated that incumbents in the IT industry responded to threats from startups by reducing their R&D spending. Ethiraj and Zhou (2019) demonstrated that premium and low-cost incumbent Southwest Airlines responded to entry threats differently in terms of capacity deterrence. In the context of mobile platforms, Wen and Zhu (2019) observed that Google's entry created threats to incumbent developers, who responded by reducing innovation and exploiting their existing consumer base by increasing app pricing. In contrast, Foerderer et al. (2018) demonstrated that the entry of Google in the photo app category stimulated innovation by threatened photo app developers. While there is a growing literature on new entry in the mobile platform literature, the focus seems limited to reactive approaches, i.e., how incumbent complementors respond to threats from new entry. While reactive approaches are important to understanding future performance, incumbent complementors can also be proactive by reducing their exposure to new entry threats. Proactive approaches are highlighted in the strategic management literature, where some studies discuss the impact of overcoming entry barriers (Castellaneta et al., 2020; Robinson & Mcdougall, 2001; Skiti, 2020). However, they have not received much attention in the mobile platform literature.

We address this research gap by investigating how mobile app startups mitigate their exposure to new entry threats through signaling in a hypercompetitive market. Specifically, using a signaling perspective, we examine the impact of the signals regarding the performance of mobile app startups on new entry threats. The signals can be sent internally by mobile app startups or externally by other stakeholders such as consumers and investors. Mobile app startups have less control over signals sent externally, while the signals sent internally are voluntary, as mobile app startups choose the types of performance-related information to disclose. Since mobile app startups have more control over the signals they send, it is important for them to voluntarily disclose performance-related signals in the “right way” (described later) to reduce their exposure to new entry threats. Consider a mobile app startup X, which has developed a mobile photo editing app that can seamlessly blend two faces into a new image. The startup has venture funding, has gone through successful prototyping, and is currently in the commercialization stage. Photo editing is extremely competitive, given the hundreds of existing mobile apps. The mobile app startup X is thus concerned that their app may be vulnerable to imitation in this crowded competitive space. It needs to promote the app on social media but wonders whether to emphasize the emotional content (e.g., how the images are exciting and fun) or the utilitarian value (e.g., how the images can allow couples to envision future children or how the images can be used to prevent identity fraud).

Signals sent internally can be received separately or jointly with the signals sent externally. We argue that the voluntary disclosure of the right performance-related information enables mobile app startups to inform competitors of their superiority, hence discouraging future rivals $^{4}$ (Darrough & Stoughton, 1990; Gigler, 1994; Li, 2010). There is growing evidence that firms can influence competitors' development of new products through signaling on social media. Bashir et al. (2017) reported the following quote from a senior product specialist:

We do some primary research on social media. I think it plays a very vital role in terms of many things, but yes, the most obvious to understand what our competitors are doing and how people are perceiving our competitor's products. We use social media and other digital media to know how we can better improve our brands in competition with other businesses. (Senior Product Specialist).

We built on signaling theory to formulate our hypotheses (Isidro & Marques, 2016). We studied the case of the voluntary disclosure of performance-related information (app merits) on social media by mobile app startups. Following prior literature (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018), we adopted a holistic approach arguing that signals sent by mobile app startups are not processed in isolation but jointly with signals sent externally, suggesting that the voluntary disclosure of performance-related information is effective at conveying signals about performance superiority in the mobile app market when combined with signals sent externally by consumers and investors. We tested the research model by collecting a large time-series dataset about mobile app startups and their apps released from June 2009 to June 2018. We conducted a panel vector autoregression and our findings mostly corroborate our hypotheses, thereby providing guidance on how mobile app startups can use social media to mitigate new entry threats.

The rest of the paper is organized into four sections. First, we present the theoretical background of the study in the second section. Second, we provide the research model and develop the hypotheses. Third, we describe the research design and report the results of the analyses in the next section. Finally, we discuss the contributions, implications, and limitations of the study.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Signaling Theory

Signaling theory provides an effective theoretical lens to understand decision-making in the presence of information gaps in the market (Spence, 1974). It indicates that one entity (e.g., individual, group, organization) can restrict information gaps by sending signals (i.e., credible information) about its quality to another entity. Signals represent an entity's attribute or activity that communicates information to other entities (Spence, 1974) and carry distinct informational value based on their ability to distinguish high-quality from low-quality senders. Notably, signals have high informational value when they are more costly for low-quality senders compared to high-quality senders (Bergh et al., 2014). An example of signals with high information value is education credentials because low-ability job seekers are less likely to obtain them compared to high-ability job seekers.

Prior studies using signaling theory identify several signals including ventures' attributes (e.g., top management backgrounds, founder ownership) and actions (e.g., funding, earning claims) that convey information to outsiders about the venture's quality (Busenitz et al., 2005; Higgins & Gulati, 2006; Hsu, 2007; Michael, 2009). Organizational outsiders (e.g., investors, potential consumers, competitors) (Drover et al., 2017; Reuer et al., 2012) rely on costly signals to infer the unobserved quality of ventures and respond appropriately for example through purchasing or investing decisions.

Closely related to our research is competitive signaling, which is built on the premise that sending signals influences competitive behavior (Heil & Robertson, 1991). The sender's competitors can directly or indirectly infer the sender's intentions, motives, goals, strengths, or weaknesses. For example, competitors can infer senders' product quality, employees' productivity, or intentions for changing pricing (Heil & Robertson, 1991). Prior literature on signaling explains how a focal firm's signals influence rivals' actions (e.g., development of new products) (e.g., Chen et al., 2017). In Table 1, we present different examples of competitors' responses to various senders' signals. The role of rhetorical signals in competitive signaling is largely unknown.

## 2.2 Rhetorical Signals

Rhetorical signals comprise language-based information and emphasize the use of rhetoric to convey information. Through rhetorical signals, firms promote the features of their products to gain greater visibility in the market (Giorgi & Weber, 2015). Gaining visibility might increase consumer demand while deterring or attracting future competition. Notably, promoting product features might lead to imitation by other firms unless the market superiority of the firm is established. Rhetorical signals however might not be called reliable signals, as they are equally costly for firms with low- and high-quality products and hence ineffective (Bergh et al., 2014; Cadsby, Frank, & Maksimovic, 1990; Farrell & Rabin, 1996). Despite being unreliable signals, they convey important information to consumers, investors, and indirectly to competitors.

While traditional signaling theory downplays rhetorical signals, management research recognizes their importance, particularly when they are not treated in isolation (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018). Notably, firms send multiple signals (both rhetorical and substantive $^{5}$ ) and receivers might not process signals in isolation. As receivers process signals holistically, there is a need to study several signals and their interactions. Holistic processing justifies the significance of rhetorical signals by explaining how rhetorical signals influence the impact of substantive signals. Recipients value rhetorical signals when the credibility of substantive signals can be extended to rhetorical signals (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018).

Prior research categorizes firm-generated content in several ways, e.g., information and brand personality (I/B) (Lee et al., 2018) and hedonic and utilitarian (H/U) categorizations (Mu et al., 2021). The H/U categorization emerges from the dichotomy between affective and instrumental approaches to consumers' information processing. Rooted in psycholinguistics and communication (e.g., Austin, 1962; Chitturi et al., 2007; Searle, 1976; Wittgenstein, 1953), it emphasizes different consumer engagement behaviors associated with the hedonic and utilitarian characteristics of firm-generated content (Mu et al., 2021). Hedonic clues stimulate consumers' affection towards the products, while utilitarian clues communicate specific instrumental benefits about the products. Hedonic clues are product-related information conveyed by the firm to appeal to the emotions of recipients, and utilitarian clues are product-related information (e.g., product-oriented facts) delivered to appeal to the reason of recipients.

The information contained in hedonic and utilitarian clues is processed differently by the consumers. System I and System II delineate two distinct types of information processing. System I processing is emotional, intuitive, and adequate for quick judgments while System II processing is rational, logical, and responsible for solving complex tasks. Hedonic clues allow firms to highlight the hedonic aspect (i.e., fun, excitement, and enjoyment) of the product's features, enhancing consumers' System I information processing (also called experiential information processing). By stimulating consumers' emotions, conveying hedonic clues is a strategy to attract the attention of consumers whose decisions to use a product depend on whether the product is fun, exciting, and enjoyable. Firms that provide utilitarian clues emphasize the utility of the product, demonstrating that the product's features are helpful, effective, and functional (Dhar & Wertenbroch, 2000; Voss et al.,

2003). By doing so, firms enhance consumers' system II information processing (also called rational information processing). Utilitarian clues reduce information asymmetry about the innovation capability of a focal firm by appealing to the reason of the recipients regarding the utility of the product's features. Hedonic and utilitarian clues are two different ways to promote the product and might interact with substantive signals differently.

Prior literature on traditional signaling emphasizes substantive signals. Such signals contain substantive information about the performance of firms and are more costly for low-quality firms than high-quality firms (Bergh et al., 2014). Additionally, they do not rely on rhetoric to convey information and persuade recipients. For example, Setia et al. (2020) demonstrated the impact of product quality signals (e.g., bug reports, feature requests, bug fixes, and version releases) on the take-off of open-source projects. Moreover, Bapna (2019) highlighted the impact of other substantive signals, including product certification, affiliation with prominent consumers, and social proof of interest in investing. Compared to rhetorical signals, substantive signals are tough to manipulate, and effective at reducing information asymmetry about the performance of the firm (see Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018).

The evolutionary economics perspective relevant to our framing describes the dual search processes of innovation and imitation as shaping the evolution dynamics of firms (e.g., Lenox et al., 2006; Zott, 2003). Firms execute an innovation search process by exploring novel ways to address a given problem (e.g., Levinthal, 1997). Moreover, firms perform an imitation search process by following innovators, thus creating similar versions of existing products and services (e.g., Rivkin, 2000). Imitation reduces technological risks associated with innovation, as firms do not engage in experimentation but copy or slightly improve existing solutions in the market.

Apps markets are complex environments, rendering the imitation search process challenging. These markets are highly fragmented because thousands of apps compete in each app category market. Such fragmentation complicates the ability of incumbent complementors to understand competitive dynamics. Further, the high number of apps in the market increases the costs of imitation search while reducing the potential benefits of imitation. On the one hand, it is costly for imitators to track the evolutions of a large number of apps in the market, learn how they work, and potentially choose the features to imitate. Correspondingly, imitation might be less beneficial, as firms cannot imitate all apps in the market and will have to engage in a selection process. Considering that most apps in the market are expected to fail, it further complicates the imitation search process as firms must be able to determine which new features in the market are likely to attract consumer attention in the future. So, overall, the high fragmentation of the market increases potential costs and reduces the potential benefits of imitation.

Mobile app startups, a small subset of businesses in the app market, stand out from the crowd because of the external financial contributions they receive. Such external financial contributions provide credibility to their economic activity in the market (Agrawal, Catalini, & Goldfarb, 2014; Ahlers et al., 2015; Courtney et al., 2017; Hill & Jones, 1992). As a result, app developers in the market have a greater incentive to target mobile app startups and imitate the startup product's features in a way that reduces costs and increases the potential benefits of imitation. The smaller number of mobile app startups in the market reduces imitation costs, and credibility due to external financial contributions increases the potential benefits.

A direct consequence of imitation is that imitators position themselves as direct competitors of mobile app startups. The ability of new entrants to imitate and effectively compete against mobile app startups creates a threat to mobile app startups. While mobile app startups might be an easy target for imitators, competing against such incumbents is not simple and depends on the “edge” these startups have in the market. We argue that as the competitive edge of startups increases, new entrants are less likely to threaten incumbent startups. This edge is visible through substantive signals that inform the market about the superiority of the focal firm. In this study, we focus on two substantive signals of firm performance, namely, the maturity stage and the consumer base (or consumer attention) (Aggarwal & Singh, 2013; Aggarwal et al., 2012; Greenwood & Gopal, 2017; Greenwood & Gopal, 2015; Susarla et al., 2016).

Maturity stage and consumer attention are distinct as a later maturity stage does not necessarily mean greater consumer attention. For example, a mobile app startup at a later stage may launch a new app to increase its portfolio diversification. In that case, the overall consumer attention (i.e., consumer attention across all apps) may increase but not necessarily the consumer attention on the focal app. In this study, we focus on the consumer attention on the focal app and the maturity lifecycle of the mobile app startup. Since consumer attention and maturity lifecycle are credible and reliable signals (i.e., more costly for low-quality senders than high-quality senders) according to traditional signaling theory, they inform competitors about the quality of the startup.

Considering that startups may not have a long history that competitors can access to learn more about the startup, substantive signals are crucial for startups to deter competition. This is consistent with competitive signaling, which indicates that sending signals influences competitive behavior (Heil & Robertson, 1991). Notably, competitors respond to senders' signals after inferring the sender's intentions, motives, goals, strengths, or weaknesses (e.g., senders' product quality and employees' productivity) (Heil & Robertson, 1991).

## 3.1 Effect of Maturity Stage

The maturity stage is determined by venture financing, a well-established signal in prior research (Davila et al., 2003). It is a signal in traditional signaling theory because it is observable (i.e., information about the type of funding rounds is released to the public) and costly in that investors fund startups that will generate high returns on investments (i.e., investors fund high-quality startups). Achieving maturity is crucial for startups to deter competition, as it informs competitors that the startup may have strong capabilities and thus be difficult to compete against. Specifically, a mature stage signals investors' confidence in the startup's ability to create novel products, market them appropriately, build complementary resources and capabilities, and expand into new markets.

As mobile app startups progress in the maturity stages (e.g., conception, commercialization, and growth (Lin & Maruping, 2021)), their performance in the market increases. Notably, mobile app startups build advanced R&D capabilities by acquiring human and technical resources to develop new solutions. This is typical of the seeding phase (also called the conception stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021), wherein startups focus on building innovative and feasible products through the generation, selection, and prototyping of ideas. As mobile app startups create new solutions based on advanced expertise and technologies (e.g., artificial intelligence), it becomes more challenging for new entrants to imitate them as they need to understand the workings of the underlying technologies. Moreover, mobile app startups build complementary capabilities such as marketing to advertise their products. This is typical of early-stage ventures (also called the commercialization stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021) where the focus is on efficiently commercializing the products. $^{6}$

Finally, mobile app startups, generally late-stage startups (also called the growth stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021) focus on expanding their economic activities into new markets through the acquisitions of other firms or the diversification of their products (see Myers & Majluf, 1981). Attaining a later stage of maturity is a signal conveyed by startups prevailing in a highly perilous transition from conception to growth via commercialization. As mobile app startups progress in the maturity stages (i.e., develop complementarity assets for commercialization and growth), the ability of new entrants to pose a threat diminishes. For example, a competitive advantage of startups such as Discord and Twitch is the global reach strategy, which attracts new consumers in foreign markets. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: A higher maturity stage diminishes new entry threats such that mobile app startups in later stages of maturity face fewer new entry threats.

## 3.2 Effect of Consumer Attention

Consumer attention is another signal in traditional signaling theory because it is observable (i.e., information about the number of app ratings is released to the public, and some platforms, such as the Google Play Store, provide a number range of app downloads, e.g., 0-10, 10-100, etc.) and costly because accumulating a large amount of consumer attention is a sign of consumer interest and hence the quality of the app. Specifically, large amounts of consumer attention signal a startup's ability to offer superior products that are difficult to compete against. Notably, such products benefit from direct network effects, data network effects, and word-of-mouth, which are hard to overcome.

As the user base increases, the value of the firm's offering rises as a result of direct network effects (De Reuver et al., 2018). Notable examples of startups include Snapchat and TikTok, as more users make the offering more valuable. Furthermore, large amounts of consumer attention facilitate the accumulation of large amounts of consumer data, reinforcing the direct network effect and the strength of the feedback loop (Sokol & Comerford, 2015). Consumer data can help firms increase the attractiveness of their offerings, strengthening the direct network effects.

Additionally, in such cases, a feedback loop occurs because firms with a large amount of collected data are effective at generating insights about consumer needs and targeting and selling ads (Sokol & Comerford, 2015). This can be seen in the example of Facebook, for instance, which relies heavily on consumer data collection for selling ads. The insights and advertising revenues generated can be used to improve service quality, attracting more consumers. Finally, a large user base also signals increased word-of-mouth, as there are more consumers to spread the word about the firm's offerings. Considering that consumer attention is not easily obtainable, as mobile app startups receive greater consumer attention, the ability of new entrants to pose a threat is diminished. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Mobile app startups with large amounts of consumer attention face fewer new entry threats.

## 3.3 Effect of Rhetorical Signals

Utilitarian signals enhance recipients' System II information processing (also called rational, Epstein, 2003; intentional, Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; deliberate, Hogarth, 2001; or extensional, Tversky & Kahneman, 1983), i.e., an effortful, deliberate, and rational type of information processing. In contrast, hedonic signals enhance recipients' System I information processing (also called experiential, Epstein, 2003; automatic, Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; tacit, Hogarth, 2001; natural, Tversky & Kahneman, 1983; or associative, Simon, 1996), i.e., a rapid, effortless, and simplified type of heuristic processing. This process is not conducive to learning, as it places minimal demand on cognitive resources. Recipients using System I information processing tend to avoid cognitive effort because it may interfere with their pleasant affective state (see Isen et al., 1982). The presence of hedonic signals interferes with recipients' ability to elaborate the message because it reduces information processing capacity.

Although startups utilize rhetorical signals to attract consumers to their offerings, such signals also inform competitors of the startup's superiority, influencing the level of new entry threats to the startup. Competitors who are likely to pose new entry threats are those who can compete successfully with the startup. We propose two main reasons why rhetorical signals moderate the effect of substantive signals on new entry threats. First, rhetorical signals enable competitors to infer consumers' attachment to startups' apps. When consumers' attachment is built on consumers' reasoning, competitors are less likely to pose threats, as this form of attachment is stronger compared to one built on consumers' emotions. Notably, startups' strength lies in their ability to build high-quality novel apps. $^{7}$ While established firms may be more successful in appealing to consumers' emotions (Mu et al., 2021), startups may struggle to convince consumers about the novelty and quality of their apps by appealing to their emotions. Compared to appealing to consumers' emotions, appealing to consumers' reasoning can enable startups to increase consumers' learning about their apps, thus strengthening consumers' attachment to their apps. Second, by shedding light on the attributes of startups' apps, utilitarian signals also increase competitors' learning about the startups' apps. As competitors learn about the startups' apps, the novelty, and quality of the startups' apps are likely to deter them from attempting to pose a threat. Hence, when competitors (1) infer a weak consumer attachment to startups' apps or (2) are unable to effectively learn about the attributes of startups' apps from firm-generated content, substantive signals may deter new entry threats to a lesser extent.

## 3.3.1 Effect of Hedonic Signals

We suggest that rhetorical signals with an emphasis on hedonic cues reduce the negative effect of substantive signals on new entry threats. According to the elaboration likelihood model, recipients' motivation to process hedonic signals increases when they are unable to process utilitarian cues (e.g., investors) or when sender credibility is established, which increases recipients' trust in the message (Xiang et al., 2019). This is not the case for consumers in the context of startups. Consumers are motivated to process utilitarian cues because they tend to focus on the benefits they can derive from products. For example, shopping for the best products increases consumer attention to product-related messages. Additionally, consumers' past experiences increase their knowledge and ability to process utilitarian cues.

Considering that startups do not have established credibility due to their newness in the market, sending hedonic signals might not appeal to consumers of startups, as it reduces consumers' learning (e.g., consumers' ability to objectively assess alternatives in the market). Notably, appealing to emotions shifts the competition away from emphasizing the product's utility to conveying the possibility of deriving pleasurable experiences from the product. It triggers emotional contagion (i.e., emotions in the message evoke similar emotions in the recipients), which occurs without the recipient's understanding (Geiger & Moore, 2022; Li et al., 2018; Pugh, 2001).

As startups focus their communication on emotions by conveying hedonic cues, it may be challenging for (1) consumers to distinguish their apps from competitors' apps based on quality and novelty, and (2) competitors to understand the quality and novelty superiority of startups' apps. As a result, new entrants imitating mobile app startups might think that they have a chance of survival (Ismagilova et al., 2020; Oba, 2020). We thus hypothesize:

H3a: The negative effect of a higher maturity stage on new entry threats weakens the more that mobile app startups send hedonic cues.

H3b: The negative effect of gaining consumer attention on new entry threats weakens the more that mobile app startups send hedonic cues.

## 3.3.2 Effect of Utilitarian Signals

We suggest that rhetorical signals with an emphasis on utilitarian cues strengthen the negative effect of substantive signals on new entry threats. According to the elaboration likelihood model, consumers of startups are more likely to process utilitarian cues as they are pursuing the best product experience. Utilitarian cues signal startups' confidence in the functional benefits of their products, leading to better results in influencing consumers, as they focus on the persuasiveness of central cues (Xiang et al. 2019).

Firms appeal to recipients' reasoning when conveying utilitarian cues. As firms focus their communication on the utilitarian aspect of the product, they reduce information asymmetry between firms and recipients (e.g., consumers and competitors) regarding the novelty and quality of product features. Moreover, conveying utilitarian cues sends a signal to existing and potential competitors about the innovation capability of the focal firm. Markets are saturated by many products, and providing relevant information through utilitarian cues helps recipients make rational decisions. Utilitarian cues help consumers and competitors evaluate the product's utility. Such evaluation is important for (1) consumers to choose between alternatives in the market based on quality and novelty and (2) competitors to understand the firm's quality and novelty superiority. Hence, new entrants that imitate mobile app startups might think that they have a lower chance of survival. We thus hypothesize:

H4a: The negative effect of a higher maturity stage on new entry threats increases the more that mobile app startups send utilitarian cues.

H4b: The negative effect of gaining consumer attention on new entry threats strengthens the more that mobile app startups send utilitarian cues.

## 4 Methods

Our data came from several sources. We used Crunchbase (https://www.crunchbase.com/) to collect entrepreneurship data, including the funding rounds of mobile app startups. We also collected social media activity (i.e., tweets posted) from Twitter (https://twitter.com). We limited the data collection to Twitter because we realized that, in our data, more than 90% of mobile app startups that were active on Facebook were also active on Twitter (e.g., MyFitnessPal on Facebook $^{8}$ and Twitter $^{9}$ ), and those startups generally posted the same content on the two platforms. Our data indicated that 67% of mobile app startups had a Twitter account. Finally, the data on mobile apps on the Google Play Store and App Store came from Appfigures (https://appfigures.com/). These three data sources enabled us to track the maturity stages of the mobile app startups, the tweets created by the mobile app startups, and the various characteristics (e.g., consumer attention, and app description) of mobile apps since their release date by incumbent complementors. We included mobile app startups with mobile apps released as early as June 2009 and as late as June 2018. Thus, we obtained a rich historical dataset aggregated at the monthly level. The final dataset contained 107,180 observations, and 10,534 unique mobile apps (across all app categories) of which 6,018 and 4,516 were on the Google Play Store and App Store, respectively. Moreover, there were 714 unique mobile app startups; 115 and 190 of these, respectively, were on the iOS and Android platforms, while 409 were on both platforms. Finally, 510 unique mobile app startups were in the seeding phase, 106 were in the early venture phase, and 26 were in the late venture stage. Fifty unique mobile app startups started at the seeding phase and stopped at the early venture stage, two unique mobile app startups evolved from the seeding phase to the early venture stage and the late venture stage, and 20 unique mobile app startups started at the early venture stage and stopped at the late venture stage.

## 4.1 Measures

Dependent variable: We measured new entry threats by first identifying credible new entrants from the crowd. We defined a mobile app as a credible new entrant if the app received at least three app ratings per year after its release date. AppBrain (2024) suggests that 73.3% of Android apps have less than three app ratings. Second, following Pan et al. (2019), $^{10}$ we calculated new entry threats (NETs) using a text-based measure. Specifically, we measured new entry threats using the cosine similarity between a focal app description and its corresponding entrepreneurial space document (i.e., aggregated text description of new apps released in a month in the same market). The measure is bounded between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating greater threats. Since our measure of NETs builds on a previously validated measure of NETs (Pan et al., 2019), conducting validation tests for the NETs measure may not be necessary.

Independent variables: First, we identified the maturity stage following Crunchbase classification (Teare, 2020). Lin and Maruping (2021) illustrate the identification of a startup's maturity stages. Our measurement is consistent with Lin & Maruping (2021). Crunchbase aggregates ventures into three main maturity stages based on funding round. The seeding stage (the equivalent of the conception stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021) comprises startups that have not yet received any funding from angel investors or venture capital firms. Moreover, the early-stage venture (the equivalent of the growth stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021) comprises startups that have received angel investments or Series A funding from venture capital firms. Finally, the late-stage venture (the equivalent of the commercialization stage in Lin & Maruping, 2021) comprises startups that have received Series B funding or later rounds of funding (e.g., Series C, Series D, Series E) from venture capital firms. We used two dummy variables early-stage venture dummy (equals 1 if the mobile app startup is at the early stage and 0 otherwise) and a late-stage venture dummy (equals 1 if the mobile app startup is at the late stage and 0 otherwise).

Second, we measured consumer attention using the monthly difference in the number of app reviews (Foerderer et al., 2018). Third, we developed metrics to measure hedonic and utilitarian cues while remaining consistent with prior literature (Mu et al., 2021). For example, Mu et al. (2021, p. 6) define utilitarian appeals as messages that

(1) emphasize the product's practical benefits, such as completing specific tasks efficiently and effectively; (2) inform users how to resolve specific user problems with the product; (3) highlight the practical value of the product to users; and (4) provide certain concrete functional attributes/benefits of the product to users" and hedonic appeals as messages that "(1) highlight the product's positive affective benefits, emphasizing emotional attachment and focusing on fun, playfulness, enjoyment, excitement, feelings of interest and pleasure; (2) are experiential and multisensory; (3) employ affective language; and (4) provide exercise of power, imagination, and aspiration for consumers.

We validated our measures of hedonic and utilitarian clues by ensuring that they matched Mu et al.'s (2021) description of utilitarian and hedonic appeals. We calculated the level of hedonic and utilitarian clues using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software. Initially, we identified the tweets that are specific to the app, excluding all unrelated tweets. Later, we identified hedonic tweets and utilitarian tweets using LIWC. We used LIWC's affective processes and cognitive processes to identify if a tweet was hedonic or utilitarian. When the affective processes were greater than the cognitive processes, we categorized the tweet as hedonic. Similarly, when the cognitive processes were greater than the affective processes, we categorized the tweet as utilitarian. Next, we calculated the extent to which a tweet presented affective processes and cognitive processes using LIWC. The scores ranged from 0 to 100, with a high score indicating a high level of affective or cognitive processes. Table 3 presents the measurement for each variable of the study.

Control variables: We incorporated several control variables including hedonic and utilitarian cues extracted from consumers' tweets, app ratings, and market network effects (i.e., the total consumer demand in the market, and the number of apps in the market). A market represented an app category, and the number of app ratings was used as a proxy for consumer demand (Foerderer, 2020).

## 4.2 Analysis

We conducted a panel vector autoregression (PVAR), as it has several advantages compared to traditional multiple regression. PVAR models are advanced econometric models that combine the strength of (1) a VAR model and (2) the structure of the panel data set. First, VAR is unique as it incorporates endogenous predictors and does not require additional explanatory variables (Wang et al., 2021). VAR is appropriate when assessing causality between a system of dependent variables without ad hoc model restrictions (Adomavicius et al., 2012). Second, the panel data structure allows researchers to control for unobserved individual fixed effects, e.g., Helmert transformation. Moreover, it enables researchers to utilize generalized method of moments (GMM) instruments to obtain unbiased estimates (Wang et al., 2021). By combining VAR and the panel data structure, PVAR is a robust model for understanding causality between endogenous variables. For example, it assesses feedback loops between endogenous variables. Also, it helps identify short- or long-term effects between the endogenous variables by assessing the response in one variable following a shock in another variable. Furthermore, PVAR estimates are robust to issues of reverse causality, spurious causality, serial correlation, endogeneity, and nonstationarity (Granger & Newbold, 1986). Thus, PVAR is a robust method to assess causality. The PVAR model is specified as follows for each app released by mobile app startups:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} N E T s _ {i, t} \\ C A _ {i, t} \\ F S _ {1 _ {i, t}} \\ F S _ {2 _ {i, t}} \\ H C _ {i, t} \\ U C _ {i, t} \end{array} \right] = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \Phi_ {j} \left[ \begin{array}{c} N E T s _ {i, t - j} \\ C A _ {i, t - j} \\ F S _ {1 _ {i, t - j}} \\ F S _ {2 _ {i, t - j}} \\ H C _ {i, t - j} \\ U C _ {i, t - j} \end{array} \right] + \beta \left[ \begin{array}{c} H C _ {U G C _ {i, t - 1}} \\ U C _ {U G C _ {i, t - 1}} \\ A v g R a t i n g _ {i, t - 1} \\ T o t a l A p p s _ {i, t - 1} \\ M a r k e t S i z e _ {i, t - 1} \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
+ \omega_ {t} + \theta_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t},
$$

where $NETs_{i,t}$ , $CA_{i,t}$ , $FS\_1_{i,t}$ , $FS\_2_{i,t}$ , $HC_{i,t}$ , and $UC_{i,t}$ represent monthly new entry threats, monthly consumer attention, monthly early-stage venture dummy, monthly late-stage venture dummy, monthly hedonic cues, and monthly utilitarian cues of app i in month t ( $t = 1, 2, ..., T$ ), respectively. The coefficients $\Phi_{j}$ represent the relationship between the lagged variables on the right-hand side and the variables on the left-hand side of the equation. We determined the optimal order (J) of the model by selecting the model with the smallest Bayesian information criterion (MBIC), Akaike information criterion (MAIC), and Hannan-Quinn information criterion (MQIC). We found first-order panel VAR—with the first four lags of predictors as instruments—to be the optimal model because it has the smallest MBIC, MAIC, and MQIC. Following prior studies, we improved our model estimations using “GMM-style” instruments (Holtz-Eakin et al., 1988; Moqri et al., 2018). Moreover, the Im-Pesaran-Shin unit root test rejected the null hypothesis for all variables of interest, thus indicating that there is no unit root in our panel. Furthermore, we controlled for individual fixed effects using the Helmert transformation in our panel VAR model. The Helmert transformation involves forward mean differencing of the variables, using the lagged regressors as instruments $^{11}$ for the forward-differenced variables, hence ensuring that the forward-differenced variables and the lagged variables were orthogonal (see Love & Zicchino, 2006). It is important to control for individual fixed effects because it can be argued that the app category matters (e.g., hedonic apps benefit more from hedonic cues) in such a way that the observed effect is caused by the app category.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Main Findings

The main goal of this study was to understand the right signals that mobile app startups can send in the market to mitigate new entry threats. We focused on two types of rhetorical signals: utilitarian and hedonic cues. We adopted a holistic approach suggesting that while rhetorical signals alone might not influence new entry threats, they interact with substantive signals to impact new entry threats. We used a panel VAR to test the hypotheses. Our findings indicate that substantive signals such as consumer attention and maturity stages reduce new entry threats. Moreover, we found that the effect of substantive signals on new entry threats depended on rhetorical signals in a way that the disclosure of hedonic cues mostly dampens the effect of substantive signals while the disclosure of utilitarian cues increased the effect of substantive signals. Notably, the negative effect of attracting consumer attention to new entry threats increased with the disclosure of utilitarian cues. Furthermore, at each stage (i.e., early-stage venture and late-stage venture), the disclosure of utilitarian cues by mobile app startups reduced new entry threats further while the disclosure of hedonic cues did the opposite. Finally, our findings indicate that new entry threats reduced consumer attention in the future.

## 5.2 Research Contributions

Our study contributes to prior literature on firm-generated content (FGC) by assessing the psycholinguistic traits of messages. The psycholinguistic literature offers a theoretical foundation to examine the hedonic/utilitarian characteristics of messages (e.g., Brisset, 2018; Chitturi et al., 2007). Following prior research, we proposed a granular assessment of the impact of FGC. Mu et al.'s (2021) study was among the first to empirically assess the role of hedonic and utilitarian characteristics of FGC on sales. While Mu et al. (2021) focused on consumers, our study extends prior work on the role of psycholinguistic traits of FGC by highlighting competitors (i.e., important stakeholders to understand firm performance). To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to empirically investigate the role of hedonic and utilitarian traits in a firm's social media messages in influencing competitors' entry into the app market. This is an important contribution, considering the significance of digital content strategy. Notably, several studies have shed light on FGC's characteristics (e.g., number of messages—Kumar et al., 2016; information & emotional content—Lee et al., 2018; Tellis et al., 2019) and their effects on consumer engagement, sentiment, and sales. Aligned with Mu et al. (2021), our study highlights the importance of considering the hedonic and utilitarian characteristics of FGC.

Our study adopts a contingent model and System I and II information processing while assessing the role of FGC, thus unraveling strategies that firms can use to manage FGC that are under their control. Prior research has examined the direct effects of FGC (e.g., Lee et al. 2018) and the joint effects of FGC and content types (Mu et al., 2021). Our study highlights the joint effects of rhetorical signals sent through FGC and substantive signals that are not under the control of firms. This is consistent with prior research on FGC that seeks to understand contingent (i.e., differential) influences of FGC (e.g., Mu et al., 2021). Interestingly, our findings indicate that hedonic and utilitarian signals have opposite effects, as utilitarian signals strengthen the effects of substantive signals on new entry threats, while hedonic signals do the opposite. Our study thereby underscores the importance of a holistic assessment of rhetorical and substantive signaling (see Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018). Regardless of the source (i.e., investor or consumer) of substantive signals, rhetorical signals matter.

Our study extends prior research on traditional signaling, which has downplayed the role of rhetorical signals because they are equally costly for firms with low- and high-quality products (Bergh et al., 2014; Cadsby et al., 1990; Farrell & Rabin, 1996). Consistent with Steigenberger & Wilhelm, (2018), our research stresses that omitting rhetorical signals limits our understanding of the impact of substantive signals. We extend attention-based and elaboration-based complementarities to competitive signaling (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018). Attention-based complementarity indicates that senders benefit from complementing their substantive signals with attention-attracting language (i.e., pathos signals). It extends traditional signaling by highlighting the role of emotions as triggers of attention. Elaboration-based complementarity indicates that senders benefit from complementing their substantive signals with language that offers information on hidden attributes. It extends traditional signaling by highlighting the role of recipients' human cognition. Our research expands both complementarities by indicating that our equivalent of attention-based complementarity (i.e., the interaction between hedonic and substantive signals) is not beneficial at deterring new entry threats, but our equivalent of elaboration-based complementarity (i.e., the interaction between utilitarian and substantive signals) is. Hence, we enrich the discussion on the benefits and limitations of attention-based and elaboration-based complementarities.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

Mobile platforms are very competitive environments, as illustrated by a large number of apps in the App Store (1.84 million apps as of the fourth quarter of 2019) and Google Play Store (2.57 million apps as of the fourth quarter of 2019) (Clement, 2020). Such environments threaten the survival of complementors. For example, 73.3% of Android apps have less than three consumer reviews (AppBrain, 2024). As mobile app startups receive external funding, they face high pressures to survive. Our findings provide several managerial implications that might help mobile app startups reduce new entry threats. Our implications relate to the use of social media by mobile app startups to send the right signals.

Social media provides an important communication channel through which mobile app startups might convey important information to multiple stakeholders including consumers, investors, and competitors in the market. Our study highlights the importance of mobile app startups to strategically use social media. While social media can be used in various ways (e.g., the announcement of financial statements, appointments of new managers, and job postings), our findings imply that mobile app startups should consider using social media to promote their apps. Although mobile app startups that are not active on social media may fail to reduce new entry threats, there seems to be a trend of mobile app startups not being very active on social media. For example, in our dataset, we found that mobile app startups create, on average, 24 app-related tweets per month.

In addition to recommending a higher presence of mobile app startups on social media, our findings imply that mobile app startups should use social media primarily to send utilitarian cues and that more information about app features should be provided through social media. Moreover, social media could offer the right place for mobile app startups to discuss the utility of apps. At the same time, while emotions are important for persuading individuals, our findings suggest that mobile app startups should limit sending hedonic cues through social media to reduce new entry threats. Our recommendations regarding the signals mobile app startups should send through social media depend on the market size and maturity status of the startup.

Mobile app startups that attract high consumer attention should place a greater priority on their communication on social media. Notably, the benefits of sending the right rhetorical signals increase with the amount of consumer attention gained by mobile app startups. Furthermore, we recommend that mobile app startups at the seeding, early venture, and late venture stages send more utilitarian cues than their peers. Mobile app startups should adjust their communication based on their maturity stage. Considering that a mobile app startup might compete against startups that are not at the same maturity stage, the focal startup should not imitate the communication of other startups without considering the maturity stage.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations. First, we collected a unique time-series dataset spanning multiple years from 2009 to 2018. While we established a Granger causality between signals sent by mobile app startups and new entry threats, further analyses might be required to establish strong causality. Future studies could use natural or field experiments to confirm our findings. Second, we focused on signals sent by mobile app startups on Twitter (now X). While Twitter represents a major social media platform, our study could be extended to other major social media platforms such as Facebook and Instagram. Third, we limited the choice of rhetorical signals to those sent by mobile app startups on Twitter. Future research might include rhetorical signals that were not sent by mobile app startups. Such signals might include investors' and consumers' rhetoric on social media platforms. Fourth, we emphasized two important substantive signals for mobile app startups: maturity stages and the level of consumer attention. Future studies might explore additional substantive signals such as app ranking.

Fifth, we calculated new entry threats using apps that have received at least three app ratings per year after their release dates. We have several reasons to think this is a valid threshold. For example, the majority of apps (e.g., 73.3% of Android apps) fail to meet this threshold. Also, the results in Table 6 show that the threshold did not dilute our measure of new entry threats because we saw a significant negative effect on consumer attention, indicating that these apps posed credible new entry threats, as they had a negative effect on the performance of the focal app. Future research might explore alternative thresholds. Finally, while we focused on mobile platforms, future studies could generalize our findings to other contexts and industries. By examining the impact of rhetorical and substantive signals on new entry threats, our study aims to create research interest in investigating factors that influence new entry threats.

## 6 Conclusion

To conclude, we hypothesize the effect of sending rhetoric and substantive signals on new entry threats. As mobile app startups have less control over substantive signals sent by investors and consumers, our study unravels how they can send the “right” rhetorical signals to mitigate new entry threats. Using a large panel dataset, we empirically demonstrate that mobile app startups should send utilitarian and hedonic cues based on the level of consumer attention and the maturity stage. By exploring the impact of rhetorical signals sent by mobile app startups through social media on new entry threats, our study examines the relationship between the use of social media by mobile app startups and competition.

## About the Authors

Franck Soh is an assistant professor of information systems at Neeley School of Business, Texas Christian University. His research interests include the business value of IT, competition in mobile platform ecosystems, healthcare IT, customer service performance, open-source, and social media. Dr. Soh's work has appeared in Information Systems Research, the Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and he has presented his research at several conferences.

Varun Grover is the Billingsley Endowed Chair and Distinguished Professor at the Walton College, University of Arkansas. He is consistently ranked among the top five researchers globally, based on top journal publications (over 400), citations (around 56,000), and h-index (of 103). His work focuses on IT/digitalization business impacts. He has served multiple terms as senior editor of premier IS journals (like MIS Quarterly) and has held numerous major roles at ICIS and AMCIS. He is an AIS Fellow and AIS LEO recipient for lifetime achievement.
