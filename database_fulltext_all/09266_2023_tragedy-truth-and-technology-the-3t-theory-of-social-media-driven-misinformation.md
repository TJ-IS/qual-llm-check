# Tragedy, Truth, and Technology: The 3T Theory of Social Media-Driven Misinformation

Nicholas Roberts,<sup>1</sup> Hamed Qahri-Saremi<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Oregon State University, USA, nicholas.roberts@oregonstate.edu <sup>2</sup>Colorado State University, USA, Hamed.Qahri-Saremi@colostate.edu

## Abstract

False claims and misinformation on social media have substantially increased in the last few years. Some people have incorrectly interpreted such content as the truth, sometimes to the extent that it transforms their view of reality and subsequently leads to actions that cause harm and suffering. Despite the burgeoning body of research on misinformation on social media, we know little about the process by which social media-driven misinformation shapes and reinforces false beliefs that result in detrimental outcomes. Building on insights from dramaturgical tragedy, theories of truth, and research on social media, we develop a process theory that explains how social media-driven misinformation transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action.

Keywords: Social Media, Misinformation, Fake News, Disinformation, Dialectic, Doubt, Platform, Tragedy, Truth

Robert Gregory was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on May 27, 2022 and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

In August 2021, Matthew Coleman took his 2-year-old son and 10-month-old daughter to Rosarito, Mexico. Shortly afterward, he confessed to having killed his children with a spearfishing gun. Coleman told law enforcement agents he had been influenced by claims on social media that his children had “serpent DNA” they had inherited from his wife, and he was “saving the world from monsters.” Serpent DNA seems to be a reference to a “lizard people” conspiracy theory that falsely purports that reptilian aliens secretly run the world and that powerful people in the government, banking industry, and Hollywood are actually human-lizard hybrids. After hearing news of the killings, Coleman’s neighbors and friends were shocked that Coleman would have done such a thing. In fact, one person noted, “This came out of left field for all of us.”<sup>1</sup>

People have acted on false claims and inaccurate information for thousands of years. However, the advent of social media<sup>2</sup> platforms has amplified the creation and dissemination of content that is misleading or outright false (Aral, 2020; Suarez-Lledo & Alvarez-Galvez, 2021). Consistent with history, people often interpret such misinformation as the truth and act accordingly, sometimes leading to detrimental outcomes. Misinformation refers to false or misleading information that is considered to be incorrect based on the best available evidence from relevant experts at the time (Vraga & Bode, 2020). Studies show that people perform poorly when assessing whether information on social media is true or false because confirmation bias leads them to believe claims that support their prior beliefs or values and dismiss claims that do not (Horner et al., 2021; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2022; Moravec et al., 2019; Rhodes, 2022). Interactions on social media can also result in limited information environments that reinforce preexisting biases, encourage groupthink, and affect truth judgment processes (Restrepo et al., 2021; Sunstein, 2001, 2017). Taken as a whole, research under this “reinforcement perspective” suggests that social media can affect truth judgments by reinforcing a user’s view of reality even when the information is false, thereby encouraging them to take more extreme ideological positions and sometimes propelling them to take actions harmful to themselves or others.

However, there may be other equally valid theoretical explanations for why and how users interpret misinformation on social media in a manner that leads to detrimental outcomes. We offer one such explanation. Specifically, we propose that social media can transform or dramatically alter a user’s view of reality in a way that leads to harm and suffering. Returning to the vignette above, although Coleman spent substantial time reading false claims and watching videos on social media leading up to his children’s deaths, he did not exhibit any beliefs regarding “serpent DNA” or related areas prior to this period. Thus, social media might have played a role in Coleman’s transformation. Similar “transformative” stories have recently emerged (Gisondi et al., 2022; Tasnim et al., 2020). For example, a series of viral social media posts in 2021 claimed that a medication known as ivermectin <sup>3</sup> can safely and effectively treat COVID-19 (Andrade, 2021). There is no scientific evidence to date that shows ivermectin prevents or treats COVID-19 in humans or animals; furthermore, animal ivermectin is not safe for humans (FDA, 2021). Even so, some people encountered claims on social media that ivermectin is a safe and effective treatment for COVID-19, interpreted misleading evidence as true, and subsequently took animal ivermectin—sometimes in doses large enough to cause gastrointestinal distress and neurological toxicity (Weill & Rawnsley, 2021). Understanding social media’s transformative role is important because it provides a fuller view of the impact of social media-driven misinformation. It also identifies alternative yet complementary concepts and mechanisms to the wellestablished reinforcement perspective. Furthermore, misinformation on social media is a widespread problem with few to no ready solutions in sight (Aral, 2020; Moran, 2020; Sylvia Chou et al., 2020). Thus, it is critical that we develop new theory explaining the processes and effects of social media-driven misinformation.

We address these research opportunities by developing a process theory that explains how misinformation on social media transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action. Our theory’s starting point is an incident where a person encounters a claim that is meaningful to them yet initially doubted. If their doubt is strong enough, the claim recipient enters a dialectic phase where they eventually judge the claim to be true or false. Social media may distort this judgment process. The recipient’s judgment results in either a supported (well-informed) belief or a suspect (misinformed) belief. Following this, the recipient might enter a phase where social media reinforces this new suspect belief. With their view of reality transformed, the recipient may act on their suspect belief in a way that leads to detrimental outcomes. Our process theory is based on three bodies of literature—dramaturgical tragedy, theories of truth, and research on social media. Dramaturgical tragedy is an art form based on human suffering; as such, it can help us understand the process by which a person’s view of reality is transformed in ways that lead to harm and suffering. We use two theories of truth— correspondence and coherence—that aid our understanding of truth judgment processes. Social media literature helps us identify ways in which social media can influence truth judgment processes and, by extension, transform a person’s view of reality. We leverage two social media influence mechanisms— feeding and signaling—and two social media learning mechanisms—sensing and matching.

Appreciating the substantial empirical and intellectual contributions of the reinforcement perspective, we seek to complement and build on it through conceptualization of a transformation perspective. We engage in phenomenon-driven theorizing (Gregory & Henfridsson, 2021) to develop an explanatory theory (Gregor, 2006) that pushes the boundaries (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Grover & Niederman, 2021) of our understanding of social media phenomena. In doing so we make four contributions. First, by identifying and describing social media’s transformative role in a person’s view of reality, we provide a perspective that complements current explanations of how misinformation on social media reinforces a person’s worldview. Second, we contribute to research by explaining how social media distorts a person’s truth judgment of a claim through a dialectic process. Third, we include social media mechanisms that exert both direct (influence) and indirect (learning) effects on a person’s view of reality, thereby enabling a more holistic perspective. Fourth, our process theory contributes to research by providing a deeper understanding of the events, phases, and temporal progressions of activities surrounding social mediadriven misinformation.

## 2 Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Tragedy: Action and Dialectic

Almost every culture has an art form based on human suffering (Banham & Brandon, 1995). One type of such art in Western culture is tragedy, a form of drama based on human suffering that is designed to entertain and potentially enlighten an audience. <sup>4</sup> Tragedy is useful to our theory because it represents human experience and social phenomena in a way that imitates life (Myers, 1956). The premise that tragedy imitates life originated with Aristotle, who wrote in the Poetics that tragedy is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude. Tragedies such as Hamlet and King Lear imitate and represent the fullness of life in poignantly real ways (Muller, 1956). Such imitation requires observation; in fact, tragedy is often referred to as a short-hand account of human experience “prepared by the most talented observers and presented in a language that is concise and to the point” (Myers, 1956, p. 180). The unique ways in which it imitates life allow tragedy to expand, refine, and intensify our view of social phenomena (Critchley, 2019; Muller, 1956). In fact, scholars have used tragedy in particular (Boje et al., 2004) and dramaturgy in general (Clark & Mangham, 2004; Gardner & Avolio, 1998) to develop management and organizational theories.

The long history of dramaturgical tragedy requires us to set boundary conditions for purposes of theory development. We draw primarily on ancient Greek and Shakespearean tragedy. Performed annually to celebrate the god Dionysus, ancient Greek tragedy was based largely on war stories and Greek mythology (Gregory, 2005). The only surviving texts from this era were written by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Ancient Greek tragedy has exerted a tremendous influence on theatre, philosophy, and literature (Critchley, 2019). Shakespearean tragedy was composed, of course, by William Shakespeare,<sup>5</sup> widely regarded as the world’s greatest dramatist (Schoenbaum, 1987). Shakespeare’s influence extends from theatre and literature to movies, philosophy, psychology, and the English language (Bloom, 1998). We discuss three characteristics of ancient Greek and Shakespearean tragedy that help us understand the process by which a person’s view of reality is transformed in a way that leads to detrimental human action.

First, actions and events are central to tragedy. Aristotle argued that the structure of the actions and events (i.e., the plot) is the most important element in tragedy. Although elements such as character and thought are important, tragedy is first and foremost “the imitation of events and not persons, actions and not character” (Critchley, 2019, p. 204). Actions have important characteristics, one of which is that they are performed primarily by humans. In Shakespearean tragedy, catastrophic outcomes “do not simply happen, nor are they sent; they proceed mainly from actions, and those the actions of men” (Bradley, 1904, p. 6). Furthermore, characters’ actions are not completely predetermined; they act with some degree of agency and intentionality (Gregory, 2005). According to Aristotle, if a character’s downfall is brought about solely by external causes, this is a misadventure and not a tragedy. Process theories offer explanations “in terms of the sequence of events leading to an outcome” (Langley, 1999, p. 692, emphasis in original). Thus, tragedy’s focus on actions and events is consistent with a process perspective.

Second, tragedy is a dialectical mode of experience (Szondi, 2002). Like tragedy, dialectic is a broad term that can be applied in many ways. For example, Hegel’s dialectics constitute a method of philosophical reasoning in which less sophisticated, opposing concepts unite into a more sophisticated, new concept (Bencivenga, 2000). The dialectic in tragedy is similar yet distinct. It is a mode of reversal, inversion, and negation where characters are implicated at each step (Critchley, 2019). There are opposing forces; however, the dialectical experience in a tragedy unites those forces in a way that results in an adverse outcome (Szondi, 2002). Furthermore, this experience may occur within a character in the form of an internal conflict. As a general rule, the protagonist (leading character) in a Shakespearean tragedy is torn by an internal conflict wherein he vacillates between two opposing forces (Bradley, 1904). The dialectical experience is usually triggered by an action or event— often in the form of an antagonist making a claim to the protagonist. Process theories attempt to explain how things evolve and why they evolve in certain ways (Van de Ven, 2007). The dialectical experience in tragedy helps us regarding this latter point; that is, it facilitates our understanding of why a person’s view of reality evolves in a particular way.

Finally, tragedy captures a temporal process in which the protagonist’s view of reality is transformed in a way that leads to detrimental human action and the protagonist’s downfall (Myers, 1956). After enduring an initial dialectical phase, the protagonist forms a mistaken belief based on misleading or false information typically produced by the antagonist. The protagonist’s belief is often reinforced or further confirmed by new information. The result is a dramatic change in the protagonist’s worldview and circumstances over time. Aristotle argued that the best tragedies are based on a reversal from prosperity to misery. Importantly, this reversal is caused not by depravity but by human action. The suffering and calamity at the end of a Shakespearean tragedy contrast with the protagonist’s previous happiness or glory (Bradley, 1904). Furthermore, the protagonist’s downfall results from action he takes based on his transformed view of reality (Critchley, 2019; Szondi, 2002). Put another way, it is highly unlikely that the protagonist would have engaged in detrimental actions if his view of reality had remained consistent throughout the play. Process theories incorporate temporal progressions of activities to explain phenomena (Langley et al., 2013). The temporal aspect of tragedy is aligned with a process perspective’s time-based aspect.

Since tragedy comprises a fundamental base of our theory—and some readers may not be familiar with it—we illustrate with a summary of Shakespeare’s tragedy Othello.<sup>6</sup> In this tragedy, Iago is a Venetian soldier (and the play’s antagonist) who is passed over for promotion by Othello (the protagonist), a general in the Venetian army who has secretly married Desdemona. Determined to destroy Othello’s happiness, Iago claims to Othello that Desdemona is having an affair with Cassio, a lieutenant in the Venetian army. Iago’s claim plants a seed of doubt in Othello’s mind regarding Desdemona’s fidelity. Othello says, “I do not think but Desdemona’s honest<sup>7</sup>” (3.3.225)<sup>8</sup>; however, he quickly doubts it: “And yet, how nature erring from itself” (3.3.227). That is, Othello does not initially believe Iago’s claim to be true, yet he does not think it is false.

Confronted with Iago’s claim that Desdemona is having an affair with Cassio, Othello enters a dialectic phase where he wavers between two opposing beliefs: Iago’s claim is true, or his claim is false. At one point, Othello says, “I think my wife be honest, and think she is not” (3.3.381). Othello looks for evidence regarding Iago’s claim, and Iago supplies it in the form of a special handkerchief Othello had given Desdemona during their courtship. Unknown to Othello, Iago tricks his wife Emilia into stealing the handkerchief, plants the handkerchief in Cassio’s room, and suggests to Othello that Desdemona gave the handkerchief to Cassio as a sign of her love. The idea that Desdemona gave away the handkerchief, which Othello then sees in Cassio’s possession, counts as proof of Desdemona’s infidelity. From this point onward, Othello believes Iago’s claim that Desdemona is having an affair with Cassio.

Having formed this belief, Othello enters a reinforcement phase. Iago tells Othello to hide and watch Cassio’s reactions while Iago questions him. Iago prods Cassio to talk about his relationship with Bianca, a courtesan, but Iago says her name so quietly that Othello thinks the two men are talking about Desdemona. Othello interprets this information as further proof of Iago’s claim, thereby reinforcing his belief. When Desdemona cannot produce the handkerchief, Othello vows to Iago that he will kill Desdemona and Cassio. In fact, Othello’s belief is so strong that he thinks killing Desdemona is the only way to stop her from future betrayal: “Yet she must die, else she’ll betray more men” (5.2.6). That evening, Othello smothers Desdemona to death with a pillow. Othello justifies himself to Emilia, citing the handkerchief as proof. Recognizing the handkerchief as the one she stole for Iago, Emilia is stunned and reveals Iago’s guilt. Iago kills Emilia, is arrested, and is almost killed by Othello, who now understands the truth. Othello draws a concealed weapon, stabs himself, and kisses Desdemona as he dies. Iago transformed Othello’s view of reality in a way that led to Othello’s detrimental action and ultimate downfall. Desdemona, the loving wife, appears as the adulteress, and Othello is transformed into the murderer of the one he loves.

## 2.2 Truth: Correspondence and Coherence

Truth has been a topic of discussion for thousands of years. Scholars, philosophers, and theologians have proposed and debated numerous theories and views of truth. Those debates and theories focus on questions such as: What is truth? What makes something true?

Does truth have value? It would be impossible to review all there is to say about truth in this paper. Instead, we discuss two historically important theories of truth relevant to our objective—correspondence and coherence (Kirkham, 1992).

The basic reasoning of the correspondence theory is that a claim is true if it corresponds to the way things actually are in reality. The theory is usually traced to Plato’s writings and is often attributed to Aristotle, Aquinas, G. E. Moore, and Bertrand Russell, among others. The “correspondence theory” label is typically applied to any view explicitly adopting the idea that truth consists in a relation to reality. A simple form of the theory is stated as follows: claim<sup>9</sup> “A” is true if and only if A corresponds to some fact; A is false if and only if A does not correspond to any fact. A fact is a piece of information that describes the world as it exists.

Correspondence holds between a claim and a fact when the claim and the fact have the same structure and the same constituents at each structural position. When they correspond, the claim and fact mirror each other. For example:

• Claim that Snow is white

$$
\bullet \quad \text { Fact } \quad \text { Snow   is   white }
$$

Although structured like facts, claims can be true or false. In a true case, we would find a fact that corresponds to the claim. In a false case, we would not find a fact that corresponds to the claim. In summary, according to the correspondence theory described here, what is key to truth is a relation between claims and the real world, which obtains when the world contains a fact that structurally corresponds to the claim.

The coherence theory of truth states that a claim is true if and only if it belongs to a coherent system of beliefs. The theory has been attributed to J. G. Fichte, G. W. F. Hegel, F. H. Bradley, and Brand Blashard. One version of the coherence theory holds that the coherence relation is some form of entailment; that is, a claim coheres with a set of beliefs if and only if it is entailed by members of the set. Another version argues that coherence is mutual explanatory support between beliefs. Consider the following illustration. If a person says, “there are orange elephants walking in the street in front of our house,” one can assess whether this claim is true by considering other beliefs one has already accepted as true, such as

• Elephants are gray.

• This area is not an elephant habitat.

• There is neither a zoo nor a circus anywhere nearby.

The claim fails to cohere with many other claims that a person believes and has ample reason not to abandon. In other words, the claim is inconsistent with the person’s belief set. The person therefore believes the claim is false.

Coherence theory differs from correspondence theory in two important ways. First, they provide different accounts of the relation that claims hold to their truth conditions. For the former, the relation is coherence with other claims and the person’s preexisting beliefs; for the latter; the relation is correspondence to facts based in reality. Second, the truth conditions differ. Coherence theory states that the truth conditions of claims consist in other beliefs. In contrast, correspondence theory states that the truth conditions of claims are in objective features of the world (i.e., facts). To quickly summarize, correspondence theory defines truth as consistency between claim and fact(s); coherence theory defines truth as consistency between a claim and a person’s belief(s).

Although philosophers have long debated the advantages and drawbacks of both the correspondence and coherence theories of truth (Schmitt, 1995), it is important to keep in mind that they are only theories that explain what truths are and what (if anything) makes them true. In practice, their application and usefulness may depend on contextual factors. For example, a person may encounter an unfamiliar claim and subsequently decide to seek out facts before deciding whether the claim is true or false. Alternatively, a person may encounter a familiar claim, assess it relative to their belief system, and subsequently judge the claim’s truth value. Correspondence and coherence theories need not operate in isolation. Studies suggest that people sometimes rely on multiple cues and methods to judge whether a claim is true or false (Brashier & Marsh, 2020). Thus, people may rely on one theory, or they may use elements of correspondence and coherence together to judge truth (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). Our theory accounts for such situations. Hence, we draw on both the correspondence and coherence theories.

## 2.3 Technology: Social Media Influence and Learning

Although social media has brought tremendous benefits to individuals, organizations, and society, researchers have recently started noticing the negative consequences of social media. One emerging stream of work focuses on the creation, dissemination, and effects of misinformation on social media platforms. We position our work in this stream. Within this literature, researchers have tried to understand how to (1) reduce the believability of misinformation on social media platforms and (2) reduce users’ engagement with misinformation. Their findings shed light on a number of important factors and mechanisms, such as source rating and the primacy of information presented to social media users (Kim & Dennis, 2019); presenting source rating information like aggregated expert ratings of articles, aggregated user ratings of articles, and aggregated user ratings of sources (Kim et al., 2019); asking users to evaluate the truthfulness of news articles (Moravec et al., 2022); including a video in fake news (Wang et al., 2022); and flagging misinformation (Moravec et al., 2020). Other relevant factors include the lack of careful reasoning and relevant knowledge of social media users and their use of heuristics (Pennycook & Rand, 2021); echo chambers and filter bubbles on social media platforms, which affect the diversification of information sources (Kitchens et al., 2020; Rhodes, 2022); social endorsement cues on social media platforms, which influence users’ credibility perceptions of misinformation (Luo et al., 2022); and the importance of confirmation bias in reinforcing users’ beliefs in false information (Kim & Dennis, 2019).

Notwithstanding the importance of these studies in furthering our understanding of social media-driven misinformation, one consistent takeaway in this literature is that users tend to interpret information on social media in a way that reinforces their beliefs and values—regardless of whether the information is accurate, misleading, or blatantly false. This research also tends to overlook the multiple stages and progressions in which misinformation influences a user’s view of reality. We address these issues. Keeping tragedy and theories of truth in mind, we identified two social media properties pertinent to our research question—influence and learning. As noted earlier in Shakespeare’s tragedy Othello, Iago influenced Othello’s truth judgment, belief formation, and subsequent behavior. Similarly, social media has the capacity to influence users (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Kitchens et al., 2020). Furthermore, Iago learned effective tactics by observing Othello’s behavior and the actions of those around him (e.g., Cassio, Desdemona). Likewise, social media platforms are capable of learning from data collected from users’ behaviors, connections, and preferences on social media as well as other sources (Gregory et al., 2021).

Of course, social media platforms have unique properties not found in dramaturgical tragedy; nevertheless, we posit that social media platforms can transform a person’s view of reality by (1) influencing users via feeding and signaling mechanisms and (2) learning about users via sensing and matching mechanisms (see Table 1).<sup>10</sup>

We first discuss social media’s influence property and corresponding mechanisms. In the persuasion literature, informational and normative influence are two widely studied social influence mechanisms (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955; Wood, 2000). Informational influence refers to the extent to which a person accepts information obtained from another as evidence about reality; normative influence refers to the extent to which a person conforms with the positive expectations of another. We apply this view to our study. First, social media’s feeding mechanism is a process by which a social media platform presents content that a user might accept as evidence about reality. This mechanism relies on intelligent content management algorithms to personalize and curate content that users view on the platform. These algorithms filter and feed content that is more likely to keep users engaged with the platform. They prioritize content that users see based on the likelihood that they will want to see it. Users’ beliefs and views regarding what is true can be influenced when they consume information fed to them by the platform. Thus, feeding primarily facilitates an informational influence (Aral, 2020). Consuming content fed by social media newsfeed algorithms is associated with a partisan shift and a broadening of diversity in terms of the information source and slant of news consumed by users (Bakshy et al., 2015; Kitchens et al., 2020). These shifts underscore changes in users’ views of reality following their exposure to the social media feeding mechanism. For example, when Facebook selects posts for each user, its feeding algorithms consider hundreds of variables to predict whether the user will click, like, comment, share, hide, or even mark content as spam, with a certain degree of confidence. This prediction is quantified into a “relevancy score” that is specific to the user and the post. Facebook’s algorithms rank and sort all possible content for feeding based on their relevancy scores for a particular user. As a result, every time a user logs into Facebook, the content the user sees at the top of the news feed is chosen over thousands of content items as the one most likely to cause reactions and engagement (Meta, 2015).

Second, social media’s signaling mechanism is a process by which a social media platform delivers reactions, notifications, and other meta-knowledge that might lead a user to conform with the positive expectations of others. This mechanism uses algorithms to channel and deliver social signals such as reactions and other information about a user’s social connections on the platform. Signaling distinguishes social media platforms from other media in that social media users are not only influenced by direct content (e.g., news feed) but also by “a reverberating cacophony of social signals, from our peers and the crowd” (Aral, 2020, p. 156). Curating these signals allows social media platforms to create a strong social and peer persuasion effect that can influence users’ truth judgment, belief formation, and behaviors. In doing so, signaling primarily facilitates a normative influence (Aral, 2020). A case in point is Facebook’s experiments with “social messages” in the 2010 and 2012 US elections. In those elections, a random group of Facebook users received a simple informational message from Facebook reminding them to vote; another random group of users saw a social message on their newsfeed that in addition to the voting reminder, displayed the photos of up to six of their Facebook friends (i.e., social signal) who had already clicked on the “I Voted” button on Facebook. Facebook found that users who saw the social signal were 0.39 percent<sup>11</sup> more likely to vote than those who saw the informational message without the social signal.

We now turn to social media’s learning property and associated mechanisms. In the organizational learning literature, scholars tend to categorize organizational learning processes into two broad categories—direct learning and indirect learning (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011; Bingham & Davis, 2012). Direct learning processes include those in which an organization learns firsthand through their experiences (e.g., trial-and-error learning); indirect learning processes include those in which an organization learns secondhand through others’ experiences (e.g., vicarious learning). Scholars have conceptualized platforms as organizations (Gawer, 2014; Rolland et al., 2018) capable of learning (Gregory et al., 2021). We extend this line of thinking. First, social media’s sensing mechanism is a process by which a social media platform learns directly about a user by observing, tracing, and analyzing the user’s behavior on the platform. The sensing mechanism relies on machine learning techniques to learn users’ preferences, interests, and prior behaviors as they use the social media platform and consume content over time (Aral, 2020). As such, sensing mechanisms learn directly from users’ behaviors on the platform. For example, Instagram learns about the pictures that users post and like.

Second, social media’s matching mechanism is a process by which a social media platform learns indirectly about a user via their social network by identifying and verifying (with the user) the most similar platform users. This mechanism uses machine learning techniques to identify and suggest who users should date, follow, connect with, or conduct business with on the platform (Aral, 2020). These techniques are also known as friend-suggestion and “people you may know” algorithms. Matching mechanisms exist on nearly every social media platform and have been crucial for platform growth in terms of network size. Matching-based algorithms utilize users’ networks, characteristics, physical locations, and behaviors to predict similarity between users. The platform then suggests that people with higher degrees of predicted similarity should connect with each other (Aral, 2020). This shapes users’ social networks over time, allowing the platform to learn indirectly about users from users network connections.

All four social media mechanisms play different roles and can have differential effects on users. Feeding and signaling are front and center in that they deliver information to users. In doing so, these influencebased mechanisms can directly affect social media users. Sensing and matching operate in the background in that they learn about users and subsequently enable the platform to modify its algorithms and other capabilities to better keep users engaged. That is, learning-based mechanisms provide personalized, user-specific input to feeding and signaling mechanisms, thereby indirectly affecting social media users. It is also important to recognize that these social media mechanisms do not operate in isolation; rather, they work together to facilitate (mis)information delivery to users.

For example, the sensing mechanism creates a personalized profile of users’ interests and contents that they engage with on social media platforms. From a machine learning perspective, the sensing mechanism labels content based on the extent of users’ engagement with prior content. This labeled content can then be used by machine learning algorithms underpinning a feeding mechanism for predicting the relevancy scores of new content for users. Similarly, the matching mechanism actively learns about users’ social connections on the platform and the strength of social ties by acquiring the labels of some unknown relationships from the users (e.g., via friend suggestions). From a machine learning perspective, the matching mechanism facilitates active learning for inferring users’ social ties (Zhuang et al., 2012). The output of this active learning is a set of labeled social ties that can be used by both (1) a feeding mechanism to improve the prediction of relevancy scores of contents for the users and (2) a signaling mechanism to identify more influential social signals for the users. Therefore, we focus on feeding and signaling when explaining the role of social media in our theory. However, as noted, feeding and signaling are dependent on sensing and matching mechanisms. Put another way, learning and influencing social media mechanisms work together to transform a person’s view of reality.

## 3 The 3T Theory of Social Media-Driven Misinformation

We now present the 3T (tragedy, truth, and technology) process theory that explains how social media transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action (see Figure 1). The process starts when a person encounters a claim. This claim triggers doubt and, if the doubt is strong enough, the claim recipient enters a dialectic phase where they must decide whether the claim is true or false. Social media may distort their judgment of the claim’s truth value. The dialectic phase produces either a supported belief (where a claim corresponds to factual evidence) or a suspect belief (where a claim does not correspond to factual evidence). Following this, the claim recipient might enter a phase where their newly formulated suspect belief is reinforced by further information consumed on social media. Finally, the recipient may act on their suspect belief in a way that leads to detrimental outcomes. We describe our theory in greater detail below.

## 3.1 Claims and Doubt

Our starting point is an event in which a person encounters a new claim. As above, we call this person the claim recipient. We define a claim as an assertion of the truth of something, and we note two aspects of claims that fall within our boundary conditions. First, we focus on claims that exhibit truth values, i.e., they are true or false. Consider two claims: (1) COVID-19 is a respiratory disease caused by a novel coronavirus called SARS-CoV-2 and (2) abortion is wrong. We can determine the truth value of the former claim by looking for research findings and other evidence regarding COVID-19. The latter claim is controversial in that there is no agreement about the ways in which claims of this type can be confirmed or refuted or even, indeed, if they possess truth value (O’Connor, 1975). It would therefore fall outside our boundary conditions.<sup>12</sup>

Second, the claim recipient perceives the claim to be meaningful; that is, it has serious, important implications relevant to the recipient. This implies that the claim requires the recipient to respond in some way, i.e., accept or reject the claim as it cannot be easily dismissed. In fact, the claim should be meaningful enough for the recipient to become involved with it. Involvement is a psychological state initiated by a claim’s importance and personal relevance (Sherif et al., 1965). A recipient’s involvement plays out during the dialectic phase (described in the next section). What is important to note here is that a claim’s meaningfulness should be strong enough to be taken seriously by the recipient, to the point where the recipient becomes directly involved with the claim. Note that meaningfulness is a perception that can vary between people. A claim that is meaningful to one person may not be meaningful to someone else.

Social media can suggest claims to users via its feeding mechanism. For example, Facebook’s “Suggested for You” posts in its newsfeed are personalized contents based on what may be relevant to each user based on Facebook’s learning of users’ characteristics, preferences, and prior activities (Facebook, 2022). Similarly, other social media platforms have followed suit and integrated “Suggested Posts” into their newsfeed for users (Hatmaker, 2021). Such suggested content can introduce new claims to users from other pages and groups outside users’ social networks. Claims found on social media platforms may vary in truth value: Some claims may be true; some claims may be false.

It is important to distinguish claimants in tragedy from claimants on social media platforms. In tragedy, a claimant is typically an antagonist who aims to directly influence another person’s beliefs and/or behavior (e.g., Iago tries to persuade Othello). A social media setting may be more complex. People may post misleading claims on social media for a variety of reasons—e.g., to persuade others to agree with them, to play a trick on others, or just to game the platform’s algorithms. In the latter case, the algorithm may inadvertently influence other people in misleading ways. Thus, while the “tragedy” claimant and the “social media” claimant both try to manipulate something, the latter may be a complex amalgam of people, algorithms, and technology.<sup>13</sup>

A claim might trigger doubt in the recipient. Doubt is a feeling of uncertainty or lack of conviction. When in doubt, the recipient is not completely confident about whether the claim is true or false. Doubt can range from weak to strong. On the one hand, a recipient can have a weak feeling of uncertainty (i.e., high certainty) regarding the truth value of the claim. In other words, there is very little doubt in their mind. In this case, they might accept or reject the claim with little to no further consideration. On the other hand, a recipient can have a strong feeling of uncertainty regarding the truth value of the claim. They will be less likely to quickly judge the claim’s truth value. If this doubt is strong enough, it will initiate a dialectic phase (described below) where the recipient will try to reduce their uncertainty by searching for relevant evidence (Lanzetta & Driscoll, 1968).

## 3.2 Dialectic Phase

Faced with doubt, the recipient enters a dialectic phase. Three concepts are important to understanding this phase—evidence type, recipient interpretation, and belief type. First, we distinguish two types of evidence—factual and illusory. Factual evidence refers to information that accurately describes the world as it exists (either objectively or based on the best available evidence from relevant experts). Illusory evidence refers to information that appears to be factual but does not accurately describe the world as it exists. Illusory evidence can be false or misleading information that is communicated regardless of an intention to deceive or information that is deliberately deceptive. The recipient can discover factual evidence and/or illusory evidence when trying to judge a claim’s truth value. This discovery can be intentional (recipient seeks evidence) and/or unintentional (unsolicited evidence is presented to the recipient).

This brings us to our second concept. Regardless of how evidence is discovered, the recipient can interpret it as true or false. A recipient’s interpretation may or may not align with the type of evidence. For example, one person may interpret factual evidence as being true; another person may interpret the same evidence as being false. Finally, we include two types of beliefs—supported and suspect. A supported belief adheres to a correspondence between the claim and factual evidence. In contrast, a suspect belief does not adhere to a correspondence between the claim and factual evidence. These beliefs are potential outcomes of the dialectic phase. Figure 2 provides a visual intersection of evidence type, recipient interpretation, and belief type. This figure helps us understand the potential paths a claim recipient may take in the dialectic phase. We describe those paths and these concepts in greater detail below.

Encountering a meaningful, initially doubtful claim, the claim recipient will seek to reduce their doubt and uncertainty. As they do so, they waver between two diametrically opposed beliefs: (1) the claim is true, and (2) the claim is false. The recipient can subscribe to only one of these beliefs. According to the coherence theory of truth, the recipient can judge the claim’s truth value by assessing whether it is consistent with their existing belief system (Brashier & Marsh, 2020). However, if the claim is meaningful to the recipient and creates some doubt, the recipient will most likely look for evidence that supports or refutes the claim. Some of this evidence may be factual; some of it may be illusory. Furthermore, the recipient may not discover both types of evidence.

Evidence discovery eventually leads to either a supported belief or a suspect belief. We first discuss situations that result in supported beliefs. In these cases, the recipient interprets all or most of the evidence they encounter “correctly.” By correctly, we mean the recipient is not mistaken in their judgment. Thus, if the recipient discovers factual evidence and interprets it as being true (upper left-hand quadrant in Figure 2), they will subsequently judge the claim as true. Likewise, the recipient will dismiss any illusory evidence as being false or misleading (lower righthand quadrant). According to the correspondence theory of truth, a claim is true if and only if the claim corresponds to some fact that represents the claim. Therefore, we can say with a reasonable degree of confidence that what the recipient believes to be true is actually true. Put another way, the belief is supported by factual evidence.

Furthermore, if the recipient fails to find factual evidence to support the claim—and correctly interprets any illusory evidence they may encounter—they will most likely conclude that the claim is false. This outcome is also consistent with the correspondence theory of truth. Namely, a claim is false if and only if the claim does not correspond to any fact that represents the claim. Again, we can be reasonably confident that what the recipient believes to be false is, in fact, false. This supported belief is corroborated by the lack of factual evidence.

We now turn to situations that result in suspect beliefs. In these cases, the recipient interprets all or most of the evidence they encounter incorrectly. That is, they are mistaken in their judgment. It follows that if the recipient discovers factual evidence and interprets it as being false (upper right-hand quadrant in Figure 2), they will most likely judge the claim as false. Similarly, the recipient will interpret any illusory evidence as being true (lower left-hand quadrant), leading them to judge the claim as true when, in fact, it is false or misleading. Either way, there is no correspondence between claim and fact. Thus, we can be reasonably confident that the recipient will have formed a suspect belief.

The recipient may act based on a supported or suspect belief. However, it is a suspect belief that leads to a protagonist’s downfall in dramaturgical tragedy (Bradley, 1904; Szondi, 2002). Thus, we suggest that the formation of a suspect belief is one key event in the process by which social media transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action. As such, we focus on suspect beliefs.

Social media can affect the dialectic phase by distorting perceived correspondence between a claim and factual evidence, in turn producing a suspect belief. One mechanism by which this occurs is social media feeding misinformation that creates an illusion of knowing—“the belief that comprehension has been attained when, in fact, comprehension has failed” (Glenberg et al., 1982, p. 597). This illusion is rooted in a discrepancy between a subjective assessment of an understanding of a phenomenon and an objective fact or the objective accuracy of an understanding of the phenomenon. The illusion of knowing occurs when the subjective assessment of understanding overestimates the objective accuracy of understanding (Glenberg et al., 1982). Put another way, the illusion of knowing is the result of overconfidence in one’s perceived knowledge of a phenomenon.

A claim recipient who experiences an illusion of knowing and subsequently overestimates their understanding of a claim lacks the requisite knowledge to identify and differentiate between factual and illusory evidence for the claim (Vitriol & Marsh, 2018). Social media can feed misinformation that fills the recipient’s knowledge gap, thereby leading them to overestimate their perceived understanding and form a suspect belief in the dialectic phase in one of two ways. First, the recipient can reject a true claim because they believe contrary misinformation to be true. Second, the recipient can believe a false claim because they interpret “supporting” illusory evidence as being true. Either way, social media-driven misinformation may play a role in the recipient’s mistaken judgment.

Social media can also affect the dialectic phase through a signaling mechanism that creates a “socially contagious” environment. People often find themselves uncertain of whether to approach or avoid certain beliefs or behaviors, a situation referred to as an approach-avoidance conflict. Social contagion alters the balance of this conflict. Namely, social contagion refers to the spread of affect, beliefs, or behavior from a producer to a consumer (Levy & Nail, 1993). When social contagion is at work, the consumer (who faces an approach-avoidance conflict) experiences a reduction in uncertainty and is more likely to either approach or avoid a particular belief or behavior (Burt, 1987). For example, the spread of information regarding successful aircraft hijackings in the United States between 1968 and 1972 generated additional hijacking attempts of the same type (Holden, 1986).

Social media signals such as “liking” or sharing a claim by other users can create a contagious environment (Hodas & Lerman, 2014). In such an environment, these signals reduce a claim recipient’s uncertainty and make it more likely that they will either judge a true claim as false or judge a false claim as true. Put another way, signaling can create a situation where people believe things if others believe them (Metzger et al., 2010). This social contagion effect is even stronger when the signal’s source is familiar (Luo et al., 2022), such as social connections on social media platforms. Overall, social signals from familiar others are stronger, and claims endorsed by social connections with stronger ties to users (e.g., “liked,” “shared,” or “done” by Facebook friends) are considered more credible and are more prone to contagion effects. As a result, social media platforms can significantly increase social contagion forces by producing strong social signals. Social contagion can lead to suspect beliefs when claims endorsed by social signals do not correspond to factual evidence.

## 3.3 Reinforcement Phase

Although the dialectic phase ends, the recipient may not necessarily take immediate action based on a suspect belief. However, the recipient will likely store that new belief in memory and judge future claims against it (Brashier & Marsh, 2020). If so, the recipient enters the reinforcement phase. We now turn to the coherence theory of truth. In the reinforcement phase, the recipient assesses whether new information (e.g., claims, evidence) is consistent with the recently formed suspect belief. According to the coherence theory of truth and extant psychology literature (Brashier & Marsh, 2020), the recipient will likely judge future claims as true if they are consistent with existing beliefs. Conversely, the recipient will likely judge future claims as false if they are not consistent with existing beliefs. This is not to say that the recipient cannot change their mind. We simply note that people tend to accept claims that fit with their beliefs and reject mismatches (Brashier & Marsh, 2020). This idea is consistent with the “reinforcement” findings in many ITbased misinformation studies (Horner et al., 2021; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Moravec et al., 2019). Thus, future claims and evidence consistent with the suspect belief will reinforce the recipient’s belief in the original claim.

Social media can affect the reinforcement phase by strengthening the coherence between the suspect belief and new information. One mechanism by which this occurs is social media feeding misinformation that induces an illusory truth effect, a phenomenon by which people are more likely to judge repeated claims as true compared to new claims (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). Repetition works because people behave like cognitive misers, using cognitive shortcuts and heuristics to avoid effortful cognitive processing. One effective cue that can serve as a heuristic for judging a claim’s truth value is cognitive fluency—the experienced ease of ongoing conceptual or perceptual cognitive processes (Unkelbach, 2006). Cognitive fluency has a profound influence on various judgments and inferences across different contexts (Brashier & Marsh, 2020). For example, fluently perceived names are typically judged to be famous (Jacoby et al., 1989), and claims made by native speakers are often judged to be truer than those spoken with a foreign accent (Lev-Ari & Keysar, 2010).

Repetition is an important determinant of cognitive fluency (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). When a person encounters a claim for the first time, the elements of that claim are processed and stored in memory. Upon repeated encounters with the same claim or claims with similar elements, the stored elements of the first claim act as corresponding references in memory (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). Repetition strengthens this correspondence. The curation of content via social media’s feeding property increases the likelihood of repeated encounters with similar claims (Aral, 2020). Repeated encounters with similar claims can induce greater coherence between the elements of those claims and references in memory, relative to new (not repeated) claims (Unkelbach et al., 2019). This happens when prior encounters with the same set of elements in a claim form corresponding references in memory that are coherently linked over time. As such, repetition not only creates and strengthens the corresponding references in memory for a claim; it can also manufacture coherence between those corresponding references and elements of the claims (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). It is in this way that the repetition of a claim can influence the reinforcement phase. Research has shown that more corresponding references in memory and stronger coherence between the stored elements can make the cognitive processing of the claim easier, leading to an experience of higher cognitive fluency (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017). The cognitive fluency shortcut creates the feeling that the claim is true, regardless of the presence or absence of factual evidence, leading to an illusory truth effect (Brashier & Marsh, 2020).

## 3.4 Detrimental Human Action

As noted earlier, the dialectical experience in a tragedy ultimately leads to calamity, suffering, and death (Szondi, 2002). Recall that Othello’s belief that his wife Desdemona is an adulteress contradicts the fact that she is not an adulteress. Unfortunately, Othello acts on his belief by killing Desdemona. Thus, whether or not a suspect belief is reinforced by related claims or illusory evidence, dramaturgical tragedy informs us that harm and suffering usually occur if the recipient takes action based on a suspect belief (Bradley, 1904; Critchley, 2019; Gregory, 2005). Consistent with the characteristics of action in tragedy described earlier, we note three aspects of actions that fit our theory. First, they are intentional acts performed by a human. Second, they are based on a suspect belief. Third, they are detrimental; that is, they tend to cause harm to the recipient and/or others. It is important to note that these actions can but do not necessarily result in harm and suffering.

Consider the Wayfair human trafficking episode. A series of viral social media posts in 2020 alleged that Wayfair was trafficking children on its website under the guise of selling home goods. According to this narrative, when someone buys a product listed for thousands of dollars on wayfair.com, the buyer allegedly indicates a desire to purchase a child whose name is also the product name. Despite numerous reports showing that these claims did not correspond to factual evidence, some people interpreted illusory evidence as being true, judged the claims as true, formed a suspect belief, and took actions such as contacting anti-trafficking organizations with concerns about Wayfair. The volume of Wayfair-related calls to the National Human Trafficking Hotline made it difficult for the trafficking hotline to provide support and services to those who needed help (Polaris, 2020). In one instance, a real victim of sex trafficking fled a safe space (which put her back in danger) because she no longer wanted to wait for help from an advocate who had been trying to connect the victim to hotline services for weeks (Contrera, 2021).

The Wayfair illustration highlights two other aspects of detrimental human actions in our theory.<sup>14</sup> One is that an action may not necessarily result in a tragic outcome. The sex trafficking victim may have returned to a dangerous situation, but she could have found refuge somewhere else. We do not know for certain what happened. Likewise, outcomes from detrimental human actions may range from mild (e.g., hurting someone’s feelings) to moderate (e.g., getting sick after ingesting ivermectin) to severe (e.g., murdering children who have “serpent DNA”). In other words, these actions can fall on a continuum from low harm to severe harm. A second aspect is that an action’s effects may be heterogeneous. The Wayfair “believers” who made calls to the trafficking hotline most likely did not experience any harm to themselves. After all, they felt they were doing the right thing. Yet the volume of calls created communication challenges for hotline workers, frustration and delays for advocates, and stress and anxiety for victims. Taken together, detrimental human actions can vary in severity and in their effects on different groups of people.

## 4 Discussion

Social media platforms have emerged as powerful breeding grounds for misinformation. In this paper, we describe the underlying processes and effects of social media-driven misinformation. Building on concepts in dramaturgical tragedy, theories of truth, and social media literature, we explain how social media transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action. Our theory contributes to the literature in multiple ways.

First, we contribute to research by identifying and describing social media’s transformative role in a person’s view of reality. Social media’s “reality reinforcement” role has been established as an explanation for why people incorrectly interpret misinformation. Many studies have shown that confirmation bias is alive and well on these platforms: people frequently consume information on social media in a way that reinforces their view of reality, even when that information is false or misleading. Yet there can be other explanations. People also modify their beliefs and form entirely new beliefs (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). In fact, they may form beliefs that dramatically alter their worldview (Albarracin & Shavitt, 2018). We unpack social media’s transformative role by describing several ways in which it can influence this belief formation process—by feeding false claims, presenting content that creates an illusion of knowing, and delivering signals that trigger a truth bias. In doing so, our theory moves us closer to a comprehensive understanding of social mediadriven misinformation phenomena.

Our second contribution builds on the previous point: we recognize that social media distorts a person’s truth judgment of a claim through a dialectic process. People draw inferences from relevant cues when constructing truth judgments (Brashier & Marsh, 2020); thus, it is imperative that we identify all the potential ways in which social media affects truth judgment processes. In addition to distorting truth judgments by confirming a suspect belief (i.e., confirmation bias), social media can disrupt a person’s ability to correctly judge a claim as true or false—before the belief can even be subject to confirmation bias. Importantly, we propose that only some claims will trigger a dialectic process. Specifically, we focus on truth-based claims that are meaningful to and initially doubted by the claim recipient. This gives us a better view of when and how social media affects truth judgment processes.

Third, we contribute to the literature by including social media mechanisms that exhibit direct and indirect effects on a person’s view of reality. Extant literature has traditionally focused on one of those mechanisms (Kim & Dennis, 2019) or has lumped them together at an abstract level (Aral, 2020). We contribute to this literature by disentangling the mechanisms underlying two main properties of social media: influencing and learning. We describe feeding and signaling as two key mechanisms that influence the user’s view of reality. However, the effectiveness of feeding and signaling mechanisms depends on social media’s learning property, which is realized via its sensing and matching mechanisms. As social media algorithms learn about users’ preferences and characteristics via sensing and matching mechanisms, they can feed and signal more personalized and engaging content to users. These mechanisms can heighten the effectiveness of misinformation by distorting users’ dialectic process. Understanding how influencing and learning mechanisms on social media work together to transform a person’s view of reality provides a more integrative perspective on social media-driven misinformation.

Finally, we contribute to research by developing an explicit process theory of misinformation and social media. Much of our understanding in this area rests on variance-based models. Notwithstanding their contributions, we lack solid knowledge regarding the events, stages, and temporal progressions that underlie social media-driven misinformation. A process perspective presumes that “the particular histories of individual cases may lead them to take different paths to different outcomes” (Van de Ven, 2007, p. 157). Furthermore, misinformation can affect people’s lives in a myriad of consequential ways (Greenspan & Loftus, 2021). With this in mind, we attempted to develop a process theory that can encompass a variety of cases, contexts, events, and patterns. We also recognize that specific cases may differ in their tempo and time span. For example, one person’s dialectic phase may involve consuming factual and/or illusory evidence on social media sporadically over a two-week time span; another person’s dialectic phase may involve consuming evidence on social media continuously over a three-hour time span. Our theory provides a versatile lens through which scholars can investigate how a person’s view of reality evolves as they exchange and consume social media content.

Our theory has several implications for future research. First, we introduce the concept of doubt to the misinformation arena. Researchers have studied similar concepts, such as article believability (Kim & Dennis, 2019) and headline credibility (Moravec et al., 2019). Doubt is distinct in that it captures a person’s level of uncertainty regarding a claim. It also plays a critical role in the early stages of our process model. A person may not enter the dialectic phase if they hold little to no doubt about a claim’s veracity; however, strong doubt can lead a person to search for information that can reduce their uncertainty (Lanzetta & Driscoll, 1968). Scholars could empirically measure and test the role of doubt in false claims that users accept as true on social media. Likewise, researchers could integrate doubt and uncertainty into work on fake news (Zhang & Ghorbani, 2020).

Second, we include a claim’s meaningfulness in our model. Scholars have investigated similar constructs. For example, Moravec et al. (2020) measured user engagement with a news headline as how likely a person would be to read, like, post a supporting comment, post an opposing comment, or share a news article. Similarly, Wang et al. (2022) assessed a user’s willingness to report a social media post as fake news. While these and similar studies have furthered our understanding of how important a piece of misinformation is to a user, they do not capture deeper aspects of meaningfulness. Meaningful claims have serious, important implications. A claim recipient is more likely to search for relevant evidence and act on more meaningful claims than they will for less or nonmeaningful claims (Lanzetta & Driscoll, 1968). In fact, in addition to acting on a claim within a social media environment (e.g., “liking” a post), a person facing a truly meaningful claim may go so far as to act outside a social media platform (e.g., killing children because they have “serpent DNA”). Sensing and matching could play salient roles here. Social media platform providers arguably want to keep users engaged. To foster such engagement, providers learn about users by observing, tracing, and analyzing (sensing) their behavior and by identifying similar users (matching). This allows the provider to feed relevant—and perhaps more meaningful—content to users. Future research could explore the nomological network of claim meaningfulness, feeding, sensing, and matching on social media platforms.

Third, our conceptualization of the dialectic phase creates new avenues in misinformation research. One such avenue involves looking at the various formats in which social media feeds information that distorts a person’s perceived correspondence between a claim and factual evidence. This area includes textual content, basic multimedia like infographics and memes, more complex audio and video, and emerging technological advances. Recent advancements in generative artificial intelligence (AI) such as large language models (e.g., OpenAI’s GPT models, Google’s Bard, Microsoft’s Bing, and Meta’s LLaMA) and their ability to generate human-like, convincing claims along with their innate tendency to “hallucinate” are of particular concern (Maynez et al., 2020). As these technologies are used for content generation on social media (e.g., Snapchat’s My AI chatbot), their hard-to-detect hallucinations can further increase users’ likelihood of falling victim to false information (Kreps et al., 2022). Moreover, advancements in these technologies have extended their capabilities to generate nontextual content (e.g., OpenAI’s DALL-E). Given that nontextual media such as images, videos, and audio often serve as persuasive components of illusory evidence (Nash & Wade, 2009), someone may interpret manipulated and fabricated multimedia as factual evidence for a false claim when, in fact, that “supporting” information is illusory and fake (Morrison, 2023). Doing so can create an illusion of knowing that might lead to a suspect belief. Scholars could investigate whether, how, and when the format of misinformation on social media affects a person’s truth judgment during the dialectic phase.

Another avenue might focus on social signals in the dialectic phase. If a user believes a claim that reinforces their beliefs, the number of “likes” linked to that claim validates their judgment and overall belief system. They will probably also dismiss “dislikes” out of hand. Alternatively, if a user is trying to judge the truth value of a claim in the dialectic phase, the number of both “likes” and “dislikes” linked to that claim might serve as indicators of the claim’s veracity. However, some social media platforms have made dislike counts private, with only the content creator being able to view the number of dislikes. Simple design decisions like displaying the number of dislikes versus not displaying the number of dislikes may affect whether a claim recipient correctly interprets relevant evidence as factual or illusory. Moreover, signaling is dependent upon the makeup of a user’s social network (Aral, 2020). Hence, matching might play an indirect role in the dialectic phase through signaling. Researchers could explore the conditions under which signaling and matching independently and/or jointly influence a recipient’s interpretation of evidence relevant to a claim.

Fourth, although we focused on influence mechanisms (feeding and signaling), our recognition of two socia media learning mechanisms (sensing and matching) creates new research opportunities. For instance, social network tie characteristics and structural features of a network could potentially help us understand the effects of social media’s matching mechanism. Tie characteristics include degree and symmetry (among others), and structural features include structural holes and various centrality variables (Kane et al., 2014). These and other social network factors may influence content delivered by feeding mechanisms and signals emitted by signaling mechanisms. Researchers could also investigate social media platforms that have weaker learning mechanisms. Consider the following illustration. A man shot and murdered 10 people in a Buffalo, New York supermarket in May 2022 (Thompson, 2022). Prior to that incident, the shoote spent considerable time browsing 4chan, an anonymous social media platform. 4chan feeds content and provides signals, so it can influence users. In fact, the shooter claimed that 4chan was instrumental to the formulation of his radical ideological stances. However, registration is not available on 4chan, and users typically post content anonymously. Thus, 4chan does not use matching and sensing algorithms typically seen in social media platforms (Aral, 2020). Even so, 4chan is structured around topics in the form of “boards” and “threads,” and that structure can direct users’ attention toward certain topics and away from other topics. This organization matches users to topics (albeit loosely). This suggests that a social media platform’s organizing structure can play a role in the spread and effect of misinformation—even when sensing and matching algorithms are not necessarily at work.

Researchers could also apply interventions to our theory. Specifically, digital interventions such as behavioral nudges, sludges, and boosting methods (Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff, 2017; Lorenz-Spreen et al., 2020) could potentially reduce the repressive and distortive effects of social media mechanisms on doubt and the dialectic phase in our model. Such digital interventions could consequently reduce users’ victimization to misinformation on social media. For example, adding an “endorsing accuracy” prompt (“I think this news is accurate”) to the sharing button on a social media page decreases false news sharing, increases true news sharing, and keeps overall engagement constant (Capraro & Celadin, in press). Likewise, researchers recently developed a trust-based news recommendation model that nudges consumers toward higher quality information without demanding a substantial challenge of one’s beliefs (Horne et al., 2019). With our theory as a foundation, future research could build on these and similar studies to design social media-specific interventions that nudge users toward factual evidence and supported beliefs and away from illusory evidence and suspect beliefs on social media.

We recognize that there are certain boundaries in which our explanations are expected to hold. These boundaries include social media platforms, the presence of misinformation, and settings where a person encounters a meaningful claim that triggers a certain threshold of doubt (among others). We also acknowledge that our process theory is one of many potential explanations for how misinformation on social media transforms a person’s view of reality in a way that leads to detrimental human action. <sup>15</sup> For example, one alternative pathway could include a truth bias—an innate tendency to believe a claim to be true (as opposed to false) (Levine, 2014). Truth bias can reduce a person’s motivation for assessing a claim’s correspondence with factual evidence. Furthermore, social media signals such as “liking” can trigger a bandwagon effect (Metzger et al., 2010) and subsequently fortify one’s truth bias toward certain claims. Taken together, this suggests that it is plausible that social media evokes truth bias within users in a way that leads them to immediately believe a false claim, bypass the dialectic phase, and form a suspect belief. Future research could enrich our theory by shedding light on the role of truth bias.

We did not discuss the role of individual differences and information processing characteristics in our theory; however, they might influence the extent to which social media-driven misinformation transforms a user’s view of reality. Potential factors include personality traits (McElroy et al., 2007), need for cognition (Watson & Hubbard, 1996), thinking styles (e.g., analytic thinking) (Pennycook & Rand, 2020), emotions (Horner et al., 2021), and information processing routes (e.g., systematic vs. heuristic information processing) (Qahri-Saremi &

Montazemi, 2019). For instance, users who are more open to experience, have a lower need for cognition, are not analytic thinkers, or follow heuristic information processing may be more easily influenced by misinformation on social media. Another limitation is the type of claim. We focused on meaningful, potentially doubted claims with truth values; however, many instances of false information on social media involve controversial claims (Cinelli et al., 2020) and claims that may be partially true but are presented in a way that is intended to deceive (i.e., “disinformation”) (Chadwick & Stanyer, 2022). Scholars could study how and when different types of people encounter controversial and deceitful claims that, in turn, trigger a dialectic phase. We also need to know more about the roles of individual and cognitive differences during the dialectic and reinforcement phases in our process model.

Approximately midway through Shakespeare’s tragedy Othello, Iago says: “Men should be what they seem” (3.3.126). This quote is important because it underlies Iago’s attempt to convince Othello that Cassio is having an affair with Desdemona. Because Cassio is allegedly involved with Desdemona behind Othello’s back, Iago maintains that the supposedly trusty Cassio is not what he seems. However, it is Iago who is not the person he seems to be, as he is actually a deceitful manipulator acting as a trusted colleague. Ironically, Cassio is truly what he seems to be—a trustworthy friend and soldier. Throughout the play, Iago “replaces truth with falsehood, being with seeming, reality with appearance Appearance and reality become so indistinguishable that their fluidity dissolves all difference between the real and the unreal” (Schreiner, 2011, p. 369). Social media-driven misinformation can operate in a similar way. Regardless of whether the information source intends to deceive others, misinformation can make falsities look like truthhoods and truth-hoods look like falsities, sometimes to the point where it renders a person’s experience unreliable and transforms their view of reality in harmful ways. We hope our process theory provides a versatile lens that helps us better understand and subsequently mitigate the underlying mechanisms and effects of social mediadriven misinformation.
