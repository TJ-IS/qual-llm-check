# Why college undergraduates choose IT: a multi-theoretical perspective

Nathan Heinze<sup>1</sup> and Qing Hu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Technology and Management, Saint Petersburg College, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>College of Business, Department of Logistics, Operations, and Management Information Systems, Iowa State University, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Nathan Heinze, College of Technology and Management, Saint Petersburg College, 13805 58th Street N, Clearwater, FL 33760, U.S.A. Tel: 1-727-341-3204; Fax: 1-727-791-2441

## Abstract

In this paper, we study factors that may influence college undergraduates’ decisions to pursue a major in information technology (IT). We develop and test a theoretical model based on social cognitive career theory and the theory of planned behavior. Data were collected through a multi-section survey given to college undergraduates at four large universities in the southeastern United States. We found that college undergraduates with positive attitudes towards IT careers and high perceived behavioral control (PBC) regarding IT majors had a greater intention of pursuing IT majors. The study also found positive links between self-evaluating outcome expectations (SEOE) and attitudes towards an IT career. In addition, males were more likely to choose IT than females, as they scored higher on computer self-efficacy, SEOE, attitudes towards IT careers, and PBC. Future research may wish to explore this disparity and may also explore factors influencing outcome expectations and attitudes. Practical recommendations include educational and media outreach efforts aimed at emphasizing the vitality of the IT job market and the interpersonal aspects of IT. ## Introduction

During the past 5 years, a broad and sharp decline in computer and information technology (IT)-related enrollments has occurred at colleges across the United States and parts of Europe (Panko, 2008), while the IT industry itself has experienced strong growth that is expected to last until, at least, 2016 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2008). Despite this trend in the job market, there is no sign that enrollment in IT-related majors (business degrees with an emphasis in database design, networking, systems analysis and design, or application programming) is increasing. The Association for Information Systems has called the problem a crisis, and there is ample evidence to support this assertion (Zhang, 2007; Panko, 2008).

This phenomenon highlights the need for understanding what influences college undergraduates’ choice of IT as their major and for expanding upon recent studies that have begun to make headway on this critical issue. It is not clear in the literature as to why the decline of enrollment in IT majors occurred and what may have contributed to or mitigated the continued decline. For example, Panko (2008) examined the possible impact of offshoring on IT job prospects in the U.S.A. and concluded that, apart from programming and help services, offshoring is not undermining the IT job market enough to drive students away from the major. Zhang (2007) used a theoretical model based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) to identify job availability, interest in the IT major, difficulty of the curriculum, and the opinions of others as factors influencing intention to pursue IT. However, Panko (2008) calls for exploration of other possible factors, with a stronger focus on gender and a lesser focus on foreign students.

The current study has two primary objectives. The first is to understand why college undergraduates choose IT as their major. The second is to extend the literature on student career choice by developing a model that integrates two prominent behavioral theories: social cognitive career theory (SCCT) and the theory of planned behavior (TPB). Based on the literature, we developed the specific hypotheses that explore how different factors combine to predict student intentions to choose IT majors. A survey instrument was used to collect data from undergraduate students in four large public universities in the United States. Using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) techniques, we tested the proposed model and the research hypotheses. An analysis of the results is presented, followed by a discussion of the findings, along with contributions, limitations, and recommendations.

## Theory integration

Two of the most widely used theories addressing behavioral choices are social cognitive theory (SCT) (Bandura, 1986) and the TPB (Ajzen, 1991). The impetus for using SCT and the TPB lies in their utility in predicting and explaining behavior in a wide range of settings. Lent et al. (1994) used SCT as the basis of SCCT which specifically addresses career choice issues. SCCT has been validated in many different domains pertaining to career choice, such as teaching (Mau & Mau, 2006), math and science (Fouad et al., 2002), engineering (Lent et al., 2003), academic research (Bieschke et al., 2004) and art, social science and English (Fouad et al., 2002). TPB has been used to predict career intentions as well (Arnold et al., 2006) in addition to many other behaviors (Armitage & Conner, 2001). Also, the TRA (the precursor to TPB) has been used to predict student intentions to major in IT (Zhang, 2007), and the findings show promise in using TRA/TPB to explain student choices of majors. The current study expands upon this initial work by using both TPB and SCCT with the belief that when combined, the two may have additional explanatory power in predicting career intentions, thus offering more relevant insights into this complex socio-behavioral phenomenon. In the following sections, we briefly review the two theories and then develop our research model.

## SCCT

SCCT is based on the SCT model of triadic reciprocity in which behavior, personal factors, and environmental factors operate as co-determinants (Bandura, 1986). The type and strength of the interactions differ based on the individual, the behavior, and the environment. In the case of SCCT, the dependent variable is represented by an individual’s choice of major or career (behavior), and the independent variables are personal and environmental in nature. The most influential of these independent variables is self-efficacy, which is defined as personal judgment regarding one’s capability to attain a particular level and type of performance (Bandura, 1989). Selfefficacy is a stronger predictor of behavior than actual ability (Bandura, 1977); for example, Bandura et al. (2001) found that academic self-efficacy, not actual academic achievement, was the key determinant of occupational self-efficacy and choice of work life. Research confirms that self-efficacy plays an influential role in occupational development and pursuits (Lent et al., 1994; Bandura, 1997; Betz & Voyten, 1997), and predicts both (1) occupational choices; and (2) level of mastery of educational requirements for those pursuits (Luzzo et al., 1999; Bandura et al., 2001). Graham & Weiner (1996) assert that self-efficacy has proven to be a more consistent predictor of behavioral outcomes than any other motivational construct, particularly in education.

In addition to self-efficacy, SCCT includes interests and outcome expectations as predictors of behavior (Lent et al., 2001, 2003). Of the two, outcome expectations have been found to be especially predictive of behavioral intent (Lent et al., 1993; Diegelman & Subich, 2001), successfully predicting career behavior (Hackett & Byars, 1996), vocational interests (Fouad & Smith, 1996), and academic achievement (Hackett et al., 1992). Akbulut & Looney (2007) found SCCT to be useful in predicting student intentions to choose a computing major.

## TPB

TPB proposes specific personal and environmental constructs that influence human intention and behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Its main premise is that behavior is influenced by behavioral intentions, which in turn are influenced by (1) attitudes towards the behavior; (2) the subjective norm (SN) regarding the behavior; and (3) perceived behavioral control (PBC). The attitude towards the behavior is an individual predisposition towards the behavior as a function of its expected personal consequences. SN refers to the social pressures an individual perceives regarding whether the behavior should or should not be performed. PBC is an individual’s perception of the difficulty of the behavior.

An expanded version of the TPB model maintains that (1) attitudes are influenced by behavioral beliefs (the perceived likelihood that the behavior will produce a given outcome); and (2) PBC is influenced by control beliefs (beliefs about the presence of factors that may facilitate or impede the behavior) (Ajzen, 1991). Behavioral beliefs are similar to outcome expectations from SCCT, and thus were not included in the model due to overlap. Control beliefs concern an individual’s judgment of factors that may help or hinder the pursuit of an IT major, and thus we chose to measure computer self-efficacy (CSE) as the control belief. Although there are other possible factors that could be measured as control beliefs, self-efficacy has been clearly established as being closely linked with PBC (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006).

TPB has helped explain behavior in regards to technology adoption (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005), music piracy (D’astous et al., 2005), Internet purchases (George, 2004), banking (Tan & Teo, 2000), and IT usage (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Chau & Hu, 2001; Morris et al., 2005; Workman, 2005). Research has also provided support for using TPB to examine career choice issues (Giles & Rea, 1999; Arnold et al., 2006; Shevlin & Millar, 2006).

## Combining SCCT and TPB

Interactions discovered in the extant literature between major constructs of SCCT and TPB provided the motivation for exploring the integration in this study. For example, self-efficacy appears to be significantly related to PBC (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006), bolstering research that has found strong support for the addition of self-efficacy to TPB’s framework (Giles & Larmour, 2000). Another motivation for combining the theories comes from the importance of outcome expectations in predicting career behavior (Hackett & Byars, 1996), vocational interests (Fouad & Smith, 1996), and academic achievement (Hackett et al., 1992). Therefore, to develop a more comprehensive model that predicts undergraduate students’ intention to pursue IT majors (INTENTs), we chose to use (1) the core TPB constructs of attitudes, SN, and PBC as direct predictors of intention; and (2) the SCCT constructs of self-efficacy and outcome expectations as antecedents to the TPB constructs. SCCT includes interest in the behavior as an antecedent to behavioral intention, but interest overlaps with the attitude variable in TPB, and therefore was not included. Finally, perceived IT job availability is included as a direct predictor of intention, as research indicates that one of the most consistent predictors of student intentions to major in IT is career prospects (Tillberg & Cohoon, 2005). ## Construct conceptualization and hypotheses development

## Construct conceptualization

CSE is self-efficacy applied to computer use. Compeau & Higgins (1995) define CSE as the judgment of one’s capability to use a computer. CSE appears to have an impact on IT use (Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990) and may also impact attitudes towards IT careers, as people with higher levels of CSE are more likely to form positive perceptions of IT (Venkatesh & Davis, 1996) and are more likely to use IT (Compeau et al., 1999).

Outcome expectations involve an individual’s perception of the likely results of his/her action(s) (Bandura, 1986) and are largely dependent on a person’s judgments of what he or she can accomplish (self-efficacy). Bandura (1986) differentiated among three forms of outcome expectations: physical, social, and self-evaluating. In regards to IT, physical outcome expectations concern job availability, pay, security, and physical requirements. Social expectations involve the perceived social ramifications of pursuing an IT degree, such as how family and friends would react, what type of people work in the IT field, and the level of social interaction associated with an IT career. Self-evaluating outcome expectations (SEOE) reflect how fulfilling a particular behavior is and reflect how satisfied a student thinks he or she would be in an IT career. In our study, physical expectations are partially addressed by perceived IT job availability and PBC, and social outcome expectations are similar to SN. Therefore, the focus is on SEOE regarding an IT career.

In TPB, attitudes are one of three primary independent variables that influence the behavioral intention of an individual. An attitude is an individual’s predisposition towards a behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Attitudes predict behavioral intentions and represent evaluations of a psychological object (pursuing the IT major) captured in attributes such as good–bad, harmful–beneficial, pleasant–unpleasant, and likable–dislikable (Ajzen, 2001). Attitudes are predictive of behavioral intention in a wide array of settings (Armitage & Conner, 2001). In the current model, the attitudes to be measured are attitudes towards pursuing an IT major.

SN refers to an individual’s perceptions of social pressure to perform or not perform a behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Although many studies within the IT field have indicated SN has little or no impact upon behavioral intention (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Tan & Teo, 2000; George, 2004; Dinev & Hu, 2007), they are not necessarily indicative of SN’s importance in career choice issues.

PBC is the final primary independent variable in TPB and is defined as the difficulty an individual associates with the performance of a behavior (Ajzen, 2001). Although perceived difficulty is sometimes equated with self-efficacy (Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006), current studies measure the two separately (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Dinev & Hu, 2007). More importantly, the CSE and PBC measures used in this study deal with different topics;

CSE focuses on computer use, whereas PBC focuses on the difficulty of IT majors.

Perceived IT job availability is included in the model as a predictor of intention to pursue IT. It is used in an attempt to measure student perceptions of the demand for IT jobs, because job markets appear to impact college enrollments (Paulsen & Pogue, 1988; Betts & McFarland, 1995; Dellas & Sakellaris, 2003), and career prospects are one of the most consistent predictors of student intentions to major in IT (Tillberg & Cohoon, 2005).

## Research hypotheses

Research indicates that self-efficacy and outcome expectations are positively correlated (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Fouad & Smith, 1996; Schaub & Tokar, 2005). Fouad et al. (2002) applied the SCCT model to four domains (math/science, social studies, English, and art) and found that self-efficacy in each area was a powerful predictor of career outcome expectations in each associated domain. Similarly, we argue that the following hypothesis can be made:

## H1 The higher an individual’s computer self-efficacy, the higher his or her self-evaluating outcome expectations regarding an IT career.

Previous studies have combined self-efficacy with TPB to better predict behavior. Pavlou & Fygenson (2006) studied online information gathering and purchase behavior by examining self-efficacy as a possible antecedent to PBC and attitudes. They found that self-efficacy and PBC were significantly related, and dropping PBC greatly reduced the variance explained in behavioral intention and behavior. Taylor & Todd (1995) modeled self-efficacy as antecedent to PBC and also found a significant relationship between the two. It thus appears likely that an individual with high CSE would anticipate a major in IT to be relatively easy – higher perceived behavioral control. Therefore:

## H2 The higher an individual’s level of CSE, the higher his or her level of perceived behavioral control regarding an IT major.

The relationship between beliefs and attitudes has also been well established (Ajzen, 2001). The most popular conceptualization of attitudes is the expectancy-value viewpoint that proposes attitudes are largely caused by beliefs (such as outcome expectations) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Studies have supported this conceptualization (Ajzen, 1991), indicating that SEOE regarding an IT career may affect attitudes towards pursuing the IT major. Thus:

## H3 The higher an individual’s self-evaluating outcome expectations regarding an IT career, the more positive his or her attitude towards pursuing an IT major.

The link between attitudes and behavioral intent is one of the three key relationships in TPB (Ajzen, 1991).

Attitudes are strongly predictive of behavioral intention in a wide variety of settings and situations (Sideridis et al., 1998; Armitage & Conner, 2001; Millar & Shevlin, 2003). They appear to be related to behavioral intention because individuals form intentions to perform behaviors towards which they have favorable attitudes. Negative attitudes toward a certain behavior have the opposite effect, making it more likely an individual will avoid performing the behavior. Therefore:

## H4 The more positive the attitude towards an IT major, the greater the intention to pursue an IT major.

The link between SN and behavioral intent is another key relationship in TPB (Ajzen, 1991). Although empirical findings have been mixed regarding SN (Armitage & Conner, 2001), it as an integral construct in the TPB model and has support for its use in the current context (Zhang, 2007). Therefore:

## H5 The higher the level of subjective norm regarding an IT major, the greater the intention to pursue an IT major.

Finally, TPB establishes the relationship between PBC and behavioral intent (Ajzen, 1991). There is strong evidence to support the validity of this relationship across many domains (Armitage & Conner, 2001). Among college-aged individuals, Morris et al. (2005) found support for the relation between PBC and behavioral intention; and Taylor & Todd (1995) also found a positive connection between PBC and behavioral intention in a study examining college student intentions to use a computer center. Studies focusing on technology use found similar results (Compeau & Higgins, 1991; Hartwick & Barki, 1994). Interestingly, PBC has an even stronger predictive ability in studies where only behavioral intention (and not the behavior itself) is measured (Armitage & Conner, 2001). The current study measures behavioral intention, not actual behavior, thus indicating that PBC should be included as a predictor variable. Therefore:

## H6 The higher the level of perceived behavioral control over an IT major, the greater the intention to pursue an IT major.

Although certain environmental influences have been invoked when dealing with career choice issues, such as family influence (Berrios-Allison, 2005), social relationships (Higgins, 2001), and encouragement by others (Compeau & Higgins, 1995), there has been little examination of broader factors like economic conditions. This may be an oversight, considering the fact that labor market demands appear to impact community college and university enrollments; there is an inverse relationship between job market strength and enrollments (Paulsen & Pogue, 1988; Betts & McFarland, 1995; Dellas & Sakellaris, 2003). Lent et al. (2001) found that ‘job market conditions’ were a relevant consideration for students interested in majoring in math and science. Based on this research and the dramatic drop in IT enrollments associated with the dot com downturn, it seems plausible that market conditions surrounding an individual occupational area may impact enrollment in its associated major. Therefore

H7 The higher the level of perceived IT job availability, the greater the intention to pursue an IT major.

## Methodology and results

We adopted the survey research methodology to collect the data in order to test the proposed research model and hypotheses. The bulk of studies employing SCCT and TPB use similar quantitative methods. The chief advantage of this method is the ability to test the theoretical relationships among underlying factors with large sample sizes for better generalizability. We employed the SEM technique with LISREL 8.80 to validate the measurement model and test the hypotheses, and SPSS 14.0 to generate descriptive statistics.

## The survey instrument

The initial questionnaire consisted of 67 items and was reduced to 41 following the pilot test and factor analysis. Twenty-two final items were used to measure the seven main constructs (see Appendix). Apart from demographic items, the survey consisted entirely of 7-point Likert-type scales, as SEM problems with ordinal data are minimized if there are five or more response categories (Johnson & Creech, 1983). All response categories used extremely unlikely (1) and extremely likely (7) as end points and neutral (4) as the center point; or strongly disagree (1) and strongly agree (7) as endpoints and neutral (4) as the center point.

Previously established scales were used wherever possible, but new items were developed to measure SEOE regarding an IT career, perceived IT job availability, and intention to pursue the IT major (INTENT). Five items were created for SEOE based on descriptions of the variable in Bandura (1986). After the pilot test, this number was reduced to three. Perceived IT job availability and INTENT also began with five items and were reduced to three in order to ensure identification and reduce standard errors for parameter estimates (Rigdon, 1998). The reduced item scales showed good reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha scores above 0.70 (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994) (see Table 1).

## Sample and data collection

Data were collected from undergraduate classes at four large state universities in the Southeastern United States. Multiple locations were selected to help improve the generalizability of results. The classes surveyed were lower division business and general education classes, where a large proportion of the students had not yet declared their majors. The collected surveys included three categories of responses: undeclared, declared IT, and declared non-IT. Approximately 1200 surveys were administered, but only those completed by undeclared students were used for this study. The questionnaires were administered to students during the 2007 spring and summer semesters. Instructors were asked to allot 15 min of class time for the students to complete the surveys. In nearly all cases, one of the authors was present to administer the survey and answer any student questions. Voluntary participation was requested for students of age 18 years or older. Respondents were told not to identify themselves on the questionnaire, and were also informed that only group data were to be analyzed. In order to eliminate self-selection bias, there were no incentives offered for participation. However, the instructors of the classes the authors visited asked their students to comply, and during survey distribution, the authors noticed that participation appeared to be high. A total of 429 surveys were collected from the undeclared students, and 47 were discarded due to incomplete and/ or unintelligible responses. This resulted in 382 responses, well above the 200 recommended for SEM (Hoyle, 1995).

## Descriptive statistics

Demographic data describing the respondents can be found in Table 2. The sample size was not large enough to make comparisons across age and race, but clear distinctions were found in gender response patterns (see Table 3). The differences between the two genders were tested using ANOVA. CSE scores differed significantly, with males averaging 4.49 and females averaging 4.01 (Po0.001). A significant gap also appeared in SEOE, attitude towards IT major (ATT), PBC, and INTENT. The difference in PBC was especially striking, as male students viewed the IT major to be much less difficult than did female students, averaging 4.43 compared to 3.54 for females (Po0.001).

## Structural equation model test results

SEM testing was conducted in LISREL 8.80, where a twostep approach was used: (1) to validate the measurement model; and (2) to fit the structural model (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Testing of the measurement model began with a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The initial SPSS exploratory factor analysis narrowed the item list and established the number of latent variables, but further testing of the measurement model was necessary to establish various validity indicators. CFA was conducted with (1) each set of items tested in its relationship to its individual construct; and (2) the entire set of items run with the entire set of constructs. Several problematic items were eliminated based on the CFA results. The final scale is presented in the Appendix.

Convergent validity was measured by examining the t-value significance of individual item loadings. Item reliability was measured by examining (1) the item scale reliabilities using Cronbach’s alpha; and (2) the coefficient of determination for each item.

Individual items with insignificant t-values were eliminated from the model (Koufteros, 1999). The t-value results for the remaining items indicated they were significantly related to their associated constructs (Table 4). Additionally, all items loaded significantly onto their respective constructs. Completely standardized loadings ranged from 0.75 to 0.98, and t-values ranged from 13.44 to 49.87, indicating excellent convergent validity and item reliability.

Next, fit indices were used to assess the overall fit of the measurement model (Table 5). Good fit is indicated by a ratio of chi-square $( \chi ^ { 2 } ) / { \mathrm { d e g r e e s } }$ of freedom smaller than 3.0 (Kline, 1998), a Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI) greater than or equal to 0.95 (Bentler & Bonnett, 1980), a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) greater than or equal to 0.90 (Bentler, 1980), and a Root Mean Square Error or Approximation (RMSEA) below 0.05 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). The model had a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of 323.83 $( P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ with 188 degrees of freedom, resulting in a ratio of 1.73. The model had an NNFI of 0.98, a CFI of 0.99, and a RMSEA of 0.044. Although a significant $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value $( P { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ in testing a CFA or SEM model normally indicates poor model fit, this situation often occurs mainly due to a large sample size. For that reason, we place more emphasis on the fact that the $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f }$ value is less than 3.0, and that the model meets various other goodness of fit thresholds.

SEM provides additional output regarding unidimensionality in the form of modification indices and residuals. Modification indices were used to remove only one item, as theoretical justification is essential (Silvia & MacCallum, 1988). The item removed was ‘Majoring in IT is a wise idea’, measured on a 1–7 scale. Upon examination, it was clear that the item did not fit theoretically with the other three items used to measure attitudes towards the IT major (see Appendix). No items were removed based on residuals, as the smallest standardized residual was -3.60 and the largest was 4.73. ## Discussion

Although research has previously found connections between self-efficacy and TPB, the relationship had not been explored within this context. The results show that CSE had a positive relationship with outcome expectations and PBC, but the relationships were not statistically significant, with P-values greater than 0.05. However, support was found for the relationship between CSE and PBC among males, indicating the CSE does have some influential power. It should be mentioned that this study differs from previous research concerning the selfefficacy/PBC relationship. Thus far, common practice has been to link the self-efficacy concept directly to the behavior associated with PBC (Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006; Dinev & Hu, 2007). However, the current study’s self-efficacy and PBC measures do not reflect the same behavior; CSE measured a student’s self-efficacy in regards to computer use, whereas PBC measured a student’s perceived level of difficulty concerning the IT major, not the perceived level of difficulty concerning computer use. Perhaps this departure from standard practice contributed to the relationship’s lack of complete support.

The study found a significant and positive relationship between outcome expectations and attitudes. Although past research has separately linked (1) outcome expectations to intentions; and (2) attitudes to intentions, the connection between outcome expectations and attitudes is unexplored. This lack of research appears to be an oversight, as the link between SEOE and attitudes proved to be quite strong, supporting the argument that outcome expectations are a strong antecedent to attitudes. Attitudes were also linked closely to behavioral intent, adding to the body of research supporting this relationship; the more positive a student’s attitude was towards the IT major, the greater his/her intent was to actually major in IT, reaffirming a basic argument of TPB.

What came as a surprise was the failure of SN to predict the intention to pursue an IT major. The data in no way indicate that students feel a lack of social influence for pursuing IT (the mean score for SN was 5.05, measured on a 1–7 scale). What the data point to is that any social influence (or lack thereof) that students may feel is simply not strong enough to influence their INTENTs. It may be that the concepts represented by SN (such as how a student thinks his/her family and friends would react to the decision to pursue IT) are necessary but insufficient conditions to influence INTENTs. This may be because students assume their families and friends would be supportive of whatever major the student chose. A high level of perceived support might make a student feel more satisfied with a major once it has been chosen, but might not contribute to a student’s choice in the first place. Another possible reason for SN’s impotence may be the lack of cultural moderators in the model (Trafimow & Finlay, 1996); SN has a more powerful effect in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures (Abrams et al., 1998), and the U.S.A. is one of the world’s most individualistic nations (Hofstede, 1991).

The study results also raise the issue of gender and IT while simultaneously serving to validate the model’s proposed causal links. Testing statistics based on the male and female data sets clearly suggest a gender-induced difference when it comes to choosing IT majors. Males had higher levels of CSE (4.49 vs 4.01) and higher resulting levels of SEOE (4.37 vs 3.70). Likewise, higher male levels of CSE were reflected in higher levels of PBC regarding the IT major (4.43 vs 3.54), higher male levels for SEOE were reflected in more positive attitudes towards the IT major (3.91 vs 3.19), and higher male levels for PBC and attitudes resulted in greater intent to choose the IT major (2.98 vs 2.15). To confirm the gender-induced differences, the structural model was run separately for males and females using a multiple groups analysis method (Diney et al.. 2006) whereby a $\chi ^ { \bar { 2 } }$ difference test is used to examine degradation of model fit for different groups. Statistically significant differences were found in two model relationships; the coefficient between CSE and PBC was 0.39 for males and 0.54 for females, and the coefficient between ATT and the INTENT was 0.76 for males and 0.42 for females. For males, PBC did not have a significant relationship with intention, whereas for females it did.

Lastly, the study found that the perception of IT job availability does not seem to affect the INTENT. This finding is perplexing; clearly, the dot com downturn and resulting IT job losses would seem to have been partially responsible for the dramatic drop in IT major enrollments; and the swelling in IT enrollments in the late 1990s and early 2000s seemed to correlate with the highly publicized and perceived dot com boom. The results may be explained by looking at the response statistics. On a 1–7 scale, the mean score for the perception of IT job availability was 4.76, and there was no difference between male and female responses (4.80 vs

4.72). Most notable of all is the small standard deviation for the scale (1.03). This lack of variance is problematic from a measurement standpoint, and also indicates that students favored middle-range answers because they may have been unaware of the state of the IT job market. In fact, the authors noticed the most common questions from students revolved around the perceived IT job availability items, and was ‘I have no idea how many jobs there are in IT!’ Therefore, the study results do not necessarily suggest perceived IT job availability is unimportant; rather, students may simply be uninformed, as there has not been recent positive media attention to the IT job market to offset the negative coverage of the IT job market following the dot com downturn (Granger et al., 2007).

## Theoretical contribution

This study demonstrates the feasibility and utility of combining SCCT and TPB into a combined framework for understanding career choice problems. The integrated model explained 40% of the variance in intention to pursue an IT major and also calls into question some relationships suggested in previous research. For example, the combination of CSE with TPB apparently contradicts previous research connecting the two (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006; Dinev & Hu, 2007), but the current study uses self-efficacy and PBC measures that reflect different (but related) behaviors, and therefore should not be interpreted as incompatible with previous findings. Rather, it may point to the importance of testing self-efficacy and PBC scales that are measuring the exact same behavior.

CSE was also found to have no significant correlation to outcome expectations, which differs from previous research showing a link between the two (Fouad & Smith, 1996; Betz & Voyten, 1997; Diegelman & Subich, 2001). A possible explanation for this disparity may be the type of outcome expectations measured in the study; SEOE measure how fulfilling an individual believes a particular behavior is and, in this context, reflect how emotionally satisfied a student thinks he or she would be in an IT career (see items in Appendix). Therefore, just because a student may have high levels of CSE does not necessarily indicate he or she thinks a career in IT would be satisfying. This finding thus may not be as confusing as it appears on first glance.

What is innovative is the positive link between SEOE and attitudes. This finding indicates that outcome expectations may play an important part in predicting attitudes. Attitudes, in turn, had a strong correlation with INTENT. These links extend the TPB model to include outcome expectations as a significant antecedent.

## Practical implications

Despite the counter-intuitive findings of this study regarding CSE’s effect on the TPB model, CSE appears to be of some importance in affecting the INTENTs, as there was a relationship between CSE and PBC among males.

Previous research indicates that higher CSE increases (1) acceptance of technological change and computer technology (Ellen et al., 1991); (2) desire to enroll in computer courses (Zhang & Espinoza, 1998); and (3) computer use (Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990; Compeau et al., 1999). Therefore, a fundamental recommendation is to improve CSE among students at all levels, possibly by increasing computer experience (Potosky, 2002; Hasan, 2003). Research suggests that certain types of computer experience, particularly programming and graphics, are more correlated with CSE than others (Kagan & Pierton, 1987; Koohang, 1989; Hasan, 2003). Thus, it may be a good idea to emphasize basic programming exercises and skills in educational settings.

The study also found that attitudes towards IT majors impact the INTENTs. This indicates that it is important for the IT industry and departments to address negative stereotypes that may affect student attitudes. The common view of the typical IT employee as a socially withdrawn male (Wilson, 2003; McConnell, 2004) does little to encourage female participation in the IT profession. The task of changing this perception is made difficult because the stereotype is born in some truth, with estimates of the female share of IT jobs hovering between 20 and 30% (Wilson, 2003) and personality tests showing that computer workers tend towards introversion (Couger & Zawacki, 1978; Lyons, 1980). Therefore, it is advisable that the business and social aspects of IT should be emphasized in order to attract a broader range of people (Robertson et al., 2001). Emphasizing the cooperative nature of IT is also a recommended strategy, as is stressing teamwork and group projects (Schinzel, 1997; Wilson, 2003).

Although changing the attitudes of students towards IT is not something that will happen overnight, it is important for IT educators and practitioners to begin taking practical steps to try to increase enrollments. As one example, some IS departments at the universities the authors are familiar with have begun new programs where representatives from multiple companies visit campus each semester and hold information sessions and lectures about the life in IT for prospective students. In this way, students can receive a broader perspective as to who IT professionals are, as well as to what skill sets and talents are needed in real business settings. At one of the universities, students enjoy a catered dinner after the sessions, and a separate meeting is held at a later date in which students receive pizza and meet with IT faculty. It is in situations like this where faculty and industry leaders can help to change student attitudes by providing a broader picture of IT careers and the team skills and business acumen that are required by today’s companies. Another example is the introduction of gaming classes, where students are taught the basics of graphics and programming in game development. A community college near one of the author’s universities introduced a gaming class, and enrollments in IT-related classes have increased significantly.

The current study found that perceived IT job availability did not appear to affect the INTENTs. However, this finding seems instead to point to a lack of student awareness of the need for IT employees in industry. This finding helps to shed light on what may be one of the core issues surrounding the current enrollment problem – student ignorance of the IT job market. Clearly, the dramatic rise in IT enrollments of the late 1990s was, at least in part, attributable to the demand for IT employees and the associated media hype of the IT job market. Research indicates that one of the most consistent predictors of student intentions to major in IT is career prospects (Tillberg & Cohoon, 2005). Therefore, a simple yet powerful recommendation is for IT departments in both industry and education to aggressively promote the field using the same message that has already been proven successful: ‘There are jobs in IT, these jobs pay very well, and the demand for IT graduates is strong and will continue to grow’.

Thus, in addition to campus activity, a key to increasing awareness is media involvement. Just as the positive media coverage surrounding the dot com boom apparently persuaded many students to pursue an IT major, the negative media coverage regarding offshoring and outsourcing (Granger et al., 2007) appeared to have the opposite effect, as prospective IT majors fear their future jobs might be shipped offshore (Malykhina, 2004; Diamond Consultants, 2005). It is imperative then, that IT departments in both industry and education work with media outlets at the national, state, and local levels to (1) debunk the notion that offshoring is threatening the domestic IT industry; and (2) emphasize the quality and number of IT jobs available now and in the near future.

## Future research

There are a number of issues that future research could address to build on the findings of this study. The first of these is the issue of gender’s relationship with CSE and related model variables. More work is needed to examine possible sources of the CSE disparity between males and females. Research thus far indicates a strong correlation between CSE and computer use (Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990; Compeau et al., 1999), and males have higher levels of computer use involving the applications most linked to CSE (Kagan & Pierton, 1987; Hasan, 2003). Therefore, future research could focus on (1) why males gravitate more towards certain types of computer use; and (2) what types of moderating variables may be involved for both genders.

SEOE and attitudes were found to be related, and research into determinants of one may shed light on determinants of the other. For example, the stereotype of the average computer worker as an introverted male (McConnell, 2004) would likely affect outcome expectations regarding IT jobs and attitudes towards IT jobs, but would not necessarily affect PBC regarding the IT major or IT jobs. Therefore. future studies may explore factors influencing outcome expectations and attitudes. Research could also further explore social influence and SN, as social influence and gender stereotyping appear to contribute at least somewhat to the digital divide between sexes (Tillberg & Cohoon, 2005; Cooper, 2006).

Future research may also wish to focus on other areas of interest that could perhaps have an influence on the likelihood to pursue IT careers (Malgwi et al., 2005), such as interest in teamwork or interest in personal contact and collaboration. High interest in these items may negatively influence attitudes towards IT, as suggested by the literature (Robertson et al., 2001; Wilson, 2003); and although perception of potential job opportunities (perception of IT job availability) and aptitude in the subject (CSE and PBC) were measured, level of pay and potential for career advancement were not, so future studies may want to address those issues. Additionally, future research might examine the impact that other forms of self-efficacy (besides CSE) may have on the choice of an IT major.

Another area for further study involves the measurement of SN. There is the possibility that the lack of explanatory power from SN may be due to inadequate content validity. ## Conclusions

This study explored the important question of why students intend or do not intend to choose IT as their major. By integrating two prominent theories on social behavior, that is, SCCT and TPB, we hope to provide a better understanding of and more insight into the factors and their interactions that influence college students’ choice of IT majors. We found that attitudes and PBC were especially useful in predicting the INTENTs, and outcome expectations regarding IT careers are a potent antecedent of attitudes. Significant differences were found to exist between male and female students in terms of all the major predictors. Based on these findings, recommendations were provided for practical steps that can be taken by IT faculty and practitioners to address the current need for more college graduates with IT majors. We hope that this study provides a foundation and motivation for future research on college students’ career choice with more refined models and measurement instruments.
