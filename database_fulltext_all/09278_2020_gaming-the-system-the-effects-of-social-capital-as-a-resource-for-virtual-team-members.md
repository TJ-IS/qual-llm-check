## Abstract

Increasingly individuals interact with one another through information and communication technologies in virtual teams. Leveraging the resource-based view of the firm and social capital theory, we examine how a team member’s social capital (a resource) affects the team member’s perception of the quality of knowledge shared within the team (a capability) and the benefits to the individual team member (an outcome). Using the context of massively multiplayer online role-playing games, we find that within virtual teams, relational and cognitive social capital contribute to knowledge quality, which in turn positively benefits the outcomes of individual team members.

## Keywords

Virtual teams; Resource-based view of the firm; Social capital theory; MMORPG

## Gaming the System: The Effects of Social Capital as a Resource for Virtual Team Members

## 1. Introduction

Many organizations use information and communication technologies (ICTs) to enable employees to work together in both geographically dispersed and collocated settings (Zigurs, 2003). Virtual teams rely on ICTs, often exclusively, for effective and efficient communication and task completion. The need for virtual teams to communicate and work together using ICTs affects the social aspects of work as individuals must communicate with one another only through technology (Persson, Mathiassen, & Aaen, 2012). The use of ICTs for communication impacts how individuals within the team interact, build trust, and collaborate (Zigurs, 2003).

As organizations increase their use of virtual teams, virtual team members must learn different ways to interact because of the constraints that occur when engaging with others through ICTs (Lin, Standing, & Liu, 2008). Prior research demonstrates that virtual teams have different dynamics than traditional, collocated teams. Trust, for example, develops differently within virtual teams as compared to traditional teams (Mitchell & Zigurs, 2009). Because virtual teams have elements that are different from traditional teams, there is value in studying phenomena in the context of virtual teams to identify potential boundary conditions of existing theories and research.

Social capital, or the goodwill developed and used among individuals or groups, has been argued to be a resource that can be used to develop intellectual capital, or knowledge, within organizations (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Social capital has been studied in many contexts, such as in departments and teams within organizations, across organizations, and in virtual communities (e.g., Bolino, Turnley, & Bloodgood, 2002; Inkpen & Tsang, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

Leveraging concepts from the resource-based view of the firm, we view social capital as a resource developed by team members who can influence capabilities and outcomes. Therefore, our research objective is to examine the effects of social capital as a resource within the virtual team for team members. We explore how a virtual team member’s social capital, as a resource, affects the capability of knowledge quality shared among the team members and the resulting outcomes to the team member in terms of individual benefits.

We examine our conceptual model in the context of virtual teams within a Massively Multiplayer Online Role-Playing Game (MMORPG), which is one genre of online games. Prior research has argued that online games are useful proxies to study team behavior (Goh & Wasko, 2012; Petter, 2017; Petter & Carter, 2017). Others have suggested that online games, and specifically the interactions that individuals have with other players in online games, can be useful in developing skills needed within the workplace (Sourmelis, Ioannou, & Zaphiris, 2017; Yee, 2014). Individuals can use experience with online games to demonstrate their ability to collaborate with others in a distributed context (Petter, Barber, Barber, & Berkley, 2018). In many online games (and specifically MMORPGs), players complete tasks, engage in markets, and work collaboratively with other players to effectively accomplish goals (Guo & Barnes, 2011), similar to activities that are performed within virtual teams in organizations.

This research offers several contributions. First, using the lens of the resource-based view of the firm, we consider how the different elements of social capital, used as a resource, affect capabilities and outcomes. Second, most studies using the theoretical lens of the resource-based view of the firm measure outcomes that benefit a collective; yet, in this study, we identify if the perceived resources and capabilities developed within a collective can provide positive benefits to the individuals who comprise the collective. Next, consistent with conceptualizations of the resource-based view of the firm that separate the concepts of resources and capabilities, we confirm the importance of identifying capabilities, such as knowledge quality, as a mediator in the relationship between resources and outcomes. Finally, this study demonstrates how MMORPGs can be a useful context to study organizational concepts, such as virtual teams.

In the next section, we discuss the resource-based view of the firm and social capital theory in relation to virtual teams as we develop and explain our conceptual and research models. Then, we describe the methods used for our research, the analysis process to examine our data, and the results of our findings. The discussion explains how our findings relate to existing research as well as identifies opportunities for future research. We conclude by elaborating upon the contributions of this research.

## 2. Theory and Research Model

## 2.1 Conceptual Model

According to the resource-based view of the firm, the organization comprised of resources used to create capabilities that help the organization achieve a sustainable, competitive advantage that ultimately impacts the firm’s outcomes (Wernerfelt, 1984). The resource-based view of the firm has been extended to suggest a knowledge-based view of the firm (Grant, 1996), applied in the information systems literature (Wade & Hulland, 2004), and studied in other contexts. Others criticize the resource-based view of the firm for being tautological or difficult to examine given that many of the constructs in the model are intangible and difficult to measure (Barney, 2001). Yet, the resource-based view of the firm offers an interesting conceptual framework to study factors that influence outcomes of collective entities such as organizations or groups. While the resource-based view of the firm considers the benefits or outcomes obtained by the collective, the individuals that make up the collective group may also benefit from the resources and capabilities used by the firm or group. Using this theoretical lens, we consider factors that influence the outcomes of individuals within virtual teams.

For individuals engaging in virtual teams, members are in geographically disparate locations and communicate and collaborate with one another without being in a face-to-face environment. Individuals within the virtual team may join or leave the group, gain new skills toward the tasks at hand, acquire new information to share with others within the team, and bring personal experiences to the team. As individuals interact, the resource of social capital is developed among team members. Social capital is “the goodwill available to individuals or groups” (Adler & Kwon, 2002, p. 23). Each individual within the team can develop their own social capital, which is comprised of “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 243). As a resource, social capital has the potential to be a benefit if individuals within the team use their relationships as resources. Similar to other types of capital, social capital can be long-lived, can substitute for or complement other resources, requires maintenance, is appropriable, and is convertible (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Bourdieu, 1985; Coleman, 1988). Yet, social capital is different from other forms of capital in that social capital is collocated in the relationships among individuals and in their connections within the community (Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). The social capital developed through relationships among virtual team members are resources that hold the potential to be a benefit or detriment to the virtual team, depending on the actions of the individuals and collective in the process of generating capabilities (Grant, 1991).

According to the resource-based view of the firm, organizations create and use resources, such as economic or social capital, to foster capabilities that lead to improved outcomes (Wernerfelt, 1984). Prior literature suggests that social capital is related to the development of capabilities, which can include the development of quality knowledge shared among individuals (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005). A capability can be described as having a “recognizable purpose expressed in terms of the significant outcomes it is supposed to enable, and that is shaped significantly by conscious decision both in its development and deployment” (Dosi, Faillo, & Marengo, 2008, p. 1166). More informally, capabilities are the activities that an organization, team, or individual is “good” at doing. While much of prior research focuses on capabilities at an organizational level, capabilities can also be developed within teams (Haas, 2006). The resource of social capital has been demonstrated as a factor that affects the development of capabilities, such as knowledge sharing within an organization (e.g., Dosi et al., 2008; Gold, Malhotra, & Segars, 2001). However, there are many different capabilities that can be developed by an individual through social capital, such as knowledge integration (Robert, Dennis, & Ahuja, 2008), knowledge contribution (Shen, Yu, & Khalifa, 2010), and knowledge acquisition (Ye & Agarwal, 2003). The acquisition and application of knowledge or the development of an individual’s willingness to collaborate to accomplish goals fits into the above definition of a capability. Viewing knowledge quality as a capability indicates that “knowledge is the potential to influence action” (Alavi & Leidner, 2001, p. 111). Knowledge is personalized, thus the receiver must be able to interpret acquired knowledge for it to be useful (Alavi & Leidner, 2001), which can be made possible through social capital.

In the resource-based view of the firm, there is an expectation that leveraging resources to create capabilities leads to desirable outcomes (Wernerfelt, 1984). Often resources are used to create collective capabilities to benefit a group or organization, with the goal of providing value (i.e., positive outcomes) to the collective group or organization (e.g., Karimi, Somers, &

Bhattacherjee, 2007; Nwankpa & Datta, 2017, Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). The resource-based view of the firm typically considers the outcome of developing a sustainable, competitive advantage (Wernerfelt, 1984), which in turn should provide positive outcomes for the organization. Other collective-level benefits to developing or possessing capabilities include group-level outcomes, such as the quality of decision-making by the team itself (Robert et al., 2008). Yet, groups are complex, nested systems entrenched within a larger context (e.g., an organization) in which a group or collective establishes its own identity and social structure (e.g., a team) that is comprised of components (e.g., individual team members) (McGrath, 1991). Individual team members work together within this complex, nested group structure to achieve a common goal (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993). To achieve a desired group outcome, individuals must share their knowledge or expertise with others within the group. As team members share their knowledge within the group, individuals achieve personal learning, which is indicated by benefits to the individual, such as improved productivity and decision-making (Al-Busaidi, Olfman, Ryan, & Leroy, 2007). As individuals leverage capabilities from the team to accomplish team goals, the nested structure of groups (McGrath, 1991) suggests that individual team members also accrue benefits that improve their own personal outcomes, such as increased decision-making effectiveness, higher levels of confidence, or deeper understanding of one’s tasks. In this case, the social capital within the team creates capabilities that can benefit individual team members (Dosi et al., 2008).

In this study, we examine how an individual’s resources or social capital impacts the team member’s perception of knowledge quality as a capability within the team, and we study the effect of the capability on the personal outcomes for the team member. ## 2.2 Research Model

In this study, we adopt the perspective that social capital is defined using three dimensions: structural, relational, and cognitive social capital (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Prior research has posited that the dimensions of social capital may influence one another or may operate independently (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998), and we assume that each dimension of social capital operates independently. While Figure 1 is the conceptual model for this research, Figure 2 provides a more specific research model with specific constructs examined in this study. We posit of knowledge quality shared within the team. Then, we propose that the capability of knowledge quality is related to positive benefits for the team member.

Structural social capital is “the overall pattern of connections between actors” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 244). As a resource, structural social capital consists of the connections among members of the community, the configuration among connections within the community, and the connectedness of members within the community (Bolino et al., 2002). The most efficiently structured social networks are not necessarily the most ideal for creating shared knowledge (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998); therefore, there are varying ways to conceptualize and understand structural social capital within a community, depending on the view of structural social capital as a resource for an organization or team.

Structural social capital has been measured at multiple levels of analysis (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998) and by examining different types of relationships (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Some research considers structural social capital based on the centrality of individuals within a network as a means to understand how embedded or connected an individual is within a network (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Other studies define the structural social capital of a group based on the density of the network or gaps within a network (Bolino et al., 2002). Still other research suggests that structural social capital is represented by the strength of the relationships within the community because this facilitates the flow of knowledge sharing within a group (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). The strength of relations or ties within the community can be defined and examined using techniques related to social network analysis by considering the betweenness and embeddedness of individuals within a group (e.g.,Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). An alternative approach defines structural social capital based on an individual’s perception of closeness or strength of interactions within virtual communities (Chiu, Hsu, & Wang, 2006). In this research, structural social capital is defined and measured based on an individual’s perception of social interactions within a group (consistent with Chiu et al. (2006)), as a means to understand the team member’s perception regarding the connectedness of members within a community (Bolino et al., 2002; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998).

Prior research examining the relationship between structural social capital and the development of high-quality knowledge within a group has mixed results, but this may be due to the different approaches used to define and measure structural capital. Considering structural social capital as one’s centrality within an electronic social network, Wasko and Faraj (2005) identified that structural social capital impacts the amount of responses shared with the community as well as the quality of those responses (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Yet, a study of digitally enabled teams found that one’s structural social capital did not have a significant relationship with the degree to which individuals shared knowledge within a team when structural social capital was measured as the intensity of exchanges and number of individuals communicating within the team (Robert et al., 2008). In a study of virtual communities, structural social capital, measured as the strength of interactions among team members, positively affected the quantity, but not quality, of the knowledge shared (Chiu et al., 2006). Another study of virtual communities found the opposite when measuring structural social capital as the strength of interactions among team members in that the structural dimension of social capital influenced the quality, but not the quantity, of the knowledge shared within the group (Chang & Chuang, 2011). Prior studies examining the effect of structural social capital on the creation of knowledge, including studies that define and measure structural social capital based on the perceived strength of interactions within the group have had varying results. Therefore, we posit that the strength of the social network structure, as defined by the individual’s perceived strength of the interac within the team, affects the team member’s perception of knowledge quality within a virtual team.

H1: In virtual teams, structural social capital based on an individual’s social interaction ties positively affects the team member’s perception of knowledge quality.

Relational social capital refers to the personal relationships and bonds that an individual develops within a community. These relationships are resources that are cultivated based on trust among members of the community, norms that encourage teamwork or creativity, obligations or expectations within a cooperative group, and identification, or sense of belonging, within a group (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Strong relationships among group members positively influence the sharing of knowledge by individuals within organizations (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005).

Relational social capital consists of multiple dimensions, and studies define and operationalize relational social capital in differing ways. Some studies examine the multiple facets of relational social capital, while others focus on a single aspect of relational social capital. Scholars have used trust to measure the effects of relational social capital on exchanging resources within an organization (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998) or on knowledge sharing within a network (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005). Other studies adopt a broader view of relational social capital and measure two or more dimensions of relational social capital as defined by Nahapiet and

Ghoshal (1998). Relational social capital may be measured by assessing norms, trust, reciprocity, obligation, and identification through the use of single items, an index, or multiple items for each subdimension of relational social capital (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Hsu & Hung, 2013; Robert et al., 2008). In this study, we focus on three subdimensions of relational social capital: trust, the norm of reciprocity, and identification. Given that trust is the most common measure of relational social capital (Hsu & Hung, 2013), we examine the role of trust within our model. We include identification consistent with other studies that consider relational social capital and because identification is part of the definition of relational social capital (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) also include (1) norms and (2) obligations and expectations as part of their definition of relational social capital. In this study, we measure the norm of reciprocity, which has elements of both a norm and expectation. The norm of reciprocity occurs when an individual within a group expects that by sharing personal knowledge to others in need, group members will respond in kind when the knowledge is needed.

Prior research finds mixed results regarding the association between relational social capital and knowledge quality, particularly among studies that examine this relationship in the context of virtual communities. Chang and Chuang (2011) identified that trust, reciprocity, and identification within a community contributes to the quality of knowledge shared within the community. In a study examining social capital theory in social networking sites, Choi and Scott (2013) found that trust is positively related to the quality of the knowledge shared within the site. Yet, other studies find no relationship between reciprocity and knowledge quality (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), even positing that the “norm of reciprocity, and identification [may] have indirect effects on knowledge quality through trust” (Chiu et al., 2006, p. 1883). Prior research suggests that social capital is a phenomenon that is context-specific in that the organizational structure and processes, as well as the nature of the work may influence and be influenced by the social capital of the individuals within the community (Leana & Pil, 2006). For example, reciprocity was demonstrated to affect one’s contribution to knowledge repositories only when the prosharing norms within the community were weak (Kankanhalli et al., 2006). There are different findings in the literature, and there is even less understanding about the relationship between the resource of relational social capital and the capability of knowledge quality in the context of virtual teams. As a result, we hypothesize that:

H2: In virtual teams, relational social capital positively affects the team member’s perception of knowledge quality.

H2a: Trust positively affects the team member’s perception of knowledge quality.

H2b: Identification positively affects the team member’s perception of knowledge quality.

H2c: Reciprocity positively affects the team member’s perception of

knowledge quality.

Cognitive social capital is the shared understanding within the group that enables individuals with diverse backgrounds to more easily communicate their knowledge, identify common goals, and build relationships (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). As a resource, when cognitive capital increases within a group, it “reduces the barriers of understanding between the partners because they have similar mental models and knowledge regarding the context” (Ye & Agarwal, 2003, p. 310).

Cognitive social capital is predominantly measured based on the degree of a team’s shared language or shared vision. Shared language is the degree to which individuals within the group use similar language, jargon, and narratives to communicate their ideas. Without a common lexicon, teams are less likely to be able to communicate complex ideas with one another. Many studies have used shared language as the means to represent cognitive social capital (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Lin et al., 2008). Other studies use the notion of a shared vision, or the collective goals and desires among a network, to represent cognitive social capital. Shared vision is measured using items, such as “Our initiative team shared the same ambitions and vision with other organizational units at work” or “People in our team were enthusiastic about pursuing the collective goals and missions of the whole organization” (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). These items might be averaged together among team members to develop a measure, or researchers may examine the correlations of these scores among team members (e.g., Lechner, Frankenberger, & Floyd, 2010; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). Other studies use these items or other similar items in a larger collection of items to measure shared vision using Likert scales (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Yoon & Wang, 2011). For studies that do not measure cognitive social capital based on perceptions of team members, other proxies have been used to determine the degree of shared knowledge and language. In a study of virtual communities, an individual’s self-assessed level of expertise and their tenure with the organization served as a means to represent if the individual would have useful content to share with others in the network (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). To assess the degree to which a team had a shared mental model of the group’s tasks, Robert et al. (2008) used the repertory grid technique to assess the similarities of task understanding within the group. In this study, we measure cognitive social capital based on the shared vision of the group, which “embodies the collective goals and aspirations of the members of an organization” (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 467) and is consistent with other studies that measure cognitive social capital (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Yoon & Wang, 2011). We specifically excluded shared language as a measure of cognitive social capital because it is unrelated within our context. In the virtual teams examined in this study, individuals already had a common language and jargon, making shared language less relevant in our context.

Theoretically, social capital has been identified as a means to develop intellectual capital, which includes the development of knowledge (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Consistently, the relationship between cognitive social capital, when measured as shared vision and the contribution of high quality knowledge within a group (e.g., communities of practice, virtual teams, or virtual communities) is supported within the literature (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Yoon & Wang, 2011). Therefore, we posit:

H3: In virtual teams, cognitive social capital positively affects the team member’s

perception of knowledge quality.

As noted in our conceptual model, the use of resources fosters the development of capabilities. There are many types of capabilities that can be developed through social capital, including knowledge sharing (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005). Within a virtual team, a benefit for individuals that are part of a team is the ability to access the knowledge among members within the team (Robert et al., 2008). There is an implicit understanding within organizations that the ability of the organization to effectively share useful, high quality knowledge among individuals and teams enables the organization to improve its outcomes (Haas & Hansan, 2007). As a team improves its knowledge quality, team members are empowered by the knowledge that they acquire. “The more network members can integrate the knowledge that is created and shared in the network into their daily work, the more the quality and efficiency of their work will benefit” (van den Hoof, van Weenen, Soekijad, & Huysman, 2010, p. 207).

Prior research has demonstrated that knowledge, depending on how it is communicated and shared, can yield benefits such as time savings, which can improve work quality and perceptions about the competence of the team (Haas & Hansan, 2007). The willingness of team members to share knowledge positively impacts the effectiveness of a team (Lin et al., 2008) or the decisionmaking of a team (Robert et al., 2008). Yet much of the prior research we found related to the relationship between knowledge quality within a team and outcomes focused on the outcomes of the collective as opposed to the outcomes of the individual team members. In this study, we propose that knowledge sharing within the collective also benefits the individual who is part of the group (McGrath, 1991). This is an important distinction from much of the prior literature that examines outcomes within a team environment. Specifically, we propose that the individual team members obtain benefits from the quality of the knowledge shared within the team. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4: In virtual teams, the team member’s perception of knowledge quality is positively related to the individual benefits recognized by the team member.

These hypotheses are identified in Figure 2 (shown previously) and support our conceptual model (Figure 1). Our conceptual model, which contends that individual resources (i.e., social capital) affect capabilities (i.e., knowledge quality), which in turn impacts outcomes (i.e., individual benefits) of the individual team member, is grounded on the theoretical lens of the resource-based view of the firm. Our research model builds on the conceptual model by introducing the social capital theory to develop and use resources to build capabilities and affect outcomes.

## 3. Research Method

Because we examine the relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes of individuals within virtual teams, our population of interest is individuals who are (a) part of a team, which is defined as a group with “complementary skills and who are committed to a common purpose, set of performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable” (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993, p. 112), and (b) part of a virtual team, in that the interaction and communication among the team members is mediated through ICTs with team members who are geographically distributed.

If we surveyed individuals in workplace virtual teams, we could introduce multiple confounds within the study. Organizations use virtual teams for different purposes, with different expectations, and with different degrees of virtuality. We also wanted to ensure that knowledge sharing as a capability was a relevant and vital component within the virtual teams. Therefore, to ensure that all participants would be members of a virtual team that had similar goals and contexts, we use a population of virtual team members in a MMORPG. An MMORPG is an online game in which players engage in an interactive environment to complete a story, often with the support of fellow players within the game (Achterbosch, Pierce, & Simmons, 2008). While individuals play an MMORPG for entertainment, research suggests that these types of games can help players gain skills necessary for the workplace (Petter et al., 2018; Sourmelis et al., 2017; Yee, 2014).

In MMORPGs, players engage in many activities that are consistent with organizational tasks (Guo & Barnes, 2011), and the interaction among team members is technology-mediated. Wigand et al. (2012) suggest that “the relationship between players is compared to the relationship between coworkers in their real job and the activities in such games are increasingly similar to the work performed in business corporations” (p. 2). Within MMORPGs, players must often collaborate to successfully complete tasks (Zhong, 2011). Communication between participants can take place using a variety of ICTs that incorporate text, voice, and/or video.

These “synthetic” worlds are becoming increasingly used as a source for research in multiple contexts (Orlikowski, 2009). Given the many similarities between the structure and dynamics of workplace virtual teams to MMORPGs, we survey players of an MMORPG to understand how individuals within virtual teams develop social capital as a resource to use knowledge quality as a capability that affects outcomes in the form of benefits to the individual player.

To examine our research model, we collected data from users of the MMORPG titled subscribers were accessing the game. To progress through the game, players must collaborate with one another. Players can create or join one of the thousands of organized Free Companies (FCs) of up to 512 players. After joining a FC, the player can communicate with the other members through a text channel separate from the main environment text chat channel, allowing for FC only communication. FC members can use the channel to coordinate events for the team to boost morale or complete game-related content. Individuals interact with FC members to engage in storyline content (quests and battles to experience the lore of the game), explore dungeons (where enemies are fought to obtain specialized gear and weapons), and complete coil (the endgame content, which has the most difficult enemy fights in the game). FC members may be collocated, but more often, the FC members are geographically dispersed and rely on ICTs to communicate and collaborate with each other to accomplish the goals of the FC.

Individuals join an FC to accomplish tasks, socialize, or collaborate with fellow players in the FC. FCs, in general, have similar objectives and goals, yet each FC is its own team with its own characteristics. Members of FCs often work together to accomplish both collective and personal goals. FCs can range in size from 4 to 512 team members. FCs have different practices and expectations within themselves based on their leadership, the amount of mentoring provided within the group, and the extent to which team members wish to collaborate with one another.

In this study, our target population comprised of individuals who had played and/or were playing in any FC at the time of data collection. We did not require that respondents be from the same FC. We chose to sample across FCs to ensure sufficient variation for the constructs within our study.

While there are differences between FFXIV FC and virtual teams in the workplace, there is value in using this context for our study. By sampling individuals who were part of an FC within an MMORPG, we address a critique of prior research related to groups and teams that has argued that too many findings about team dynamics and interactions is based on short-term, ad hoc groups that are often not generalizable to organizations (McGrath, 1991). In our study, the average respondent had been a member of their FC for over nine months. Furthermore, this study uses a theory application approach to generalizability. The theory application approach toward generalizability does not require the sample nor the intervention to mimic practice, but rather seeks to identify a sample that would allow for a rigorous test of the theory; an effects application approach to generalizability, in contrast, seeks to obtain results that would be identical in both a research and practice setting (Calder, Phillips, & Tybout, 1981). With a theory application approach, a sample may be drawn from a homogenous group, which is a different process than identifying a random sample from a population, in an effort to reduce the likelihood of determining that there is no covariation among the constructs (Calder et al., 1981). While some have criticized this approach in that it can lessen external validity (Lynch, 1982, 1983), the theory application approach, in research, has been used in several IS studies (e.g., Lee, Cuellar, Keil, & Johnson, 2014; Petter & Carter, 2017). This study examines the boundaries of social capital theory and the resource-based view of the firm in the context of virtual teams. We study the phenomenon in a setting that will allow for testing the boundaries of the theory in a homogenous group (i.e., virtual team members within a single MMORPG), although some argue that virtual teams in an MMORPG are not consistent with virtual teams in an organizational context (Schultze, Hiltz, Nardi, Rennecker, & Stucky, 2008).

## 4.1 Research Measures

We measure Structural Social Capital (resource) through 5-point Likert scale questions regarding the interaction ties among the actors within the FC community. In this study, we operationalized structural social capital by asking respondents about their interaction and communication with members of their FC using measures adapted from Chiu et al. (2006) for social interaction ties.

Relational Social Capital (resource) comprised of three dimensions: trust, identification with the team, and the norm of reciprocity. All items were measured using a 5-point Likert scale and adapted from studies examining relational social capital in a technology-oriented context considering the concept of integrity and the belief that FC members will conform to a set of expectations regarding values and principles of the virtual community using items from validated studies (Chiu et al., 2006). Identification examines the extent an individual perceives themselves as part of the larger group (or FC), and the measures are adapted from existing measures (Chiu et al., 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2006). Norm of Reciprocity examines whether individuals in the FC share knowledge with each other and if individuals expect to receive knowledge in return. These measures are adapted from Kankanhalli et al. (2005) and Chiu et al. (2006).

Cognitive Social Capital (resource) measures of the shared goals and objectives held by the community members. The items, adapted from Chiu et al. (2006), ask individuals if they perceive that members of their FC have consistent goals for sharing knowledge and helping one another.

We measure Knowledge Quality (capability) using a 5-point Likert scale using items from relevance and usefulness of the knowledge, shared within the FC.

Individual Benefits (outcome) measures if being a member of the team has impacted the individual’s gameplay. We created items in which individuals identified if being a member of an FC impacts their personal ability to perform tasks, make decisions, and increase their confidence within the game (based on individual impact measures identified in DeLone and McLean (1992)).

## 4.2 Data Collection

Prior to data collection, we pretested the instrument with 13 individuals having experience with MMORPGs similar to FFXIV. We made minor edits after reviewing the responses and obtaining feedback from participants. We performed a pilot of our instrument with players of FFXIV. Using the insights from the pilot, we made small adjustments to specific items and flow of the survey. For data collection, we promoted the survey to players of FFXIV through in-game text chat and social media platforms. We collected data using a single survey at a single point in time. In total, 296 individuals completed the survey; after removing seven responses due to outliers, we retained 289 responses for our analysis.

The average age of the sample was 28 years old, with 34% of the respondents being female. Over 65% of our respondents worked part time or full time, and 28% of our respondents were full-time or part-time students. Table 1 summarizes the demographic information of our sample.

## 5. Discussion

This paper contributes to our understanding regarding how the use of social capital, as a resource in virtual teams, offers benefits to individual team members. This research leverages prior research on social capital in virtual teams and virtual communities (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2006; Robert et al., 2008), and it extends prior research to examine a conceptual model inspired by the resource-based view of the firm. Although the research model in Figure 2 has similarities to other studies (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Chang & Chuang, 2011), our research has a different context and theoretical framing.

This research differs from other studies, in part, due to our focus on virtual teams. Other studies with research models that exhibit similarities to Figure 2, examine their models in the context of virtual communities (e.g., Chiu et al. 2006; Chang & Chuang 2011; Wasko & Faraj 2005). Individuals join virtual communities (whether a professional or hedonic community) to gain knowledge or enjoyment. Given this goal of virtual communities, the typical dependent variable of interest is knowledge quality or knowledge quantity. Yet, our study uses a different lens (i.e., resource-based view of the firm) to understand the outcomes in the context of virtual teams. Unlike a virtual community, the purpose of a team, virtual or not, is to accomplish a collective goal (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993). Therefore, our final dependent variable focused on outcomes, as measured by the benefits obtained by the individual team member, which is different than prior research that has studied social capital in the context of virtual communities.

Our study also differs from prior studies in that we use the resource-based view of the firm research model (Figure 2). Prior research has examined portions of the research model, such as the effect of one or more dimensions of social capital on knowledge sharing within a community or team (e.g., Chui et al., 2006; Chang & Chuang, 2011; Robert et al., 2008; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), and other studies have examined the effect of one or more dimensions of social capital on outcomes (e.g., Lin et al., 2008; Moran, 2005). Yet, this study examines a chain of events (shown in Figure 1) consistent with the resource-based view of the firm (Wernerfelt, 1984), and we focus specifically on the resource of social capital, guided by social capital theory (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). By integrating the theoretical lens of the resource-based view of the firm and social capital, new insights emerge beyond prior research related to social capital in virtual teams or virtual communities.

One insight from our research is the consideration of capabilities as a mediator between resources and outcomes in our conceptual model and research model. This extends prior work that examined how social capital affects knowledge sharing (e.g., Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005) and contributes to prior literature that did not consider mediating relationships between social capital and outcomes (e.g., Bolino et al., 2002; Hsu & Hung, 2013; Lechner et al., 2010). The mediating relationship identifies that resources alone are not sufficient to generate positive outcomes, but rather resources are used to develop capabilities that affect outcomes. As Grant (1996) explains, resources are building blocks used in conjunction with one another to develop capabilities. The relationship between capabilities and outcomes has been well-established in studies of the resource-based view of the firm in the information systems field (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Nwankpa & Datta, 2017). Yet, the results of our mediation analysis further demonstrate the importance of exploring mediating relationships between resources (and more specifically, social capital) and outcomes. Given that knowledge quality fully mediates the relationships between trust and individual benefits and cognitive social capital and individual benefits, this demonstrates that there may not always be a direct relationship between social capital and outcomes. The capability of knowledge quality also partially mediates the relationship of identification and individual benefits. There is value in continuing to study the mediating effect of knowledge quality, as well as other capabilities, as we seek to understand how the resource of social capital influences outcomes for individuals or collectives.

Furthermore, in contrast to prior research that has focused on the benefits of resources and/or capabilities to a group (Lin et al., 2008; Robert et al., 2008), we demonstrate that the resources and capabilities used by team members can benefit individuals. Individuals often have their own personal goals when engaging within a team (Petter & Carter, 2017); therefore, there is value in understanding how resources can be used by a collective that offers benefits to not only the group, but also the individual contributors. Expanding research that leverages the resourcebased view of the firm to consider other possible outcomes, beyond the collective’s benefits, may offer new insights on how resources and capabilities not only affect the collective, but those that comprise the collective.

Our supported hypotheses are consistent with some of the prior research that has examined the various dimensions of social capital and knowledge quality in a manner similar to our approach. We found significant relationships between trust and knowledge quality, consistent with other studies that used similar measures to examine these relationships in virtual communities (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Choi & Scott, 2013). Our results demonstrate that cognitive social capital predicts knowledge quality, also consistent with prior research (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006). Although the relationship between identification and knowledge quality has received mixed support in past studies, we found support conflicting results regarding the relationships between social capital and quality of knowledge sharing or outcomes. One reason for these mixed results is due to the many different measures that researchers use to assess social capital. This variation in measurement for the dimensions of social capital limits the ability to synthesize future research using techniques, like meta-analysis, to better understand the relationships among social capital and various outcomes. Therefore, in this study, we used previously developed measures for each dimension of social capital to aid in building a tradition of research that uses consistent measures for the dimensions of social capital.

Two hypotheses are not supported in our model. First, structural social capital, the relationships among members and the strength of those relationships among members (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), was not found to have a relationship with knowledge quality in our context. Prior research has found mixed results in identifying a relationship between structural social capital and knowledge quality (Chiu et al., 2006; Robert et al., 2008; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Our measures for structural social capital are consistent with Chiu et al. (2006), who examined this relationship in the context of virtual communities, and our lack of significance for this relationship is consistent with Chiu et al. (2006). As a post hoc test, we examined if structural social capital had a direct effect on individual outcomes. The relationship was not significant. It may be that structural social capital is not a relevant resource in the development knowledge quality in virtual teams, or there may be other explanations for this result. Additional research should explore why the relationship between structural social capital and knowledge quality varies across contexts, beyond the measures used to assess structural social capital.

The second hypothesis not supported in our study was the relationship between the norm examining the relationship between reciprocity and outcomes also had no effect. We note that the lack of significance for this relationship is also consistent with Chiu et al. (2006). Chiu et al. (2006) suggest that trust may mediate the relationship between the norm of reciprocity and knowledge quality. Yet, for our context, we suggest an alternative explanation. In conducting additional analysis to examine the lack of significant relationship, we noted that the measures for reciprocity had the highest mean and lowest standard deviation compared to all other dimensions of social capital. Given the high ratings respondents provided for reciprocity, with little variation, it may be that reciprocity is expected among members of an FC and consistent across FCs. In other contexts or in virtual teams in which reciprocity is not a norm, this dimension of social capital may become more important in influencing capabilities. Qualitative research examining how reciprocity is developed and affects outcomes in virtual teams may be useful to explore this relationship.

In this study, we used a nontraditional context to study virtual teams: members of FCs within an MMORPG. We made this choice as part of our goal for a theory application approach to have a sample that would allow us to test our conceptual and theoretical model (Calder et al., 1981). By using MMORPG as a context, we examine the relationships among social capital, knowledge quality, and individual outcomes in a setting that allows us to examine the theory without introducing additional confounds. We had concerns that if we used a sample of workers in virtual teams across organizations, functions, industries, and types of tasks, there may be large differences across individuals due to the differing nature of the teams that individuals were recalling for the study. By using members of a virtual team within the same type of online game, our sample would be more homogenous in that there would be more similarities among members and teams in terms of their goals, tasks, and team structures. As we examined our data, we noted strong covariances among the various constructs in this study; therefore, our homogenous sample of individuals in virtual teams within a specific MMORPG supported our goal for theory application (Calder et al., 1981). Furthermore, the longevity of our respondents as members of the same FC (i.e., over nine months) has consistency with the type of longevity that can be experienced in an organizational context. Our use of MMORPG as a research context is consistent with calls for using online gaming as a context to study phenomenon in information systems research (Petter, 2017). Similar to virtual communities or communities of practice, which are voluntary to join or participate (e.g., Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), the virtual teams studied in our context are voluntary as well. Individuals do not have to join an FC to play the game; however, choosing to be part of an FC may provide value in helping the individual achieve their goals. This type of voluntary virtual team extends the prior research on the study of social capital in virtual communities to certain types of virtual teams (i.e., those that are voluntary). Yet, in an organizational context, many organizations may assign individuals to a virtual team and individuals may have little autonomy in these decisions. Therefore, further study could examine how the resource of social capital may be used to develop capabilities and outcomes differently when the virtual team is imposed, rather than chosen.

## 5.1 Implications for Practice

Organizations increasingly are shifting toward more networked, collaborative organizations that are comprised of distributed teams in which workers interact remotely through ICTs (Schwab, 2016). We can use the findings of this research to offer guidance for those that are leaders within virtual teams or managers that oversee virtual teams.

First, we recognize that there is an increasing need for a virtual, digital workforce because of the changing nature of work. There are many opportunities for potential employees to develop the skills needed to be successful on a virtual team. One is for managers to consider the role of online games, such as FFXIV and others, to help individuals gain skills to communicate and collaborate with others virtually (Goh & Wasko, 2012). Useful skills for the workplace, such as collaboration and decision-making can be developed through playing online games (Petter et al., 2018). The current study supports the importance of this finding through the demonstration that trust significantly impacts the quality of knowledge shared within a team and thus the resulting benefits of team membership. For hiring managers that need employees who will be working in virtual teams, it can be useful to ask employees about their experiences in all forms of virtual teams, including those experiences obtained through online games (Petter et al., 2018).

Our study identified three forms of social capital, which are resources that can be used to develop the capability of knowledge sharing within the team: identification with the team, trust of the team, and shared vision or goals (i.e., cognitive social capital). Managers or virtual team leaders need to find ways to develop opportunities for virtual team members to have shared experiences through virtual worlds, simulation-based training, or other team-based activities to develop a shared language, vision, and experiences to support the team. For established virtual teams, creating opportunities for the members to share knowledge and expertise through informal conversations or activities can support the development of social capital resources. These strategies can support the development of trust within the team as well as a feeling of belonging. The creation of a shared vision, the development of trust, and identification with the group can establish other positive norms within the team, such as reciprocity. Teams that are struggling with developing a shared vision, trust, or identity need to quickly establish team-oriented goals and interests. Individuals who are struggling within the group may benefit from a mentor o individuals feel that they are a part of a larger team (Bendaly, 1996).

## 5.2 Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Some of the limitations of this study introduce opportunities for future research. This study is cross-sectional, meaning the relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes are measured at a single point in time. Thus, we are unable to examine how these resources evolve into capabilities, which in turn influence outcomes over time. It is unknown how the relationships used to develop social capital strengthen or attenuate over time, particularly in virtual teams, as capabilities and outcomes are developed. This suggests an opportunity to study virtual team members using longitudinal and/or qualitative approaches to further examine the evolution of social capital as resources to develop capabilities and improve outcomes for virtual team members. Furthermore, our survey only examines the perspective from a single point of view, that of the individual within the team. There is an opportunity for future research to conduct mutlilevel research related to social capital, to understand how individual resources are used by the collective to offer benefits to both the collective and the individual.

Another limitation of this research is the context of this study. We chose to survey players of an MMORPG because their interactions within FC have many similarities with virtual teams in the workplace. Furthermore, it has been argued that online games can be a useful environment to study various phenomena within information systems research, such as team dynamics (Petter 2017). While there are many similarities between interactions in the online games and the workplace, we recognize that there are limits of the generalization of the findings. For example, in FFXIV, players interact with fellow players who are in similar regions of the world and may not be as global as some virtual teams. Individuals responding to this survey were from North America and were only playing with other players from North America; therefore, other cultural factors that can influence the development of social capital as a resource would likely exist if a similar survey was conducted in global organizations with members around the world. This research can also be extended to perform a similar study using an effects application approach to understand how well the results translate to a specific context (Calder et al., 1981). It would also be relevant to consider a cross-cultural study, to understand differences for global organizations in which culture might influence the development of social capital as a resource.

Finally, this study used subjective measures of outcomes for individuals rather than objective measures of individual benefits. Furthermore, all measures were based on a single person’s perspective about the team. We were not able to gather multiple responses from team members to examine team-level constructs. If we had focused on objective benefits, we may have received different insights. If we had been able to obtain both individual-level and team-level measures, we would be able to understand more about the differences of team members within teams. Future research could examine how resources influence capabilities and outcomes using measures that are objective to determine how well these resources and capabilities could improve the actual outcomes of the individual, virtual team, or organization. There is also an opportunity to gather data at multiple levels of analysis (e.g., individual and team) in future research to better understand how individual resources can be aggregated to form team-level capabilities.

## 6. Conclusion

As organizations increase their reliance on virtual teams, it is important to understand how virtual teams interact because of their need to use ICTs for communication, collaboration, and coordination (Khazanchi & Zigurs, 2006). In this research, we leveraged the resource-based view of the firm as a means to consider the relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes for individuals who are part of a virtual team. Learning which dimensions of social capital, specifically cognitive social capital and relational social capital (i.e., trust and identification) developed by team members, contribute to the team member’s perception of knowledge quality capital and the relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes in the resource-based view of the firm.

Another contribution of this research is the discovery that resources and capabilities of a collective can accrue in positive benefits for those that comprise the collective. Specifically, we considered the relationship between an individual’s perception of the group’s knowledge quality and the benefit to the individual team member. We have much to learn regarding why some virtual teams achieve more positive outcomes than others. As individuals participate, engage, and contribute in virtual teams, we should consider not only the value of the team member’s contributions to the entire team, but we need to consider how participating in a team benefits the individual team member. If individual team members fail to derive value from the virtual team experience, the team may be less successful or individuals will cease to continue to engage with the team. Prior research on the resource-based view of the firm tends to only assume benefits for the collective; however, this research demonstrates that individuals within the collective can also benefit from the use of resources and capabilities shared by the team.

As our third contribution, we note the importance of considering the mediating relationship of capabilities when examining the relationship between resources and outcomes. By distinguishing resources and capabilities, consistent with Grant’s (1996) knowledge-based view of the firm, we realized that for two resources: relational social capital as trust and cognitive social capital are fully mediated by knowledge quality. This finding further reinforces the need to carefully consider the relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes when considering the resources leveraged by a firm to achieve outcomes.

Finally, we demonstrate the potential of a different type of sampling frame to identify virtual team members. We chose to collect our sample from a popular MMORPG, consistent with suggestions offered by Petter (2017). Online gaming (as a subset of video games) is not a traditional context in information systems research to study team dynamics, but it is growing in its application and promotion (e.g., Goh & Wasko, 2012; Petter, 2017; Petter et al., 2018). This study demonstrates how online gaming can be useful to gain insight about a phenomenon that is relevant to practice: how virtual teams can improve outcomes. Using a theory application approach (Calder et al., 1981), we tested our conceptual and research models and gained insights that contribute to theory and offer insights to practice.
