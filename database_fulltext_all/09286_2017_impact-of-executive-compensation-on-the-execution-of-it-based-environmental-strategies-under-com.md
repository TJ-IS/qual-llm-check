# Impact of executive compensation on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies under competition

Ricky Y. K. Chan and Katherine H. Y. Ma

Department of Management and Marketing, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong

Correspondence: Ricky Y. K. Chan, Department of Management and Marketing, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong. Tel: (852) 2766 7110; E-mail: msricky@polyu.edu.hk

## Abstract

Drawing on agency theory and the contingent natural resource-based theory, this study employs firm-level archival and survey data of US manufacturing firms to examine the impact of CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies and the moderating influence of competitive intensity on this impact. The findings reveal that CEO fixed pay and bonus negatively affect the execution of the two types of IT-based environmental strategies, green IT strategies and IT-enabled green strategies, whereas CEO stock option positively influences such execution. The moderation analysis further highlights that while competitive intensity reinforces the positive impact of CEO stock option on both strategies, it weakens the negative impact of CEO fixed pay and bonus only on IT-enabled green strategies. The findings suggest that a highly competitive operating setting represents an ideal setting for using stock option to motivate CEOs to execute these two strategies. At the minimum, this setting helps mitigate the deterrent effect of fixed pay and bonus on CEOs’ execution of IT-enabled green strategies. The findings also suggest that firms should align their CEO compensation package with the characteristics of high uncertainty and long payback periods of IT-based environmental strategic endeavors and the competitive conditions they face. ## Introduction

Amidst the global trend of heightening environmental consciousness in this digital era (Sharma & Henriques, 2005), firms face soaring pressure from various stakeholders to employ information technology (IT) to support corporate environmental practices (hereafter ‘‘IT-based environmental strategies’’) (Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012). However, research on the role of IT in environmental management is still in its infancy (Malhotra et al, 2013). In view of this, some information systems (IS) scholars have called for ‘‘a better theoretical understanding and stronger empirical evidence on the role of IT as a solution for environmental sustainability’’ (Wang et al, 2015, p. 161).

To address this call, a growing number of IS researchers have recently drawn on such theoretical foundations as the resource-based perspective (Dao et al, 2011), institutional theory (Butler, 2010) and the technologyorganization-environment framework (Bose & Luo, 2011) to explicate the role of IT for corporate sustainability. Still others have undertaken empirical investigation on the strategic process underlying green IT adoption (e.g., Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012). Overall, while these recent studies have highlighted various opportunities to use IT to green corporate operations, they also recognize the presence of various environmental challenges (e.g., carbon emissions) created by the IT products themselves (Elliot, 2011).

In view of these environmental opportunities and challenges, we classify IT-based environmental strategies into green IT strategies and IT-enabled green strategies. Green IT strategies refer to firms’ strategies to minimize the negative environmental impacts that resulted from the production, use and disposal of IT equipment. Google’s effort to economize the energy consumption of its data centers compared with the industry average exemplifies this type of strategies (Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012). The second type relates to the use of IT as part of the solution for corporate sustainability and the positive impacts of IT-enabled environmental measures for greener corporate operations. Multinational companies’ increased use of teleconferencing instead of having their executives physically travel across subsidiaries worldwide exemplifies this type of strategies.

IS researchers and, in particular, those subscribing to Hart’s (1995) natural resource-based perspective have suggested that firms executing IT-based environmental strategies will have the potential to improve their financial performance and provide society with a more sustainable natural environment (Benitez-Amado et al, 2015). However, the execution of environmental strategies (including IT-based ones) often incurs a considerable amount of time and financial investments and adds complexity to organizational practices (Sharma, 2000). Coupled with the presence of various industry (e.g., industry-wide lack of technical knowledge) and organizational (e.g., organizational inertia) barriers (Delgado-Ceballos et al, 2012; Starik & Rands, 1995), such execution is bound to be risky, and its payoffs are seldom realized within a short time (Sharma, 2000). Indeed, IS research has asserted that the execution of IT-based environmental strategies is a long-term strategic process that takes typically five years to bring about a complete attitude change in employees at all levels of the firm, reorganize the business architecture, substantially revamp the physical infrastructure (e.g., data centers), implement the relevant governance mechanisms and secure collaboration from firms within the same supply chain (Unhelkar, 2011). Based on this, this study uses five years as a threshold to distinguish between ‘‘short-term’’ (i.e., \5 years) and ‘‘long-term’’ (i.e., C5 years) strategies. The adopted threshold is also consistent with the findings reported in the literature on strategic time horizons (Judge & Spitzfaden, 1995).

In short, the foregoing discussion highlights the uncertainties Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) face when deciding whether to execute these strategies. These uncertainties imply lower accuracy in predicting the outcomes of a strategic decision (Balkin et al, 2000).

When their strategic decision does not render the desired outcomes, CEOs, being ultimately responsible for corporate performance, need to bear the risks of pay reduction, lower personal reputation, or even dismissal (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Hence, if CEOs are not properly compensated for their perceived personal risks associated with executing these highly uncertain strategies, they will simply lack the motivation to do so (Gray & Cannella, 1997).

Despite the possible significant influence of CEO compensation structure on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies, their relationship has been virtually unexplored. This research paucity represents an important research gap, and bridging it would provide valuable insights into designing an appropriate CEO compensation package to promote corporate sustainability. Against this backdrop, this study aims at investigating how different CEO compensation forms influence the execution of IT-based environmental strategies. Since fixed pay, bonus and stock option are the three most popular forms of CEO compensation, accounting for around 70% of the total CEO compensation in dollar values (Yeung et al, 2011), this study focuses on these forms for analysis. According to upper echelons theory, CEOs are the key decision makers of critical resource allocations pertaining to major strategic moves (Hambrick & Mason, 1984). Prior environmental management research has also demonstrated that CEOs play the most pivotal role in executing environmental strategies (Egri & Herman, 2000). All this literature provides justification for focusing on CEOs as target executives of this study.

In view of today’s increasingly competitive business environment (Keller, 2003), this study further examines the moderating effect of competitive intensity on the relationship between CEO compensation form and the execution of IT-based environmental strategies. Prior research on the business value of IT has shown that the influence of IT capabilities on organizational performance depends on various external business environmental factors (Chen et al, 2014). Recently, IS researchers have also specifically demonstrated that the internal process underlying the impact of IT capabilities is contingent upon competitive intensity (Chen et al, 2015). These studies suggest that the present moderation analysis should help better understand whether the (de)motivational effect of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies would vary alongside different competitive conditions. It should also help rectify the sparse prior research attention to the impact of the business environment on IT-related corporate practices (Ravichandran & Liu, 2011).

In sum, given prior research’s scant attention to the impacts of executive compensation on the execution of environmental strategies, this study contributes to fill this gap by theoretically explaining and empirically demonstrating how different CEO compensation forms would impact this execution under different competitive conditions. By explicitly taking both types of IT-based environmental strategies into consideration, this study is among the first to examine the respective influences of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of these strategies. Such examination further advances understanding of their conceptualization and relationships with corporate environmental practices.

## Theoretical background

Although executing IT-based environmental strategies would further shareholders’ interests in the longer run, the uncertainties inherent in this execution could stil discourage the CEO from doing so to avoid risking his/ her personal interests. This difference manifests the classic ‘‘agency problem,’’ whose solution hinges on how to provide the CEO (the agent) with the right kind of compensation to align his/her interests with those of the shareholders (the principal) (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Given its relevance to examining the impact of CEO compensation on risky strategic pursuit, agency theory constitutes an important theoretical basis of this study. This theory, together with other relevant literature, is discussed below.

## Executive compensation and environmenta sustainability

Despite the existence of a large amount of studies on the relationship between executive compensation and corporate performance (Musteen et al, 2009), how this compensation would influence the execution of environmental strategies (let alone IT-based environmental strategies) remains largely unexplored. In this limited research, researchers have, in turn, focused on the relationship between executive compensation and corporate environmental performance (e.g., Berrone & Gomez-Mejia, 2009; Russo & Harrison, 2005). In particular, Berrone and Gomez-Mejia (2009) have demonstrated that corporate environmental performance serves as an important non-financial determinant of CEO pay within polluting industries. Moreover, they have found that long-term CEO pay positively affects the successful execution of pollution prevention strategies.

From a broader perspective, it is noted that some previous studies were devoted to examining the relationship between executive compensation and corporate social responsibility (CSR) issues. These studies, in general, demonstrated a positive relationship between longterm executive pay and corporate social performance (Mahoney & Thorne, 2005, 2006). Despite this, they often suffer from two limitations. First, they are confined to the influence of executive compensation on CSR performance rather than practices (e.g., Deckop et al, 2006; Mahoney & Thorne, 2005, 2006). Second, they often singled out a particular compensation form for analysis rather than examining the respective influences of different compensation forms (e.g., Adam & Schwartz, 2009; Mahoney & Thorne, 2005), thus undermining their practical significance for designing an appropriate CEO remuneration package to promote CSR/corporate environmental practices. These limitations suggest the need to examine the respective influences of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of CSR/environmental strategies simultaneously.

## Major CEO compensation forms and their characteristics

As mentioned, this study focuses on three key CEO compensation forms, fixed pay, bonus and stock option for analysis. Fixed pay refers to the basic cash compensation that CEOs receive within a specified period and is independent of their performance, at least during that period. As their fixed pay will not be increased even if they achieve higher performance, CEOs heavily compensated by this pay have a strong propensity to follow the status quo (Balkin et al, 2000). Bonus pay is compensation over and above the amount specified as fixed pay and is usually contingent upon a CEO’s performance. As the CEO’s performance significantly affects corporate performance (Hambrick & Mason, 1984), the board of directors often takes corporate performance as an important criterion to determine the amount of bonus paid to him/her (Mahoney & Thorne, 2005). Stock option pay is also a variable pay form and widely used to remunerate high-ranking executives such as CEOs (Larraza-Kintana et al, 2007). It gives CEOs the right to buy a certain number of firms’ shares at a specified price during a specified period of time. This suggests that if the share price rises, CEOs can make a profit by exercising their buying rights.

Although it is generally believed that CEOs will be more inclined to pursue risky strategies if they are remunerated by variable pay (Jensen & Meckling, 1976), this view needs to be qualified as their inclination may also vary with time horizon of the pay form in question. Time horizon refers to the extent to which the compensation provides financial rewards to CEOs based on longterm performance outcomes (Gray & Cannella, 1997). While both bonus and stock option are variable pay contingent upon CEOs’ performance, they differ significantly in time horizon.

Time horizon difference between bonus and stock option Bonus has long been tied to short-term performance outcomes, and is, in practice, used to reward CEOs’ annual performance (Musteen et al, 2009). The short-term horizon of bonus pay is likely to motivate CEOs to focus on investments with short payback periods so as to increase their personal (e.g., financial and reputational) gains more quickly (Laverty, 1996). Moreover, although long-term risky strategies may enhance the firm’s long-term competitiveness, they may dampen its short-term financial performance (Kane, 2002). Hence, to avoid risking their future bonus earnings and job security due to perceived incompetence in managing short-term corporate performance, CEOs who are heavily compensated by bonus pay are thus reluctant to execute long-term risky strategies.

Contrary to bonus, stock option is a form of equitybased variable pay contingent upon long-term performance outcomes. It is regarded as a long-term compensation form typically exercisable over a period of 10 years (Dunford et al, 2005; Lambert et al, 1989). It has long been regarded as a powerful compensation tool to align interests between shareholders and their CEO (Cheng, 2004). International business researchers have further found that CEOs heavily compensated by pay tied to long-term performance (e.g., stock option) are more willing to choose the riskier full-control (vs. sharedcontrol) mode for foreign market entry (Musteen et al, 2009). This willingness is attributed to their being assessed mainly by long-term performance targets, thus greatly relieving them of the worry about lower future earnings and/or job security due to poorer short-term performance. This points to the effectiveness of stock option in motivating CEOs to pursue riskier strategies whose value-adding potential may take longer time to realize.

Despite the plausibility of the foregoing analysis, it may still be possible that long-term strategies such as ITbased environmental strategies would have positive impacts on the short-term corporate performance, thus making bonus also serve as an incentive to motivate CEOs to execute these strategies. In this respect, Hart (1995, p. 998) has explicitly contended that ‘‘there is little reason to believe that [environmental] investment wil result in enhanced short-term profits.’’ Moreover, although there is empirical evidence that such investment will improve long-term corporate financial performance (e.g., King & Lenox, 2001), studies have demonstrated that it dampens short-term financial results (e.g., Hart & Ahuja, 1996; Sarkis & Cordeiro, 2001). By studying publicly listed firms in the USA, Deckop et al (2006, p. 337) have concluded that investment in corporate social performance (of which environmental performance is a major component) ‘‘is likely to have a limited or possibly negative effect on short-term financial performance.’’ They have further shown that while CEO compensation with short time horizon (e.g., bonus) would deter CEOs’ engagement in activities leading to higher corporate social performance, that with long time horizon (e.g., stock option) would encourage such engagement.

## Agency theory

According to agency theory, executive compensation is an important mechanism that relates to the conflicts of interest between shareholders and executives (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). The theory posits that CEOs are employed agents of shareholders and may have incentives to act in ways against the shareholders’ best interest (Gray & Cannella, 1997). It also posits that CEOs differ considerably from shareholders in their risk-taking attitudes (Sharpe, 1964). Unlike shareholders that can easily diversify their risks through investing in many firms, CEOs often have no choice but to invest their human capital only in the firms for which they work. This undiversified investment of human capital inevitably ties CEOs’ personal interests (e.g., incomes, job security) closely to uncertainties associated with firm performance, thus making them more risk-averse than shareholders (Wright et al, 1996).

Through adjusting the level and/or proportion of various compensation components (e.g., salary, bonus and stock option), agency theorists, however, believe that the board of directors can tie the CEO’s personal interests with the firm’s short- or long-term performance and thus motivate him/her to execute risky but potentially promising strategies (Berrone & Gomez-Mejia, 2009). The ability of agency theory to link CEOs’ preference for strategic pursuit with their compensation justifies its use for developing hypotheses regarding the direct effect of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies.

## The contingent natural resource-based theory

Through synthesizing perspectives from the natural resource-based theory (Hart, 1995) and dynamic capabilities (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000), Aragon-Correa and Sharma (2003) have proposed the contingent natural resource-based theory to contend that firm-specific resources will affect the practice of proactive corporate environmental strategies. The theory also asserts that this practice will enhance corporate performance through better aligning the firm with changes in its business environment (Ruede-Menzanares et al, 2008). Moreover, this theory posits that there are some contingent variables that affect the development of environmental strategies and the influence of these strategies on firm performance (Wang et al, 2015). These variables include certain internal factors such as a firm’s number of interlocking directors (i.e., those simultaneously holding directorship for more than one firm) (de Mandojana & Aragon-Correa, 2015), and certain external conditions such as regulatory pressure (Chan, 2010). Of particular relevance to the present study is that the theory also posits that competitive intensity will moderate the internal process underlying the development of corporate environmental practices including the influence of firm-specific resources on such practices (Aragon-Correa & Sharma, 2003).

Besides, since the characteristics of different CEO compensation forms, as discussed earlier, manifest a firm’s overall risk-taking attitude (e.g., risk-taking vs. risk-averse) and stance adopted for competition (e.g., short- vs long-term oriented), they could be conceived as a proxy for its strategic posture. Strategic posture is defined as the firm’s overall competitive orientation and is reflected by its senior managers’ preference to take business-related risks and initiate innovative strategic changes for competition (Covin & Slevin, 1990). It is the firm’s overall strategic orientation or grand mode of strategy making (Thoumrungroje & Tansuhaj, 2005).

Drawing on the attention-based view, marketing researchers have regarded strategic posture as a critical driver that determines how managerial attention is paid to certain business activities and how corporate investment is made (de Clercq & Zhou, 2014). IS researchers have also conceived strategic posture as a major determinant of various strategic choices and corporate activities (Mithas et al, 2013). Given that this overall strategic stance can guide the firm’s strategic practices and motivates its employees to work along a particular direction (de Clercq & Zhou, 2014), resource-based researchers have also regarded it as an essential firm-specific intangible resource that bears positive implications for corporate performance (Aragon-Correa, 1998; Chan, 2010; Hult & Ketchen, 2001). In short, the above analysis suggests that CEO compensation form as a proxy of strategic posture or intangible resource would significantly affect strategic practices including those proenvironmental ones. Based on this and the aforementioned contingent natural resource-based theory, it is likely that CEO compensation form (as a proxy of strategic posture or resource) would influence the execution of IT-based environmental strategies, and such influence would further be moderated by competitive intensity. For this reason, this theory serves as a theoretical basis to hypothesize the moderating effects here.

## CEO fixed pay and IT-based environmental strategies

Green IT strategies Agency theorists have long argued for the importance to configure a remuneration package that can ascertain proper alignment of the interests between CEOs and shareholders (Baysinger et al, 1991). These theorists believe that if CEOs are mainly rewarded by a stable amount of remuneration but without regard to their achievement of any specific corporate targets, they will lack the motivation to execute risky strategies even though these strategies have high value-adding potential for the firm. This is because they do not perceive that they are properly compensated for the increasing personal risks associated with such pursuit (Jensen & Meckling, 1976).

As mentioned, green IT strategies refer to strategies aimed at minimizing the negative environmental impacts associated with their production, use, and/or disposal of IT. In view of the nature of these strategies, their execution would be highly risky. Specifically, while piecemeal decisions concerning the replacement of existing small-time computing devices with more energy-efficient or lower-carbon emitting ones can be executed within a year, more profound results (e.g., attitudinal change among employees and revamped physical infrastructure) associated with the execution of green IT strategies throughout the organization take approximately five years to realize (Unhelkar, 2011). Moreover, the successful execution of these strategies often requires strong financial support and commitment from corporate-level executives and involves formulating various sustainability policies and procedures for green procurement, operation, and disposal, forming working groups to cope with technical challenges associated with the replacement of existing devices, and bringing about a consensus on following the firm’s revised environmental standards. In addition, the adoption of green IT strategies involves complex decisions regarding the execution of the green data strategy. This execution refers to the use, storage, mirroring, security, backups, cleanups, and architecture for data in an ecologically responsible manner, and concerns such important managerial decisions as optimization and virtualization of data servers and storage consolidation via the latest technologies (Murugesan, 2008). On some occasions, the execution of the green data strategy also involves a major revamp of data centers or other related physical infrastructure and thus would take even longer time to complete (Unhelkar, 2011). In sum, the foregoing discussion suggests that the execution of green IT strategies would incur high levels of strategic, financial, and technical risks, and hardly produce immediate results. On this basis, it is likely that CEOs will be reluctant to sacrifice their self-interests (e.g., job security, pay rise) by executing green IT strategies if most of their compensation comes from fixed pay, thus suggesting a negative influence of this pay form on such execution.

These solutions would enable the firm to derive environmental benefits that include making its operating practices and processes more ecofriendly, optimizing the power consumption, substituting CO -intense traveling with virtual meetings, and monitoring the emission along its supply chain (Unhelkar. 2011).

Despite IT’s enabling potential to achieve corporate sustainability, there is no guarantee for its realization. Indeed, as the adoption of IT-enabled green strategies often involves some major changes in current organizational practices and processes, executives fostering this adoption first need to conduct a thorough review not only on the firm’s existing IT systems, but also on the entire set of its existing organizational processes to clearly identify areas for greening (Hammer & Champy,

Similar to the execution of green IT strategies that incur significant investments in more ecofriendly computing equipment, the execution of ITenabled green strategies also requires substantial investments in the latest IT-enabled solutions. Such investments may be even higher if the firm intends to employ some advanced and customized IT systems to improve and monitor its environmental performance in a holistic fashion (Hammer & Champy, 2006).

Another characteristic of IT-enabled green strategies is that its adoption often requires collaboration among firms within the same supply chain (Ajamieh et al, 2016). For instance, in order to reduce carbon emissions from its operations, a firm may need to develop some collaborative web-based processes to govern the total emissions of partnering firms within the entire supply chain. Taken together, the following hypothesis is offered:

H1: The proportion of CEO fixed pay negatively affects the execution of green IT strategies (H1a) and IT-enabled green strategies (H1b).

## CEO bonus and IT-based environmental strategies

Green IT strategies Typically, bonuses are employed to reward CEOs’ short-term or annual performance. This contrasts with the execution of green IT strategies, which takes a much longer time to realize its benefits to the organization. This execution also incurs a considerable amount of financial and managerial resources to finetune existing corporate sustainability policies and procedures, address technical issues involved in equipment replacement, and reach an organizationwide consensus on meeting the revised and often more stringent environmental standards (Unhelkar, 2011). The activities pertinent to the execution of green IT strategies inevitably add complexity to organizational operations and lead to resistance from at least some employees (Delgado-Ceballos et al, 2012). These complexities and resistance make it difficult to predict how much and when the firm would benefit from such execution. Facing these uncertainties, CEOs will be reluctant to execute green IT strategies if most of their compensation comes from bonus pay, thus suggesting a negative influence of this pay form on the execution.

IT-enabled green strategies When compared with the execution of green IT strategies, that of IT-enabled green strategies may require even more corporate (e.g., managerial and financial) resources and time to realize the relevant benefits as it involves more fundamental change in existing organizational members’ ways of practice. In order to execute these strategies successfully, the firm needs to thoroughly review its current organizational processes, source and adopt advanced IT solutions to enable more sustainable operations, and tackle employees’ resistance to the changing (though greener) practices. All of these tasks inevitably add complexity to its operations and are unlikely to yield immediate benefits. To maximize the effectiveness of executing these strategies, the firm also needs to secure collaboration from other firms within the same supply chain. Hence, it is unlikely that CEOs will be motivated to execute these strategies if most of their pay comes from bonuses. The above discussion leads to:

H2: The proportion of CEO bonus pay negatively affects the execution of green IT strategies (H2a) and IT-enabled green strategies (H2b).

## CEO stock option and IT-based environmenta strategies

Green IT strategies Stock option is a variable pay form characterized by a long-term horizon. By typing CEOs’ remuneration with the firm’s long-term values, stock option is likely to provide significant incentives to motivate CEOs to undertake risky but potentially promising strategies such as green IT strategies, which involve various major strategic and financial decisions on how to replace existing computing devices with more eco-friendly ones in a systematic and coordinated manner. As stock option is granted upon the achievement of long-term performance targets, it also effectively matches the relatively long time span required for realizing benefits associated with these strategies. This suggests a positive impact of stock option pay on the execution of green IT strategies.

IT-enabled green strategies As mentioned, the execution of IT-enabled green strategies requires a fundamental change to the existing ways of operations as well as cultivating inter-organizational collaboration with other firms within the same supply chain. These characteristics suggest that CEOs would perceive the execution of these strategies as even riskier and requiring a longer time to realize the corporate benefits (vs. that of green IT strategies). As such, a pay form which is contingent upon long-term performance outcomes would help compensate CEOs’ perceived high risks associated with such execution. This discussion leads to:

H3: The proportion of CEO stock option pay positively affects the execution of green IT strategies (H3a) and IT-enabled green strategies (H3b).

## Moderating effects of competitive intensity

Competitive intensity refers to an operating setting under which competition is fierce due to the presence of numerous competitors and the lack of opportunities for further growth (Auh & Menguc, 2005). It reflects the degree to which firms face competition within their industry (Gu et al, 2008). An industry with a high level of competitive intensity is characterized by fierce price wars and the availability of many highly similar and thus competing product offerings (Gatignon & Xuereb, 1997).

Competitive intensity has long been conceived as a major contributor to the hostility of the operating environment (Zahra & Covin, 1995). In prior research, competitive intensity has been treated as a contextual factor that moderates the relationship between various elements in the strategic decision-making process (Chan et al, 2012; Murray et al, 2011). Drawing on the contingent natural resource-based theory (Aragon-Correa & Sharma, 2003), IS researchers have also contended that certain business environmental conditions would facilitate or impede the process underlying the development of environmental management capabilities (Wang et al, 2015). Based on the above discussion, it is proposed here that competitive intensity moderates the relationships between different CEO compensation forms and the execution of IT-based environmental strategies.

Marketing researchers have argued that, in a less competitive market (i.e., low competitive intensity), a firm may not suffer significant loss in revenues even if it does not pay enough attention to customer requirements. This is because customers have to stick to the firm’s offerings anyway due to the lack of alternatives (Chan et al, 2012). Conversely, as customers in a highly competitive market (i.e., high competitive intensity) are much freer to change their suppliers, a firm that better satisfies customer requirements than its rivals is likely to significantly enhance its market and financial performance. This suggests that, when compared with those operating in a less competitive market, CEOs operating in a more competitive market tend to be more responsive to the various requirements of their customers.

Of these requirements, customers’ growing demand for offerings and/or production processes with less environmental impacts has become a pressing issue that CEOs need to tackle (Banerjee et al, 2003). When compared with CEOs facing low competitive intensity, those facing high competitive intensity are expected to be more responsive to meet this ecological demand. This is because the high competitive pressure will force them to do so in order to prevent their customers from switching to other readily available competing suppliers (Chan et al, 2012). Prior research has further shown that, in a highly competitive setting, CEOs would adopt a riskier strategic stance to find innovative ways to outperform their competitors (Chen et al, 2009).

In sum, the above discussion suggests that high competitive intensity would provide a facilitating condition under which CEOs would be more prone to adopt innovative and risky strategies (e.g., IT-based environmental strategies) to better satisfy their customers. Logically, this facilitating condition would dilute the aforementioned negative influence of fixed and bonus pay on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies and bolster the aforementioned positive influence of stock option pay on the same execution. This leads to:

H4: Competitive intensity moderates the aforementioned direct effects of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies. Specifically, it weakens the negative direct influence of fixed pay (H4a-H4b) and bonus pay (H4c-H4d) on the execution of green IT strategies and IT-enabled green strategies, but strengthens the positive direct influence of stock option pay (H4e-H4f) on the execution of these two strategies.

## Research methodology

## Data and sampling

The target population of this study comprised manufacturing firms listed in the US stock markets. As one can easily comprehend how the production of physical goods consumes scarce resources and generates undesired byproducts, (Lucas & Wilson, 2008), manufacturing firms have long been subject to much greater social and regulatory pressures to act green compared with their service counterparts (Salzman, 2000). These pressures would prompt managers of manufacturing firms to be more sensitive to seek ways to improve corporate environmental performance. Hence, the chosen target population helps further enhance the practical relevance of this study.

The study combined two sources of data to test hypotheses, survey and archival data. The collection of survey data was aimed at measuring firms’ IT-based environmental strategies and the level of competitive intensity they faced. The survey was conducted by randomly selecting 1,000 manufacturing firms from a sampling frame compiled from the COMPUSTAT EXE-COMP and COMPUSTAT (North America) databases. All the selected firms needed to satisfy the following two criteria in order to ensure continuity of their corporate practices: (1) there was no CEO turnover during the period of investigation; and (2) the CEO had been serving in the chosen firm for at least two consecutive years (Banker et al, 2013). In the survey, the interviewer also confirmed with the responding CEO that there was no change in his/her firm’s IT-based environmental strategies during the period of observation.

Although CEOs and Chief Information Officers (CIOs) may view some IT issues differently (Moynihan, 1990), they generally agree on IT’s current role in and contribution to their organizations (Johnson & Lederer, 2007, 2010). Prior IS studies have also indicated that top management guidance is essential for creating an IT culture conducive for successful IT implementation (King & Teo, 1996; Tai & Phelps, 2000) and many CEOs are knowledgeable about and experienced in the corporate use of IT (Schein, 1994). Based on this literature and given that the present survey is aimed at soliciting views on the general situation regarding the extent of executing IT-based environmental strategies rather than sophisticated technical issues concerning such execution (see ‘‘Appendix’’), CEOs should be able to provide the valid answers.

To further determine whether responding CEOs were appropriate informants for their firms’ IT-driven environmental initiatives, this survey included two questions to represent a formative construct labeled ‘‘top management support to environmental activities’’ (TMSEA). The two seven-point (1 = ‘‘small extent’’ to 7 = ‘‘great extent’’) questions, respectively, measured their knowledge about and involvement in such initiatives and were worded as follows: ‘‘To what extent are you aware of the development of IT-based environmental strategies in your firm?’’ and ‘‘To what extent are you involved in the development of IT-based environmental strategies in your firm?’’ The composite score derived from the two questions was 5.321 $( \bar { X } _ { \mathrm { k n o w l e d g e } } = 5 . 5 3 2 $ , SD = 1.148; $\bar { \bar { X } } _ { \mathrm { i n v o l v e m e n t } } = 5 . 1 1 0 , \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 9 3 \bar { 3 } )$ . The relatively high score suggested that these CEOs had a reasonable level of knowledge about and involvement in their firms’ development of IT-based environmental strategies, thus further justifying their serving as informants of this survey.

The survey was conducted between October and December 2013. Survey measures were first developed based on a thorough literature review (see below). To ensure their content validity, these measures were reviewed and fine-tuned by three academics knowledgeable about the topic under investigation. They were then further pilot-tested with 32 senior executives attending an international conference. With the assistance of a USbased research agency, a survey packet containing the finalized questionnaire (‘‘Appendix’’), a cover letter and a self-addressed stamped envelope was mailed to the CEO of each of the selected firms. The letter informed all contacted CEOs of the purposes of this study and assured them of the confidentiality of responses.

After around three months of telephone and/or emai follow-up efforts, 272 replies were received. Nine replies were discarded, as their percentages of missing values exceeded Ghoshal and Nohria’s (1989) threshold of 15%. Of the remaining 263 replies, 10 had incidental missing values for two questions or less. These missing values were estimated using the expectation–maximization method. This method preserves the underlying relationships in the data without reducing sample variability (Noorderhaven & Harzing, 2009), and as such, it is generally considered to be superior to other competing methods such as listwise or pairwise deletion, mean substitution, or imputation by multiple regression (Fichman & Cummings, 2003).

The remaining 263 replies were used for subsequent data analysis and constituted an effective response rate of 26.3% (i.e., 263/1000). This rate compared well with that attained in some prior IS (e.g., Quaddus & Hofmeyer’s (2007) 14%) and environmental management (e.g., Chan’s (2010) 24%) research on senior executives. Post hoc statistical checking by two-tailed t test or Chi square test also revealed that asset size, industry type, and return on assets of the responding firms did not differ significantly from those of the target population at $p < 0 . 0 5$ . To further assess non-response bias, Armstrong and Overton’s (1977) approach, which involved comparing responses between the first 25% respondents with the last 25% respondents, was also applied. The comparison did not detect any significant difference in responses between the two responding groups at $\begin{array} { r } { p < 0 . 0 5 , } \end{array}$ , thus suggesting little threat of non-response bias.

## Measures for variables

The dependent (green IT strategies and IT-enabled green strategies) and moderating variables (competitive intensity) were operationalized by survey measures, whereas the corresponding archival data were extracted from the aforementioned two databases to capture the independent variables (CEO compensation) and various control variables for background information of the responding CEOs or firms. All measures are detailed below.

Dependent variables The items used to measure the two IT-based environmental strategies, green IT strategies (GITS) and IT-enabled green strategies (ITEGS), were adapted from the instrument of Molla and Abareshi (2012). This instrument was, in turn, developed based on prior research (e.g., Chen et al, 2009) and comprised six items to measure green IT strategies and four items to measure IT-enabled green strategies. The items were coded on a seven-point scale anchored with 1 = ‘‘small extent’’ to 7 = ‘‘great extent’’ (Table 2). With reference to Jarvis et al’s (2003) four criteria, namely the direction of causality between the construct and its indicators, the interchangeability of the indicators, the covariation among the indicators and the nomological net of the indicators, we classified the two constructs as formative. In brief, the classification was based on content analysis of the indicators, which showed that they define rather than manifest their hypothesized construct (Petter et al, 2007).

Moderating variable Competitive intensity (COMP) was measured by adapting the measures of Murray et al (2011). The finalized instrument comprised five items, which were coded on a seven-point scale anchored with 1 = ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to 7 = ‘‘strongly agree’’ (Table 2). The construct was treated as reflective based on the foregoing four criteria.

Compensation variables Three CEO compensationrelated variables were included as independent variables. The three variables, respectively, concerned the percentages of CEO fixed pay (FIXED), bonuses (BONUS), and stock options (OPTION), as compared to the corresponding total CEO compensation. Black and Scholes’ (1973) method was employed to compute the amount of CEO stock option pay. Post hoc checking of the dataset did not detect any significant change in the CEO compensation structure of all the investigated firms during the period of observation. Moreover, as the impact of different CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies, if any, is likely to take place some time after the compensation was granted, the first-lagged compensation data (i.e., the 2012 data) were used (e.g., Balkin et al, 2000).

Controls Given that male (vs. female) executives, younger (vs. older) executives, or executives with shorter (vs. longer) tenure are more inclined to pursue riskier strategies (Barker & Mueller, 2002; Hambrick & Mason, 1984), a CEO’s gender (GENDER; 1 = male and 0 = female), age (AGE), and years of service in the present position (TENURE) were included as control variables. Moreover, as prior research showed that corporate involvement in environmental activities may be affected by the industry to which a firm belongs and its size (Banerjee et al, 2003), this study employed six industry-related dummies (based on the two-digit SIC classification) and the natural logarithm of total assets (first-lagged) to control for the effect of industry type (INDUSTRY) and firm size (SIZE), respectively. (The natural logarithm of total assets was replaced by that of total employees in the preliminary analysis, but the replacement did not change any conclusion of hypothesis testing.) Since a firm’s prior performance may have a significant impact on its risk-taking behavior (Larraza-Kintana et al, 2007), the first-lagged return on assets (ROA) was included for controlling purposes. As mentioned, the composite score representing top management support to environmental activities (TMSEA) was also included to further mitigate the concern about using CEOs as informants.

## Post hoc analyses

Several post hoc analyses were conducted to triangulate the aforementioned results, the details of which are presented below.

Using 2013 archival data The 2013 archival data were used to replace the 2012 data to re-run the analysis. The results are depicted in Figure 3. Overall, the results were similar to those derived from the 2012 data with the conclusion for all the hypothesis testing results remained unchanged. This post hoc analysis provides additional support for the robustness of the findings.

Actual time for executing IT-based environmental strategies Owing to the possible time-lag effect of CEO compensation granted in 2012, the survey on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies was conducted in 2013. Despite this approach, it might still be difficult to fully ascertain that the responding CEOs really referred to those green strategic initiatives undertaken in 2013 but not earlier. Moreover, it is possible that some initiatives were, indeed, not initiated by the responding CEOs but by their predecessors.

In the present survey, the responding CEOs were specifically requested to refer to their firms’ IT-based environmental strategic practices in the current year (‘‘Appendix’’). This approach could provide a unified frame of reference for respondents to give views on practices in which they were more immediately involved before the survey. To further triangulate the present findings, all responding CEOs were contacted again between February and March 2015 via email/telephone to solicit their views on an additional question: ‘‘As compared with the industry average, how would you describe your firm’s investment in IT-based environmental initiatives in 2013?’’ The question was coded on a seven-point scale anchored with 1 = ‘‘little investment’ to 7 = ‘‘huge investment.’

This follow-up investigation resulted in 97 valid replies (response rate = 36.9%). The replies constituted the dependent variable for re-running the PLS-based analysis. To summarize, this structural model explained 26.5% of the variance in IT-based environmental initiatives. It also revealed that CEO fixed pay (b = -0.198, p \ 0.05), bonus pay $( \beta = - 0 . 1 9 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 ) _ { i }$ , and stock option pay (b = 0.265, p \ 0.05) remained to exert a direct effect on the corporate investment in IT-based environmental initiatives as expected. The analysis further showed that, among the three CEO compensation forms, stock option pay’s direct effect was moderated by competitive intensity (b = 0.170, $\textstyle p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). The insignificant moderating effect of competitive intensity on other relationships might be attributed to the small sample size. While far from fully rigorous, this follow-up study suggested reasonable robustness of the findings from the main survey.

With regard to the possibility that some IT-related environmental measures might have been initiated by the previous rather than the responding CEOs, it is believed that the use of CEO tenure (TENURE) as a control variable throughout the analysis should have mitigated this concern. Given that most IT-based environmental projects would be completed within eight years (Unhelkar, 2011) and the responding CEOs had an average tenure of 8.132 years, it is likely that most of them actually referred to their self-initiated environmental measures when completing the main survey. Besides, it should be noted that the employed sampling frame, as mentioned, ensures that all responding CEOs had been serving in the chosen firms for at least two consecutive years during the survey period.

Lastly, post hoc t test was also conducted to examine if long-tenured and short-tenured responding CEOs (as categorized by median split) significantly differed in their survey responses toward the strategy items. The test did not reveal any significant difference between the two groups at $p < 0 . 0 5$ . These findings further suggested that the responses toward IT-based environmental strategies did not differ between those CEOs who were more susceptible to environmental practices initiated by their predecessors and those who were not.

The role of CEO contract horizon It is possible that a CEO employed under a contract lasting for a shorter period will be more reluctant to execute IT-based environmental strategies, and vice versa. This implies that a CEO’s contract horizon may also moderate the influence of CEO compensation form on the execution of these strategies. Owing to data unavailability, a thorough analysis of this issue is prohibited here. However, as CEOs with longer tenure (i.e., longer time serving in the current position) are likely to have longer contract horizon (i.e., longer duration of their current contracts) due to their stronger bargaining power with their employers (Xu, 2011), CEO tenure would serve as a surrogate of CEO contract horizon. ## Discussion

## Summary of findings

Based on the analysis of the survey and archival data collected from 263 US manufacturing firms, this study has discovered several intriguing findings regarding the impact of CEO compensation form on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies and how competitive intensity further moderates this impact. Although these findings, as mentioned, are practically more significant to manufacturing firms, the wide application of green IT and IT-enabled green strategies in today’s business operations suggests that they would also serve as a useful reference for service firms to promote the use of IT to achieve corporate sustainability.

Overall, these findings suggest that CEOs will be demotivated to pursue both green IT and IT-enabled green strategies if most of their compensation comes from fixed pay. Although bonus pay is contingent upon CEOs’ performance, it also exerts a demotivational effect on CEOs’ pursuit of the two IT-based environmental strategies. It is believed that this demotivational effect is attributed to the short time horizon of bonus pay. In contrast, the findings suggest that the long-term oriented stock option pay serves as a strong driver for this pursuit.

The analysis of the moderating effect of competitive intensity provides further insights into the boundary conditions that may strengthen or weaken the aforementioned relationships between CEO compensation form and the execution of the two IT-based environmental strategies. Relating specifically to IT-enabled green strategies, the findings suggest that a highly competitive operating setting will weaken the negative impact of fixed pay and bonus pay on the execution of these strategies, whereas it will strengthen the positive impact of stock option pay on the same execution. In other words, competitive intensity positively moderates the relationship between these three compensation forms and the execution of IT-enabled green strategies. With regard to green IT strategies, it is found that competitive intensity only strengthens the positive impact of stock option pay on the execution of these strategies $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ only $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { e } }$ rather than $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { a } }$ or $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } }$ is supported). These findings suggest that a highly competitive operating setting represents an ideal setting for using stock options to motivate CEOs to undertake both types of IT-based environmental strategies. At a minimum, this operating setting helps mitigate the deterrent effect of fixed pay and bonus pay on CEOs execution of IT-enabled green strategies.

Considered together, the findings from the moderation analysis suggest that, when compared with the impact of CEO compensation form on IT-enabled green strategies, green IT strategies are less susceptible to the moderating influence of competitive intensity. According to the environmental marketing literature, a highly competitive business environment is particularly conducive to the internal process underlying the adoption of innovative and/or proactive environmental strategies (Chan et al, 2012). When compared with green IT strategies that focus on minimizing IT-associated negative environmental impacts, IT-enabled green strategies are conceived as more proactive as they involve seeking innovative IT solutions to maximize environmental benefits for firms and society at large. This higher ‘‘environment–strategy coalignment’’ (Venkatraman & Prescott, 1990) helps explain why competitive intensity plays a more salient facilitative role for the execution of IT-enabled green strategies (vs. green IT strategies). Moreover, it should be noted that the execution of IT-enabled green strategies involves more interfirm collaboration and interactions with external stakeholders compared with that of green IT strategies. This may also explain why this execution is more susceptible to changes of the business environment including competitive intensity. Future investigation is encouraged to further verify all these plausible explanations.

## Limitations

Despite its contributions, this research has some limitations. First, as firms adopting different IT governance mechanisms may vary in their CEOs’ effectiveness in executing IT-based environmental strategies, the lack of control for this in analysis due to data unavailability constitutes a limitation. Second, although the post hoc analysis using CEO tenure as a surrogate for CEO contract horizon helps lessen the concern about the latter’s possible moderating effect on the compensation–strategy relationship under study, the lack of the relevant data to conduct more rigorous analysis still constitutes a limitation. In future studies, researchers are encouraged to compile the relevant data to address this issue more thoroughly. Third, the present study examines the respective influence of the three CEO compensation forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies. Although this examination already covers various important aspects of how different compensation forms could affect corporate strategic decision (e.g., short vs. long orientation, risk-taking vs. risk aversion), their combined influence on such execution remains unexplored due to space limitation. Future study is thus encouraged to examine this potentially interesting and practically significant issue.

Fourth, owing to data unavailability, it is infeasible to identify the exact time points for the determination of CEO compensation and the adoption of IT-based environmental strategies in this study. This raises the concern that the compensation might be determined after the strategy adoption. In the present survey, responding CEOs were specifically asked to refer to the IT-based environmental strategies implemented ‘‘in the current year’’ when answering. Given that CEOs are likely to adjust their current strategies based on how they were compensated in the previous year, the use of lagged CEO compensation data for analysis would help mitigate the above concern. Despite this, it is acknowledged that the lack of information about the exact time points still constitutes a limitation and warrants further study.

Fifth, while the combination of both archival and survey data in this study paves a new path to examine the relationship between CEO compensation and IT-based environmental strategic pursuit, some important psychological and firm-specific factors that may affect this pursuit have still not been investigated. These may include how CEOs actually perceive risks and respond in different firm-specific corporate governance contexts. To verify and supplement the present findings, researchers should consider employing this combined approach to further examine firm-level contextual factors that may further influence the relationship between CEO compensation and IT-based environmental strategic pursuit. When examined more closely, the last two limitations suggest the lack of checking for potential endogeneity problems in this study. In view of the inability of PLS software packages to test endogeneity, this lack is commonly found in IS empirical research using PLS (Benitez-Amado et al, 2016). With IS researchers’ increasing efforts, it is hoped that the endogeneity issue will be addressed more adequately in the future.

## Research implications

This study has several important research implications. First, although environmental management researchers have long advocated the importance of top management commitment to promote corporate environmental practices (Banerjee et al, 2003), the relevance of compensation, a crucial determinant of this commitment to environmental strategies, has only been investigated in a limited way (e.g., Berrone & Gomez-Mejia, 2009). Drawing on agency theory, this research is among the first to examine how different CEO compensation forms affect the execution of the two types of IT-based environmental strategies, green IT strategies and ITenabled green strategies. This examination helps bridge an important gap in the environmental management and IS literature and offers a new perspective to investigate the pressing issue of how to motivate senior executives to undertake IT-related environmental measures. The contribution of this research would become even more apparent when considering the fact that prior compensation research has mainly focused on how compensation could affect corporate performance rather than strategic practice (Musteen et al, 2009).

Second, to achieve the foregoing research objective, this study extends Benitez and Walczuch’s (2012) view on the enabling role of IT for implementing corporate environmental strategies by proposing two distinct types of IT-based environmental strategies. This proposed classification has been supported by the subsequent empirical validation process. These results, in turn, provide IS researchers with useful conceptual guidance and the relevant validated measures to undertake research on the antecedents and/or consequences of ITbased environmental strategies in the future.

Third, while agency theorists have long believed that fixed pay serves as a deterrence for risky strategic pursuit, prior compensation research has seldom examined this belief empirically. The present study thus contributes to fill this gap by verifying a major proposition of agency theory in the context of IT-based environmental strategic pursuit. Similarly, although it is generally believed that variable pay can motivate CEOs to pursue risky strategies, the difference in time horizon between various variable pay forms has only received limited attention in prior compensation research. By demonstrating that variable pay forms with different time horizon significantly vary in their impact on IT-based environmental strategic pursuit, this study helps extend the extant compensation literature. The present study is also among the first to apply agency theory to investigate firms’ execution of ITbased environmental strategies. This further enriches the compensation literature, which has mainly focused on employing agency theory to examine the effect of CEO compensation on the pursuit of non-environmentally related strategies (e.g., Balkin et al, 2000). For IS and environmental management researchers, this study also offers a new perspective to investigate IT-related environmental strategies in the future.

Fourth, the analysis of the moderating effect of competitive intensity further advances understanding of the role of the business environment for the pursuit of IT-based environmental strategies. This understanding, in turn, provides empirical evidence to validate a major tenet of the contingent natural resource-based theory, namely the firm’s internal process underlying pro-environmental strategic practices would be facilitated or inhibited upon some changes in the business environment (Aragon-Correa & Sharma, 2003). With a few exceptions (e.g., Chen et al, 2014, 2015, Mithas et al, 2013; Xue & Ray, 2011), IS researchers have only paid sparse attention to the effect of the business environment on IT-related organizational practices. The present analysis thus further enriches the extant IS literature. It also highlights the usefulness of this theory to delineate the boundary conditions that boost or deter the influence of different CEO compensation forms on IT-based environmental pursuit.

Lastly and alongside the second implication mentioned above, this study, by conceptualizing IT-based environmental strategies as two distinct types of strategies, sheds further light on the different organizational complexities and risks involved in pursuing these two strategies. The foregoing moderation analysis effectively reflects such a difference. Specifically, the analysis reveals that, while competitive intensity moderates the impact of all the three compensation forms on the pursuit of IT-enabled green strategies, it only moderates the impact of stock option pay (but not fixed and bonus pay) on the pursuit of green IT strategies. These findings remind IS researchers of the importance to take into account the inherent difference of the two IT-based environmental strategies when conducting research in this area.

## Managerial implications

This research also generates some useful managerial implications. First, the findings reveal that both fixed pay and bonus pay will deter rather than motivate CEOs to execute IT-based environmental strategies. In view of these findings, boards of directors intending to achieve corporate sustainability should thus avoid relying on these pay forms to reward their CEOs. Second, the findings show that stock option pay constitutes a powerful tool to motivate CEOs to achieve longer-term performance targets such as IT-based environmental strategic pursuit, and its motivational power will become even stronger under a highly competitive business environment.

As noted, firms today are increasingly interested in employing IT to create business values and enhance financial performance (Bernroider 2013; Chen et al, 2015; Hanseth & Bygstad, 2015; Schryen, 2013). ## Concluding remarks

This study has empirically examined the impact of various CEO pay forms on the execution of IT-based environmental strategies and the moderating influence of the competitive intensity on this impact. Overall, the empirical findings reveal that CEO fixed pay and bonus pay exert a negative impact on the execution of the two types of IT-based environmental strategies, whereas CEO stock option pay exerts a positive impact on the same execution. The moderation analysis of competitive intensity further highlights that this contextual factor exerts various moderating effects on the aforementioned relationships. Specifically, while competitive intensity is found to reinforce the positive impact of CEO stock option pay on both green IT strategies and IT-enabled green strategies, it weakens the negative influence of CEO fixed pay and bonus pay on only IT-enabled green strategies. All in all, the findings highlight the superiority of using stock options to motivate CEOs to engage in ITrelated environmental pursuit, especially under a highly competitive operating setting. They also suggest that firms align their CEO remuneration package with the characteristics of high uncertainty and long payback periods of IT-based environmental strategic endeavors and the competitive conditions they face.
