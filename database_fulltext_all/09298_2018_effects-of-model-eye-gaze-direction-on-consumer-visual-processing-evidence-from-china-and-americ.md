## Abstract

The question of how to design attractive model in online advertising is especially important for online operators of electronic commerce websites. This study examines how a model’s eye gaze direction influences consumers’ visual processing, taking into consideration smiling facial expressions for both Chinese and American consumers. Drawing on shared signal theory and cultural differences, a set of eye-tracking experiments was performed. The results show that consumer arousal is much higher when the model has a smiling facial expression paired with a direct eye gaze, compared to an averted eye gaze. This effect is much stronger for Chinese consumers than American consumers. However, for cognitive information processing, we found that consumers process product information on a deeper level when the model has a smiling facial expression paired with an averted eye gaze, compared to a direct eye gaze. Although there is a significant difference between Chinese and American consumers in processing the product brand, no significant differences were identified in their processing of the text. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

Keywords: Visual processing; eye gaze direction; smiling facial expression; Chinese consumers; American consumers.

## 1 Introduction

Eyes are the windows to the soul. They are considered to be one of the most important organs in people’s daily communication [55]. There is no exception in advertising, where researchers consistently agree that the design of a model’s eyes is important for communicating with their consumers [9, 28, 40]. This becomes especially significant with the rapid development of electronic business because, compared to traditional advertising, the design of a piece of attractive online advertising could directly lead to the final transaction. In this regard, the literature suggests that there are two prominent eye gaze direction in advertising designs: namely, the direct gaze and the averted gaze [27, 52]. Direct gaze means that a model’s eyes towards the product, but not consumers.

In order to understand which type of gaze direction is more attractive to consumers, we performed a website analysis. Specifically, we examined more than 200 product web pages across eBay, Gmarket, Macy’s, Amazon, Tmall, JingDong, and JUMEI<sup>1</sup>, which cover two large product categories: beauty products and women’s apparel. The content analysis shows that some of them (e.g., Tmall, JingDong, and JUMEI) use averted gaze frequently, while others reveal the reverse. Thus, there appear to be large differences in the practice of using the facial cues of models on websites, and no consensus on which is the most effective.

Our careful review of the extant literature also suggests inconclusive findings. For instance, Ewing et al. [25] demonstrated that direct gaze is more attractive, while Mason et al. [52] found that the effect of direct gaze is not significant. Moreover, researchers like Schilbach et al.[69] have suggested that advertising is more attractive when the model’s gaze direction is averted. This practically and theoretically inconsistent evidence prompted us to seek a deeper understanding of this issue, in order to better answer the question: Which type of model eye gaze direction is more attractive to consumers?

Although IS and marketing researchers have mainly focused on the effects of models’ eye gaze direction in isolation, researchers in broader disciplines, such as psychology [e.g., 2, 58] and communication [e.g., 60, 76], have implied that the effect of gaze direction on viewer response is contingent on contextual cues. In particular, researchers such as Ozono et al. [58] have suggested that facial expression is the most important social cue that can provide additional signals for viewers to explain the eye gaze. That is, the same eye gaze would be differently interpreted by a viewer when it is embedded into faces with different degrees of smiling. Accordingly, in advertising, it is imperative to explore how a model’s eye gaze influences consumers’ visual processing while considering a smiling facial expression.

We focus on taking a smiling facial expression as the background contextual cue in this study, not only because the smile is the most common and universal human facial expression that could influence consumer interpretations of models’ eye gaze [22], but also because the study of this aspect is timely. In particular, a model’s smile, as a key facial expression, has been considered to be an effective way to transfer positive affect to consumers [4, 83]. However, it is also suggested that consumers in different cultures have varying interpretations of smiles. In particular, prior research has demonstrated significant differences in the display [53, 62] and perception [5, 59] of smiling facial expressions between Western and East Asian cultures. In the current era, in which cross-border electronic commerce is developing rapidly, the investigation of cultural differences in E-commerce is especially significant. Evidence is available from both practical<sup>2</sup> and theoretical<sup>3</sup> sources. Taking China and America as sample countries, this study aims to answer the questions: Does a smiling model’s eye gaze direction lead to different consumer responses in China and America? If so, how?

Drawing on shared signal theory [2, 56], which states that the consistency of signals delivered by gaze behavior and facial expression matters to consumers in interpreting models facial expressions in advertising, we propose that consumers’ visual processing could be significantly impacted by the culturally driven interpretations of smiling facial expressions and eye gaze direction. In order to validate this proposition, we conducted a set of experiments using eye-tracking technology, to examine visual information processing from both cognitive (depth of information processing) and affective (consumer arousal) dimensions. Specifically, we designed images of models’ faces with different gaze directions and performed experiments in both China and America. The experiments revealed some interesting findings. This study not only theoretically extends the current knowledge, but also provides practical guidelines to systems designers and marketing managers.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature. Then, we propose theoretical hypotheses in Section 3. In Section 4, the details of two eyeimplications and future research directions.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Eye Gaze Direction

Eye gaze direction plays an important role in face processing and social communication [35]. It is considered to be one of the most important facial cues in communicating with consumers [e.g., 67, 80]. Eye gaze direction refers to the direction in which the eyes look. Two typical types of gaze direction are the direct gaze, which is conceptualized as a model looking at viewers directly, and the averted gaze, which refers to a model who does not look at viewers directly, but looks in a different direction [27]. In regard to the advertising research context, in this study, the averted gaze is conceptualized as a model looking towards the focal product in advertising.

Eye gaze direction is considered to be associated with different types of viewer outcomes, such as visual attention and emotions. In particular, in regard to the effect on attention, psychology researchers have pointed out that eye gaze direction plays a critical role in influencing people’s information processing. For instance, Senju et al. [70] demonstrate that, compared to an averted eye gaze direction, a direct eye gaze delays the detection of a peripheral

target when viewers are required to fixate on a central face. Thus, it appears that a direct eye

gaze captures and retains attention to the face [8]. An averted gaze, on the other hand, has the

ability to shift a viewer’s attention and lead to a faster classification of targets in the direction

of the perceived gaze [26, 43, 49]. The effect of eye gaze direction has also attracted the interest

of marketing researchers. For instance, Hutton and Nolte [37] investigated a model’s multiple

directional cues (i.e., eye, head, and body) in a print advertisement and found that participants

looked longer at the product and brand regions of the advertisement when the model’s gaze was

directed towards the product. Sajjacholapunt and Ball [67] demonstrated that the inclusion of

human faces with averted gaze in banner advertisements increases the attention paid to such

ads, thereby enhancing respondents’ memory of the advertised information.

Eye gaze direction is also considered to play an important role in influencing viewers

perception of emotions and of the person viewed [5, 25]. On the one hand, faces displaying a

direct gaze increase an observer’s arousal more than faces displaying an averted gaze, which

has been shown in psychology studies using, for example, skin conductance responses [31],

heart rate [45], and subjective ratings of arousal [5]. On the other hand, it has been argued that

gaze direction can act as a signal of attraction between people. While some researchers, such

as Ewing et al. [25] and Akechi et al. [5], suggest that humans tend to rate a person who makes

eye contact as more likable, pleasant, and attractive, compared to a person exhibiting an averted

gaze, others, such as Mason et al. [52], found no such effect. These results regarding the effect

of gaze direction on the viewer’s emotional experience are ambiguous.

Previous research in psychology [e.g., 2, 58] and communication [e.g., 60, 76] has implied that the effect of gaze direction on viewer responses is contingent on other social cues in interactive contexts, such as facial expression [75]. In the present research, we focus on smiling expressions, because they are by far the most frequently used expressions on e-commerce websites. Although some marketing research has uncovered a series of interesting findings regarding the effects of eye gaze direction [e.g., 9, 73], they focus on uncovering the isolated effect of these aspects, ignoring the impact of contextual cues such as smiling facial expressions. Thus, the exploration of the effects caused by gaze direction on both attention and emotions, while considering the effects of a smiling facial expression, is imperative. Before we theorize our predictions regarding these effects, we discuss how a smiling facial expression is interpreted by viewers in different countries.

## 2.2 Smile and Culture

The smile is one of the most common and universal human facial expressions [22]. In example, in the work of Howard and Gengler [34], researchers have discovered that receivers can mimic the smiles of the senders that they favor; this mimicking stimulates positive emotions and then creates a positive bias in their evaluation of related products. In a later work, Cho and Schwarz [13] found that the preference for one’s own smiling face on a website on which the user can upload their picture can cause a strong preference for the product displayed on that site. Furthermore, in a recent work, Kulczynski et al. [47] demonstrated that, when an endorser is pictured in an advertisement with a smiling facial expression, consumers tend to report pleasant feelings; this positive emotional response mediates the relationship between the source

#

expressive display and the attitude toward the advertisement, brand, and purchase intention.

Although the smile is thought to be a simple and easily recognizable facial expression, researchers have also pointed out sources of potential confusion in regard to smiles [23]. Specifically, the same smile can be differently interpreted by viewers from different countries. The main differences between these, according to previous research, are the amplitude of zygomatic movement [21]. In other words, an open-mouth smile with the lip corners extended and cheeks raised is considered to be big, while a closed-mouth smile with the lip corners a little extended is considered to be slight [29, 62]. Prior research has demonstrated the differences in consumer perceptions of such types of smiles. For example, Pogosyan and Engelmann [62] suggested that Americans perceive a big smile as significantly less calm, while Japanese people perceive a slight smile as significantly more excited. This is also supported by studies such as Matsumoto et al [53] and Ozono et al. [59], in which researchers consistently reveal such cross-cultural variability in perception due to cultural display rules. In particular, Westerners tend to favor the overt expression of emotions, whereas Asian people encourage the de-amplification and masking of emotional states [54, 86].

Considering the differences in interpreting smiles across varying countries, in this study, we consider two representative countries in large electronic commerce markets, namely, China and America. In a variety of reports<sup>4</sup>, China and America are stated to be the two largest markets in the world. Therefore, it is imperative to understand how consumers in these two countries differ in this specific respect. The content analysis mentioned in the introduction of this paper further shows that a slight smile is common in online advertising, which leads us to take it as the main focus of this work. To this end, this study seeks to uncover the effect of eye gaze direction on people’s visual processing, while considering cultural differences in the perception of a slight smile. In order to understand why and how this works, we draw on shared signal theory.

## 2.3 Shared Signal Theory

The central thesis of shared signal theory is that both eye gaze and facial expression are signals associated with the underlying behavioral motivations to approach or avoid [2, 3]. As a signaling system, facial expressions regarding emotion and gaze behavior are integrated to transfer signals of basic behavioral tendencies. The congruence in signal value (i.e., the common approach-avoidance behavioral tendency communicated) between gaze direction and facial expression will enhance the perception of the underlying emotion [3].

Different frameworks have been used to support hypotheses derived from shared signal theory. For instance, using a speed reaction time paradigm, Adams and Kleck [2] demonstrated that approach-oriented emotions (e.g., happiness) are better recognized with a direct gaze, and avoidance-oriented emotions (e.g., unhappiness) are better recognized with an averted gaze. Using a different tool, similar findings are uncovered. In particular, by using the self-reported perception of emotional intensity, Adams et al. [3] further found that a direct gaze enhanced the perceived intensity of approach-oriented emotions, whereas an averted gaze enhanced the perceived intensity of avoidance-oriented emotions. This is also reinforced by a

#

recent work [65], in which researchers suggested that both adults and infants discriminate between approach- and avoidance-oriented emotions, by matching them with direct and averted gazes, respectively.

Other research provides additional evidence showing that gaze direction and facial

expression share approach-avoidance signal values in the cognitive processing of stimuli. For

example, Jones et al. [41] and Conway et al. [14] have consistently found that faces with a

direct gaze are perceived as more attractive when smiling than when they have a neutral

expression, whereas faces with an averted gaze are less attractive when smiling than holding a

neutral expression. Bayliss et al. [7] reported that objects that were gazed at with a happy

expression were liked more than objects that were gazed at with a disgusted expression. This is

further supported by Soussignan et al. [75], who found that the emotion displayed by avatars

affected participants’ reward judgments; avatars looking at food with joyful faces, as compared

to disgusted faces, slightly increased how much participants liked the food.

Although these studies reveal the power of shared signal theory in explaining the

interpretation of signals transferred by facial expression and eye gaze direction, most previous

studies in psychology and neuroscience were not conducted in naturalistic settings; models

faces were shown in isolation and participants were under forced-exposure conditions in which

they were asked to pay attention to these faces [48, 85]. It remains unclear, therefore, whether

the effects that were demonstrated in these studies will also emerge in real-life (commercial)

settings, such as those provided by the frequently encountered context of models on websites.

As suggested by previous researchers [e.g., 12, 44], in different contextual settings, a model

will be differently perceived by consumers. Therefore, it is of interest to apply shared signal theory in regard to understanding how consumers interpret a model’s eye gaze direction on a smiling face in an e-commerce context, because such websites are expected to shape visual processing differently [11].

## 3 Hypotheses Development

In this study, according to shared signal theory, we theorize how eye gaze direction influences consumers’ visual processing while accounting for different perceptions of a smiling expression by consumers from different countries. In line with previous studies [e.g., 81], we consider both the affective and cognitive dimensions of this issue, in order to understand visual processing more comprehensively. In particular, with respect to the affective dimension, we consider consumers’ arousal, while, in the cognitive dimension, we focus on the depth of consumer information processing.

Emotions comprise two main components: valence and arousal [24, 51, 66]. Valence refers to the direction of behavioral activation and the degree of positive or negative emotion regarding a stimulus [71]. Arousal refers to the intensity of the emotion [1, 66]. Valence varies on a continuum from negative to positive, and arousal varies on a continuum from calm to excited [19, 66]. In this research, we only focus on the arousal of emotion since, in the ecommerce context, negative emotions are not often evoked, and e-commerce websites do not generally contain elements that may cause negative emotions. An individual’s arousal can be assessed with subjective psychometric scales [1], but also with objective physiological measurements, such as pupil dilation [10].

#

According to shared signal theory [2], an emotion will be perceived as more intense, leading to higher levels of arousal, when gaze and facial expression share a consistent signal value, compared to when their signal value is inconsistent. In particular, as mentioned above, a smiling expression is categorized as “approach-oriented” [75]. A direct gaze, compared to an averted gaze, is considered to be “approach-oriented”, rather than “avoidance-oriented” [2]. This leads us to conjecture that the arousal of a consumer would be stronger for a model with a direct eye gaze and with a smiling face, compared to an averted gaze.

Although the smile is considered “approach-oriented”, consistent with the direct eye gaze, it is not understood whether or not the effect would be the same for Chinese and American consumers. As mentioned above, a slight smile, defined as a closed-mouth smile with the lip corners a little bit extended [62, 82], is more likely to be favored by Chinese consumers, compared to American ones. In contrast to this, a big smile is more likely to be favored by American consumers, compared with Chinese ones [59, 62]. This leads us to theorize that, although there is consistency between a smile and a direct eye gaze, the effect of this may vary in strength for consumers in different countries. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1a: Consumer arousal is much stronger when a model displays a smiling expression and a direct gaze, compared to an averted gaze;

H1b: The effect is stronger for Chinese consumers, compared to American consumers.

Depth of processing varies along a continuum. Shallow processing refers to processing information only in terms of its perceptual features. Deep processing refers to fully analyzing information in terms of its meaning and importance, and leads to a more elaborate mental representation [15, 16]. In advertising, deep processing is desirable from the point of view of advertising investors, not only because deep processing could lead to better memory accessibility [e.g., 6, 74], but could also lead to cognitive advantages, such as increased recognition or comprehension [e.g., 36, 39]. Both gaze direction and facial expression indicate whether or not nearby objects are liked [75]. Bayliss et al. [7] reported that objects gazed at by a model with a smiling expression are liked more by the viewer than objects gazed at with a disgusted expression, but objects that are not gazed at are liked equally for both expressions. According to shared signal theory [2], a product will be liked more when gaze and facial expression share a consistent signal value with respect to the product (averted gaze, looking at the object, and a smiling expression), than when their signal value is inconsistent (direct gaze and a smiling expression). Consequently, consumers observing these cues might like the product more, which will motivate them to process its information on a deeper level.

In addition to this, as mentioned above, people from China and America interpret facial expressions differently. This leads to different degrees of perception of a smile, even if the same smiling face is presented. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2a: Consumers process information more deeply when a model displays a smiling expression and an averted gaze, compared to a direct gaze;

H2b: The effect is stronger for Chinese consumers, compared to American consumers.

## 4 Research Methodology

We tested our predictions with two eye-tracking experiments. In Experiment 1, we

#

manipulated the gaze direction (direct/averted) and the facial expression (neutral/smile) of models on e-commerce websites that were viewed by American participants, in order to test whether or not a smiling expression would result in stronger positive responses, compared with the baseline where a model has a neutral expression [4, 83]. Later, in order to test differences between Chinese and American cultures, we replicated our experiment with Chinese respondents. In the experiments, eye gaze direction was manipulated via digital processing of the eyes in an image of the model’s face, while a smiling facial expression was manipulated via digital processing of the mouth in the image of the model’s face.

In these two experiments, we investigated the effects on pupil dilation as a measure of arousal; and on fixation durations as indicators of the depth of processing the product information. In line with previous studies, such as Holmqvist et al. [33] and Wang et al. [81], we used average pupil diameter to measure participants’ emotional arousal because researchers agree that, when the experimental task and ambient light conditions are kept constant, as was the case in the current research, pupil dilation can be used as a reliable indicator of the emotional arousal of participants. In regard to the depth of information processing, we focused on two specific regions of interest (ROIs) related to product information: the brand name and the text (product description) [61]. For each ROI, we computed the average fixation duration (in msec.) as a measure of the depth of information processing, with a longer duration indicating deeper levels of processing [33, 79].

## 4.1 Experiment 1

This experiment used a 2 × 2 between-subjects design, with two levels of facial expression (neutral versus a slight smile) and two levels of gaze direction (direct: looking at consumers versus averted: looking at the product). The experimental conditions were obtained through the digital manipulation of images of faces. This allowed us to control for other features of the face by only changing the specific facial cues of the eyes and mouth.

## 4.1.1 Stimuli

We obtained images from the internet of female Caucasian models shown from above the shoulder, looking straight ahead, with neutral facial expressions. Adobe Photoshop digital imaging software was used to manipulate the mouth and eyes in the original images, to produce smiling faces and faces with an averted gaze, respectively. We manipulated the models’ facial expressions using image processing software. This had the potential to make the smiling faces look unnatural, thereby impairing their attractiveness, especially when producing a big smile; thus, we manipulated the image to produce a slight smile. In a pretest, 20 participants were asked to rate the images on their attractiveness, naturalness, and perceived intensity of smile using a 7-point Likert-type scale. Based on the results, 16 images with high scores were chosen. In the main study, we measured the perceptions of the smile and gaze direction using a 7-point Likert-type scale. Manipulation checks among the 126 participants (see below) revealed a significant difference $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ in perceived smile between the images with a smiling face $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 4 . 1 1 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 7 8 )$ and a neutral face $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 3 . 4 4 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 9 3 )$ , and a significant difference $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ in perceived gaze direction between images with a direct gaze (mean = $5 . 6 6 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 3 9 )$ and those with an averted gaze $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 3 . 5 5 , \mathrm { S D } = 2 . 0 3 )$ ), which supports our manipulations.

To simulate an online US cosmetics store, we created 16 web pages for each treatment condition. This consisted of the manipulated images integrated with the pack-shot, brand name, textual product information, and other product-related information, such as prices and product ratings. The latter components were all identical across the conditions of the study. The web pages were designed using Microsoft Visual Studio 2008 to look like common web pages for two product categories (cosmetics and perfumes) that usually employ human faces on their websites. Similar to Hutton and Nolte [37], we used real brands in the experiment to approximate natural viewing behavior as closely as possible. In order to reduce interference caused by brand familiarity, in the pretest, we also asked participants to rate their familiarity with the brands and selected the 16 well-known international brands that were most familiar to participants.

## 4.1.2 Participants

We recruited 130 participants from undergraduate programs at a major US university. They participated in the experiment for course credit or were paid US\$10 for their attendance. Participants were screened to ensure normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Four participants could not be used because they failed to be calibrated in the eye-tracking test. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four treatment conditions. Among the 126 participants, 64 were male and 62 were female, and they had an average age of 21.0 years. A total of 93% of the participants had online shopping experience and 61% shopped online at least once or twice per month.

## 4.1.3 Experimental procedure

The stimuli were presented on a 19-inch monitor with a resolution of $1 0 2 4 \times 7 6 8$ pixels. Each participant sat individually in a cubicle in front of the monitor at a distance of approximately 60 cm. Eye movements were collected with a Tobii 1750 binocular infrared eye tracker at a box of around $3 0 \times 3 0 \times 3 0$ cm.

Participants were calibrated, then looked at a fixation cross on a blank page for one second (to get a baseline measurement of pupil diameter). They were then shown an introduction page explaining the task. They were instructed to freely view the web pages: “Imagine that you want to buy some gifts for a female friend and you are freely browsing an online gift store, which sells various kinds of cosmetics and perfumes. A series of product web pages will be presented. Please look through these pages at your own pace.” The web pages exactly occupied the entire screen; scrolling was neither needed nor allowed. Each participant viewed the 16 pages, including both product categories, in random order, for as long as they desired. After viewing all pages, participants were asked to complete a survey with questions on age, gender, and participant.

## 4.1.4 Analytical Method

We used a Bayesian approach to ANOVA [84] to analyze the eye-tracking data. The Bayesian approach to ANOVA has several benefits [20]; specifically, it accommodates a wide variety of distributions of the dependent variable, as well as hierarchical data structures that

#

arise from mixed between- and within-subject designs. It also provides inferences based on small samples and may not only reject but also supports null hypotheses [46]. The models used here tested the between-subject effects of the factors of gaze direction (D), facial expression (E), and the D × E interaction on each of the three dependent measures, pupil diameter, and the average fixation duration on the brand and text. We included the effect of participant gender (G) as well, to control for differences between men and women. We modeled average pupil diamete with a normal distribution and the fixation durations on the brand and text with log-normal distributions. The models were estimated with JAGS<sup>5</sup>, using a burn-in of at least 20,000 and standard methods to check convergence. We report the posterior means and standard deviations of the parameters, and compute Bayesian p-values to indicate statistical significance.

## 4.2 Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, we replicated Experiment 1 in China with Chinese participants and Chinese stimuli, using a 2 (facial expression: neutral versus slight smile) × 2 (gaze direction – direct: looking at consumers versus averted: looking at the product) between-subjects design.

## 4.2.1 Stimuli

In the pretest, to check the validity of the images, 20 participants were asked to rate the images, as in Experiment 1. Based on the results, 16 images were chosen as stimuli. Manipulation checks among 80 participants in the main study (see below) revealed a significant difference (p < 0.001) in perceived smile between images with a smiling expression (mean = 5.33, SD 1.545) and a neutral expression $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 3 . 6 6 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 6 2 2 )$ difference $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ in perceived gaze direction between images with a direct gaze (mean = 2.39, SD = 1.829) and those with an averted gaze $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 5 . 2 8 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 8 2 0 )$ . In order to replicate Experiment 1, we used the same product brands as those used in Experiment 1. The pretest showed that these brands are familiar to Chinese participants. Moreover, we collected general evaluations of brand familiarity in the experiment; including these measures in the analysis did not show a significant effect. We therefore dropped them from further analysis.

To simulate an online Chinese cosmetics store, we created 16 web pages for each treatment condition, consisting of the manipulated images integrated with the pack-shot, brand name, textual product information, and so on, similar to the web pages in Experiment 1. Examples of the stimuli are presented in Appendix A.

## 4.2.2 Participants

A total of 80 Chinese college students took part in the experiment. The participants were recruited from an online discussion forum affiliated with a university in China and were paid US\$5 for their participation. Exclusion criteria were similar to those in Experiment 1. There were 43 women and 37 men, with an average age of 22.8 years. All participants had online shopping experience and 74% of the participants shopped online at least once or twice a month. The 80 participants were randomly assigned to the four treatment conditions.

## 4.2.3 Experimental procedure and analyses

The procedures in Experiment 2 were identical to those in Experiment 1. The eye-tracking device used in this experiment was the SMI Hi-Speed iView X infrared monocular eye tracker, with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. The experimental websites were presented on a 19-inch monitor with a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels. Participants’ heads were stabilized using a chin rest, at a distance from the monitor of about 60 cm. The ROIs, eye-tracking measures, and analyses were the same as in Experiment 1. As a control variable, gender has significant main effects on brand fixation durations and pupil dilation. For Chinese participants, facial expression interacted with gaze direction to affect brand and text fixation durations. ## 5 Discussion

This study examines how a smiling model’s eye gaze direction influences consumers’ visual processing (assessing both affective and cognitive dimensions). As predicted, for both American and Chinese consumers, direct eye gaze and a smiling facial expression leads to higher levels of arousal. This indicates a higher level of viewer excitement if the model smiles and looks directly at him or her. Approach-oriented emotions, such as happiness, are better recognized and are perceived as more intense with a direct gaze than with an averted gaze [2, 3], and faces that look at the viewer with a smiling expression are perceived as more attractive [41]. The present results provide evidence that an emotion will be perceived as more intense when gaze and facial expression share a consistent signal value (direct gaze and a smiling expression), supporting the prediction of shared signal theory [2]. Moreover, we also found that this effect is stronger for Chinese consumers, compared to American ones, which is consistent with the arguments of previous research that Chinese people feel more comfortable with (slight) smiles than Americans [18, 62]. O

With respect to the cognitive dimension, as predicted, a model’s averted eye gaze, accompanied by a smiling facial expression, leads to deeper information processing of product information, which further supports the predictions of shared signal theory (i.e., sharing a consistent signal value generates better performance). This finding is in line with previous research, such as Bayliss et al. [7] and Adams Jr and Kleck [2]. However, although we found that the difference between Chinese and American consumers in regard to the effect of gaze direction on the depth of brand information processing (fixation durations) is significant, there is no significant difference between cultures in text information processing. Possible reasons for this are as follows.

Previous research suggests that heightened arousal may reduce the amount of processing capacity available for performing cognitive tasks and distract from the processing of subsequently presented information [68, 72]. In line with this argument, we found that a smiling face with a direct gaze leads to higher levels of arousal and simultaneously reduces the depth of processing of the information contained in the text for both American and Chinese customers.

#

This did not hold for brand information processing. We conjecture that this may be due to the facts-versus-feelings information mode of the text-versus-brand elements [32, 57, 63]. While the text with the detailed product description is an informative component of the website that intends to present functional information about the product, the brand name has a large emotional component [77], which is associated with affect toward the product and the company selling it. Therefore, processing of the brand name may be strongly impacted by emotion contagion. Considering the documented differences in analytical versus holistic cognitive styles between Westerners and Asians [42, 87], information processing of the brand can be more inhibited by the heightened arousal resulting from a smiling face with a direct gaze for Chinese people, who focus more on the relationship between a salient object (the brand) and its context (the models on the website). However, this distracting effect may be weakened for Americans, who tend to focus on salient objects independent of context. We suspect that this is why we observed differences in the depth of processing of the brand between participants from these two countries.

## 5.1 Implications

The main theoretical contribution that this study provides is a study of the effects of the eye gaze direction of a model with a smiling facial expression on consumer information processing. The intended contributions of this study are as follows. First, while previous studies on gaze direction in management, including IS and marketing, have uncovered a series of interesting findings regarding the effects of eye gaze direction [e.g., 9, 73], they focus on uncovering isolated effects, leaving room for an understanding of the effects of contextual cues, such as

#

smiling facial expressions. This study, as one of the very first attempts to do so, fills this gap and reveals several novel findings. Second, although the joint effects of gaze direction and facial expression have been uncovered in psychology and neuroscience [e.g., 64], faces are shown in isolation and participants are under forced-exposure conditions in these studies [48, 50, 85]. The use of e-commerce websites as a contextual platform and self-controlled exposure settings increased the ecological validity of the present study. The findings of this study provide additional support for shared signal theory [2]. Third, the findings of this study add to the culture literature by revealing differences between Chinese and American consumers in interpreting facial cues of models on websites. In particular, although previous research works have shown that culture can be an important factor that influences how people interpret smiles [e.g., 38, 60], it has not yet been shown that a smile plays different roles in affecting consumer affective and cognitive responses. The findings of this study fill this gap by revealing that Chinese and American cultures differ in consumers’ emotional experiences and information processing in regard to a particular brand, but are similar in their influence on consumer information processing of textual product descriptions.

This study also offers guidelines for practitioners to design better websites to promote their products. In particular, the findings of this study suggest that seemingly contradictory findings a direct eye gaze with a smiling facial expression leads to stronger levels of arousal, while an averted eye gaze with a smiling facial expression leads to deeper information processing – should caution practitioners that, when designing websites with advertising models, different strategies can be adopted. Specifically, when the aim is to attract consumers to the website and elicit their arousal, then the best strategy to use is direct eye gaze with a smiling facial

#

expression. When the aim is to elicit consumers’ deep impressions and information processing, then the best strategy is to feature the model with an averted eye gaze and a smiling facial expression.

Furthermore, the current research suggests that, given the rapid development of crossborder electronic commerce, online retailers need to consider the impact of models’ facial cues in relation to the culture of the viewer. There is evidence to suggest that websites require cultural adaptation when site users differ in terms of their cultural backgrounds [78]. Websites adapted to the local culture have been shown to have the potential to make consumers remain on the site longer [17] and increase trust [78]. With the development of technologies such as website morphing, automatically changing the characteristics of a website to match the culture and demographics of viewers has become both feasible and practical [30]. This study shows that matching facial cues to the viewers’ cultural backgrounds to evoke brand processing appears to be critical, because a smiling face with an averted gaze has a positive effect on the processing of brand information for Chinese consumers, but not for American consumers. In addition to this, the results showed that a slightly smiling face with a direct gaze elicits stronger arousal among Chinese consumers, as compared to American consumers, which suggests that eretailers may use human faces with these facial cue combinations on websites targeted at Chinese customers.

## 5.2 Limitations and Research Directions

As with all research, this study has limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, we used student samples. Although previous studies have widely argued that college students

#

constitute a large proportion of online buyers, an investigation of a broader sample may be needed to obtain more generalizable results. Second, scholars in adverting and consumer behavior have widely argued that product type is one of the most important influences on consumers’ information processing [36, 83]. In this study, we considered beauty products only, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. It would be interesting to study the effect experience). Third, this study investigated smiling facial expressions only, in particular the “slight smile”, because these are widely used on e-commerce websites. Future research could study other types of smiles and other less common facial expressions, such as surprise. Fourth, although gender effects are not the focus of the present study, it would be interesting to see if gender interacts with facial cues. More importantly, the gender of the model on the website may interact with the gender of the viewer [37]. In future research, both male and female models could be employed to investigate differences in the effects of facial cues when the gender of the model and the viewer match or do not match. Finally, we used two different types of eyetracking equipment for the experiments in China and America. While using the same type of equipment in these two countries would have been ideal, we expect there to be a main effect of the equipment (for example, slightly longer or shorter fixation durations or pupil dilations for one eye tracker relative to the other), but not an interaction effect with our experimental treatments; we do not expect that the use of different eye trackers would have directionally changed our findings.
