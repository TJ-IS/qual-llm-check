# Information provision for challenging markets: The case of the accessibility requiring market in the context of tourism

Eleni Michopoulou <sup>a,</sup>\*, Dimitrios Buhalis <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Derby, 1 Devonshire Road, Buxton SK17 6RY, UK

<sup>b</sup> eTourism Lab, Bournemouth University, Poole BH12 5BB, UK

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 18 January 2012 Received in revised form 22 March 2013 Accepted 7 April 2013 Available online 15 April 2013

Keywords: User requirements Disability Accessible tourism System design

## A B S T R A C T

The paper investigates the requirements of users with disabilities and the implications that these tourists have for developing accessible tourism information systems. A series of focus groups and interviews revealed the informational needs of people with disabilities, as well as the relevant technical difficulties involved in addressing these needs. The results indicated that the indispensable requirements include the following: (1) the veto or absolutely minimal prerequisites principle; (2) an indication of holistic accessibility paths; and (3) door-to-door access maps. The technical challenges identified focus on interoperability, content integration and personalization. The paper concludes by demonstrating how the tourism industry can overcome these challenges and address disabled travelers needs.

## 1. Introduction

User satisfaction is one of the primary measures of information system (IS) success. To create satisfaction, a user-centered process is needed to ensure that the system meets the requirements of the target users and that people are motivated to use it [12,36,33]. One of the major failures for an IS occurs, however, when the user requirements are not properly identified or elicited. Hence, determining complete and correct information requirements is a vital part of designing an IS. Many IS failures can be attributed to a lack of clear and specific information requirements [23,35,43,52]. Numerous studies have indicated that the misidentification of requirements is one of the primary causes of customer dissatisfaction with the delivered system [34,42].

Tourism is an area on the forefront of IS deployment. Tourism is an information intensive industry that strongly relies on electronic systems to distribute its products in the marketplace as well as to provide information to customers and to facilitate purchase decisions [4,69,6]. Tourism organizations, including airlines, tour operators, and hotels, are among the pioneers in Internet adoption and e-commerce activity, with a significant focus on customer satisfaction [6,45]. However, there is evidence that the tourism industry has had limited success in ensuring the satisfaction of one rapidly growing market segment: people with disabilities [53,72,71].

The disability market is considerable in terms of both size and spending power [5]. It has been estimated that there are over a billion disabled people worldwide. People with some level of disability are believed to comprise 15–20% of the world’s population [70,65]. Over 125 million people in Europe alone have accessibility needs [7], including the elderly, as there is a strong and positive correlation between aging and disability [31,29,37]. The size of this market is also expected to increase substantially over the next 30 years due to three primary factors. Firstly, the baby-boom generation from 1945 to 1965 is reaching 65+ in 2010. Secondly, as fertility rates have decreased since 1965, the elderly population is increasing at a faster rate than the younger population. Thirdly, life expectancy at older ages is increasing [50]. In terms of spending power, it has been reported that people with disabilities have a combined spending power of approximately \$200 billion in the US [9] and 80 billion pounds in the UK [62]. Destinations and tourism organizations around the world are gradually learning how to serve this particular market, and some best practice cases in accessible tourism have recently emerged [8].

Hence, this paper aims to answer the following questions:

\- What are the disabled users’ requirements for an information system?

\- What are the challenges involved in building such a system?

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Disabled users’ requirements

To design a system to accommodate the needs of the disability market, user requirements must be investigated in depth. The disability market requirements literature is quite limited [9,13,18– 20]. Disabled traveler market requirements can be grouped into three major categories, as follows: physical accessibility, information about accessibility and information accessible online [7]. With regards to physical accessibility, some countries have introduced legislation that makes it compulsory for tourism businesses to provide an environment that is accessible to people with disabilities. Leading examples of this type of national legislation include the UK (British Disability Discrimination Act), the US (Americans with Disabilities Act) and Australia (Commonwealth Disability Discrimination Act). These countries represent the strongest attempts to create legal accessibility standards.

With regards to making information accessible online, the Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI) provides design principles and guidelines that clearly demonstrate how to ensure website accessibility for different users [67]. While the architectural requests for barrier free environments as well as the web accessibility requirements are relatively well established [2,67], the information about accessibility that is required prior to travel has not been adequately elaborated. To effectively address disabled user requirements, an investigation is required to provide an overview that encapsulates the extent and weight of these requirements.

## 2.1.1. Accessibility of the physical/built environment

The first basic requirement of the disabled market is the accessibility of the physical/built environment. People with disabilities are hindered by multiple barriers when they participate in tourism [1,11,28,48]. The physical barriers are barriers in the built and external environment that make it difficult or impossible for people with disabilities to access a particular physical space. People with different impairments are faced with a variety of barriers. Examples of physical barriers include steps, stairs, and heavy doors for people with mobility impairment; inadequate lighting, poor color contrast, lack of tactile guide paths for visually impaired people; and a lack of induction loops and alternative fire alarm signals (e.g., vibration pad or flashing lights) for people with hearing impairments. These barriers make tourism experiences less accessible for people with disabilities or, in some cases, suggest that they cannot practice tourism at all [64]. When assessing site accessibility, Israeli [38] explained that the decision-making process of disabled people varies in relation to able-bodied travelers. The main reason for variance is in their restricted choices, due to limited venue accessibility, which in turn determines whether a trade-off is possible among different travel attributes. In particular, a wheelchair user may not be able to compensate for a hotel’s overbooking because there may be no alternative option. Therefore, wheelchair users are much more cautious and require more information at each stage of travel planning.

## 2.1.2. Information regarding the accessibility of tourism facilities

While accessible infrastructure is critical for participation, information regarding the accessibility of tourism facilities and destinations is another a fundamental requirement for the disabled market [13.20.64.44.57.63.73]. Even if facilities are accessible, unless they provide relevant information regarding accessibility, disabled travelers will not be able to complete their planning process [10] and engage in travel [18,11,21].

However, information about a single accessible facility per se is not adequate for planning and organizing the complete travel experience [7,21]. Information should be inclusive and detailed and should entail all accessible components [73]. Hence, the richness of information is a key criterion for the travel decisions of disabled travelers.

The disabled population should not be seen as homogenous and consistent. People have different abilities/disabilities and they need to assess for themselves whether a facility or a service is accessible to them. The market consists of nano-sub-segments that vary according to the extent of the individual abilities/disabilities. No other market segment has requirements as specific as those of the accessibility market. Accordingly, Burnett and Baker [9] suggest that the degree of disability/ability determines and explains the variation in the depth and detail of the informational requirements. The need for detailed information is more intense for those with the highest degree of disability because severe disabilities have more complicated access requirements. However, according to Miller and Kirk [47], the higher the level of accessibility requirements, the more inadequate the provision of specialized and detailed information tends to be. As illustrated in Fig. 1, people with more severe access needs require additional (and more specialized) information. However, the supply of this specialized information becomes more scarce for those with higher accessibility requirements. Hence, the gap between the demand and supply of accessibility information increases, with a greater effect on those who need this information most.

Reliability is also a key determinant for disabled market decisions [11]. Reliability is important because the lack of reliable and accurate information can result in disabled people choosing not to travel altogether or facing severe inconvenience if they receive inaccurate information [18,63,21].

Darcy [18] elaborates on the importance of objective measurements through the use of reliable assessments and reporting mechanisms, in contrast to using classifications or prescriptive information. Objectivity allows the disabled person to judge for his or herself the suitability of a facility, rather than relying on the perception of the facility owner or assessor. The provision of objective and absolute measurements is significant because it enables disabled people to appraise a facility according to their own personal criteria. This ability is even more important for people with greater access requirements, who are often restricted by a large number of barriers. Objective information also allows the disabled users to select between factors if there are no ideal conditions in the market. For example, a disabled user may choose a less accessible hotel if it is cheaper or has a better location than a more accessible one. Objective information enables trade-offs between attributes.

Tourism information relative to accessibility is provided by (or distributed by) a variety of players and can be generic or specific. For instance, Destination Management Organizations (DMOs) provide generic tourism information with little focus on accessibility. In contrast, national, regional or local accessibility schemes provide primarily accessibility information that occasionally applies to tourism suppliers and destinations [28]. The reliability and quality of this information is often inconsistent, and disabled people are repeatedly disappointed to find that in reality, the facilities do not match the descriptions provided. Hence, the accuracy of information is of paramount importance. The need for accuracy is intensified by the fact that information regarding tourism accessibility is highly fragmented and scattered among the different industry players covering different geographical areas. In addition, travelers, by definition, are away from home and out of their comfort zone, which adds to the unfamiliarity risk.

## 2.1.3. Online information accessibility

Online information accessibility is the last of the three fundamental requirements of the disability market. Disabled travelers need to utilize multiple information sources because the quality of information given by a single source is usually insufficient, only partially accurate or inaccessible [20,44,17]. Ray and Ryder [54] investigated the preferred sources of travel information used by disabled travelers and found that the Internet was one of the most frequently deployed sources. There are websites that provide specialized accessible tourism information. Examples include www.disabled-world.com/travel, www.flightcenter.biz/accessible-travel, and http://www.scandichotels.com/ Always-at-Scandic/Special-needs/. However, the information needs of this market are still not satisfactorily met, as the reliability, adequacy, and accessibility between the different websites varies considerably.

Increasingly, online communities, social networks, and social media are emerging as an important information resource for consumers with special needs. In fact, User Generated Content (UGC) is often perceived as more reliable than the information provided by the destination or tourism service provider and, subsequently, UGC is considered to be a substitute for word of mouth [49]. Many disabled travelers gain information through social media and Web 2.0 by watching videos and photos on communities such as Flickr or YouTube and by using social networks and wikis. Drews and Schemer [27] found that over 50% of their respondents use social media resources such as travel review platforms, photo- and video-sharing platforms or social networks to gather trustworthy accessible travel information.

Reviews produced by other disabled travelers are particularly trusted because users appreciate that the authors share their perspective.

To access online content, disabled users often employ a variety of assistive technologies, such as screen readers, alternate keyboards, and refreshable Braille displays [26,40,3]. For a website to be accessible, it must be sufficiently flexible to be used by all of these assistive technologies [60]. Accessible design is therefore essential to allow people with disabilities to use the Internet more effectively [56].

## 2.2. The major challenges

To address these requirements, it is essential to create a system that provides accessible, reliable, and accurate information about accessibility. The tourism industry has been slow to design and operate systems that meet disabled users’ requirements. This slowness can be partly attributed to the inherent challenges involved in developing these systems. Some of the challenges are generic within the tourism industry, but when considering disabled users’ requirements, the existing challenges are enhanced and intensified.

## 2.2.1. Interoperability

Interoperability is a generic and much discussed issue. Definitions of interoperability vary from generic interpretations (business sense) to highly technical ones (that apply to specific types of software and hardware). The IEEE Glossary refers to interoperability as ‘‘the ability of two or more systems or components to exchange information and to use the information that has been exchanged’’. Interoperability refers to the technology infrastructure as well as the semantics involved in performing regular business processes between partners. Interoperability is considered essential if the various stakeholders within the economy are to cooperate to effectively promote and supply a combination of products and services to customers. Only then, can the tourism experience be successfully supported by all of the vendors involved. However, there are issues that complicate this process. Tourism is not homogenous; it rather consists of over 30 identified sectors [59] such as education, agriculture, and sports. The very essence of tourism lies, therefore, within the network. Each of these sectors, however, has a different scope of services and uses a different system, which makes interoperability critical for the creation of their product.

There is also a huge variation in the size of companies. The bulk of tourism organizations comprise small and medium enterprises (SMEs) that compete together with the vast and well-established brands. The implication of this range is that all of these organizations have different rates of growth and the capital invested in technical infrastructure varies significantly. The differences can be traced to multiple areas, such as the type of technology deployed, the system architecture, and the information structure. Even similar services (e.g., accommodations) are supported by dissimilar system designs, creating a cooperation barrier for a variety of stakeholders within the value chain.

This problem is intensified when the multinational character of tourism is taken into account. Interaction, collaboration, and even regular business transactions are often obstructed by the language barrier between countries. Interoperability is therefore a major issue in the tourism industry.

Interoperability therefore needs to be addressed both in terms of a business configuration and at a technical level in the accessible tourism context. Interoperability within accessible tourism is particularly important for several reasons. The product is a confidence good, and consumer decisions rely on the information available beforehand. More importantly, the tourism industry is highly networked and based on worldwide cooperation between stakeholders of different types. Interoperability is therefore critical to allow tourism organizations to exchange information seamlessly [69].

## 2.2.2. Content integration

Information is the ‘‘lifeblood’’ of tourism, which indicates its importance for the industry [58]. The intangibility of products makes information an essential prerequisite for traveling. With regards to disabled travelers, information is even more critical because the detail, depth, and type of information determine the very decision to engage in travel. Hence, the integration of different types of content should enable disabled travelers to participate in tourism experiences. However, there are a number of issues that make content integration problematic. Information is absolutely critical for allowing disabled travelers to engage in travel, especially for those with more severe disabilities. The informational requirement of these travelers is 2-fold. On the one hand, they need generic tourism information, like any other able-bodied traveler, and on the other hand, they need accessibility specific information. Generic tourism information can be obtained from Destination Management Organizations (DMOs), local authorities, travel guides, etc. Tourism suppliers (e.g., hotels, restaurants) may also occasionally provide some type of accessibility information regarding their respective facilities. Accessibility specific information can also usually be retrieved from disability-related organizations, charities, and accessibility schemes. Hence, the required information is scattered among a range of organizations, including DMOs, national, regional or local accessibility schemes, and hotel aggregators. This scattering makes the information gathering process for the disabled traveler difficult and time consuming.

Furthermore, research has demonstrated that the provision of specialized and detailed information becomes inadequate as the level of facility accessibility requirements increases [47]. A survey of 43 accessibility schemes in 19 countries revealed that although these organizations provide accessibility specific information, the content primarily focuses on mobility impairments and neglects other types of disabilities. Information is often available in different patterns and languages and is frequently inaccurate or inadequate. Information is also often scattered among different industry players, including DMOs, national, regional or local accessibility schemes, and hotel aggregators. This content can be found in different formats, placing an extra burden on the integration process that will enable reliability and consistency [16,68]. Often the information is provided in a language that the traveler may not speak or understand sufficiently.

Another complication is that each person has a different type of disability and to a different extent. Therefore, disabled travelers require different types of accessibility information providing different details. For example, a blind traveler has very different requirements from a tetraplegic traveler. People with a low degree of disability, such as prescription eyeglasses for example, have minimal accessibility information requirements that are similar to those of able-bodied travelers. However, the higher the degree of disability, the more detailed the required accessibility information is. Currently, the existing content provided by a wide range of organizations varies considerably in terms of detail, accuracy, and reliability. The provision of specialized and detailed accessibility information tends to be inadequate the higher the level of accessibility requirements [47,46].

The lack of comprehensive information provision regarding available and accessible travel and tourism opportunities is apparent in most countries [63]. Accessibility information, when available, is very fragmented and unevenly distributed within different geographical areas. The accessibility content can reflect coverage that ranges from national, regional, city or even facility levels. The available content is also to be found in different languages and formats, setting an extra burden on the creation of an integration process that will enable reliability and consistency.

## 2.2.3. Personalization

Finding the right content online can be difficult and time consuming, especially for people seeking very specific information. Personalization is a technique used to generate individualized content for each customer [32]. To increase their overall value [14] and profit [15], organizations focus on their customers using personalization [24]. Personalization allows Internet users to specify their requirements and interests to access the required information in the desirable format. Travelers normally have a wide range of requirements and preferences, which has created a need for travel recommender systems [55]. These requirements and preferences may range from the preferred hotel chain to the language in which information should be presented [51]. Hence, personalization allows Internet users to specify their requirements and interests to access the required information in the desired format.

The disabled travelers market has additional personalization requirements that relate to the type of disability, and the disabled need to be able to personalize both their travel information search and media according to their needs. Their needs may vary from ‘‘nice to have’’ to ‘‘absolutely essential’’. Hence, personalization may be a precondition for traveling. A blind person may therefore search only for places that have Braille displays while a full-time wheelchair user may require access information only for fully accessible hotels. Different assistive technologies may be required to access the information, from screen readers to ergonomic and specifically designed keyboards. Hence, personalization needs to cater to all different types of impairment.

The actual need for personalization is focused on filtering the information according to type and degree of disability. For instance, a mobility impaired person using a walking stick will be able to select a preferred tourism venue from a significant number of options ranging from almost inaccessible to totally accessible venues. Personalization, in this case, is required to narrow down the available options. However, a tetraplegic full-time wheelchair user may have limited options due to the high level of accessibility required. Personalization is then needed to ensure that the search results include only the venues that actually meet the level of accessibility requirement. Therefore, personalization should act as a decision support mechanism for narrowing down the available options as well as acting as a verification mechanism, by assuring requirements-system/content fit.

To plan a personalized travel experience, users often need to trade-off between various attributes such as time, location, or price. Disabled travelers must also be able to select the compromises between travel elements and accessibility. Hence, a purpose-built tourism venue is not necessarily the best choice if a person is willing to sacrifice ultimate accessibility for partial accessibility and a better price or location. One may select a less accessible hotel if it is closer to the desired location or if it cheaper than the alternatives. Personalization is therefore required to enable disabled travelers to make selections according to individual preferences as well as their abilities/disabilities.

## 3. Research objectives

The literature review demonstrates that the tourism industry has had limited success in providing effective and accepted information systems that distribute suitable information for disabled travelers. Therefore, this paper aims to explore the information provision requirements for the accessibility requiring market. This aim prompted the further investigation of these issues, and the following research objectives evolved:

\- Identify the particular informational needs of disabled travelers and their requirements within the tourism industry.

\- Explore the technical challenges involved in building and designing an IS according to those requirements.

\- Apply the findings in the Europe For All (EFA) portal that provides information about the accessibility of tourism facilities in an accessible IS environment.

## 4. Methodology

A review of the literature identified the key themes, issues, and challenges. Secondary data were collected from books, academic journals, reports, and the Internet. This process demonstrated that research in this area is at its infancy and that we should better understand the key needs and requirements of these markets.

The review concentrated primarily on qualitative research that included a number of workshops and focus groups. The participants represented a range of stakeholders, including National Tourism Boards, hotel distributors, tourism operators, destination management systems and both organizations and active individuals from the disabled community. The particular issues related to the requirements of the disabled traveler and the disabled market were examined in eight focus groups that investigated two different thematic areas (four focus groups per thematic area); these groups took place in two international workshops (Table 2). The two primary thematic areas included disabled traveler requirements and the technical challenges of building an IS for this market. The participants elaborated on a number of issues relative to access needs and requirements, information required, the format and nature of the information, the sources of information, the use of technology, and assistive technologies. This process provided the researchers as well as the participants with considerable scope to explore. The four focus groups for each thematic area provided the researchers with access to a larger number of experts. The sets of data obtained from the two workshops were then compared and contrasted, offering great insights on the issues examined.

The participants were also provided with the opportunity to make a more holistic contribution to the debate by attending groups in both thematic areas.

The first workshop took place in London, UK, with a total of 68 participants, while the second workshop numbered 131 participants and occurred in Athens, Greece. The workshops were attended by leading European disability travel experts from over 17 countries, offering a broad perspective. The participants in the workshops and focus groups consisted of top level disability specialists, travel and destination professionals, and technology experts. Most of the participants were members of the European Network for Accessible Tourism (ENAT), and they included the Executive Manager of Disability Now, the Chief Executive of Tourism for All UK, the Managing Director of Turism fo¨r Alla Sweden, the Vice President of ANLH (Association. Although the sample design was purposive and judgmental, it enabled the research team to access considerable expertise and to accumulate in-depth knowledge from various specialists. Each focus group was kept to a maximum of 20 people and was facilitated by two coordinators and a minute-taker. Content analysis was used as the most appropriate technique for coding the discussion and the comments.

Based on the findings, a prototype was developed incorporating these requirements. The prototype was produced under the OSSATE project [One-Stop-Shop for Accessible Tourism in Europe, co-funded by the EC e-Content Program] and resulted in the ‘‘Europe for All’’ (EFA) Web Portal. The prototype was then provided to the experts who had participated in the workshops for feedback. The sample included a total of 28 people who commented on the prototype. These people came from six major disability schemes in Europe, one leading technology company, two tourism related public bodies and one university. All of the participants had some form of impairment, including visual, mobility, and hearing/speech impairment. However, the extent of the impairment varied, ranging, for instance, from walking difficulties to full-time wheelchair users. Additionally, some cases demonstrated multiple impairments, i.e., low vision and limited arm dexterity. The participants were offered access to the prototype and were asked to perform a number of tasks. The participants were given nine scenarios detailing typical tourism set-ups and were asked to find specific types of information, as indicated in the example below (Fig. 2).

During the testing process, the Thinking Aloud Protocol was deployed, which allowed the participants to vocalize their thoughts and feelings while performing the task scenarios. This method enabled the researchers to gather rich data on the interaction between the user and the prototype (Figs. 3 and 4). The relatively unstructured nature of the testing also allowed for clarification of both the questions asked and the feedback given. After completion of the tasks, the participants were interviewed and asked to report any problems encountered during this process.

The results of the focus groups, workshops and interviews revealed the disabled traveler requirements and the main technical challenges involved in designing a system that addresses these needs. The results were also used as input to develop the EFA portal and to test it according to the user requirements. Hence, the results produced not only a research report but also an integrated system that incorporates best practices, where all challenges can be tested against the user requirements.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. User requirements

The results of the workshops indicated and verified the disabled traveler market requirements in terms of the accessibility of the physical/built environment, information regarding facility accessibility and accessible information.

With regards to the accessibility of the physical/build environment, the participants claimed that it is critical for disabled tourists to have access to more facilities and services and the right to choose according to personal preference. Legislation, corporate responsibility and architectural practice are gradually addressing these requirements. However, it was emphasized that even if the legislation and regulations are in place, the majority of tourism SMEs do not particularly care for this market and therefore are reluctant to invest in making adaptations to improve accessibility. On the one hand, these venues do not understand the financial opportunity and, on the other, they are unaware of the accessibility requirements.

Information regarding the accessibility is fundamental for the disabled traveler market because it often determines the very decision of whether to travel or not. Richness, reliability, and the accuracy of information are key determinants because information regarding tourism accessibility is highly fragmented and scattered among the different industry players.

As one participant quoted, ‘‘It is very frustrating that I need to spend an enormous amount of time to plan my vacations. And the worst part is that after I have researched a number of resources, I visit the place, but the reality is totally different’’. The limited flexibility of the disabled market to make trade-offs between travel attributes also signifies the importance of trust in the information source. The quality of access can be measured on the basis of subjective and objective factors by using qualitative and quantitative data. An objective assessment approach usually uses quantitative indicators such as the number and size of steps or the width of doors in centimeters. The need for objective measurements, rather than prescriptive information or classifications, was also noted by the respondents because it enables the users to judge accessibility according to their individual abilities.

According to a wheelchair user, ‘‘Saying that a hotel is accessible for wheelchair users without providing detailed information has no use for me. When I know, for example, the width of the room door, then I can decide if I can go, first, and which chair to take with me, second. Otherwise, I have to call them up for this information, which I’m not even sure they’ll get it right’’.

Finally, the accessibility of information online was also noted as a primary requirement to enable disabled travelers to access more content. The respondents explained that websites do not always follow the WAI guidelines and cannot always support a variety of assistive technologies. The respondents also indicated that the more generic tourism content on a website tends to be more inaccessible.

The participants also identified three additional requirements that have not been discussed before. First, the interviewees discussed the veto principle. The ‘Veto’ is regarded as the absolutely minimal prerequisite that allows a person to enter a building or make use of a service. The Veto is different for each individual user and, hence, the objective and accurate provision of information allows disabled travelers to decide for themselves. Hence, because the built environment is not always completely accessible, IS should at least facilitate the early filtering of the minimal requirements for each type and degree of disability.

As one participant stated, ‘‘as I am a full time wheelchair user, there is no point of showing me properties that require me to have to address steps. Therefore, I would appreciate it if I can filter those properties out as they simply do not match my needs’’.

However, the same venue may be perfectly accessible for a parttime wheelchair user or a deaf person. Therefore, it is critical to demonstrate that the provision of information determines the range and depth of the market that can be served.

Most importantly, the accessibility of the built environment should be communicated through online media. Knowing if a venue is not accessible is as important (if not more) as knowing that it is accessible. Second, the participants explained that although information about the accessibility of tourism facilities and venues is very important, the systems should also include information on the links between the individual accessible components. It is critical to demonstrate how the entire destination can be accessed rather than just individual facilities.

One participant stated, ‘‘once I stayed in a perfectly accessible hotel but going around the resort was a nightmare. You really need comprehensive information on where you are able to go and what to do given your own abilities. So accessibility information about the whole resort and the paths are essential’’.

Therefore, disabled travelers need to know not only that venues A and B are accessible but also that the route from A to B is accessible. Hence, it is critical that information about accessibility paths is provided. Third, the respondents argued that although available accessibility paths are essential at the destination level, this is still not sufficient to plan a holistic travel experience. For the entire travel planning process to be complete and comprehensive, accessibility paths should be leveraged and should cover the origin, the transit and the destination area.

Another participant stated, ‘‘if any parts of the route are inaccessible, I cannot continue the trip and I need to ask for assistance that may not be available or to abandon the trip and return to base’’ IS can therefore be utilized to provide door-to-door interactive access maps that will allow disabled travelers to coordinate their travel arrangements from the moment they leave their house until they reach their destination and the individual tourism facilities that they would liketo visit. Understanding the type and degree of disability can enable systems to provide alternative paths that better suit the user. For example, the accessibility path for a wheelchair user may be different from that for a visually impaired traveler. Hence, IS should be used to provide comprehensive, customizable, dynamic, and interactive accessibility path plans.

## 5.2. Interoperability

Interoperability is a major issue throughout the tourism industry. All elements of the tourism industry operate using different systems, as their scale and scope of service vary significantly. Research has shown that interoperability obstacles can be overcome at a semantic level by using specialized ontology development [25,30]. Work by the OpenTravel and Travel Tourism Initiative (TTI) has made progress on many operational issues in the industry. However, the interoperability of accessibility information remains a major challenge. Several attempts have been made by major industry players to provide standardization guidelines for accessible facilities, including the checklists from the Hotel Electronic Distribution Network Association (HEDNA) and activehotels.com. Still, the need to assure interoperability among these diverse systems is pressing. Interoperability must be ensured not only with the various stakeholders but also with the end-users. Interoperability is critical for creating an accessibility path online, i.e., for interlinking all of the accessible products and services required. In addition, interconnecting accessibility schemes, national databases and transportation systems depend on the interoperability of data. Hence, interconnecting the value chain elements of the tourism industry and creating an accessibility path among the accessible components is critical for providing an accessible tourism experience. Within the range of EFA interests, a comparative analysis of the database structure of three major European accessibility schemes, namely Visit Britain, Toegankelijk Vlaanderen Bureau and Association Nationale pour le Logement des Personnes Handicape´es, was conducted. This analysis revealed that there are a total of over 1200 different fields describing the accessible tourism supply. Additionally, not more than 10% of the fields had the same descriptors. Hence, there is a need to develop the accessibility tourism ontology to help the major players develop interoperable systems that are oriented toward building integrated services.

To overcome interoperability issues, the EFA provided interfacing modules to the existing national and regional tourist information databases and also made use of the XML language and the Harmonise project outcomes. Additionally, to support a multidisciplinary group of stakeholders, data gathering tools for new venues and accessible tourism information schemes are required, with a dedicated data entry service. By promoting interoperability and interconnectivity among the different players, the EFA platform allows for the comprehensive integration of a wide range of products and services. Furthermore, the platform supports the ability of accessible tourism companies to network and exchange information in a seamless manner.

## 5.3. Content integration

It was evident that the perceived information quality has a direct effect on system use. The respondents were adamant about the quality of some of the systems, and any previous negative experience harmed the reputation and reliability of the systems.

Consequently, the EFA platform integrated content from the largest possible range of reliable sources, addressing the multifaceted needs of a wide range of users. This holistic approach encapsulated accessibility-specific and tourism-generic content to satisfy different sets of requirements and covered all markets from the severely disabled to able-bodied persons.

For this purpose, EFA developed three scalable data sets that reflect three informational levels, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The EFA system allows for three levels of assessment of venue accessibility, as follows: level 1 (self-assessment by venue owners), level 2 (professional assessment by auditors) and level 3 (third-party regional or national accessibility schemes). The more detailed the information available, the more reliable the source was assessed. The developed database schema caters to all three levels by identifying and analyzing the imported data at different component levels. If a component (and all underlying checklists and questions) needs to be filled at more than one level, then two different component entities can be created in the database. This ability means that a venue owner may have entered a self assessment questionnaire, but when the auditor fills in the level 2 assessment, the previous data are not deleted. Obviously, the precedence between the three levels is given to the higher levels when querying the database (L3 > L2 > L1).

## 5.4. Personalization

The disabled travelers market has particular requirements that relate to the type and severity of the disability. Hence, personalization is ideal for this market because a set profile will enable the users to preset their requirements and to be directed to the relevant products and services. The interviewees therefore expressed the need to be able to personalize their travel information search according to their own needs. A blind person may choose to visit only places that have Braille displays and audible queues, while a full time wheelchair user may request and consider only information regarding wheelchair accessible hotels. The users may also request that information be provided in the particular format that best suits their computer usage needs.

One of the respondents explained, ‘‘I need the system to be able to understand both my specific needs as well as the assistive technology I am using to access the system. Understanding my needs and filtering information accordingly will save a consid erable amount of time and will increase my loyalty to the service.’’

The EFA system was designed to enable the individual users to build their individual profiles, thus supporting their ability to perform personalized searches and retrieve suitable specialized content [32]. The configuration of individual user profiles is a continuous process in which the system allows the users to build their profiles in the initial stage. Users can then progressively update the system according to their input and actions over time. In discussions with participants of the workshops, it was evident that some requirements were explicit and some quite implicit. Often, even the users themselves had not realized their specific requirements, and although the researchers could identify issues and needs during the discussion, they were not seen as explicit requirements. Therefore, accessible systems need to be able to learn from usage over time and to accumulate knowledge from the actions taken.

User profiling is utilized so that the system can filter the search results and provide the relevant content to the target user. An example of a venue search use case scenario is illustrated in Fig. 6.

On the design side, the EFA portal also included personalization features to provide customized content aggregation or syndication to users. To achieve these functionalities, Portlets (Portlet Specification and API 1.0) were deployed. EFA uses Portlets as pluggable content components that are integrated into the presentation layer. The content retrieved by a Portlet can be aggregated with the content of other Portlets to form a portal page, thus allowing for the further customization of the user’s personal space. Additionally, EFA, as a tourism content distributor, exploits the XML-based RSS feature to provide information to RSS-aware programs, which can check the feed for changes and deliver the new content to the end-users’ personalized web pages.

Still, because the degree of disability varies, users should be able to choose the products and services that best serve their individual abilities and preferences by trading off parameters such as available facilities, venue location or price. These tradeoffs are possible for a wide range of facilities, products and services that are not restricted by the veto principle. According to the veto principle, when a certain condition cannot be met, then the venue in question cannot be considered as suitable for the accessibility needs of the particular person. For example, a hotel with no elevator cannot be considered accessible for a full time wheelchair user (veto principle applies) but could be suitable for a person with a hearing impairment. Therefore, the provision of a flexible, personalized service should allow the end-user to make informed choices and compromises between different travel products according to his or her ability, preference, and individualized criteria.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper makes a contribution to theory by enhancing the existing knowledge regarding the requirements of the disabled users of an IS within the particular context of the tourism industry. The results validate the existing literature in terms of the tourism information requirements of people with disabilities and also identify three additional requirements, namely the following: (1) the veto principle, (2) the accessibility paths within a destination and (3) the door-to-door access map. These requirements are imperative because disabled travelers can only plan a holistic travel experience when they are fulfilled. Consequently, the failure to incorporate them into the system design will result in dissatisfaction and rejection.

This research also enhances the current understanding of the technical challenges involved in creating a system that addresses the tourism informational requirements of people with disabilities. The research identifies three major challenges in particular: interoperability, content integration, and personalization. To overcome these challenges, specialized information collection tools were created; a scalable database to store and manage the data was designed; and technologies such as RSS and Portlets were deployed to deliver dynamic, specialized content to the end-users. The results demonstrate how these challenges can be overcome so that the IS can successfully satisfy the disabled users’ requirements for a tourism information system.
