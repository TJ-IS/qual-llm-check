## Abstract

Network effects and switching costs are two major economic forces in information technology industries. Although the consequences of these mechanisms on competition and firm strategy have been well documented in the literature, research on their impact on customer behavior has received less attention. In this study, the authors investigate the role of personal network effects and switching costs in explaining customer choice in the Spanish mobile telecommunications industry. Personal network effects are present when an individual user’s utility increases more when some individuals adopt (social network) than when others do. Switching costs refer to costs associated with the process of switching from one provider to another. In addition, this paper studies the drivers of personal network effects and switching behavior. The results reveal that personal network effects and switching costs play a key role in determining mobile users’ choice: the probability that a customer selects a mobile phone company increases with the number of members of her social network already subscribed to that firm, and switching costs are significantly present in the mobile phone market making switching providers costly. Concerning the drivers of both mechanisms, the authors find that relationship characteristics (length, depth and breadth) and demographics differently affect persona network effects perceptions and consumer switching behavior. Implications for decision makers are discussed.

## Introduction

H <sup>igh-technology</sup> <sup>markets</sup> <sup>have</sup> <sup>been</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>focus</sup> <sup>of</sup>attention in recent academic research (Shapiro and attention in recent academic research (Shapiro and Varian, 1998; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). Most of the theoretical and empirical literature suggests that profitability in these markets is strongly driven by both the size and the stability of the customer base, which is mainly determined by network effects and switching costs (Farrell and Klemperer, 2007).

A good exhibits network effects when the utility of a user increases with the number of other users consuming the good (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). The traditional approach to network effects – which we refer to as classic network effects – gives the same weight (importance) to all the customers in the utility function. This implies that adding one customer to the market equally increases the utility of all the users who are already in the network. Recently, researchers have recognized that network effects may be localized (Birke and Swann, 2006; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). For instance, a mobile phone user gains more when her friends adopt than when a stranger does. In other words, there are different densities in the network (Ahuja, 2000) which should be taken into account to analyze user behavior. Personal or local network effects, microexternalities and strong ties are the terms used by prior research to refer to these effects (Swann, 2002; Sua´rez, 2005; Birke and Swann, 2006).

Switching costs can be defined as all the costs associated with the process of switching from one provider to another (Chen and Hitt, 2007). Klemperer (1995: 515) postulates that ‘in many markets consumers who have previously purchased from one firm have (or perceive) costs of switching to a competitor’s product, even when the two firms’ products are functionally identical.’ Although switching costs are present in many markets (Ausubel, 1991; Borenstein, 1991; Knittel, 1997; Stango, 2002), their importance is particularly significant in informationintensive markets (Shapiro and Varian, 1998; Amit and Zott, 2001; Chen and Hitt, 2007). Shapiro and Varian (1998: 111) emphasize that switching costs are the norm, not the exception, in the information economy.

Network effects and switching costs have been increasingly studied in the literature (Chen and Hitt, 2007; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). Prior research has mainly focused on investigating their role in competition and strategy in several markets, ranging from banking (Kim et al., 2003) to Internet portals (Goldfarb, 2006). However, research on the impact of network effects and switching costs on customer behavior remains limited. Areas in particular need of research are the following. First, a more thorough understanding of the nature of network effects is needed. Most studies investigating network effects take a classic approach and make the assumption that users give the same weight to all the individuals in the network. However, both theoretical studies and anecdotal evidence suggest that network effects in demand may be personal: utility increases more when some individuals adopt (social network). Thus, the density of the network is not homogeneous as the classic view of network effects assumes. Given the absence of empirical evidence on this subject, we believe that it is important to examine the extent to which customer behavior is affected by personal network effects. Second, switching costs and network effects are two major economic forces that should be simultaneously considered when investigating consumer behavior. So far, the literature has mainly examined the role of network effects and switching costs separately (Chen and Hitt, 2002; Birke and Swann, 2006). Although these studies can provide some insight into the way these mechanisms operate in driving consumer behavior, omitting one of them can lead to unrealistic results and bias in the estimations. Studies have particularly ignored the joint effect of these mechanisms when investigating the choice of supplier in informationintensive markets.

To fill these gaps, we have the following research objectives. First, we aim to study the joint effect of personal network effects and switching costs on user choice behavior. Second, we want to investigate the drivers of personal network effects and switching behavior in terms of customer-related characteristics. To do this, we use data from the mobile communications industry that combines information at the firm level (firm strategies and service attributes) with information at the customer level (relationship characteristics and demographics). We conduct our empirical analysis in the Spanish market. Mobile communications in Spain are a mature industry in terms of market penetration, with three companies operating at the moment of carrying out our research.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the following section, we present an overview of the network effects and switching costs literature, with special attention to the mobile communications industry. The subsequent section discusses the sources of network effects and switch ing costs in mobile communications. The methodology used to model the effects of personal network effects and switching costs on customer behavior and the drivers of personal network effects and switching behavior will be discussed in the further section. Another section describes the data and variables, and the subsequent section presents the empirical results and key findings. We conclude with a discussion of the main findings and contributions of our study.

An overview on network effects and switching costs literature According to the existing literature, there is an important gap between the theoretical and empirical research on network effects and switching costs (Stango, 2002; Chen and Hitt, 2007; Grzybowski, 2007; Viard, 2007). This section reviews the theoretical and empirical literature on both mechanisms and shows the increasing interest among academics and practitioners in studying them in mobile telecommunications services.

From a theoretical point of view, research on network effects has mainly focused on their impact on competition and firm strategy. When network effects are present, the size of the installed base is a key asset for firms (Shapiro and Varian, 1998). Prior research has shown that, in growing markets, firms or technologies with a high market share are able to obtain a higher level of profitability (Farrell and Saloner, 1985; Katz and Shapiro, 1985, 1994; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). Taken to an extreme, network effects may create winner-takes-all markets (Arthur, 1996), in which one company emerges as dominant, and the other firms, which may have superior products or technologies, must abandon the market (Shapiro and Varian, 1998; Liebowitz, 2002).

Switching costs have important consequences on competition and firm strategy as well. Most theoretical studies conclude that the existence of switching costs confers market power on firms, allowing them to (1) charge higher prices, (2) reduce product or service quality, (3) create entry barriers and (4) obtain abnormal returns (Klemperer, 1987; Lieberman and Montgomery, 1988; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). As a result, firms develop new strategies in order to maximize profitability – for instance, bargain then rip-off strategies (see, e.g., Klemperer, 1995 or, recently, Farrell and Klemperer, 2007 for an excellent survey).

From an empirical point of view, the current availability of more detailed information, both at user and at firm level, has increased the amount of empirical research on network effects and switching costs. Concerning the first mechanism, most empirical studies find support for the theoretical postulates in that (1) network effects are very important in high-technology markets and (2) they significantly influence market competition. For instance, Gandal (1994) analyzes the spreadsheet market and concludes that network effects are very important and that there is a prize for compatibility with Lotus. In the same market, Brynjolfsson and Kemerer (1996) find that the price of the market leader in the late 1980s was much influenced by the size of its installed customer base. More recently, Dranove and Gandal (2003) study the battle between DVD and DIVX and show that the preannouncement of a competing technology (DIVX) delayed the adoption of DVD by many consumers.

Empirical research on switching costs has also paid particular attention to information technology industries. Consistent with the theoretical arguments, Knittel (1997) finds that switching costs confer market power on longdistance telephone carriers. Viard (2007) investigates the effect of switching costs on competition in the toll-free services market and finds that a drop in switching costs leads to an increase in price competition. Chen and Hitt (2002) investigate switching costs in the online brokerage industry and find that they vary substantially across firms. The authors show that product design and retention strategies are at the origin of these differences. Chen and Forman (2006) study switching costs in the market for telecommunications equipment and show that these costs can be strategically managed by firms despite the presence of open standards in the industry. Kim et al. (2006) incorporate switching costs in order to explain users’ intentions to switch their e-mail service provider. They conclude that switching costs have only a relative importance in explaining user switching behavior.

Network effects and switching costs have been increasingly studied in mobile communications. Here, we summarize some of the most important studies in this industry. Birke and Swann (2006) explore the role of network effects in consumer choice of mobile phone supplier in the United Kingdom. They find that (1) there are classic network effects even in the absence of price differences between on-net and off-net calls and that (2) individual choice is heavily influenced by the choices made in the individual’s household – personal network effects. Doganoglu and Grzybowski (2007) study demand for mobile telecommunications in Germany, and their results suggest that network effects play a significant role in the diffusion of mobile services. Corrocher and Zirulia (2008) also study personal network effects in mobile communications services. They conclude that users are highly heterogeneous with respect to the evaluation of personal network effects when choosing mobile providers, and that this heterogeneity is basically related to specific characteristics such as individual innovativeness and patterns of mobile phone usage.

As for switching costs, Shy (2002) calculates them in the Israeli mobile communications sector and concludes that their magnitude is significant (it is close to the investment necessary to buy a new handset). Lee et al. (2006) investigate the impact of number portability on switching costs and competition in the Korean mobile telecommunications industry. The authors show that number portability significantly reduces switching costs. Grzybowski (2007) empirically estimates switching costs in mobile communications in the United Kingdom and concludes that users face significant switching costs that vary according to the network operators.

Based on this literature review, we can infer that there is a growing interest in understanding network effects and switching costs in information-intensive markets. Using aggregate data, several studies provide a clear understanding of the impact of network effects and switching costs on market competition. In most of the cases, they constitute a weapon to create market power. In spite of these attempts, there are still some areas in need of research. For instance, less attention has been paid to the analysis of the joint impact of network effects and switching costs on customer choice behavior. Thus, an extra effort should be made to a better comprehension of the impact of both elements on users’ behavior. The distinction between classic and personal network effects in determining users’ choice can be particularly interesting and is one of our goals in what follows.

## Sources of network effects and switching costs in the mobile communications industry

In this section, we aim to illustrate the most common sources of network effects and switching costs in the mobile communications industry.

The empirical literature has suggested that network effects are present in the mobile communications industry even when the networks are perfectly compatible (Kim and Kwon, 2003; Birke and Swann, 2006). In this market, classic network effects mainly arise due to the differences between on-net and off-net tariffs (Laffont et al., 1998).<sup>1</sup> Operators charge different prices depending on whether the call made by the user is directed to a member of the same operator (low or on-net tariff) or is made to a member outside the operator (high or off-net tariff). Consequently, the larger the installed base of the operator, the lower the expected communications cost for its users.

Personal network effects also mainly come from the specific tariffs offered by the operators. In this case, the benefit for the user is not associated with the calls directed to the members of the same operator, but with the calls made to a particular group of users that constitutes her social subset (family, friends, y).<sup>2</sup> The reasoning is that, although all users in a network are a potential source of network effects, some users matter more than others (Birke and Swann, 2006). Thus, network benefits are not homogeneous (Ahuja, 2000) but consumers find more benefits from interacting with their social subset than with the rest of the installed base. Consequently, customer choice behavior will be more influenced by her social subset than by the rest of the users in the network.

With regard to switching costs, number portability gives users the right to keep their phone number when switching from one service provider to another. The absence of this possibility has been regarded as one of the most important switching costs within the industry, causing customers a lot of trouble from having to inform people about their new number and missing valuable calls (NERA, 2003). Number portability has previously been analyzed in the context of mobile telecommunications (Grzybowski, 2005; Buehler et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2006; Viard, 2007), and its absence clearly constitutes an important exit barrier. In an increasing number of countries, users now keep their number when changing operators. For instance, in Spain, mobile number portability was finally implemented in November 2000. Ported numbers have grown significantly in recent years: in 2001, there were 240,000 ported numbers, and in 2006, the number was 3,454,000 (CMT, 2007).

Switching costs in mobile communications are also originated by the impossibility of unlocking handsets.

Handsets are often locked by manufacturers at the request of mobile operators to be used exclusively within their networks. Thus, users interested in changing their current operator have to invest in a new handset. In most countries, operators and handset manufacturers have recently been forced to unlock handsets.<sup>3</sup>

Loyalty or reward programs are an important source of switching costs in many markets. These programs reward the loyalty of customers with discounts and enhanced services (Bolton et $a l . _ { \cdot }$ 2000; Hartmann and Viard, 2007) and have been implemented by mobile operators in most countries. They constitute an important barrier to switching, as changing mobile phone suppliers implies losing the rewards and discounts accumulated throughout the relationship.

Finally, additional sources of switching costs, not directly related to mobile phone services, are the following: (1) psychological or noneconomic costs (Burnham et $a l . ,$ 2003); (2) contractual or artificial costs deliberately created by firm strategies (Klemperer, 1995) and (3) the time and effort required to properly evaluate the alternatives and take an informed decision (Jones et al., 2002).

## Research methodology

This study investigates (1) the joint impact of personal network effects and switching costs on customer choice and (2) the drivers of personal network effects and switching behavior. To accomplish the first objective, the random utility framework is used (McFadden, 1974). According to this framework, customer i faces a choice of J alternatives in her choice set. Assuming a utility-maximizing behavior, the selected option will be the one with the highest utility (Ben-Akiva and Lerman, 1985). The utility $( U _ { i j } )$ that customer i obtains from selecting firm j (out of J) is a function of the attributes associated with the alternative (systematic component, $V _ { i j } )$ and of a random disturbance (random component, $\varepsilon _ { i j } ) \colon$

$$
U _ {i j} = V _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}\tag{1}
$$

In order to derive an operational model, the random component $( \varepsilon _ { i } )$ is assumed to be independently and identically distributed with type I extreme value distribution (McFadden, 1974).<sup>4</sup> Choice probabilities can then be derived as follows:

$$
P _ {i} (j) = \frac {\exp^ {V _ {i j}}}{\sum_ {j} \exp^ {V _ {i j}}}\tag{2}
$$

In our empirical setting, the choices are mobile phone operators. Following prior research about the drivers of customer choice of mobile phone supplier (NERA, 2003; Birke and Swann, 2006; Lee et al., 2006), the systematic component of utility $( V _ { i j } )$ includes alternative-specific factors. In our model, we introduce the following variables: personal network effects $( P N E _ { i j } )$ , a switching dummy variable $( S W _ { i j } )$ , price (P ) and firm size $( S Z _ { j } )$ . To account for the impact of nonobserved variables on choice $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , differences in quality), a vector of alternative-specific constant terms is added $( \beta _ { 0 j } )$ . The random disturbance $( \varepsilon _ { i j } )$ captures the effect of customer-specific tastes and unmeasured variables on customer utility. This leads to the following expression for the utility model:

$$
\begin{array}{c} U _ {i j} = \beta_ {0 j} + \beta^ {P N E} P N E _ {i j} + \beta_ {j} ^ {S W} S W _ {i j} \\ + \beta^ {P} P _ {j} + \beta^ {S Z} S Z _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Four things should be mentioned about this model. First, following the work of Chen and Hitt (2002), the cost of switching is introduced into the model by including a vector of firm-specific dummy variables $( S W _ { i j } ) _ { : }$ , which measures whether the customer of a particular firm would bear a cost of switching by choosing another provider (potential switcher). Therefore, the firm-specific parameters accompanying the switching variable $( S W _ { i j } ) , \mathrm { ~ } \beta _ { j } ^ { S W } ;$ are interpreted as the marginal utility derived from switching service providers (Lee et al., 2006). It is important to note that different approaches to estimate switching costs have been suggested in the literature. The indirect approach uses aggregated firm information to infer switching costs (Knittel, 1997). Another stream of research focuses on some of the categories of switching costs – procedural, economic, artificial and psychological costs (Jones et al., 2002; Burnham et $a \hat { l . } ,$ 2003). Although this approach has the advantage of accounting for the contribution of these switching costs categories to the overall magnitude of these costs, it has the inconvenience of requiring very detailed information about the customer– firm relationship and about the different categories of switching costs. In addition, considering only a subset of these consumer behaviors and switching costs categories would lead to an underestimation of the magnitude of these costs. The direct approach, the one employed here, uses information on individual consumer behavior to identify and quantify switching costs. This approach provides the best information on switching costs, because it allows the identification of their existence and their measurement (NERA, 2003). It is also consistent with the increasing number of researchers who adopt this direct approach to investigate switching costs (Chen and Hitt, 2002, 2007; Lee et al., 2006; Grzybowski, 2007).

Second, consumers may continue to buy from a specific service provider over time for different reasons. Some of them have been explicitly considered in our model (personal network effects, switching costs). Other factors, such as product or service quality and brand image, are difficult to observe and cannot be explicitly introduced in our model. To account for the impact of these variables on choice, alternative-specific constant terms are added to the deterministic component of the utility function $( \beta _ { 0 j } )$ These constants are also known as ‘bias constants,’ because they represent customer preferences toward or against a specific alternative.

Third, with respect to personal network effects, it is important to note that similar choices among people in a network can respond to geographic proximity or to differences in service attributes among providers (i.e., one provider offers full coverage or better services compared with the others). Or, perhaps, customers in a network work for the same company and are required to contract mobile phone services from the same operator. However, in our empirical analysis, we focus on customers and do not consider business users. This reduces the probability of similar choices being attributable to the abovementioned reasons. Moreover, our empirical analysis is carried out in a medium-sized city in which there is full coverage of all the operators competing in the market. As a result, geographic coverage is not expected to affect customer choice in our analysis.<sup>5</sup>

Fourth, the estimation of the model parameters is accomplished using the conditional logit model, which simultaneously estimates individual logistic choice equations for each company and for each customer. Thus, the total number of data points used to obtain the model parameters consists of I individuals  J firms $( I \times J )$ . The main concern of our analysis is the estimation of the parameters accompanying the personal network effects and switching costs variables $( P N E _ { i j }$ and $S W _ { i j } )$

Additionally, in this study, we aim to provide some insight into the drivers of personal network effects perceptions and switching behavior.<sup>6</sup> To do so, we separately model them as functions of customer-specific variables – relationship characteristics and customer demographics. Both prior research (Birke and Swann, 2006) and anecdotal evidence suggest that customers are heterogeneous in the importance that they give to personal network effects when selecting service providers (Corrocher and Zirulia, 2008). That is, while some customers are very influenced by the choices made by people in their social network, others do not pay much attention to this variable when deciding which operator to select. Customers are also heterogeneous in their willingness to switch service providers. They bear different switching costs and behave differently within the relationship, resulting in different propensities to switch service providers. Thus, to gain more insight into the drivers of both personal network effects perceptions and switching behavior, we estimate the following equations:

$$
P N p _ {i} = \alpha_ {1} R c _ {i} + \alpha_ {2} C d _ {i} + \tau_ {i}\tag{4}
$$

$$
S B e _ {i} = \gamma_ {1} R c _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} C d _ {i} + \phi_ {i}\tag{5}
$$

where PNp is defined as the customer perception of personal network effects and $S B e _ { i }$ is the customer switching behavior; $R c _ { i }$ is a vector containing relationship characteristics, that is, the factors that describe the development and progression of a relationship over time: the length, depth and breadth (Bolton et al., 2004); Cd includes additional factors (customer demographics) that potentially explain the dependent constructs; and $\tau _ { i }$ and $\phi _ { i }$ are the error terms with logistic distributions.

## Sample and variables

This research is carried out in the Spanish mobile communications industry. Some important characteristics of this industry should be mentioned. First, three companies provide the mobile phone services in the Spanish market:<sup>7</sup> Movistar, Vodafone and Orange.<sup>8</sup> Second, the penetration rate in the Spanish market is very high, slightly over 100% at the end of 2007 (GWM, 2008).<sup>9</sup> Third, the mobile phone industry is considered one of the most important sectors, not only in the communications field but also in the economy as a whole, and it has been the focus of attention in prior studies dealing with network effects and switching costs (Kim and Kwon, 2003; Birke and Swann, 2006; Lee et al., 2006; Doganoglu and Grzybowski, 2007; Grzybowski, 2007) and with its diffusion (Gruber and Verboven, 2001a, b; Wareham et al., 2004).

To estimate the demand model for mobile communications, we collected information from multiple data sources. Information on customer choices and mobile user behavior (relationship characteristics) was obtained from a survey carried out in November 2006 in a medium-sized Spanish city. A total of 256 young customers – ranging from 20 to 27 years old – were randomly selected from the local university database and agreed to participate in an academic study. A questionnaire was given to them that contained questions related to their mobile user behavior (actual choice of supplier, switching behavior, service usage, purchase of additional services) and demographics. After eliminating respondents with missing data and with a low level of knowledge about the mobile phone service,<sup>10</sup> a total sample of 221 individuals was retained for the empirical analyses. Information on customer characteristics was combined with data about service attributes and firm practices of the three companies that offered mobile phone services in the Spanish market when the survey was carried out: Movistar, Vodafone and Orange. These data come from the Global Wireless Matrix database.

Table 1 describes the variables included in the three equations to be estimated in our study (equations (3)–(5)). In equation (3), our main focus is on the measurement of personal network effects and switching costs. The operationalization of personal network effects is as follows. We measure this variable as the percentage of customer i’s social network (family and friends) that is subscribed to a particular operator. That is, we have three data points per customer: (1) percentage of her social network subscribed to operator 1 (Movistar); (2) percentage of her social network subscribed to operator 2 (Vodafone); and (3) percentage of her social network subscribed to operator 3 (Orange). Similar to Chen and Hitt (2002), switching costs are introduced into the empirical analysis as a dummy variable that measures whether the customer of a particular firm would bear switching costs. That is, if customer i is subscribed to company $^ { 1 , }$ switching costs would be incurred if she changes to firms 2 or 3 (dummy variable takes the value 1), and no cost would be borne if she stays with firm 1 (dummy variable takes the value 0). Price and firm size are introduced into the model for control purposes: price is measured as the average revenue per user (ARPU), and firm size is operationalized as the total number of subscribers.

Equations (4) and (5) aim to complement equation (3) and to provide some insight into the factors explaining the heterogeneity in personal network effects perceptions and switching behavior across consumers. In equation (4), the dependent variable is defined as the customer perception of personal network effects (PNp ). The questionnaire contains a single item about the customer perception of personal network effects: ‘When selecting a mobile phone operator, how important are the choices made by your relatives, partner and friends?’ ranging from 1 – Not at all important, to 7 – Very important. Based on this measure, we divided the sample into two groups: (1) those with higher perceptions of personal network effects (a rating higher than 4 in the seven-point Likert scale), and (2) those with lower perceptions of personal network effects (a rating lower than or equal to 4). Thus, the dependent variable (PNp ) will take the value 1 for customers who value personal network effects more, and 0 otherwise. In equation (5), similar to Chen and Hitt (2002), the dependent variable is the customer switching behavior (SBe ), and it is operationalized as a dummy variable that equals 1 if the customer switches, and 0 otherwise.

## Estimation and results

The equations to be estimated in this study are the following:

## Discussion and conclusions

Network effects and switching costs are two major forces in information-intensive markets. This study investigates their impact on customer behavior and the drivers of both personal network effects perceptions and switching behavior in the mobile communications industry. In doing so, we aim to extend current research in two important ways. First, more empirical research is needed in order to better understand network effects and switching costs in hightechnology markets. Second, prior studies mainly focus on their impact on competition and firm strategy, while research on their influence on customer behavior remains limited.

The research objectives of this study were twofold. First, we wanted to understand the joint impact of network effects and switching costs on customer choice. This can be of interest to academics and practitioners because the studies carried out to date have usually considered switching costs and network effects separately. Second, we attempted to show how certain relationship characteristics and customer attributes may help explain the differences in customers’ perceptions of personal network effects and their switching behavior.

Starting with the first objective, our results indicate that personal network effects and switching costs contribute to explaining the choice of mobile operator. Concerning personal network effects, we find that the probability that a customer selects a specific company increases with the number of members of her social network already subscribed to that firm. This is an interesting result, as it implies that the increase in a user’s utility as a consequence of adding a new consumer to the market varies depending on whether the new consumer is a member of her social network or not. Thus, we extend current research by showing that network effects in demand are personal, that is, utility does not only increase with every new adopter, but especially when some individuals adopt.

This result has important implications for decision makers. Managers have mainly focused on increasing both switching barriers (by implementing loyalty programs or creating artificial costs) and the size of the installed base (by using aggressive customer acquisition strategies) to increase firm profitability. Our results suggest that firms should pay more attention to social networks, as customer choice is mainly driven by the behavior of their social group. Therefore, understanding how personal network effects work is a key strategic issue for academics and practitioners interested in expanding the size of the installed base. In the mobile phone industry, operators can manage their growth in two different ways: (1) they can use aggressive acquisition strategies – that is, subsidizing the new user’s handset, which can be very costly and resource consuming; and/or (2) they can deploy appealing tariff plans in order to attract not only new consumers but also members belonging to the social network of existing consumers who may potentially benefit from these advantageous tariffs. In fact, companies understand competition in this way. Prices based on social networks are the rule and not the exception in the industry and the 3G technology has brought the possibility to reinforce the power of social networks integrating community services such as Facebook, Messenger, Flickr or YouTube. Offering these services at a very low price (even free) in a first stage is a good example of strategy to create personal network effects.

Concerning switching costs, we find that they are significantly present in mobile communications and that they differ across network operators. This is consistent with prior research in this field (Grzybowski, 2007), which shows that customers bear different switching costs depending on the mobile provider to which they belong. In our view, this is an interesting result, as it suggests that switching costs are affected by the company the user switches from. Therefore, to increase the effectiveness of a firm’s customer acquisition strategy, managers should be aware that the customer switching cost depends, to a great extent, on the company the targeted customer belongs to. This would suggest a customized acquisition strategy for each user instead of a standard rule to attract new users.

To accomplish the second objective, we have investigated the drivers of both personal network effects perceptions and switching behavior. Regarding the former, we can conclude that relationship characteristics – depth, length and breath – are important drivers of personal network effects perceptions. Users with an intense service usage seem to be more sensitive to personal network effects. This is an intuitive result, as users who spend more time on their mobile phone find more benefits from the reduced tariffs associated with calls directed to their social subset. Time in the market and the acquisition of additional services negatively influence the user perception of personal network effects. These results are in line with our prior expectations, as customers who have stayed longer are likely to be those with more influence on the decisions of the network members, while customers who have recently acquired the service are those whose decisions are expected to be more influenced by their network. The negative impact of the acquisition of additional services is also an intuitive result. Customers buying additional mobile services (which normally involve better economic conditions or special tariffs) will be those less interested in personal network effects, as they are currently benefiting from the advantages provided by these additional services. Our results also show that more sophisticated users have a higher perception of personal network effects. This means that consumers with a deep knowledge of the set of tariffs or services offered by the company are more aware of the benefits derived from communicating with a particular group of users.

From a practical point of view, these results can be of interest to mobile communications managers in that they can provide valuable information about the profile of the users who have a higher perception of personal network effects. For instance, in line with our previous reasoning, an interesting strategy would consist of encouraging these customers to form social networks by offering better tariffs and economic conditions to them and their network.

Our results reveal that, of the relationship characteristics, only breadth (purchase of additional products/services) has a significant influence on switching behavior. Although this could be seen as an unexpected result in that a significant influence of these factors on relationship duration has been found in noncontractual relationships (Reinartz and Kumar 2003), it is in line with recent research that empirically demonstrates that firm strategies are more important than customer relationship characteristics in explaining switching behavior (Chen and Hitt, 2002; Chen and Forman, 2006). However, our results show that other variables (other than relationship characteristics) play a central role in the explanation of the customer switching behavior in mobile communications. For instance, we find that the type of contract (postpaid vs prepaid subscription) is positively related to the customer switching behavior: compared with prepaid consumers, postpaid users have a higher probability of switching mobile operators. This is not an expected result. Postpaid customers are believed to develop stronger ties with the company and to find switching providers more costly than prepaid users, which would lead us to think of a lower switching probability. A possible explanation for this counter-intuitive result is that postpaid users are targeted by competitors that use aggressive marketing strategies to increase the size of the installed base and acquire more profitable customers (i.e., postpaid customers are frequently offered free handsets or special economic conditions). Our results suggest that the effect of these aggressive strategies dominates and that postpaid customers tend to be less loyal than prepaid ones. Consistent with our expectations, we find that sophistication is positively related to switching behavior, as customers with a deep and better knowledge of the service are able to better assess the alternatives in the market and to select the best option at each time period.

In summary, this paper tries to contribute to a better understanding of network effects and switching costs in the mobile communications industry. Future lines of research should consider a longitudinal perspective to study the two mechanisms in order to develop a more accurate analysis of the relationships between the variables introduced into our study. A promising avenue for further research would consist of considering the distinction between classic and personal network effects and evaluating their differential impact on customer choice. In addition, future research could analyze the role of additional factors in driving customer perceptions of network effects and switching behavior. For instance, considering more customer-specific attributes or introducing firm strategies might help to better explain the drivers of both personal network effects and switching behavior.
