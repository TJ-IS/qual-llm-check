## a b s t r a c t

Market demand uncertainty and time-based competition make capacity investment and managerial incentive decisions for service facilities such as high-end diagnostic medical imaging centers, modern IT services, or contract manufacturing shops particularly challenging. These facilities compete on service quality, short queuing times and speed. Therefore, having insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity can be economically devastating for them. Given the high up-front costs involved, <sup>fi</sup>rms want to make sure that they neither over- nor under-invest in service capacity. These problems get exasperated by the fact that typically <sup>fi</sup>rms are unfamiliar with the local market conditions and do not closely observe the demand-generating efforts of the hired managers. Most prior studies of cost allocation methodologies, contract design, and service resource management tend to address these aspects of the problem separately. They ignore the interaction effects between the capacity decisions and the managerial adverse selection and moral hazard issues, which are crucial elements for successfully running services with <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity, random arrivals, and stochastic service times. Our paper instead focuses on the development of an integrated-approach to the simultaneous design of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient managerial contracts and of capacity planning for capital intensive service facilities. We derive optimal linear contracting structures under information asymmetry between the <sup>fi</sup>rms and management, and analyze their impact on capacity decisions, service levels, service volumes, and the allocations of costs. Surprisingly, we prove that even though a franchise (charge-back) contract induces the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort from the manager, it is not always the best choice for the <sup>fi</sup>rms as it may lead to inferior pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for them. In fact, our results explain why a <sup>fi</sup>rm's eventual contract choice should be a function of its prior on the probability distribution of the local market demand. We also explain when it may be optimal (for both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the manager) to charge the manager up front a <sup>fi</sup>xed franchising fee that is even greater than the total costs of capacity. Our study applies to many capital-intensive and congestion-prone service systems, where the success of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant up-front capacity investments also hinges on the daily operations of those facilities run by hired managers—who typically possess speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge—that gives them a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant information advantage.

## 1. Introduction

Effectively managing service centers such as call centers, computerized diagnostic imaging facilities, data centers, SaaS businesses, and telecommunication networks has always been a challenging task. Owners of the centers (<sup>fi</sup>rms) are responsible for investing in capacity, which is often capital intensive and involves signi<sup>fi</sup>cant up-front <sup>fi</sup>xed costs for equipment, software, and installation. While high utilization is a critical pro<sup>fi</sup>tability driver, facing uncertain market demand, <sup>fi</sup>rms also have to maintain an acceptable service level and relatively short waiting times in order to compete in the market successfully. Managers of these centers are often contracted to run them as pro<sup>fi</sup>t centers. As such, they are usually responsible for directing daily activities and generating demand through additional marketing or service quality efforts. For example, we have seen that at most free-standing radiology facilities that are run by the national networks in the US (such as Insight Imaging and Radnet Inc.), the local managers are responsible for staf<sup>fi</sup>ng, scheduling, marketing, and other demand-generating activities in their territory.

What makes the issue complicated is that managers, as the agents running the service centers, often possess private information about local market conditions, referral patterns, demographic preferences, and their own demand-generating efforts, and they may not willingly or truthfully share that information with their <sup>fi</sup>rms. Working with a national <sup>fi</sup>rm that operates high-end diagnostic medical imaging centers in various states, we had to make the correct capacity investments in each market and to obtain the true market demand information from the local managers who are typically being incentivized by the patient volume at their center. If the local managers overestimate the demand, to allow ample capacity and fast turnaround times for all exams, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will <sup>fi</sup>nd itself over-investing in capacity, such as MRI, CT, or PET-CT equipment. On the other hand, if the local managers underestimate the demand, in order to have a lower ‘service quota’ in their contract, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will under-invest in capacity, resulting in long lines and lost pro<sup>fi</sup>t opportunities.

In this paper, we outline the solution to this problem that has been motivated by our practical experience and by the latest research on optimal mechanism design. In our model, the manager (agent) possesses private information regarding the local market and the level of his own demand-generating effort; facing uncertain market demand and the combined agency issues, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to make upfront capacity investment decisions and design incentive contracts to motive the manager to truthfully share his speci<sup>fi</sup>c market knowledge and to exert the desired level of effort to generate demand. Several studies [4,10,22,27] have pointed out that optimal mechanisms predicated by economic theory are often sophisticated and rarely used in the real world, whereas linear contracts are commonly used in practice. Our focus is on evaluating three effective linear performance-based incentive contracts under information asymmetry: a uniform contract, a variable-rate contract, and a charge-back contract. We show that the uniform contract, which offers the same contract terms regardless of market conditions, fails to elicit market information from the manager and leads to a distorted capacity investment decision. The variable-rate and charge-back (franchise) contracts, on the other hand, can successfully elicit true market information from the manager. We show that the latter even induces the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best (full-information) effort level from the manager and hence leads to the optimal capacity investment, the same as the full-information level.

We embed a queuing model within the principal-agent framework because most service <sup>fi</sup>rms are constrained by a <sup>fi</sup>nite installed capacity, yet they have to deliver reasonably fast turnaround times when both demand and service rates could be random and outside their control in the short run. Integrating the capacity decision with the incentive contract design and the service level constraints, therefore, enables us to address a complicated and yet practical problem that has not been fully studied before. We not only derive analytical solutions for this intricate problem but also <sup>fi</sup>nd some interesting results. For example, we show that if the <sup>fi</sup>rm selects the proper <sup>fi</sup>xed and variable charge terms, its franchise contract can induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best demand-generating effort and it will successfully elicit truthful market information from the manager; however franchise contracting fails to produce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best pro<sup>fi</sup>t and it may not always be the best choice for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The <sup>fi</sup>xed charge term in the franchise contract is naturally associated with the classical <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocation. It has been shown in managerial accounting that <sup>fi</sup>rms can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocation because the allocation may in<sup>fl</sup>uence agents' consumption of perquisites or serve as a proxy for the dif<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-calculate opportunity costs [28]. Overall, the accounting literature tends to emphasize the role of <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocation as an effective cost control to hold agents accountable so that they will not over-consume resources at the expense of the <sup>fi</sup>rm [2,15,27]. We extend that literature and show here in a service queuing setting <sup>fi</sup>rms should also use cost allocation as a way to prevent the managers from under-consuming resources. A high capacity request is associated with a high <sup>fi</sup>xed charge, and the managers have a natural incentive to request low capacity when they privately observe a high-demand market. By doing so, they can shirk in their demand-generating effort and gain greater utility. But, underconsuming resources can be harmful to the <sup>fi</sup>rms as well, because low capacity restricts the imaging center from serving more patients, for instance, and also causes long patients' waiting times, which translate to high delay costs and potential losses in future revenue due to a degraded service-level reputation. We show that with proper <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocations <sup>fi</sup>rms can avoid both over-investing and underinvesting in capacity.

Our work contributes to the literature in multiple ways. First, we develop and analyze a new contracting problem in a service environment in which <sup>fi</sup>rms compete on meeting certain response times and local managers possess valuable private information on market demand. These managers can in<sup>fl</sup>uence market demand by exerting demand-generating effort, such as marketing promotions or by providing an outstanding customer service experience. Second, we optimally integrate <sup>fi</sup>rms' operations capacity decision with their incentive contract decision for a business environment with stochastic arrivals of customers and randomly distributed service times per customer. We investigate three practical mechanisms and show that two of them can successfully solicit the manager's market information. Third, we demonstrate the value of the local manager's private information and prove that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's optimal contract choice is a function of the market demand distribution. As expected, we show that the cost of the manager's private information is associated with market demand variance, with a higher demand variance corresponding to a higher information cost. However, the manager's market knowledge is more valuable when there is a low demand variance. Overall, this research provides guidelines for <sup>fi</sup>rms that deal with congestion-prone systems and sheds light on how to effectively manage service facilities with combined moral hazard and adverse selection issues.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst review the relevant literature in Section 2. Then we present the model setting in Section 3 and analyze the benchmark case with full information in Section 4. In Section 5, we derive three contracts for the information asymmetry case in which the manager's effort is not observable and the manager has private information regarding local market condition. We explain why two of these contracts can effectively solicit true market information from the manager. We then analyze the <sup>fi</sup>rm's optimal contract choice and evaluate the factors affecting the installed capacity, the average throughput rates, and the expected waiting times in Section 6 and conclude the paper with practice guidelines for cost allocation and incentive design for service systems in Section 7.

## 2. Literature review

This paper is related to research that applies agency theory as well as the service resource management and accounting cost allocation literature. Prior research on IT resource management has modeled IT service centers as queuing systems and mostly focused on capacity allocation within a <sup>fi</sup>rm through an internal pricing scheme [6,7,18]. In these works, demand is exogenous and there is no agency issue. Clearly, this model setting does not apply to many modern IT service centers that provide services to external clients because external random demand cannot by controlled by the proposed internal transfer pricing scheme, and it fails for any service centers that do experience agency issues.

Marketing literature that applies the principal-agent framework, for example, Basu et al. [3] and Lal and Srinivasan [14], often focuses on the compensation contract design of a sales force selling a commodity good with an unlimited supply and does not work for a service environment. Harris et al. [8] study effective resource allocation in a manufacturing setting under incomplete information. This is one of the early works on mechanism design. In their model, agents have private information on their productivity and effort levels. The objective of the <sup>fi</sup>rm is to minimize the costs of producing a given level of output by designing an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism. However, since the output level is given there is no demand uncertainty in their model.

Among recent research that combines queuing models with agency theory, Jiang and Seidmann [12] model the capacity management and contract design issue of a service organization with a <sup>fi</sup>nite capacity. In their model, the service demand can be affected by the agent's marketing effort, and the agent is both risk and effort averse. They extend prior agency theory literature by introducing delay costs and the capacity decision into the model and taking an integrated approach to derive the optimal incentive contract and capacity investment for the principal. Plambeck and Zenios [20] study dynamic incentive contracts for make-to-stock production systems. They deal with a moral hazard problem in which the agent dynamically controls the production rate and has no in<sup>fl</sup>uence on market demand. Kim et al. [13] analyze and compare two contracts—a material contract and a performance-based contract—in the after-sales product support context in which a multitasking agent is responsible for service part inventory and product reliability decisions. They focus on a moral hazard problem only, and in their model the agent has no in<sup>fl</sup>uence on demand. Baiman et al. [1] also introduce a queuing model in the principal-agent framework, but they model a manufacturing environment, and the agent's effort affects the throughput rate of production, not the demand arrival rate.

Porteus and Whang [21] study a similar multiple agent problem in a manufacturing setting as in Harris et al. [8], but they introduce demand uncertainty and stochastic capacity and focus on the moral hazard issue (agents' efforts) without information asymmetry (no hidden productivity). They propose two incentive contracts; one of them is a franchising plan that achieves the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best outcomes. Hasija et al. [9] study call-center outsourcing contracts. In their model, the call-center vendor has private information on its staff's ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (i.e., the service rate). The client offers outsourcing contracts that specify <sup>fi</sup>nancial and service quality terms. In their work, the vendor makes a staf<sup>fi</sup>ng decision that affects the call center's service quality, but it has no in<sup>fl</sup>uence on market demand.

While we also embed a queuing model in the principal-agent framework, our research differs from these papers because we study a combined moral hazard (hidden action) and adverse selection (hidden information) problem in a service environment. The agent possesses private information on market demand, and the agent's effort can also in<sup>fl</sup>uence market demand. In a service queuing setting, the principal needs to make a capacity investment decision while facing uncertain market demand and service standard constraints and design proper incentive contracts to motivate the agent to take desired actions. We propose a charge-back contract that is similar to the franchise contract in [21], and we show that the contract can induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort from the agent, but unlike that in [21], it cannot produce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best pro<sup>fi</sup>t and may not even produce the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t for the principal.

Our paper is also related to Chen [5], in which the agent (a sales agent with a negative exponential utility function) has private information on market conditions and exerts selling effort that is not observable by the principal. The principal makes an inventory stocking decision and designs a compensation scheme (linear contract) to induce the agent to truthfully reveal market conditions so that it can better match supply with demand. The focus of [5], however, is to derive the corresponding Gonik's scheme and compare it with a menu of linear contracts. We also study a combined moral hazard and adverse selection problem, but in a service setting, and we focus on evaluating different incentive contracts and recommending the optimal contracting choice for the principal.

This research is also connected to accounting cost allocation literature [2,15,19,25,26,28]. These papers all provide justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the allocation of <sup>fi</sup>xed costs to agents, but they either do not consider agency issue or only consider the cases in which the agents possess private information on the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of resources or their own types or productivity, and they do not model a service environment and the impact of capacity investment and agents' effort on service quality. Our paper instead models a practical service environment with complicated agency issues, and we further demonstrate that incentive contracts that involve <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocation terms can effectively motivate the agent to communicate his private market information to the principal so that he will not over-demand or underrequest capacity from the principal.

Overall, we extend the literature by considering incentive contract design together with operations resource management. Our work provides a complete solution for <sup>fi</sup>rms dealing with <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity planning decisions combined with adverse selection and moral hazard issues in an uncertain service environment characterized by timebased competition, whereas prior studies on cost allocation methodologies, contract design, and service resource management tend to address these aspects of the problem separately

## 3. The model

Consider a service facility with random demand, such as a data center or a computerized diagnostic imaging center. We model the service center as an M/M/1 queuing system, where the arrivals of service requests are independent and follow a Poisson process with a rate of λ requests per unit time, and the service time has an exponential distribution with a service rate $\mu$ The <sup>fi</sup>rm is responsible for investing in capacity, and the hired manager is responsible for managing the center's daily activities and exerting additional effort to boost the center's service demand.

Let θ be the expected local base demand, which is the center's expected service demand affected by pricing, general advertising, and other decisions made by the <sup>fi</sup>rm but without the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the manager's demand-generating effort. Different from prior researches [6,7,18], here service demand can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the manager's marketing and service effort $\alpha ,$ where $\alpha { \in } A { \subseteq } R ^ { + }$ . In particular, $\lambda { = } \theta + \phi ( \alpha )$ , i.e., when the manager invests in marketing and service effort $\alpha ,$ the expected demand increases by $\phi ( \alpha ) ,$ , which measures managerial effectiveness. Thus the center's service demand N is a random variable with an expected value λ, which is a function of the base demand θ and the manager's effort α.

Because the manager is usually responsible for managing the daily operations of the service center, prior research that utilizes a queuing model in the principal-agent framework only considers how the agent's effort affects service rate [1] or product availability [13] and often ignores the fact that the manager can in<sup>fl</sup>uence service demand by exerting marketing and service effort. For instance, the manager can ease patient anxiety and provide patients with a pleasant service environment in a digital imaging center or provide additional valueadded features in a data center. Clearly, such service efforts as well as the manager's direct marketing efforts can help the center attract additional demand. Here, we focus on the manager's responsibility for generating demand.

Let r be the revenue from processing a service request. We assume that the marginal cost of processing a request is constant, and it is normalized to zero. Because of demand uncertainty, stochastic arrivals of service requests, and random service times, the service center often experience queuing delays. Let W be the expected time in the system for a service request. Here, W includes waiting time in queue and service time. In order to compete in the market, the service center has to meet a service standard: $W { \le } W _ { 0 } ,$ which means that the center promises to its clients that a service request's expected time in the system is no longer than $W _ { 0 } .$ . We can think of $W _ { 0 }$ as an industrywide service standard. Here, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's capacity investment decision directly affects the center's service quality, measured by the user waiting time W. For the service industry, long waiting times reduce users' satisfaction and negatively affect a center's reputation and its future referrals. Thus to ensure the center's long-term pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and competitiveness in the market, the <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to make a corresponding capacity investment so that the center will meet the service standard. Let c be the marginal capacity cost. We assume a linear capacity cost function in the form of $c _ { 0 } + c \mu$ The linear capacity cost function may not capture the exact cost structure of service facilities, which often have a stepwise cost feature, but it is a reasonable model for approximating IT costs and costs of other service centers; see [6,18,24,26,28], among others.

We assume that the managerial effectiveness is linear $( \phi ( \alpha ) =$ kα), since any nonlinear increasing managerial effectiveness function can be renormalized to be linear [3]. In addition, the expected local base demand θ is a random variable, which could be $\theta _ { h }$ and $\theta _ { l } \left( \theta _ { h } > \theta _ { l } \right)$ with probability q and $1 - q ,$ respectively. This setting better captures the characteristics of many modern service centers, and it creates two management challenges for the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

First, the manager running the center often has better knowledge about the realized local base demand θ, whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rm only observes the distribution of θ. Thus the <sup>fi</sup>rm would like to solicit market information from the manager in order to make the proper capacity investment to meet the service requirement. However, the manager, with his own agenda, may not truthfully share the market information with the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Second, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may be able to observe advertisement spending and some marketing and service activities conducted by the manager, but it cannot observe or verify the exact level of the manager's demand-generating effort. While the <sup>fi</sup>rm would like the manager to exert the optimal managerial effort to maximize its net bene<sup>fi</sup>t, the manager is effort-averse and incurs a disutility $V ( \alpha ) = \alpha ^ { 2 } / 2$ for an effort level α. Because given the same compensation the manager always prefers to exert less effort (i.e., shirking), the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to design proper incentive contracts to induce the manager to reveal the true market demand information and exert the desired level of effort, and the compensation contracts should be based on observable variables such as the number of service requests processed.

Following the general principal-agent adverse selection model [2,8,9,11,22], we also assume that both the principal and the agent are risk neutral. Let s be the compensation contract and s(N) be the compensation to the manager at N service requests. In addition, let M be the manager's net utility from an outside alternative. Thus, the manager will choose an optimal effort level to maximize his net utility given by his expected compensation minus his disutility of effort ${ \cal E } _ { N } [ s ( N ) ] - V ( \alpha )$ , and he will accept the contract s only when his expected net utility is no less than M. Here, because the manager is protected by limited liability, the agent utility assumption is economically similar to risk aversion [22]. If the <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses to compensate the manager M, then it is only paying the manager for managing the daily operations of the center without seeking his demand-generating effort and local market knowledge. Introducing the marketing aspect of the agent into the model enables us to better understand the impact of contract design in a service environment in which the manager has an information advantage and can in<sup>fl</sup>uence market demand. Next, we analyze the model to provide an integrated solution of operation resource management and compensation contract design for <sup>fi</sup>rms dealing with market uncertainty and with combined moral hazard and adverse selection issues.

## 4. Full-information case

Suppose for now the <sup>fi</sup>rm can perfectly observe the expected local base demand θ and the manager's effort level α. Hence the <sup>fi</sup>rm will determine the optimal capacity μ, the manager's effort α, and the compensation to the manager s to maximize its net bene<sup>fi</sup>t given by revenue minus costs of capacity and compensation to the manager, subject to the service standard (SS) constraint and the manager's individual rationality (IR) constraint. Suppose the realized expected local base demand is θ $( i = l , h )$ ; then the <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem is characterized in Eq. (1):

$$
\pi_ {i} = \max _ {\alpha_ {i}, \mu_ {i}, s _ {i}} (\lambda_ {i} r - c \mu_ {i} - s _ {i})\tag{1}
$$

$$
\text { subject   to }: W _ {i} \leq W _ {0} (i = l, h)\tag{SS}
$$

$$
s _ {i} - V (\alpha_ {i}) \geq M.\tag{IR}
$$

We exclude the <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity cost term $\left( c _ { 0 } \right)$ since only the variable capacity cost term is relevant for the capacity decision. Because the <sup>fi</sup>rm can verify exactly the manager's effort level, it will design the contract that compensates the manager only when he exerts the desired effort level. Given the optimal contract, the (IR) constraint will be binding, since the <sup>fi</sup>rm will not offer the manager more than what is required to make him do the job. In this case, the compensation $s _ { i }$ will be a <sup>fi</sup>xed amount $\left( M + V ( \alpha _ { i } ) \right)$ ), independent of the number of requests processed. That means by paying the manager a <sup>fi</sup>xed amount the <sup>fi</sup>rm is bearing all of the market uncertainty risk, since the market demand N and hence revenue are random variables. We know that for the M/M/1 queue in steady state the expected time in the system is given by $W _ { i } { = } 1 / ( \mu _ { i } { - } \lambda _ { i } )$ . Because capacity is costly, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will also only invest in the minimum capacity required to satisfy the service standard. Thus, the service standard constraint will be binding as well $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \mu _ { i } = \lambda _ { i } + 1 / W _ { 0 } )$

Taking the two binding constraints back into Eq. (1), the <sup>fi</sup>rm will determine the optimal effort level to maximize Eq. (2):

$$
\pi_ {i} = \max _ {\alpha_ {i}} [ (\theta_ {i} + k \alpha_ {i}) r - c (\theta_ {i} + k \alpha_ {i} + 1 / W _ {0}) - V (\alpha_ {i}) - M ].\tag{2}
$$

Since Eq. (2) is concave in effort, solving the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order condition (FOC), we have the optimal effort $\alpha _ { i } = ( r - c ) k ,$ which is independent of the market condition θ . Given the optimal effort level, we can solve for the optimal capacity through the binding service constraint and the optimal compensation through the binding (IR) constraint. If the manager exerts the optimal effort level $( r - c ) k ,$ the <sup>fi</sup>rm will compensate the manager a <sup>fi</sup>xed amount $s _ { l } = s _ { h } = M + \gamma _ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } ~ k ^ { 2 }$ the second term of which is the manager's disutility of effort $V ( \alpha _ { i } )$ The <sup>fi</sup>rm can enforce this contract by penalizing the manager if he does not exert the desired effort level, and the manager will accept the offer. The optimal capacity is given by $\theta _ { i } + ( r - c ) k ^ { 2 } + 1 / W _ { 0 } ,$ , which varies for different market conditions. In the full-information case, with the optimal contract, effort, and capacity, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net pro<sup>fi</sup>t can be written as $\pi _ { i } = r \lambda _ { i } - c \mu _ { i } - s _ { i } = ( r - c ) \theta _ { i } - c / W _ { 0 } - M + \gamma _ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 }$ The <sup>fi</sup>rst three terms $( r - c ) \theta _ { i } - c / W _ { 0 } - M$ represent the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net pro<sup>fi</sup>t when it does not induce the manager's demand-generating effort. The last term $\gamma _ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 }$ represents the additional pro<sup>fi</sup>t for the <sup>fi</sup>rm (net of the compensation to the manager) from inducing the manager's demand-generating effort. We assume that the parameters satisfy the condition $( r - c ) \theta _ { l } - c / W _ { 0 } - M > 0 ,$ , which means that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t even without the manager's demand-generating input.

The result of the full-information case is also called the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best result, which is often used as a benchmark for the case of information asymmetry. Next, we consider the case in which the manager privately observes the local market condition θ and his own effort level α.

## 5. Information asymmetry case

In reality, the <sup>fi</sup>rm often cannot directly verify the manager's effort level, which is known only to the manager. In addition, the manager often observes the realized local base demand, whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rm only knows its distribution. Both parties can observe the realized demand N. In this section, we analyze three contracts: a uniform contract, a variable-rate contract, and a charge-back contract. We focus on linear contracts in the form of a <sup>fi</sup>xed payment (or charge) plus a per-unit payment term. We choose linear contracts for their simplicity and popularity in practice and also because a restriction to linear contracts is standard in the literature [4,22]. We show that by designing incentive contracts that reward the manager based on the realized demand, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can solicit true market information and induce a desired level of demand-generating effort from the manager.

## 5.1. Uniform contract

We <sup>fi</sup>rst consider a uniform contract; that is, the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a single contract $s ^ { U } ( N ) = f ^ { U } + b ^ { U } N$ regardless of the realized market condi tion. This contract does not seek market information from the manager, but it can be used to induce the manager to exert demand-generating effort. While the full-information case offers the benchmark with the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t that can be achieved, this uniform contract offers another benchmark, with the pro<sup>fi</sup>t's lower bound. It can be used to evaluate the value of incentive contracts that solicit market information from the manager.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem is captured in Eq. (3):

$$
\pi = \max _ {f ^ {U}, b ^ {U}, \mu^ {U}} \left[ q \left(r \lambda_ {h} - b ^ {U} \lambda_ {h}\right) + (1 - q) \left(r \lambda_ {l} - b ^ {U} \lambda_ {l}\right) - c \mu^ {U} - f ^ {U} \right]\tag{3}
$$

$$
\text { subject   to }: W _ {i} \leq W _ {0} (i = l, h)\tag{SS}
$$

$$
f ^ {U} + b ^ {U} \lambda_ {i} - V (\alpha_ {i}) \geq M\tag{IR}
$$

$$
\alpha_ {i} \in \underset {\alpha_ {i} ^ {'} \in A} {\arg \max} \left[ f ^ {U} + b ^ {U} \lambda_ {i} - V \Big (\alpha_ {i} ^ {'} \Big) \right].\tag{IC}
$$

Here, in addition to the service standard and the manager's (IR) constraints, the <sup>fi</sup>rm also needs to consider the manager's incentive compatibility (IC) constraint, since the manager's effort level is his private information. The (IC) constraint indicates that the manager will exert an effort level $\alpha _ { i } = b ^ { U } k$ , which maximizes his net utility. Thus, the expected demand satis<sup>fi</sup>es $\lambda _ { h } = \theta _ { h } + k ^ { 2 } b ^ { U } > \lambda _ { l } = \theta _ { l } + k ^ { 2 } \dot { b } ^ { U }$ Since the uniform contract does not seek market information from the manager, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to consider demand uncertainty when making its capacity decision. In fact, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will invest in a capacity level such that the service standard constraint is binding in the high-demand market. Hence the service standard constraint is not binding in the low-demand market, which means that if the realized local market demand is low the center has more capacity than what is required to satisfy the service standard. In addition, the (IR) constraint is not binding in the high-demand case, which means that the <sup>fi</sup>rm compensates the manager more than what he can get from an alternative job.

Proposition 1. The optimal uniform contract is given by $b ^ { U } = \operatorname* { m a x } \{ 0 ,$ $r - c - q ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } ) / k ^ { 2 } ]$ and $f ^ { U } { = } M { - } b ^ { U } \theta _ { l } { - } ( b ^ { U } ) ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 } / 2 ;$ under this contract the manager will exert an effort level $\alpha _ { l } = \alpha _ { h } = b ^ { U } k$ , and the <sup>fi</sup>rm will install a capacity level $\mu ^ { U } = \theta _ { h } + b ^ { U } k ^ { 2 } + 1 / W _ { 0 }$

When this uniform contract is offered, the manager exerts the same level of demand-generating effort in both markets since the per-unit incentive is the same. However, the expected compensation differs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the expected local base demand is $\theta _ { h } ,$ the manager gets paid $( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } ) b ^ { U }$ more. It is also possible that the <sup>fi</sup>rm optimally sets $\overset { - } { b } \overset { U } { = } 0$ and only offers a <sup>fi</sup>xed payment M when the market uncertainty $\left( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } \right)$ is high, in which case it is only compensating the manager for directing the center's daily activities without seeking his demand-generating effort. Next, we limit to the case of $b ^ { U } > 0 ,$ i.e., assuming $k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) > q ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } )$ ).

Corollary 1. The per-unit rate in the uniform contract is decreasing in q (the probability of having a high expected local base demand), the <sup>fi</sup>xed term in the contract is increasing in q, and the capacity under the uniform contract is decreasing in q.

When q is low, it is less likely that there will be a high expected local base demand. However, the center still needs to invest in enough capacity to satisfy the service standard should the realized local base demand be high. Thus, if the realized local base demand is low, with ample capacity on hand the <sup>fi</sup>rm would like the manager to work hard to generate additional demand, and it induces more effort from the manager by offering a higher per-unit payment. However, if the realized demand is high, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will overpay the manager by $( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } ) b ^ { U }$ . When the probability of having a high expected local base demand increases (i.e., q increases), naturally, the <sup>fi</sup>rm should increase its capacity investment. However, since the uniform contract only induces demand-generating effort from the manager, the <sup>fi</sup>rm actually reduces capacity investment when q increases. This is because the expected demand $\lambda _ { i }$ decreases as the <sup>fi</sup>rm reduces the per-unit payment $b ^ { U }$ to reduce the overpayment to the manager. In fact, when q approaches 1 (the center most likely will have a high expected local base demand), the capacity $\mu ^ { U }$ is close to $\mu _ { l } ^ { F } ,$ , the full-information capacity level in the low-demand market, and when q approaches 0 (the center most likely will have a low expected local base demand), the capacity $\mu ^ { U }$ is close to $\mu _ { h } ^ { F } ,$ the full-information capacity level in the high-demand market, where the superscripts represent full information (F) and the uniform contract (U). That is, the uniform contract severely distorts the center's capacity investment decision. Comparing with the full-information case, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the following relationships: $\mu _ { i } ^ { F } { < } \bar { \mu } ^ { U } { < } \mu _ { h } ^ { F } ,$ $\lambda _ { i } ^ { U } { < } \lambda _ { i } ^ { F }$ , and $\alpha _ { i } ^ { U } { < } \alpha _ { i } ^ { F } ( i { = } l , h )$ . In general, the uniform contract induces less demand-generating effort from the manager, and as a result, the center processes fewer service requests and realizes less pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

The value of information is thus clear. First, precise market õinformation leads to an accurate capacity decision. The <sup>fi</sup>rm can avoid excess capacity costs while still satisfying the service standard in the low-demand market and process more service requests in the high-demand market with a higher capacity level. Second, having the ability to verify the manager's exact effort level enables the <sup>fi</sup>rm to induce a desired level of demand-generating effort from the manager without overcompensating him and not to be constrained by the manager's (IC) constraint.

The uniform contract can motivate the manager to invest in demand-generating effort (subject to his (IC) and (IR) constraints), but it fails to seek market information from the manager. For the <sup>fi</sup>rm, a common solution for the adverse selection problem is to offer a menu of contracts to the manager; each contract is designed for an expected local base demand $\theta _ { i \cdot }$ By observing the contract selected by the manager, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to infer the true market information and invest in an appropriate capacity level. Next, we analyze two incentive contracts that can successfully solicit market information from the manager; we continue to focus on the simple linear contract form.

## 5.2. Variable-rate contract

Instead of offering a uniform contract, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can design a menu of contracts, again in the form of a <sup>fi</sup>xed payment plus a commission term: $\begin{array} { r } { s _ { i } ( N ) = f _ { i } + b _ { i } N . } \end{array}$ . Note that the <sup>fi</sup>xed payment term can be negative, which indicates a charge for the manager. The decision timeline is as follows: First, the <sup>fi</sup>rm announces a set of contracts; each contract (s (N)) corresponds to an expected local base demand (θ ). We call this a variable-rate contract because generally the unit rate $b _ { l } \neq b _ { h } .$ . Second, the manager observes the realized local base demand and reports to the <sup>fi</sup>rm (or selects a contract). Note that the manager may or may not report truthfully to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Third, the <sup>fi</sup>rm, based on the manager's report (or the contract selected by the manager), invests in capacity. Fourth, the manager exerts demand-generating effort. Lastly, the <sup>fi</sup>nal demand is realized, and both parties observe the realized demand N. The <sup>fi</sup>rm compensates the manager according to the selected contract, and it incurs a penalty if the service standard is not met. Since the demand N is a random variable, the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot infer the value of α even after learning the expected local base demand θ and observing the realized value of N. The challenge for the <sup>fi</sup>rm is to design a menu of contracts so as to solicit true market information and induce the desired level of effort from the manager and thereby maximize its net bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

Following the revelation principle [16], we only need to search for the optimal menu of contracts from the class of truth-telling contracts. Under such contracts, it is in the manager's interest to truthfully reveal the expected local base demand information to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Assume the cost of not satisfying the service standard is high such that the <sup>fi</sup>rm always invests in suf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity to meet the service standard. The <sup>fi</sup>rm therefore needs to solve $\operatorname { E q . } \left( 4 \right)$ for the optimal contracts and capacities, subject to a series of constraints:

$$
\pi = \max _ {\mu_ {i} f _ {i}, b _ {i}} [ (1 - q) (\lambda_ {l} r - c \mu_ {l} - f _ {l} - b _ {l} \lambda_ {l}) + q (\lambda_ {h} r - c \mu_ {h} - f _ {h} - b _ {h} \lambda_ {h}) ], (i = l. h).\tag{4}
$$

First, in order for the manager to accept it, the contract $\mathsf { S } _ { i } ( N ) \left( i = l , h \right)$ has to satisfy the manager's individual rationality (IR) constraint:

$$
E _ {N} [ s _ {i} (N) ] - V (\alpha_ {i}) \geq M,\tag{IR}
$$

where $E _ { N } [ s _ { i } ( N ) ] = f _ { i } + b _ { i } \lambda _ { \mathrm { ~ } }$ . Second, because the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot observe and verify the manager's effort level, the manager will choose the effort level that maximizes his own net utility. This gives the manager's incentive compatibility (IC) constraint:

$$
\alpha_ {i} \in \operatorname * {a r g   m a x} _ {\alpha_ {i} ^ {'} \in A} E _ {N} [ s _ {i} (N) ] - V \Bigl (\alpha_ {i} ^ {'} \Bigr).\tag{IC}
$$

These constraints are for the case in which the manager reports truthfully about the realized market condition to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In order for the manager to report truthfully, the optimal contracts also need to satisfy the following two incentive compatibility constraints:

$$
E _ {N} [ s _ {l} (N) / \theta_ {l} ] - V (\alpha_ {l}) \geq E _ {N} [ s _ {h} (N) / \theta_ {l} ] - V (\tilde {\alpha} _ {l})\tag{IC - LH}
$$

$$
E _ {N} [ s _ {h} (N) / \theta_ {h} ] - V (\alpha_ {h}) \geq E _ {N} [ s _ {l} (N) / \theta_ {h} ] - V (\tilde {\alpha} _ {h}),\tag{IC - HL}
$$

where $\tilde { \alpha } _ { i }$ refers to the optimal effort level when the manager observes $\theta _ { i }$ but reports $\theta _ { j } ( i , j = l ,$ h and $i \neq j )$ . The left-hand sides of these two constraints represent the manager's net utility if he truthfully reports the realized local market condition, and the right-hand sides represent the manager's net utility if he misreports the market condition. These two constraints ensure that it is in the manager's interest to report truthfully to the <sup>fi</sup>rm about the realized local market condition. In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>rm also needs to choose the optimal capacity to satisfy the service standard constraints: $W _ { i } { \le } W _ { 0 } \left( i { = } l , h \right)$ .

Proposition 2. Following the decision timeline outlined above, this combined adverse selection and moral hazard problem has a unique solution of the optimal capacity and the menu of variable-rate contracts that solicit truthful market information from the manager: (i) the optimal menu of contracts are given by $s _ { l } ( N ) = f _ { l } + b _ { l } N$ with $f _ { l } { = } M - b _ { l } \theta _ { l } { - } b _ { l } ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 } / 2$ and $b _ { l } { = } \operatorname* { m a x } \{ 0 , \ r { - } c { - } q ( \theta _ { h } { - } \theta _ { l } ) / ( k ^ { 2 } ( 1 - q ) ) \}$ and $s _ { h } ( N ) = f _ { h } + b _ { h } N$ with $f _ { h } { = } M - \theta _ { h } b _ { h } { - } b _ { h } ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 } / 2 + b _ { l } ( \theta _ { h } { - } \theta _ { l } )$ and $b _ { h } = r - c ;$ under these contracts the manager will exert the optimal efforts $\alpha _ { h } = k b _ { h }$ and $\alpha _ { l } = k b _ { l } ; { \mathrm { \Omega } } ( \mathrm { i i } )$ the optimal capacity levels are given by $\mu _ { h } = \theta _ { h } + b _ { h } k ^ { 2 } + 1 / W _ { 0 }$ and $\mu _ { l } = \theta _ { l } + b _ { l } k ^ { 2 } + 1 / W _ { 0 }$

Comparing the contract terms for different market conditions, we see that the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a higher <sup>fi</sup>xed payment $( f _ { l } > f _ { h } )$ in the low-demand market so that the manager will not misreport. In the high-demand market, to counter the manager's misreporting tendency since $f _ { l } > f _ { h } ,$ the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a higher per-unit payment $\left( b _ { h } > b _ { l } \right)$ in the contract. The proposed contracts in Proposition $^ 2$ ensure that the manager will accept the contracts and truthfully reveal the realized local market condition to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. When the expected local base demand is $\theta _ { h } ,$ the contract $s _ { h } ( N )$ can in fact induce the same level of effort as that in the full-information case, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm will accordingly invest in the same level of capacity. However, because of information asymmetry the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to compensate the manager more than in the full-information case in order to elicit his market information. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rm realizes less pro<sup>fi</sup>t when the expected local base demand is $\theta _ { h } .$ On the other hand, when the expected base demand is $\theta _ { l } ,$ the contrac $| s _ { l } ( N )$ provides a lower incentive $( b _ { l } < r - c )$ and therefore induces less effort than in the full-information case. Accordingly, the <sup>fi</sup>rm invests less in capacity, provides less compensation to the manager, and in the end realizes less pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

Here, the incentive contract serves two purposes: (1) it elicits true local market information from the manager so that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can make the correct capacity investment decision; (2) it induces demandgenerating effort from the manager so that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can realize a higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t. The <sup>fi</sup>rm accomplishes the <sup>fi</sup>rst purpose by adjusting both the <sup>fi</sup>xed term $( f _ { i } )$ and the per-unit rate $( b _ { i } )$ in the contract so that it is in the manager's interest to reveal market information. The <sup>fi</sup>rm accomplishes the second purpose mostly by offering the manager a per-unit payment $b _ { i } ,$ which motivates the manager to exert effort $\alpha _ { i } = b _ { i } k$ to increase the demand for the center's services, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm shares the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts generated by the manager's effort with the manager through the <sup>fi</sup>xed term in the contract $( \mathrm { t h e } - b _ { i } ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 } / 2$ term in $f _ { i } ) _ { }$

Corollary 2. i) The per-unit rate $b _ { l }$ (when $b _ { l } > 0 )$ in the variable-rate contract is decreasing and concave in $q$ (the probability of having a high expected local base demand), the <sup>fi</sup>xed term $f _ { l }$ in the contract is increasing in $q ,$ and the capacity $\mu _ { l }$ is decreasing and concave in $q .$ ii) The per-unit rate $b _ { h }$ and capacity $\mu _ { h }$ are independent of $q ,$ but the <sup>fi</sup>xed term $f _ { h }$ in the contract is decreasing in $q .$

By offering different contract terms for different market conditions, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to elicit true market information from the manager and make corresponding capacity decisions. However, because of information asymmetry the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to pay a price to obtain this information. In this case, the manager is able to keep an information rent in the high-demand market. This information rent, which comes as the overpayment term $b _ { l } ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } )$ , is increasing in $\left( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } \right)$ and $b _ { l } .$ When q increases, the probability of having a high expected local base demand increases, and so does the chance of overpaying the manager. Thus, to counter the overpayment effect, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will reduce the per-unit rate $b _ { l }$ and accordingly increase the <sup>fi</sup>xed payment term $f _ { l } .$ As a result, the manager incurs less effort when the expected local base demand is low, the center's expected demand decreases, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm invests less in capacity. When $\left( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } \right)$ is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t from inducing the manager's demand-generating effort in the low-demand market cannot offset the overpayment to the manager in the highdemand market. Thus, it is optimal to set $b _ { l } = 0 ; \mathrm { i . e . }$ ., the <sup>fi</sup>rm induces no demand-generating effort in the low-demand market. As a result, it does not have to over-compensate the manager and is still able to elicit true market information and induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level in the high-demand market. Thus, when $b _ { l } = 0$ , the <sup>fi</sup>rm can achieve the same pro<sup>fi</sup>t level as that in the full-information case in the highdemand market.

To summarize, with the proposed variable-rate linear contracts in Proposition 2 the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to elicit true market information from the manager, but this comes at a cost because of information asymmetry. Compared with the full-information case, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will realize less expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t, even though the expected compensation to the manager is lower (since $( 1 - q ) ( f _ { l } + b _ { l } \Lambda _ { l } ) + q ( f _ { h } + b _ { h } \Lambda _ { h } ) < M + \gamma _ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } \ k ^ { 2 } = \mathrm { f u l l } -$ information compensation). The manager, on the other hand, realizes a higher expected net bene<sup>fi</sup>t, since in the high-demand market his net utility satis<sup>fi</sup>es $f _ { h } + b _ { h } \lambda _ { h } - \gamma _ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } \ k ^ { 2 } > M .$

## 5.3. Charge-back contract

We now consider an alternative contracting approach that not only elicits true market information but also induces the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level from the manager. Suppose the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a uniform per-unit rate to the manager and delegates the capacity investment decision to the manager by letting him select a capacity level. In return, it charges the manager a <sup>fi</sup>xed fee for using capacity and sharing pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and this <sup>fi</sup>xed charge term is like a franchise fee. The decision timeline is as follows: First, the <sup>fi</sup>rm announces a menu of contracts; each contract corresponds to a capacity level $\mu _ { i }$ and includes a uniform per-unit payment to the manager $( p ) ,$ a <sup>fi</sup>xed charge (F ), and a penalty term (T ) if the service standard $W _ { i } { \le } W _ { 0 }$ is not met. In the second stage, the manager observes the realized local market condition and asks the <sup>fi</sup>rm for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c capacity level; i.e., the manager selects a contract. Here, the manager is subjected to the service standard constraint directly, and he is penalized if the service standard is not met. Third, the <sup>fi</sup>rm delivers the capacity level selected by the manager. Fourth, given the capacity provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, the manager then invests in demand-generating effort. Fifth, the <sup>fi</sup>nal demand is realized. The manager is compensated at the given unit rate, and he also pays the <sup>fi</sup>rm the pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>xed amount for using capacity, and possibly a penalty. This is equivalent to delegating all the decision rights to the manager, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm then can subtract a fee to extract all surpluses.

Suppose for now that the <sup>fi</sup>rm sets a penalty term such that the manager will always choose to satisfy the service standard. We can then omit the penalty term in both parties' objective functions. The <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is given by $F _ { i } - c \mu _ { i } + ( r - p ) \Lambda _ { i } .$ . Its problem is to determine the contract terms, which include the uniform per-unit payment rate (p) and the capacity charge (F ). The <sup>fi</sup>rm will select the contract terms $\{ p , F _ { i } \}$ to ensure that the manager will participate, report truthfully regarding the local market conditions, and exert the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level. The manager's problem is to determine the optimal effort level and capacity choice to maximize his net utility, given by $p \lambda _ { i } - V ( \alpha _ { i } ) - F _ { i } ,$ subject to the service standard constraint. As the manager maximizes his net utility, clearly, he prefers the optimal effort level $\alpha _ { i } = p k$ (derived from the FOC). Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can set $p { = } r { - } c ,$ which equals the per-unit pro<sup>fi</sup>t, to induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level from the manager. The <sup>fi</sup>rm can then extract all surpluses while ensuring that the manager will participate (i.e., $p \lambda _ { i } - V ( \alpha _ { i } ) - F _ { i } = M )$ by setting $F _ { i } { = } p \lambda _ { i } { - } V ( \alpha _ { i } ) - M .$ . Given that the manager exerts the optimal effort level $\alpha _ { i } = p k ,$ and suppose for now that he also reports truthfully, the optimal capacity choices should be $\mu _ { l } { = } \theta _ { l } { + } k ^ { 2 } p { + } 1 / W _ { 0 }$ and $\mu _ { h } { = } \theta _ { h } { + } k ^ { 2 } p + 1 / W _ { 0 } .$ . Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can offer the capacity and contract set $\{ p , F _ { i } , \mu _ { i } \}$ from which the manager will choose.

We next check whether this proposed menu of contracts $\{ p , F _ { i } , \mu _ { i } \}$ can induce the manager to reveal market information truthfully and invest in the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level. First, suppose the expected local base demand is $\theta _ { l } .$ Even at a higher capacity level, the manager still invests in the optimal effort level $\alpha _ { l } = p k$ since effort is costly. Because the capacity charge term satis<sup>fi</sup>es $F _ { h } > F _ { l } ,$ the manager will truthfully reveal his private information about the realized local market demand $\theta _ { l } ,$ ask for capacity $\mu _ { l } ,$ and pay $F _ { l } .$ In this case, the service standard can be met even with the penalty term $T _ { l } = 0 .$

However, if the realized expected local base demand is $\theta _ { h } ,$ with a zero penalty term the manager would prefer the contract {p, $F _ { l } ,$ μ<sub>l</sub>} over $\{ p , F _ { h } , \mu _ { h } \}$ , since the former charge for capacity is lower. In that case, the manager would ask for capacity μ and still prefer to invest in the same effort level $\alpha { = } p k _ { \mathrm { { \ell } } }$ , and the center cannot meet the service standard, since the capacity level is too low. One quick <sup>fi</sup>x is to include a high penalty term in the contract. To prevent the manager from choosing $\mu _ { l }$ when he observes $\theta _ { h } ,$ the <sup>fi</sup>rm can set a high enough penalty term $T _ { l } ,$ which leads to a negative net utility for the manager if he does not comply with the service standard. This, however, still does not solve the problem, because given $\mu _ { l } ,$ the manager can either invest in no managerial effort or invest less in managerial effort $( \hat { \alpha } _ { h } )$ with $\theta _ { h } + k \hat { \alpha } _ { h } = \lambda _ { l }$ such that the center meets the service constraint, assuming $k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) > \left( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } \right)$ , which indicates $\theta _ { h } < \lambda _ { l }$

First, we rule out the case in which misreporting and incurring no managerial effort are a viable choice. We assume the parameters satisfy $k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) > 2 ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } )$ , which implies that if the manager observes $\theta _ { h }$ but asks for capacity μ , without incurring demandgenerating effort his net utility is less than M, since $p \theta _ { h } - F _ { l } =$ $M + ( r - c ) ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } ) - k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } / 2 < M .$ . The manager still has a second option, which is to incur effort $\hat { \alpha } _ { h }$ such that $\theta _ { h } + k \hat { \alpha } _ { h } = \lambda _ { l } .$ . By doing so the service standard is met when he observes $\theta _ { h }$ but asks for capacity $\mu _ { l } ,$ and he still can realize a higher net utility $p \lambda _ { l } - V ( \hat { \alpha } _ { h } ) - F _ { l } > p \lambda _ { l } - V ( \alpha _ { l } ) - F _ { l } = M ,$ , since $V ( \hat { \alpha } _ { h } ) { < } V ( \alpha _ { l } )$ . To prevent the manager from shirking and under-requesting capacity, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to adjust the <sup>fi</sup>xed charge term to induce him to share market information truthfully and exert the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level.

Proposition 3. When the parameters satisfy $k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) > 2 ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } )$ , the following menu of contracts with a uniform unit rate plus a capacity charge and a service standard penalty term will solicit true market information and induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort levels from the manager: The <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a uniform per-unit rate $p { = } r { - } c$ to the manager and in return charges the manager $F _ { l } = ( r - c ) \theta _ { l } + ( r - c ) ^ { 2 } k ^ { 2 } / 2 - M$ for capacity level $\mu _ { l } = \theta _ { l } + k ^ { 2 } ( r - c ) + 1 / W _ { 0 }$ and $F _ { h } { = } F _ { l } { + } ( \theta _ { h } { - } \theta _ { l } ) ^ { 2 } / ( 2 \ : k ^ { 2 } )$ for capacity level $\mu _ { h } { = } \theta _ { h } { + } k ^ { 2 } ( r { - } c ) { + } 1 / W _ { 0 } .$ . In addition, there is a penalty term attached to the capacity selection μ : the manager has to pay a <sup>fi</sup>ne $T _ { l } =$ $( r - c ) ( \theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } ) + \varepsilon$ and $T _ { h } = \varepsilon > 0$ if the service standard $W _ { i } { \le } W _ { 0 }$ is not met.

Given the proposed charge-back contracts in Proposition 3, the manager <sup>fi</sup>nds it is in his best interest to report local market conditions truthfully and exert the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort level. This charge-back contract is equivalent to a franchise contract with $F _ { i }$ as the franchise fee and $r - p$ as the royalty rate. That is, in order to run the center and retain the center's revenue, the manager has to pay the <sup>fi</sup>rm the <sup>fi</sup>xed franchise fee and a per-unit royalty (c). Similar to the variable-rate contracts described in Section 5.2, when there is market information asymmetry, the manager is able to reserve an information rent for the high-demand market under the proposed charge-back contracts. The two contracts both have a per-unit payment and a <sup>fi</sup>xed term, but the variable-rate contract offers different per-unit rates for different markets, and the <sup>fi</sup>xed term could be a payment, whereas it is always a charge in the charge-back contract. Also, the manager is not explicitly subject to the service standard constraint and the <sup>fi</sup>rm makes the capacity decision under the variable-rate contract. In addition, although both kinds of contract can induce the manager to reveal the true local market condition, the charge-back contracts can induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort levels in both markets, whereas the variable-rate contracts only induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort in the high-demand market. The two contracts also differ in that the variable-rate contract terms and capacity are a function of the probability distribution of the expected local base demand, but the charge-back contract terms and capacity are independent of the probability distribution of the expected local base demand.

## 6. Contract analysis

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst illustrate the operational and <sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes of the center in a simple numerical example. We then analyze the results under the three contracts with information asymmetry, recommend the contract choice for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and evaluate the cost and value of the manager's private information.

## 6.1. Numerical example

Consider a service center such as a computerized medical imaging center characterized by the following parameter values: $r = \$ 100, c =$ $\mathfrak { H } 3 3 0 , W _ { 0 } = 0 . 1 , M = \mathfrak { H } 1 0 0 , k = 0 . 7 , \theta _ { l } = 3 0 , \theta _ { h } = 4 5$ , and $q = 0 . 6 . \mathrm { T a b l e } \ 1$ summarizes the optimal contracts, the manager's effort, the center's operational performance, the costs of capacity, and the center's pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in the full-information case and the information asymmetry case with uniform, variable-rate, and charge-back contracts. The example in Table 1 illustrates that the manager's market knowledge is valuable for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, since both contracts that solicit the manager's market information outperform the uniform contract in the information asymmetry case. Even with information asymmetry, the chargeback contract is able to induce effort levels identical to those in the full-information case, and it therefore generates the same volume levels and the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to invest in the same capacity levels as in the full-information case. However, the <sup>fi</sup>rm realizes less pro<sup>fi</sup>t when there is information asymmetry, even with the charge-back contract, mostly because the manager is able to keep an information rent. Moreover, while the charge-back contract seems to be superior to the variable-rate contract in terms of the center's operational outcome, the variable-rate contract in this case outperforms the charge-back contract because it generates more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Under the charge-back contract, the per-unit payment to the manager equals the center's per-unit pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin $( r - c )$ . Hence the manager is equivalently reserving all the revenues of the center by paying the variable cost of the consumed capacity (cλ<sub>i</sub> out of cμ<sub>i</sub>) and the <sup>fi</sup>xed charge term (or franchise fee), which is naturally associated with the <sup>fi</sup>xed cost of operations, with a higher charge for a higher capacity installation. In this numerical example, the franchise fee is much higher than the cost of capacity buffer $( c / W _ { 0 } { = } c / \mu _ { i } { - } c \lambda _ { i } )$ , and, combining that with the variable charges, it means the manager is paying much more than the total variable cost of capacity. One may wonder why the manager would need the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This can be explained by examining what each party contributes to the business. In this service setting, the manager and the <sup>fi</sup>rm each brings two unique assets to the operation. The former possesses knowledge of local market demand and can help the center increase service demand by managing day-to-day operations and exerting demand-generating efforts, and the latter provides capital for capacity and brings in base demand through its brand name, reputation, and promotional efforts. Thus to reserve the whole revenues of the center the manager not only needs to pay the <sup>fi</sup>rm for capacity but also for the base demand brought by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's reputation and effort. The charge-back contract is designed to motivate the manager to share his assets and help the <sup>fi</sup>rm recoup the values of assets that it contributes to the center and extract all surpluses. Paying the manager the full pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin per unit effectively motivates him to exert the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best demand-generating effort. The <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t is driven by the <sup>fi</sup>xed franchise fee only. This <sup>fi</sup>xed charge serves two purposes: it elicits market information from the manager and recoups reputation value and extracts all surpluses for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Because part of the center's demand (local base demand) is generated by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's reputation and effort, not by the manager, the latter has to pay a reputation premium (the $( r - c ) \theta _ { i }$ term) through the franchise fee.

The analysis of the charge-back contract con<sup>fi</sup>rms the positive theory of cost allocation proposed by [28] that cost allocations are often used to solve certain organizational problems, here, in particular, incentive and information asymmetry agency issues. It is also consistent with the franchise contract literature that the franchising fee structure is driven by factors such as moral hazard, franchisor services, and risk sharing between the two parties [17,23]. We did not model the double-sided moral hazard issue that commonly appears in the franchising network, but we derive the optimal incentive contract for the <sup>fi</sup>rm facing an effort-averse manager who also possesses private information regarding local market conditions. Unlike [21], which shows that a franchising plan achieves the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best outcome in terms of agents' effort and the principal's pro<sup>fi</sup>t, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a similar franchising (charge-back) contract can induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort and truthful local market information from the manager and lead to the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best capacity investment, but it fails to produce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best pro<sup>fi</sup>t and may not even produce the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t for the <sup>fi</sup>rm in a service environment with a combined moral hazard and adverse selection problem.

## 6.3. Cost and value of the manager's private information

## 7. Conclusions

Market demand uncertainty and time-based competition, make capacity investment and managerial incentives decisions for service facilities such as high-end diagnostic medical imaging centers, modern IT services, or contract manufacturing shops particularly challenging. These facilities compete on service quality, short queuing times and speed. Therefore, having insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity can be economically devastating for them. Given the high up-front costs involved, <sup>fi</sup>rms want to make sure that they neither over- nor under-invest in service capacity. These problems get exasperated by the fact that typically <sup>fi</sup>rms are unfamiliar with the local market demand and do not closely observe the demand-generating efforts of the hired managers. Most of prior studies of cost allocation methodologies, contract design, and service resource management tend to address these aspects of the problem separately. They ignore the interaction effects between the capacity decisions and the managerial adverse selection and moral hazard issues which are crucial elements for successfully running services with <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity, random arrivals and stochastic service times. We contribute to literature by developing an integrated approach that simultaneously determines the design of managerial incentive contracts and the optimal capacity planning for capital intensive service facilities, facing a combined moral hazard and adverse selection agency issue, and providing a complete solution to the complicated practical problem.

We derive the closed-form solutions for three incentive contracts under information asymmetry between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and management, and analyze their impact on capacity decisions, service levels, service volumes, and the allocations of costs. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the uniform contract, which offers the same payment terms regardless of market conditions, is able to induce the desired demand-generating effort from the manager but fails to elicit the manager's market knowledge and greatly distorts the <sup>fi</sup>rm's capacity decision. To effectively elicit market information from the manager, the <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to design a menu of incentive contracts. Two contracting approaches that are simple to implement in practice can elicit this information. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is to offer a menu of variable-rate linear contracts in which the per-request payments are modi<sup>fi</sup>ed for different market conditions. Under this contracting mode, the manager is induced to report the realized local market demand and the <sup>fi</sup>rm is responsible for investing in capacity and meeting the service standard, based on the manager's report of market information. Under the second approach, the <sup>fi</sup>rm charges the manager a differential franchise fee for using the installed capacity and pays the manager a uniform per-request fee regardless of the market conditions. Here the manager is explicitly responsible for capacity selection and for meeting the service standard. We prove that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can elicit true market information by properly setting the <sup>fi</sup>xed franchise fee, and this approach (of charge-back contracting) can also induce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best (full-information) demand-generating effort from the manager. As we demonstrate in the numerical example, the manager's market knowledge is valuable to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. By eliciting this market information from the manager with the proposed variable-rate or charge-back contracts, the investing <sup>fi</sup>rm can make accurate capacity decisions and realize 10.38% to 24.4% more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts than offering the uniform contract.

While both the variable-rate and charge-back contracts can help the <sup>fi</sup>rm successfully elicit the manager's local market information, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's eventual optimal contract choice depends on the probability distribution of the local base demand. Surprisingly, the charge-back (franchise) contract, which produces the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best operational results across the board, may not always maximize the <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t when the manager also has private information about the local market. In particular, this franchise approach only dominates the variable-rate contract when the probability of having a low local base demand is high $( q < 1 / 2 )$ This contrasts with and extends results in [21] that a franchising plan achieves the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best outcome in terms of both the agents' effort and the principal's pro<sup>fi</sup>t. In our setting, the franchising contract not only fails to produce the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, it can also be inferior to the variable-rate contract.

In our service setting, the manager and the <sup>fi</sup>rm each brings two unique assets to the operation. The former possesses knowledge of local market demand and can help the center increase service demand by managing day-to-day operations and exerting demand-generating efforts, and the latter provides capital for capacity and brings in basedemand through its brand name, reputation, and promotional efforts. The charge-back contract is designed to motivate the manager to share his assets and help the <sup>fi</sup>rm recoup the values of assets that it contributes to the center and extract all surpluses. Thus it may be optimal (for both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the manager) to charge the manager more than the costs of capacity as the <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to recoup its reputation values and extract all surpluses through the franchise fee. The accounting literature tends to focus on the role of <sup>fi</sup>xed cost allocation as an effective cost control to hold agents accountable so that they will not over-consume resources at the expense of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, in a service queueing setting, under-consuming resources can be harmful to the <sup>fi</sup>rm as well, because low capacity restricts the center from serving more customers and also causes long customer waiting times. We show that when properly designed, the franchise fee in the charge-back contract can prevent the manager from both over-using and under-using service capacity.

In summary, we solve a practical capital budgeting and managerial incentive contracting problem in a service setting and extend prior literature on cost allocation, contract design, and service resource management. We show that by integrating service resource investment and incentive contract design, <sup>fi</sup>rms can optimize their capacity investments to better cope with market uncertainty and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve their pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Our study applies to many capitalintensive and congestion-prone service systems, where the success of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant up-front capacity investments also hinges on the daily operations of those facilities run by hired managers—who typically possess speci<sup>fi</sup>c-knowledge—that give them a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant information advantage.
