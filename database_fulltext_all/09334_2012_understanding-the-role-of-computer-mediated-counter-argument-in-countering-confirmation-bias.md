# Understanding the role of computer-mediated counter-argument in countering con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias

Hsieh-Hong Huang <sup>a</sup>, Jack Shih-Chieh Hsu <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Cheng-Yuan Ku <sup>c</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias has long been discussed in the behavioral decision-making research stream. Although decision support systems were designed to counter cognitive biases and speed up information processing, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias still can be observed during the decision-making process and causes some unwanted behaviors, such as selective reading. An experimental design was conducted to examine the impact of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias in a computer-supported decision-making context. In addition, we attempted to explore whether the providing of computer-mediated counter-argument can effectively eliminate the impact caused by selective reading. The experiment results show that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias can be observed when decision makers possess strong preconceptions and selective reading behaviors, caused by con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, resulting in skewed adjustment and high con<sup>fi</sup>dence. This means that computer-mediated counter-arguments can effectively reduce the effects caused by con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias as well as lead to higher satisfaction with the decision outcome. Lastly, the research results were discussed and implications of this <sup>fi</sup>nding for academics and practitioners were provided.

## 1. Introduction

Psychologists have pointed out that people rely on various heuristic methods in their daily lives to simplify the information processing necessary in decision making. However, subjective judgments present in the course of the heuristic cognitive processes, of which people are unaware, have a negative impact on the decision-making process. One type of subjective judgmental behavior is con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, which impedes individuals’ capacity to make correct decisions when rationally accessing and utilizing information. With con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, individuals are interested in only the part of the available information that <sup>fi</sup>ts into their current beliefs. Information is recognized as the core of effective decision making. The quality of decisions largely depends on the quantity and variety of information considered during the decision-making process [38]. However, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias inhibits people from fairly reviewing all the information available, which results in the frequent making of inappropriate decisions. Empirical studies have shown that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias is one of the critical factors leading to overtrading and low pro<sup>fi</sup>tability [4,9,27].

Computer-supported decision-making tools are designed to assist individuals in enhancing their information processing capabilities and avoiding the potential effects of cognitive biases. However, researchers have indicated that the rationalization of a decisionmaking process by formalizing it within a computer-based information system does not seem to make the process itself more rational [21]. Instead, bias that operates without the information system continues to operate within it. For example, con<sup>fi</sup>dence level is increased in the computer-supported context when accuracy of information is the same [12] or even when accuracy is worse [37]. Jiang et al. [32] also indicate that the use of DSS increases the decision maker's con<sup>fi</sup>- dence to an irrational level when model performance information is provided. Moreover, the use of computer-aided decision-making tools might create the illusion of control and, therefore, increase irrational con<sup>fi</sup>dence [12], which is the driver of low performance decision. For example, Barber and Odean [4] found that self-attribution bias, the tendency for people to attribute high value to their abilities, and the illusions of knowledge and control increase the con<sup>fi</sup>- dence level after investors switch from a traditional phone-based to on-line trading channel. This leads to more frequent transactions, increasing transaction costs and harming the performance of on-line investors. Therefore, understanding what can be done to avoid cognitive biases accompanying computer-supported decision-making tools is critical.

Researchers highlighting the impact of cognitive bias and the importance of de-biasing propose counter-argument and warning as two approaches to the reduction of such bias. However, to the best of our knowledge, functions embedded in computer-aided decision support tools to facilitate decision makers to counter cognitive biases are rare. In fact, many proposed de-bias approaches have not even been formally evaluated in environments without computer-support, or have failed to generate statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results (e.g. Lau et al. [39]). In light of the above-discussed issues, this study attempts to: (1) revisit the effect of cognitive bias (e.g. con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias) on the decision-making process (e.g. decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence and adjustment) in the computersupported decision-making context; and (2) examine whether the effect of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias can be reduced by means of a DSS incorporated with a de-bias function, by providing computer-mediated counterargument.

Decision making is a complicated process that is affected by various contextual factors. To simplify the process and to avoid possible interferences, an experiment was conducted to test those ideas. In the following sections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review related theories and empirical studies. Hypotheses are then proposed based on the literature review. In the third section, detailed information about decision design, including task, procedure and subjects, are introduced. Study results and discussion are followed by conclusions.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Mental models and heuristic decision making

A mental model is a psychological representation of the environment. It represents cognitive constructs and their relationships in one's mind, which provides a conceptual framework for describing and explaining current states, and predicting future system states [8,30,45]. On the basis of mental models, individuals interpret and respond to their environment. Mental models may be viewed as the basis for information processing and are critical in decision making. For example, one responds to problems by generating a situated cognition; the interaction between mental model and external context [14]. Actions are based on the situated cognition formed by one's understanding of the environment and pre-existing mental models (how to deal with this problem). Mental models also serve as guidance for information search, enabling individuals to determine the usefulness of the information, to digest it, and even to abandon useless information [15]. With a pre-existing mental model, information processing is speeded up by the information being organized in a robust and easy-to-understand way. This approach can also help avoid information overload by retaining only useful information according to the existing mental models. At the same time, mental models may also limit the information acquisition, searching, <sup>fi</sup>ltering, coding, storing, and retrieving processes. Truly useful information may be blocked out and not taken into consideration in the further decision-making process.

The consequences of a mental model are two-fold. Not only does it facilitate information processing, but it also creates cognitive bias which drives individuals to ignore information perceived as unimportant that might actually be critical. In addition, it has an effect not only on the information search, but also on concept forming or assumption veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. With restricted perception, individuals tend to limit their attention to a favored hypothesis; to prefer to attend to evidence in support of existing beliefs; to look exclusively or primarily for positive cases; to over-value positive con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory instances; and to see what they are looking for [42]. Researchers observed that decision makers, in general, seek con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory evidence and ignore discon<sup>fi</sup>rming information [2,26,46]. Wason [52] de<sup>fi</sup>ned this as con-<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias. He indicated that the decision-making process is harmed by such bias because rational decision processing including the evaluating of information from both sides is then impossible.

In sum, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias may hamper decision making in several ways [42]. First, people tend to restrict themselves to preferred hypotheses only, ignoring, either intentionally or unintentionally, other possible hypotheses. Second, people tend to look for information that <sup>fi</sup>ts their existing beliefs. Belief refers to what an individual holds to be the truth and is represented in the mental model as the variable and the way in which two or more different variables relate to each other. If a person believes one of multiple hypotheses to be true, he or she tends to seek information which shows that the preferred hypothesis is true. For example, based on their beliefs and preferences, auditors react differently to audit evidence [40]. Hat<sup>fi</sup>eld [25] also found that manager-level accountants judge the staff accountants’ research reports according to whether the reports con-<sup>fi</sup>rm their initial opinions. As a result of ignoring the contradictory evidence, decision makers maintain or strengthen their current beliefs [1,28,29]. Third, with information at hand, people tend to over-value the information according to their current beliefs and under-value that which does not <sup>fi</sup>t their hypothesis. Even in ambiguous situations, where both positive and negative support is provided, people only need a little positive evidence to support their hypothesis [44]. For example, medical doctors may overemphasize some test results and underplay, or even ignore, other possible explanations in the course of diagnosis [10], which often results in the drawing of wrong conclusions.

Based on the above discussion, the decision-making process is clearly problematic when it is affected by con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias. As a result, low quality decisions may be made based on wrong assumptions, even if there is much evidence to suggest that the decision is wrong [11]. Therefore, exploring approaches to eliminate the occurrence of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or to reduce its impact on the decision-making process is critical.

## 2.2. Impacts of confirmation bias on decision making

Cognitive biases many generate impacts on two broadly discussed characteristics of decision making: decisional adjustment and con<sup>fi</sup>- dence. Decision adjustment refers to the difference between one's <sup>fi</sup>nal decision and one's initial preference. According to DSS literature, decision makers suffer from error anchoring and inadequate adjustment [51]. Anchoring refers to one's initial judgment with respect to a decision and is determined by individual preference. On the other hand, adjustment refers to the tendency for the decision maker to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by additional information. Behavioral decisionmaking studies indicate that the receiving of new information and evidence leads to the revision of belief [13]. During the decisionmaking process, possible information for reference is sought. This additional evidence may be positively or negatively related to the decision maker's belief, which is adjusted in either direction accordingly in light of the additional evidence [3]. The presentation of additional evidence changes one's belief, which then becomes the anchor for the next stage. Tversky and Kahneman [51] indicated that in general: (1) it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for decision makers to anchor in the correct value initially; and (2) insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient or inappropriate adjustment is made in light of other positive or negative information related to the initial decision.

A preconception is formed by the decision maker's pre-existing preference or on the basis of their <sup>fi</sup>rst impression of the option. In a multi-option context, the distance between the initial anchoring point and speci<sup>fi</sup>c option represents the extent to which that option is preferred. An option is preferred when a short distance between the anchoring point and that option, compared with others, is observed. The emergence of new evidence may drive the decision maker to move their decision either closer to or further away from the initial preferred option. Applying this concept to our study, in a two-option context, distance from the anchoring point to each realm represents the preferred level of each option. Subjects may adjust their decisions either closer to or further away from their preconception in light of new evidence. If the subject's initial anchoring settles in either realm, there is a tendency for the subject's decision to be adjusted away from their preference when receiving information that contradicts their preconception. In contrast, the decision maker adjusts his/her decision to the preferred realm when receiving information or evidence in support of their preference. Since decision makers tend actively to read information which matches their preconception, according to con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias theory, a move closer to the preferred realm is then expected.

Since the impact of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias on adjustment is broadly con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, the testing of the relationship between these two variables is then not included in this study. Instead, we propose that another decisional characteristic, named decision confidence, is affected by con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias. Decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence refers to subjects’ beliefs about their decisional performance and their perception of the probability that their decisions are correct [12,43]. It is not rare for people to be over-optimistic. Optimism drives people to over-estimate the possibility of the occurrence of the favored outcome [24]. As a result, they tend to under-estimate the risk and over-estimate their problemsolving ability [35]. We measured con<sup>fi</sup>dence twice: before and after reading news. Initially, con<sup>fi</sup>dence re<sup>fl</sup>ects the belief that decision makers think their preferred option is true. One's belief is strengthened when evidence supporting the preferred option is provided and is weakened when evidence countering the preferred option is provided. Since individuals with con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias tend to seek information or evidence that <sup>fi</sup>ts into their current belief rather than to seek discon<sup>fi</sup>rming evidence, their con<sup>fi</sup>dence level is forti<sup>fi</sup>ed through this process [47]. In contrast, those without con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias tend to read information favored both options and, therefore, their con<sup>fi</sup>dence level is similar to the initial status. Hence, we argue that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias leads to selective reading which drives individuals to increase their con<sup>fi</sup>dence level.

H1. Compared with those without con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, more increased con<sup>fi</sup>dence can be observed for individuals with con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias.

## 2.3. Computer-supported de-biasing approach

According to con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias theory, people tend to search for supportive information after a preliminary decision has been made. This results in insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient adjustment and high levels of con<sup>fi</sup>dence. However, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias and its impact are not irreversible [19,20,33]. Fairly simple bias is easy for humans or machines to spot. Since we restricted the research context to computer-supported decision making, experimental design was undertaken to test the effect of providing the computer-supported de-bias tool on con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias.

Various de-bias functions have been proposed and examined by previous studies [5,18,36,47]. Among those functions proposed, counterargument, or warning, has been examined by several studies. For example, Block and Harper [7] found that warnings can usefully reduce the effect of anchoring and insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient adjustment although this effect cannot be eliminated completely. The essential purpose of counterargument is to dissuade the decision maker from upholding his/her current belief. Theory indicates that once one's belief is formed, it is not easy to change without any stimulus [28]. This study attempts to test the effect of counter-argument, i.e., information contrasting with that held in the mind of a decision maker, on the decision-making process. Reading counter-argument is believed to be useful in avoiding con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias [22,47] because decision makers may start to question their initial assumptions. Lau et al. [39] argue that before making a <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, asking users to assemble the body of evidence for and against a proposition allows decision makers to override their previous assumptions with evidence from both sides.

## 2.4. Effect of counter-argument

As we hypothesized, the consequences of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias include abnormally high decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence and inadequate adjustment of decisions. Those consequences imply that decision makers not only anchor their preliminary decision inappropriately, but also pay attention to news items which accord with their preconceptions and ignore important news items during the decision-making process. We attempt to block con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias through an embedded de-bias function: computer-mediated counter-argument. The DSS provides counterargument according to the decision maker's information reading behavior. We believe that deeper thinking is elicited when decision support systems present information con<sup>fl</sup>icting with or in contrast to those prede<sup>fi</sup>ned hypotheses or beliefs. Thinking deeply allows decision makers to reconsider the appropriateness of their preliminary decision. As a result, individuals adjust their decisions away from their preconception. In contrast, subjects in the context without computermediated counter-argument read information based on their preferences. The biased reading behavior drives decision makers to reinforce their preferences and so their <sup>fi</sup>nal decisions tend to be closer to two realms.

H2a. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias has less effect on decision adjustment for subjects with the computer-mediated counter-argument function than for those without it.

A similar effect will be evident with respect to decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence. The decision maker's con<sup>fi</sup>dence is <sup>fl</sup>oating and changeable. Beliefs about the preferred hypothesis are strengthened when supportive evidence is available. We argue that the receiving of evidence that counters the current belief tends to reduce a decision maker's con<sup>fi</sup>- dence level. Counter-argument allows decision makers to re-evaluate their preferences and prevent the growth of abnormal con<sup>fi</sup>dence [47]. Therefore, we believe that although con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias drives decision makers to search for supportive evidence and leads to a higher level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, counter-argument can be used to reduce this effect by providing evidence which shows that the decision maker's preferred option may not be the answer. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2b. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias has less effect on decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence for subjects with the computer-mediated counter-argument function than for those without it.

## 2.5. Other effects of CMCA

In addition to the possible impact of counter-argument on con<sup>fi</sup>- dence and adjustment in the decision-making process, there is a potential effect on decision makers’ psychological status. Among proposed psychological outcomes of using computer-supported decision making, satisfaction is mentioned and discussed broadly in the literature (e.g. Ref. [6]). Decision satisfaction can be separated into process satisfaction and outcome satisfaction [23,53]. Process satisfaction refers to users’ perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and <sup>fl</sup>uency in using the decision support system to facilitate decision making. In contrast, outcome satisfaction refers to users’ perceived effectiveness and expected performance with respect to the decision that has been made [48]. For subjects in the group with CMCA, the existence of counter-argument increases the chances of decision makers challenging their previous assumptions. Although this process is believed to have a positive impact on decision outcome, it may also generate a negative impact on emotional outcomes. First, in terms of the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the decisionmaking process, reading counter-arguments requires that people spend more time making a decision. Second, interruptions caused by counter-argument reduce the <sup>fl</sup>uency of the decision-making process. The consequences of frequent interruptions include negative moods and increased fatigue [49,54]. In light of this, we anticipate that when CMCA is present, decision makers tend to rate satisfaction with the decision-making process at a lower level, even if the interruption is helpful [50]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H3a. Subjects who use DSS with computer-mediated counterargument will feel less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the decision-making process.

However, different results may be anticipated with respect to the satisfaction outcome. Although the presentation of counter-arguments interrupts the decision-making process, it also allows or forces the decision maker to pay attention to both positive and negative evidence provided by the system. Consideration of information that both supports and counters the preferred hypotheses means that thorough decisions can be made. As a consequence, decision quality is expected to be higher in a more rigorous decision-making process. Since outcome satisfaction ties in with the expected performance of the decision, it is reasonable to assume that a higher level of outcome satisfaction will be observed in the group with computer-supported counterargument. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H3b. Subjects who use DSS with computer-mediated counterargument will feel more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the decision-making outcome.

## 3. Research methods

In this study, focusing on the moderating effect of information overload and counter-argument, an experiment was conducted to test the hypotheses listed in the previous section. Subjects were assigned randomly into two groups - with/without computer-mediated counterargument and were asked to accomplish a stock investment task by using the provided DSS. A stock investment task was selected because it is a context in which DSS is extensively adopted to support decision making, enabling decision makers both to cope with high levels of uncertainty and to process data in large quantities (Fig. 1).

## 3.1. Task

In general stock investment decision-making tasks, individuals are allowed to invest certain amounts of money in one or more selected investment targets. The real decision making is more complex and the <sup>fi</sup>nal investment portfolio may contain several combinations of investments. Since this study focused on understanding “information reading behavior” only, a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed investment environment was constructed. That is, rather than including all available companies in the open market, study participants were requested to invest their money in two companies in two industries.

The stock investment task referred to in this paper was modi<sup>fi</sup>ed from Melone et al. [41]. Subjects assumed the role of treasurer/ controller of a small <sup>fi</sup>rm which had a certain amount of revenue in the previous year. Subjects were requested to put the money into two different stocks. To maximize their return, subjects allocated that money to two different options (a company in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial holding industry or high-tech industry) based on whatever subset they deemed appropriate. Decision support systems with “what if analysis” were provided as decision aids. The system-provided basic market information and current market status in news format. Basic market information included the name of company, capital, business scope, expected ROI in the next 5 years, and stock price for the past <sup>fi</sup>ve years. Current market status included positive and negative information about these two companies and the industries in which they belong. Information was revised from that published in investment-related journals and magazines.

All news items about each company in this study were collected from actual investment reports prepared by two Ph.D. candidates. We erased all company names and adjusted the date, amount, quantity, etc. to <sup>fi</sup>t the scenario. The title of each news item was limited to 10–20 words and the length of the content of each news item was restricted to 150–200 words (in Chinese). 8 professionals, including professors in <sup>fi</sup>nance, research associates in accounting, CPAs, and auditors, were invited to review these news items. We printed each news items on a name card and asked our reviewers to rank each one as positive or negative, and to give weight to each according to its importance, the strength of the title, and content respectively.

The content validity was then assured by eight <sup>fi</sup>nance professionals, including professors, experienced investors, senior managers in the high-tech industry, and accountants. News with ambiguous titles or content was dropped or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The direction (positive or negative) and importance of each piece of news and the accompanying headline were determined based on opinions provided by the above eight <sup>fi</sup>nance professionals. Furthermore, all positive and negative news items were categorized into three groups according to their importance and strength. This was done to ensure that an equally-weighted counter-argument was provided so as to avoid possible unwanted effects. For example, a counter-argument that was very signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly weaker than the news item could have generated either no or little effect.

## 3.2. System

A GUI-based DSS, with 4GL programming language and a relational database management system, was developed for the experiment. The designed system was able to support Simon's decision processes, which allowed participants to undertake intelligence, design, and choice activities. The actual design of activities was based upon Hung et al.'s [31] recommendations. The detailed system tools are shown in Table 1. The <sup>fi</sup>nal system includes six basic decision support functions: selection, aggregation, estimation, simulation, equalization, and optimization. Mapping to a stock investment environment, selection and aggregation functions refer to functions allowing users to select speci<sup>fi</sup>c targets and to zoom in and out based on different time periods. Users can obtain detailed transaction information, e.g. price, quantity, and trend, according to different time periods, e.g. daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly. Estimation function refers to the function allowing individuals to view estimated trends based on system-provided estimating tools. Simulation function refers to the function allowing users to test possible investment performance after certain time periods based on provided parameters. Equalization and optimization functions refer to tools allowing users to obtain the best performance based on provided constraints and quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed data.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Procedures

Before the experiment started, each participant read and signed a consent form describing the purpose of the research. Subjects were told that any decision they made would be independent of their academic performance and that the experiment process would be anonymous so that their con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality was assured. All experiments were held by the same facilitator to reduce the possibility of bias.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, the researcher gave a 5-minute introduction of the two investment options (a <sup>fi</sup>nancial holding company and a high-tech company), during which basic information about these two companies was presented. After this brief introduction, subjects were asked to provide their preconceptions by making a quick decision <sup>fi</sup>rst. The system popped up a message asking them to arrange their total money at hand between the two companies. They were also asked to provide their level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence about their current belief or decision.

After participants made their initial decision, “what if” analysis tools and the current status of each market was shown on the screen. Positive and negative information was represented with a brief title only. Participants had to click on the title to obtain detailed information. Decision makers could use “what if” analysis tools to calculate possible returns according to their understanding of the market and the provided information. The “what if” tool included built-in equations for subjects to calculate expected returns, and understand different conditions, e.g. risks and return rates. In this stage, each subject's information acquisition behaviors, including reading sequence and time, were automatically recorded by the system.

When participants felt ready to make their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, they pressed a button on the screen and the system led them to the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision screen. In this stage, they had to decide how much money should be invested into which market. After participants had made their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, they were asked to rate their level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence about their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision and answer prede<sup>fi</sup>ned questions, including satisfaction with the decision process and outcome. Finally, they were thanked for their participation.

## 4.2. Pilot tests

The experiment was pilot tested twice with master and senior students from the MIS department. After the <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot test with eight volunteer students, we re<sup>fi</sup>ned the process <sup>fl</sup>ow and divided the company information page into two: one for company information and the other for a preference test. In addition, we invited three students studying Human-Interface to review our experiment system and to provide us with comments on the functional and interface design. On the basis of their comments, we improved our system, making its operation smoother and more user-friendly. The second pilot test was conducted with 15 students. As no revisions were needed after the second pilot test, this version was considered to be the <sup>fi</sup>nal one.

## 4.3. Subjects

A total of 241 subjects were recruited from students enrolled in on-the-job MBA programs at 7 universities and a training program at one non-degree level academic institution. We used the following approaches to recruit subjects. First, an advertisement was posted on both electronic and physical bulletin boards in each university and institution. Second, face-to-face recruitment was undertaken during the break time between some classes. A gift coupon to the value of NTD100 was provided as a reward for each subject. (At the time of the study, the price of a McDonald's Big Mac in Taiwan was NTD75.) Since our prede<sup>fi</sup>ned task was stock investment, we focused on subjects with stock or other investing experience. All of the sub jects had experience with stock or other investments. The average age was 33.00 years old with a standard deviation of 6.118. The oldest subject was 52 years old and the youngest was 19 years old. ## 4.4. Measurement

## 4.4.1. Confirmation bias

Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias refers to the extent to which subjects read news items that <sup>fi</sup>t with their preconception. In this study, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias is measured by decision makers’ biased information reading behavior. A two-step approach was used to obtain the level of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias. First, the preconception was derived from their initial preference. For example, we believe that subjects deciding to put more money in the high-tech industry (or <sup>fi</sup>nance industry) in the initial stage indicate that they possessed a preconception about the high-tech industry (or <sup>fi</sup>nance industry). Second, once the preconcep tion was determined, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias was ascertained by calculating the number of news items they read on each industry. A binary score represents the existence of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias: “1”; or no con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias: “0”. Individuals holding preconceptions about the hightech industry were assigned with 1 (with con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias) if the number of read positive news items about the high-tech industry (or <sup>fi</sup>nance industry) or negative news items about the <sup>fi</sup>nance industry (or high-tech industry) divided by the total number of read news items was higher than 50%. For example, because subject A prefers the high-tech industry, A will tend to read positive messages about the high-tech industry and negative messages about the <sup>fi</sup>nance industry if con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias is present. Let us assume that A has read 10 messages, 3 of which are positive messages about the high-tech industry, and 4 of which are negative messages about the <sup>fi</sup>nance industry. A is then categorized into the group with con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias since $^ { * } ( 3 + 4 ) / 1 0 = 7 0 \% ^ { \dprime }$ is higher than 50%. In addition, it is worthy of note that, for those in the counter-argument group, pop-up messages were not included in the total number of read news items. This is because, in order to align with our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, only selective reading behaviors were counted. It is noticeable that 31 subjects who have no initial preference (arranging equal amount money to two companies) were not included in the later analysis stage because the determination of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias is impossible for those people. As a result, the <sup>fi</sup>nal analysis only included 209 subjects and 109 of them indicated a tendency towards con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias.

## 4.4.2. Satisfaction

User satisfaction includes two major dimensions: satisfaction with the decision-making process and with the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision [48]. A total of three questions adopted from Sanders and Courtney [48] were used to measure each subject's satisfaction with the outcome. The subjects were asked if they agreed or disagreed with the following: “I am happy with the decision”; “I am satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the decision”; and “It is a pleasure to use this system to help me make a decision”. On the other hand, four questions adopted from Green and Taber [23] were used to measure satisfaction with the decision-making process. The subjects were asked whether they agreed or disagreed with the following: “The decision-making process is very ef<sup>fi</sup>cient”; “The decision-making process is high quality”; “The function and information provided by the system is clear to me”; “A better decision process is obtained by using the decision support tools provided.”

## 4.4.3. Decision confidence

A total of two questions were used to measure decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence [41]. Perceived Con<sup>fi</sup>dence measured a decision maker's level of con-<sup>fi</sup>dence that their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision was correct: “I have con<sup>fi</sup>dence about the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision.” Another item measured the decision maker's perception of the ensuing result arising from their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision: “I have con<sup>fi</sup>dence that my investment performance is high.” A 7-point Likert-scale was adopted as a measure for this.

## 4.4.4. Decision adjustment

Each subject made two decisions throughout the entire experiment. The <sup>fi</sup>rst decision was made after examining the basic information about the two companies. This was viewed as initial anchoring. The second decision was made after using DSS. Decision adjustment refers to the level of change between the two decisions, in terms of the initial anchoring. We <sup>fi</sup>rst recorded the exact distance between two decisions. Decision adjustment was then determined by dividing the exact distance with the <sup>fi</sup>rst decision. The purpose of considering initial decision is to capture actual signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of adjustment. For example, one decision maker allocates 60% of own investment on hightech industry initially and assume that he/she reduced 10% of their investment on high-tech industry in the second round decision. The decision adjustment is 17% (10/60). A positive value indicates that the decision maker moved their decision closer to their preconception; their second decision was to invest more money in the preferred company. In contrast, a negative value was found when the decision maker moved their decision further away from their preconception;

## 4.6. Manipulation check

Counter-argument in this study refers to information that opposes the preferred argument. If the participant was reading positive information about the high-tech industry, the counter-argument would be negative information about the high-tech industry or positive information about the <sup>fi</sup>nancial holding industry. One question was used to measure the counter-argument effect: “did you notice that there was another screen that popped up after you read each news item?”

Since we wanted to manipulate the effect with and without counter-argument, subjects were asked to recall whether they had noticed the pop-up window containing the counter-argument information during the experiment. We found that 115 subjects in the group with CMCA had been aware of the pop-up window and only two subjects had not. A total of 86 subjects in the group without CMCA had not been aware of the pop-up window and only 13 subjects had been. The result of subjects’ awareness is shown in Table 4.

Since there were two options (IT and Finance companies), we randomized the location of the news items related to the industry to avoid the possible confounding effect caused by the location of the information. As shown in Table 5, the number of IT-related news items that appeared on the left-hand side and the right-hand side for with and without counter-argument groups was very similar.

## 4.7. Hypotheses testing

A two-step testing approach was adopted to examine our proposed hypotheses. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, two t-tests were conducted to check whether signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences could be found between people who do and do not have con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias (H1). It is worthy of note that only subjects receiving no CMCA were included in this stage because the including of subjects in receipt of CMCA may have generated a confounding effect. That is, subjects tend to adjust their decisions away from their preconceptions and have lower con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels when they receive CMCA (as we argued). On the other hand, for those in the group without CMCA, subjects tend to adjust their decisions away from the center and demonstrate higher con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels. In view of this, the mixing of these two groups when calculating the impacts of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias on decisional outcomes was deemed inappropriate in this study.

## 5. Conclusion

As human beings are structured by rationality, decision makers frequently divert their decision-making process off-track. Decisionmaking support systems have been designed to enhance the computing power of the human brain and to avoid some biases which tend to appear when information loading exceeds processing capability. This indicates that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation biases can, at least, be reduced by using computer-mediated counter-argument. This study focuses on con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias and attempts to introduce one de-bias function which may reduce the negative impacts of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias on the decisionmaking process. An experiment was conducted to illustrate how con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias misleads decision makers during the information-seeking and reading process. The relationship between con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias and decision outcomes, between counter-argument and satisfaction, and the moderating effect of counter-argument were hypothesized. After collecting data from 241 subjects with <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment experience, most of our proposed hypotheses were supported, as shown in Table 9. The effect of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias has been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed once again. In addition, we have also shown how con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias can be reduced through the introduction of counter-argument.

## 5.1. Implications for research

Several implications for academia can be gleaned from our research. First, various cognitive biases may lead human decision making in the wrong direction in either a with-computer or without-computer context [11,42,47]. Although several de-bias functions have been proposed in the extant literature, none has been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in the context of computer-supported decision making. This study has addressed this research gap and demonstrated that the use of computersupported decision-making systems with de-bias embedded functions can reduce the effect generated by cognitive bias. Our research results have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias (a cognitive bias) during the decision-making process may be reduced through the use of computer-supported de-bias functions, named computer-mediated counter-argument. ## 5.2. Implications for practice

For a decision support systems designer, de-bias functions should be included in the system design. Our study shows that systems should provide certain functions to prevent, or reduce the level of bias. However, designers should also note that some unwanted effects may be caused by those de-bias functions. In our study, we provided one piece of counter-argument when subjects read one piece of news. However, the pop-up counter-argument may lead to negative emotional consequences, e.g. annoyance or confusion. Too many counter-arguments or too much information may increase the effect of information loading. Therefore, the frequency and timing used for providing counter-argument is important. At the same time, it is important for system designers also to take the type of task and other factors into consideration.

In addition, the counter-arguments used in this study were predetermined by the researcher. The direction and importance of each piece of information provided was veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by experts to increase the validity of the study. However, in a real-world context, where news and information come from various resources, there is an increase in the degree of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in determining the direction and importance of each new piece of information. As a consequence, the determining and choice of counter-argument become dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. An effective algorithm to determine the direction of incoming information is needed before this function can be incorporated into system design to generate the anticipated effect.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

In addition to our contributions, several limitations can be identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed. First, a single task, consisting of two options only, in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial area was used to test the proposed concept. As con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias takes place in every common daily decision, the replicating of this study in different decision settings (e.g. in a context where there are more than two options; or in different decision areas) is needed to verify the effect of counter-argument. Second, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias may be eliminated or reduced through different approaches. To simplify the experimental settings, only one de-biasing function was included in this study. Future research should include other de-biasing functions and compare the effect of those different de-biasing functions in different settings. Third, this was a cross-sectional study and the existence of counter-argument was unexpected by the subjects. People may become used to the appearance of counter-argument and begin to ignore it automatically. Future research may extend this research by conducting a longitudinal study to examine the above assumption. Fourth, the source of information, which may play a role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing belief revision, is not included in this study. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory information search behavior may be enhanced by various contextual variables, such as strong commitment, high choice levels, limited availability of information, incentives and rewards, framing effect, and information presentation [16,17,33,34]. Future research should take those factors into consideration in the research design to ascertain the impact of counter-argument in different contexts. Finally, although the effect of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias and methods to prevent it has been illustrated, the research result is based on decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence and adjustment only; other decision outcomes, such as decision appropriateness, are not included.
