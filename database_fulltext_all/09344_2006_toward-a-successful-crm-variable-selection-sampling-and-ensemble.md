# Toward a successful CRM: variable selection, sampling, and ensemble

YongSeog Kim<sup>\*</sup>

Business Information Systems, Utah State University, UMC 3515 Old Main Hill, Logan, UT 84322, USA

Received 3 June 2003; received in revised form 1 September 2004; accepted 2 September 2004 Available online 2 November 2004

## Abstract

This paper studies the effects of variable selection and class distribution on the performance of specific logit regression (i.e., a primitive classier system) and artificial neural network (ANN; a relatively more sophisticated classifier system) implementations in a customer relationship management (CRM) setting. Finally, ensemble models are constructed by combining the predictions of multiple classiers. This paper shows that ANN ensembles with variable selection show the most stable performance over various class distributions. <sup>D</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: CRM; Variable selection; Sampling; Ensemble; Neural network

## 1. Introduction

Advances in data warehouses and the vast amount of customers’ demographic, psychographic, and behavioral information provide marketing managers a new marketing channel—database micromarketing. Traditional mass marketing channels (e.g., advertisements in TV and newspapers) have been very successful and are still important. However, customer relationship management (CRM) programs for macromarketing slowly give way to new CRM programs for micromarketing. This is because, in micromarketing programs, the firms can develop a marketing message directed toward a specific group of households that are most likely to open to the customized message.

Both marketing [7,13] and data mining researchers [3,10] have presented various database marketing approaches for successful CRM programs. The simplest example is the RFM (recency, frequency, monetary) approach that targets households by using knowledge of the customer’s purchase history [21]. When targeting new households with no prior relationship, the analysis of the relationship between demographics and the response to a test mailing of a representative household sample can be utilized. In Piatetsky-Shapiro and Masand [18], the protability condition of a campaign was explicitly formulated as a function of the lift of the model, uniform campaign cost per mailing, and marginal revenue per identified positive record. Chou et al. [9] devised an effective model for identifying prospective insurance buyers when buyer versus nonbuyer information is not available. Gersten et al. [12] presented a model to select prospects in the automotive industry where the buying decision takes a long time. A good summary of related studies on CRM programs from marketing and data mining communities can be found in Kim and Street [15].

Traditionally, the optimal selection of customer targets has been considered one of the most important factors for a successful CRM program. Thus, many models have been proposed to identify as many customers as possible who will respond to a specific solicitation campaign letter, or who will end further relationships with the firm. In particular, with exceptionally high annual churn rates (20–40%), firms in mobile telecommunications industry try to develop predictive models that accurately identify which customers are most likely to churn. In addition to the predictive accuracy, the comprehensibility of a model becomes another important issue in developing CRM programs. A rule-based system that consists of too many if–then statements makes it difficult for marketing to understand key drivers of consumer behaviors. The poor comprehensibility can greatly reduce managers’ trust in the system itself, and prevent decision makers from developing long-term CRM programs.

The ultimate goal of this study is to provide practitioners useful guidelines to building predictive models for effective CRM programs. In particular, this paper investigates the importance of variable selection and class distribution, and how they can affect the performance of predictive models. Specific implementations of two different learning algorithms are used: logit regression (i.e., a primitive model) and artificial neural networks (ANNs; a relatively more sophisticated classifier model). These algorithms have been widely used in developing predictive models for CRM applications. In particular, ANNs have been used in many other marketing applications such as customer clustering [1] and market segmentation [14]. In this study, ANNs and logit linear regression are used to estimate each individual’s likelihood of ending current relationships after learning linear or possibly nonlinear relationships between given input variables and the churner indicator.

Variable selection is used to enhance the comprehensibility of models in this paper. Variable selection is the process of choosing a subset of the original predictive variables by eliminating variables that are either redundant or possess little predictive information. By identifying key determinants of churning behaviors of customers, variable selection can not only enhance the comprehensibility of predictive models but also save a great amount of computational time and cost. However, eliminating many input variables may have different effects on the predictive accuracy of models depending on their representational powers and structural complexities. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the relationship between variable selection and the predictive accuracy of predictive models over various class distributions.

Various sampling techniques are also used to shorten computational time and enhance the accuracy of a predictive model by removing noise records that have the same values for input variables except class indicator. Sampling in this paper is also used to vary class distributions to study the relationships between class distribution and the predictive accuracy of classifiers. When class distributions in the training and test data are significantly different, the calibrated model on the training data may not perform well on the test data. However, the same class distribution of the training and test data does not necessarily make the calibrated model perform best on the test set [23]. Therefore, it is necessary to vary the class distribution of the training data to the optimal class distribution for calibrating predictive models. In particular, this paper intends to identify optimal class distributions for different types of classifiers.

This paper also presents an ensemble approach that combines the predictions of multiple models. In order to build an ensemble, the estimated probabilities of being a churner from multiple models are combined with the equal weight. Furthermore, the effects of variable selection, structural complexity of models, and class distribution on the ensemble models are empirically estimated to provide decision makers and data analysts useful guidelines on how to develop an accurate model for CRM programs.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly reviews variable subset selection, sampling, and ensemble decision making. Section 3 introduces the original data set and presents a reduced variable subset by a simple variable selection algorithm. In Section 4, the experimental results of both ANN and logit models are presented. In particular, the relative importance of variable selection, sampling, and ensemble decision making in two different algorithms is contrasted and highlighted. Section 5 concludes the paper and provides suggestions for future research directions.

## 2.1. Variable selection

Variable selection is a process of choosing a subset of the original predictive variables by eliminating variables with little predictive information. If one extracts as much information as possible from a given data while using the smallest number of variables, it is possible to save a great amount of computational time and cost. Variable selection can also significantly improve the comprehensibility of resulting models and make resulting models generalize better. Most of all, finding the correct subset of predictive variables is critical for managers who want to develop comprehensive marketing strategies based on key drivers of consumer response.

Many algorithms for variable selection are based on sequential search, nonlinear optimization [5], and genetic algorithms (GAs) [15,25]. However, this paper employs a simple statistical procedure, forward variable selection, which starts with the empty set of variables and greedily adds variables that most improve performance. The forward selection is a very simple algorithm and hence makes it easy to study the effect of variable selection procedure on the performance of different classifiers over various class distributions. The choice of a standard variable selection algorithm also makes it easy to replicate results presented in this study by using most statistical packages including SAS and SPSS. Furthermore, more sophisticated algorithms are computationally expensive and often require subjective parameter settings.

## 2.2. Sampling and class distribution

It is often desirable to use a small number of examples to develop a predictive model because more records require more computing power, computational time, and memory space. There are also costs associated with large data to obtain, clean, and transform them [22]. In addition, models built on a smaller number of examples have advantages over models built on a larger number of records in terms of predictive accuracy and robustness [4]. For example, it is shown that there is a linear relationship between model size and data size beyond the point at which accuracy ceases to increase [16]. Models based on many examples are more likely to find fluke rules or fit noise well, and may not generalize well to new data.

Sampling is the most intuitive way to analyze huge amounts of data. However, sampling should be carried out carefully because biased sampling can change the true patterns in the original data and guide classifiers toward incorrect solution spaces. For example, assume that there are 100,000 examples for calibrating a model, and the examples are evenly distributed to two classes: churners and nonchurners. In an extreme case, if examples from only one of the two classes are taken (say, 30,000 examples from churners) to train classifiers, the classifiers cannot learn patterns to distinguish churners from nonchurners at all.

It is often believed that if the class distribution of the training data matches the natural distribution of the test data, induction algorithms can perform very well. However, the naturally occurring class distribution often is not best for learning [23]. This paper applies an undersampling approach to the original data set to both reduce the number of records and to change the class distributions in the training set. Chan and Stolfo [8] also studied the relationship between class distribution and classifiers performance. They ran preliminary experiments to determine the best class distribution for learning, and then generated multiple training sets with the best class distribution. However, the evaluation metrics in their paper are based on the classification accuracy over all examples. In this paper, the evaluation metrics are based on the classification accuracy over only a prefixed percentage of examples. The effects of various class distributions would likely be more significant if only a prefixed percentage of examples is considered in the evaluation process. In particular, this paper considers the interactive effects of variable selection and sampling on the performance of learning algorithms with different levels of complexity.

## 2.3. Ensemble decision making

Recently, many researchers have combined the predictions of multiple classifiers to produce an ensemble to improve the performance of a single classifiers. Bagging [6] and Boosting [11] are the most popular methods for creating an accurate ensemble. Bagging is a bootstrap ensemble method that trains each classifier on a randomly drawn training set. Each classifier’s training set consists of the same number of examples randomly drawn from the original training set, with an equal probability of drawing any given example. Samples are drawn with replacement so that some examples may be selected multiple times while others may not be selected at all. As a result, each classifier could return a higher test set error than a classifier using all of the data. However, when these classifiers are combined (typically by voting), the resulting ensemble produces a lower error on the test set than a single classifier.

Boosting [11] produces a series of classifiers, with each training set based on the performance of the previous classifiers. New classifiers are constructed to better predict examples for which the current ensemble’s performance is poor. This is accomplished using adaptive resampling (i.e., examples that are incorrectly predicted by previous classifiers are sampled more frequently, or alternately, given a higher cost of misclassification). Boosting can be implemented in two different ways: Arcing and AdaBoosting. In Arcing, the classifiers’ votes are weighted equally, while AdaBoost weights the predictions based on the classifiers’ training error.

The diversity among component classifiers of ensemble has been proven critical to attaining higher generalization accuracy [2,17]. An ensemble generalizes well by combining many accurate component classifiers that make errors on different parts of data. Although an ensemble in this study still shares common properties with other ensemble methods, it is constructed differently from traditional ensembles. For example, samples in this study are drawn with no replacement and no examples are selected multiple times. This is reasonable when abundant data records are available as in this study. Furthermore, the number of examples to be sampled is set to half of the number of records in the original data to reduce training time of classifiers. Note that the sampling procedure in this paper is used to both reduce data size and vary class distribution.

An ensemble classifier in this paper is created by combining the estimated probabilities of churning, instead of combining the predicted binary class indicator. To combine the probabilities, the estimated probabilities from multiple classifiers are simply added (or can be averaged). Combining predicted class indicators of multiple classifiers may not be appropriate in this paper for the following reasons. When a classifier predicts the class label, its decision is based on a predetermined threshold. Typically, if the estimated probability is less than 0.5, a classifier returns <sup>b</sup>0<sup>Q</sup> to indicate a nonchurner; otherwise, it returns <sup>b</sup>1<sup>Q</sup> to indicate a churner. Since the return value of an ensemble classifier is based on the majority of class labels from multiple classifiers, many records can belong to the same class. In an extreme case, all records can belong to one group (e.g., nonchurner) and, in such a case, it is almost impossible to rank individuals by breaking the ties. However, combining the estimated probabilities makes it easy to rank records and choose the top x% of records based on estimated probabilities of becoming a churner even when they belong to the same class.

## 3. Data description and variable selection

## 3.1. Data description and evaluation

The data sets used in this study are provided by the Teradata Center for CRM at Duke University, and the following description of the data sets is a short summary of the detailed description in http:// faculty.fuqua.duke.edu/teradatacenter/. The original data set for calibration has 171 predictor variables of 100,000 examples. The complete set of variables is shown in Table 1. The predictors include three types of variables: behavioral information such as minutes of use, revenue, handset equipment; company interaction information such as customer calls into the customer service center; and customer household demographics. Customers were sampled during July, September, November, and December 2001 among customers who were with the company for at least 6 months. For each customer, churn was calculated based on whether the customer left the company during the 31- to 60-day period after the customer was originally sampled. Although the actual percentage of customers who churn in a given month is approximately 1.8%, churners in the original data set were oversampled to create roughly a 50–50 split between churners and nonchurners.

As a preprocessing step for further analysis, the original data are divided into two sets: a training set with the first 60,000 examples for model calibration and a test set with the 40,000 examples for model evaluation. Note that there are roughly the same number of churners (18,767) and nonchurners (21,233) in the test data. However, this class distribution does not reflect the actual percentage of churners in the telecommunications industry (1.8% per month for data collected in periods over 6 months). Therefore, a new test set with a 10–90 split between churners and nonchurners is constructed by randomly selecting 2000 churners (about 10% of churners) and 18,000 nonchurners (about 90% of nonchurners) from the current test data.

Five different subtraining sets are constructed from the training set that has 60,000 examples. Each subtraining set is denoted as $S _ { \mathrm { c h u r n e r s ^ { \circ } / o ; n o n c h u r n e r s ^ { \circ } / o } } ,$ where (churners percent, nonchurners percent) split is one of the following: (10%, 90%), (20%, 80%), (30%, 70%), (40%, 60%), and (50%, 50%). In order to keep the same number of examples in the subtraining sets and to reduce computational times and spaces for model calibration, each subtraining set consists of 30,000 examples. For example, $S _ { 1 0 , 9 0 }$ consists of 3000 churners and 27,000 nonchurners. Each subtraining set is simply referred as a training set for the convenience of notation.

A brief review of evaluation metrics for marketing campaigns can be found in Rosset et al. [19], and two representative metrics are often used to evaluate the performance of predictive models: hit rate and Gini index. The hit rate is defined as the number of correctly identified churners out of churner candidates. When only x% of customers predicted most likely to churn are considered for the model evaluation, it is called hit rate at target point x%. For example, if the model is required to select 1000 customers who are most likely to churn from 10,000 examples, and 200 of them turn out to be actual churners, then a hit rate at target point 10% (1000/ $1 0 , 0 0 0 { = } 1 0 \% )$ is 20% (200/1000=20%). Another metric, the Gini index, is a measurement of the difference in hit rates of random selection and a proposed model over all target points. The hit rate curve of the random selection is the diagonal line because it is expected to identify 10%, 20%, . . ., 100% of churners at target points 10%, 20%, . . ., 100%. The Gini index can measure the robustness of a model because it measures the performance over all target points. This paper uses the raw number of correctly identified churners when the top 10% of customers ranked in descending order of churn probability values are chosen for model evaluation. This is mainly because the firms have limited budget and time constraints to develop marketing programs.

## 3.2. Data reduction with forward variable selection

For further analysis, the original data are preprocessed as follows. First, all categorical variables (mainly demographic variables) are excluded. This is because each categorical variable has very little predictive power in general [20], and can greatly increase the search space when it is encoded into multiple binary variables in ANNs. Second, continuous variables with more than 20% of missing values are eliminated. Through this explanatory data preprocessing step, the total 117 continuous predictors are identified and this variable subset is denoted as Variable<sub>117</sub>.

Another new variable subset is constructed from ${ \mathrm { V a r i a b l e } } _ { 1 1 7 }$ in order to measure the effects of variable selection. The PROC logistic regression with forward variable selection in SAS is used for this purpose. When provided with the churn indicator as a dependent variable and all 117 continuous variables as independent variables, the forward variable selection method starts with the empty set of variables, and greedily adds variables based on chisquare scores. It stops adding new variables when there is no additional variable that satisfies the predefined significance level (e.g., a=0.05) for entry into the model.

The forward variable selection method selects 27 variables out of 117 input variables, and the lists of chosen variables with corresponding chi-square values are summarized in Table 2. With the exception of two demographics variables entered at steps 2 and 26, all other chosen variables reflect customer’s usage behaviors and interactions with the company. This findings are in line with marketing science work on customer segmentation, which shows that information about current behaviors is most predictive of future choices [20]. For the convenience of notation, the chosen variable subset is denoted as Variable .

Using a tuning set to determine training epochs is well known and practiced.

## 4.2. Sampling and ensemble decision making

An ensemble is a composite classifier that combines the predictions of multiple classifiers. In Tables 3 and 4, for each classifier system (logit or ANN), the average performance of 25 classifiers is used to estimate the effects of variable selection and class distribution of the training set. In order to build an ensemble for each classifier system, the estimated likelihood values by 25 classifiers for each record in the test set are combined, and the top $10 \%$ of records are chosen as the output of an ensemble classifier. The results are summarized in Table 5.

In general, ensemble models greatly improve the accuracy of a single classifier. However, the accuracy gain from combining multiple classifiers is dependent on classifier types. For example, ANN ensembles show great improvement compared to a single ANN model over all samples (comparison between Tables 4 and 5). Logit ensembles with feature selection also perform better than a single logit model on most samples except $S _ { 5 0 , 5 0 }$ (comparison between Tables 3 and 5).

The effects of variable selection on the performance of logit and ANN ensembles are almost identical to the corresponding effects on the performance of a single logit and an ANN model. ANN ensembles with $\mathrm { V a r i a b l e } _ { 2 7 }$ show higher performance over all samples than ANNs with ${ \mathrm { V a r i a b l e } } _ { 1 1 7 }$ . However, logit ensembles with variable selection are superior to logit ensembles without variable selection only on two samples $( S _ { 1 0 , 9 0 }$ and $S _ { 2 0 , 8 0 } )$ , and show worse performance on the remaining three samples.

One possible explanation for this finding is the positive relationship between the diversity of an ensemble and its accuracy. According to many studies [2,17], the accuracy of an ensemble is dependent on both the accuracy of individual classifier and the overall diversity of an ensemble as a collection of classifiers. That is, the more diverse and the more accurate classifiers are, the more accurate an ensemble model is. As each logit model becomes more accurate with more balanced examples of two different classes and more diverse as more information is available from more variables, the logit ensemble becomes highly accurate. However, each ANN model shows worse performance as the gap of class distribution between the training and test sets increases. Therefore, the performance of ANN ensembles could be worse than logit ensembles over samples of which class distributions are different from natural class distributions.

Note also that the performance of ANN ensembles is relatively more stable than logit ensembles over various class distributions. In particular, ANN ensembles with $\mathrm { V a r i a b l e } _ { 2 7 }$ show the most stable performance and are accurate over various class distributions. The best class distribution for logit ensembles without variable selection is a 30–70 split, followed by a 40– 60 and a 50–50 split. However, for ANN ensembles with and without variable selection, the first two distributions (10–90 and 20–80 splits) lead to good results.

## 5. Conclusion and future research

This paper studies the effects of variable selection and class distribution on the performance of individual and ensemble classifiers. Fig. 2 graphically shows the performance of logit and ANN models in previous three tables. As a general guideline, it is very important for data analysts to consider the relationship between the amount of information and the complexity of predictive models. When highly sophisticated predictive models are chosen for data analysis, compact information via variable selection is highly recommended. Sophisticated models with compact information show great improvement in terms of predictive accuracy and robustness. However, when the predictive model is primitive, more input variables provide additional information to enhance the performance of classifiers. Most of all, by combining multiple classifiers as an ensemble, market managers can have an accurate model. Note, however, that an ensemble model is computationally expensive compared to a single predictive model.

Although the results presented in this study are promising, they may be suboptimal solutions. One of possible reasons comes from the fact that the forward selection may produce suboptimal solutions because of its irrevocable variable selection process and inability to consider the interaction among variables. For example, consider a variable $f _ { k + 1 }$ , which is not very predictive alone and, therefore, is not chosen among top k variables. However, it is possible that $f _ { k + 1 }$ can contribute more than the variable $f _ { 1 } ,$ , chosen at the first stage to the predictive power when combined with other k1 selected variables. However, this paper does not analyze this possibility further to focus on the more fundamental research goal: estimating relative effects of various factors to develop successful CRM programs.

In future work, various classifiers for CRM programs will be compared and analyzed to confirm and complement the findings in this paper. Note that the current paper investigates a particular instance of primitive (logit regression) and sophisticated classifiers (ANNs); hence, the findings in this paper apply only to the instances examined. The structural and representational characteristics of classifiers can affect the relative importance of variable selection and class distribution. For example, a naive Bayes classifier, one Bayesian learning method, can be more sensitive to variable selection and class distribution than classifiers analyzed in this paper.
