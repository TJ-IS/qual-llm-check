# To Monitor or Not To Monitor: Effectiveness of Cyberloafing Countermeasure

Jeremy Glassman, Marilyn Prosch, and Benjamin B. M. Shao<sup>\*</sup>

Department of Information Systems W. P. Carey School of Business Arizona State University Tempe, AZ 85287-4606, USA

## Abstract

The goal of this study is to explore and analyze the effectiveness of a possible countermeasure to this so-called "cyberloafing" problem through a technical solution of Internet filtering and monitoring. Through a multi-theoretical lens, we utilize operant conditioning as well as one's psychological morals of procedural justice and social norms to study the effectiveness of such countermeasure in addressing the associated agency problem and promoting behavior compliance with the organization's Internet usage policy. We find confirmation and quota modules of Internet filtering and monitoring also can prevent shirking and promote better compliance through employee empowerment and attention resource replenishment.

Keywords: Cyberloafing, Internet filtering and monitoring, agency theory, operant conditioning, procedural justice, social norms.

Revised May 2014

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Computers were primarily created and used for work-related functions in their early days. However, with the advent of the World Wide Web and ubiquitous computing, computers have morphed into being used as a hobby and for social connections as well. The use of web-enabled computing devices for personal leisure combined with business use has dramatically grown in recent years. Since computers and Internet access play an increasingly important role in personal and professional lives of knowledge workers, the overlapping of the two aspects has posed a serious issue for management across organizations [19].

In essence, the ubiquity of information technology (IT) and the high penetration of the Internet have led to the prevalent mixture of personal and business Internet usage at work [4]. While users can access the Internet to complete their tasks more effectively, a greater chance also exists for them to get distracted from their work while engaging in such activities as shopping online, visiting news sites, emailing family and friends, or sharing pictures on social networks. This new way of loafing on the job has been coined as cyberloafing [27] [28].

Muhl [35] found that in many organizations, management and employees have an implicit understanding that a certain amount of personal Internet usage is allowed at work. However, with the multitude of addicting Internet services such as social networks, games, and the ever-quickening news cycle [47], many more employers are concerned that this “allowance” can be abused. The practice of Internet abuse may affect organizations in several ways. For example, companies have to increase IT expenditures, combat the exposure of their networks to potential threats, and monitor employee

productivity more closely [38]. In an effort to reduce exposure to these risks, organizations have adopted and implemented different control mechanisms that include Internet usage policies [17] and software systems designed to block access to certain websites [44].

In a survey on the compliance and enforcement of Internet usage policies [17], onerous web filtering rules and severe sanctions for non-compliance are found that could cause negative effects contrary to what policymakers intended. Henle et al. [17] utilized personal norms to measure the likelihood of an employee to comply with the Internet usage policies. They found employees with high personal norms when faced with an increase in the severity of sanctions for non-compliance actually would be less likely to comply with the policies, while those with low personal norms increase their likelihood to comply. Their finding suggests that any effective solution to cyberloafing would need to remain non-intrusive for those who are more likely to comply with the Internet usage policy but be more direct for those who are less likely to comply [30]. In addition, researchers in a cross-cultural study found that perceptions about the utilization and benefits of information technology may affect their usage of such technology [3].

The possibility for the abuse of Internet resources is an example of an agency problem where employees (agents) and the employer (principal) have incongruent goals. Information asymmetry between employees and the employer exists because the employer is unaware of how employees utilize the Internet resources of the organization [22]. As with most agency problems, monitoring is the readily available solution, for example, through the use of an Internet filtering and monitoring software package in this case. The goal of this study is to investigate the effectiveness of such countermeasure to cyberloafing through prolonged exposure to a conditioning stimulus that reinforces the notion that excessive non-work-related use of the Internet is unacceptable at work.

Sales of Internet filtering software show that its popularity is continually rising, as industry in 2012 was estimated to be \$1.18 billion by Gartner Reports [36]. From their analysis, the market grew approximately 15% from 2011 to 2012, and they anticipate that the market will grow another 13% to 15% in 2013. The leaders in the Internet filtering and security market are Websense, Cisco, McAfee, and Bluecoat Systems, among others. Within the web filtering industry, different types of filtering systems are available. Internet filtering systems currently available are all capable of blocking sites placed on a black list and/or only allowing sites placed on a white list. Some Internet-filtering systems also monitor and record all employee access to the Internet and produce reports for management. Previous research provides evidence that this type of system does not sufficiently address the underlying issues associated with abusive Internet access; moreover, it also engenders a sense of employee resentment to potential surveillance from its enforcement [44].

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss different types of Internet usage filtering software available on the market and review the related theories that can be leveraged to study the agency problem of cyberloafing in the workplace. We then examine individual filtering modules of an industry-leading Internet filtering system, state the goal of each module for hypothesis formulation, and link each module to the associated theories. The filtering system that we describe has been operating in a mid-sized diversified company, and we examine the monitoring data

gathered by the organization for hypothesis testing to study the effectiveness of different Internet filtering modules. Finally, we discuss the results and draw their implications for practice and future research before we conclude the paper.

## 2. THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

Prior research has examined several potential methods to mitigate inappropriate Internet usage, including the establishment of Internet usage policies, training, and monitoring. Researchers have found that periodic monitoring through software that tracks Internet usage leads to less cyberloafing [17]. In a laboratory experiment, employees who were informed that their Internet usage would be electronically monitored were more task-focused, meaning they engaged in less cyberloafing than those who were monitored but had no knowledge of whether the monitoring system was actually implemented and functioning [44]. Hence, an electronic monitoring system alone would not be a perfectly effective solution and has to be supplemented by other methods. One method by which employees would become aware of an electronic monitoring system is through an established corporate policy that raises employee awareness documented by their signature [48]. Another method, which we examine in this study, is the use of a filtering mechanism to establish awareness of this monitoring policy and deter inappropriate use through prohibition of cyberloafing activities.

The extant research on the phenomenon of abusive Internet practices at work focuses on short-term experiments to determine how users respond to deterrence systems. The goal of this study, on the other hand, is to investigate prolonged exposure to a conditioning stimulus that reinforces the notion that excessive non-work-related use of the Internet is unacceptable at work. In most existing web monitoring software, this

#

conditioning process involves a managerial role that requires employees’ supervisors to act on the information from a generated report and to inform users of their acceptable or unacceptable behaviors. Existing related research has followed this approach where users were notified of their monitored actions if they had abused their Internet access [44]. In essence, a system that reminds users the site they are visiting has questionable work value every time they access it provides real-time feedback to employees. Furthermore, such systems serve another purpose of deterrence to dissuade employees from abusing their Internet access. In the following subsections, we review relevant theories that pertain to the explanation of cyberloafing and its potential avoidance. The theories include agency theory, operant conditioning, procedural justice, and social norms.

## 2.1. Agency Theory

The basis of agency theory is a relationship between a principal and an agent who acts on behalf of the principal [22]. Such relationships occur when the owner of resources (principal) hires another party (agent) to perform work. The most recognizable form of such agency relationships is that of employer (principal) and employees (agents). While both parties work toward the same goal, they may not always share the same interests. A key element of an agency problem is the goal incongruence where the goal of the agent is inconsistent with that of the principal. When the agreement is made through mechanisms like a contract, the lack of perfect information about goals and productivity of the agent can lead to information asymmetry and result in a monitoring problem whereby the principal cannot easily observe an agent’s actions or verify the information provided by the agent without exerting significant efforts or costs [12]. Common methods to address agency problems are intensive monitoring of agents and effective incentives for their appropriate behaviors.

Researchers in the IS field have used agency theory to explore various agency issues. For example, Dawson et al. [10] studied the relationship between consultants and clients, and they pointed out that a limitation of agency theory in its application is that the theory espouses monitoring as the primary method of combating the issues. In some cases, the intrinsic complexities of a situation with necessary specialized knowledge prevent effective monitoring. However, in our study, the technology being examined is able to automatically and effectively monitor employees in a workplace setting without involving too much human intervention.

Inappropriate Internet usage is a prime example of principal-agent relationship where the core issue can be viewed as an agency problem. In other words, cyberloafing illustrates an agency problem when an employee (agent) is cyberloafing and he/she is doing so against the wishes of the employer (principal). Viewed from the perspective of agency theory, cyberloafing occurs as many organizations do not seek to specifically address this particular agency problem and, as a consequence, do not verify whether employees have been using Internet resources appropriately or not.

## 2.2. Operant Conditioning

Operant conditioning is the process of associating a particular behavior with a certain change in the subject’s environment. This change can be a reinforcement which involves a reward to strengthen a behavior, or it can be a punishment which incurs a penalty to discourage the behavior [45]. The difference between operant conditioning and other forms of behaviorism is that subjects can generate their own activities instead of

only responding to external stimuli. According to this theory, behavior can be changed by creating consequences for the normal action of a test subject. Reinforcement is a key element of operant conditioning. A positive reinforcer strengthens the desired response by instilling a sense of pleasure or contentment for the test subject. By the same token, negative reinforcers can also exist to reprimand or cause displeasure to the test subjects for inappropriate behaviors [41].

The use of a reinforcement schedule is an essential finding from the development of the theory. A schedule of reinforcement reflects the idea that an action will only solicit a temporary reinforcement feedback. In his experiments, Skinner [41] incrementally increased the variation of the schedules of reinforcement where a rat could press a lever to receive a food pellet. A continuous schedule of reinforcement is the control scenario where every time the rat presses the lever, it would receive a food pellet. With a fixed ratio schedule, when a rat presses the lever for a constant predetermined amount of time, a food pellet would be given. A fixed ratio schedule is similar to a work environment where stipend payment is made for a pre-determined quantity of work performed. Another schedule of reinforcement is the fixed interval schedule, which uses a timing mechanism to enable or disable the food lever at a predetermined interval. With the fixed interval schedule of reinforcement, the number of pellets that could be dispensed is limited to one until the next interval with one more pellet becoming available. The last type of schedule is the variable schedule of reinforcement. With a variable schedule, the reinforcer of food pellet is given to the rat with constantly changing intervals and ratios. This variable schedule is found to be most effective at encouraging consistent actions of test subjects [41].

The idea of operant conditioning with a schedule of reinforcement can be applied to the context of Internet usage monitoring. The use of a passive monitoring system closely matches a variable schedule of reinforcement where a manager can generate abused Internet access. In terms of operant conditioning, the way in which an employee browses the Internet is the behavior of our study focus and the reprimand or dismissal of employees is the punishment.

## 2.3. Procedural Justice

Cyberloafing stems from a failure of corporate governance policies and is a type of counterproductive work behavior. Counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs) include intentional behaviors of an organizational member viewed by the organization as contrary to its legitimate interests [16]. The way the CWBs of cyberloafing have been justified is linked to employees’ attitude towards job satisfaction and organizational commitment [2]. Many reasons have been given to explain why perceived organizational justice can affect CWBs. Increased perceptions of procedural injustice, for instance, can lead to employee unwillingness to comply with an organization’s rules [7].

Previous research has shown that employee behavior is directly tied to the way in which governance policies are drafted and applied [15] [50]. This phenomenon links directly back to the social psychology theory of procedural justice. Procedural justice exists when procedures embody certain types of broadly accepted normative principles [29]. It has been shown that procedural justice is connected with cyberloafing and organizational norms underlie workplace procedures [39]. When an employee is faced with seemingly unfair procedures, the Internet misuser may develop feelings of

skepticism towards formal regulations and perceive himself/herself as being involved in a conflict [6]. These negative perceptions of a normative conflict are found to mediate the relationship between procedural justice and cyberloafing [50]

Extant research has also shown that a relationship exists between procedural justice and an employee’s perception of a conflict at work [7]. Lind and Tyler [29] found that a reduction in conflict within organizations could be explained by strong policies supporting a culture of procedural justice. From this insight, Kidwell and Martin [26] were able to find that conflicting rules and procedures, inflexible policies, and task difficulty also lead employees to perceptions of unfairness. This feeling of unfairness in turn would trigger deviant behaviors that could do damage to the organization [26].

Procedural justice has also been used to explore the attitudes toward perceived authority, as a feeling of fairness has been found to mediate attitudes toward authority [46]. In the case of cyberloafing prevention systems, the transparent nature of the classification and reclassification of Internet sites would provide employees with a signal that they are being treated in a respectful and consistently fair manner. This utilization of procedural justice is also consistent with the practice of increasing the empowerment of employees [9]. Research has indicated that a satisfactory level of discretion is important for fostering empowerment on the job [25].

## 2.4. Social Norms

Social norms are defined as “group-held beliefs about how members should behave in a given context, and these norms can be laws or informal guidelines that govern behavior in the society” [37]. People normally obey norms when they envision a particular outcome in mind. By abiding by norms, individuals want to avoid the

disapproval of others that can range from raised eyebrows to being considered a social outcast of the “in” group. Social norms are distinguished from other social constructs. For example, social norms differ from legal norms because legal norms are enforced by individuals charged with upholding the norms (e.g., police officers) while social norms are enforced by one’s peers and the enforcement can sometimes be selective [13].

The workplace can be viewed as a venue for social norm-guided behaviors [13]. Social norms in the workplace include the perception against living off other people (i.e., shirking) and a corresponding pressure to earn one’s income from their own effort. Oftentimes informal norms also exist among co-workers of a firm who regulate the effort of other co-workers. These norms set upper and lower bounds of what is perceived as a proper level of effort to prevent a feeling of “freeloading” or, on the other end of the continuum, “outshining” fellow workers. These norms can also extend to the loyalty among co-workers as it has been observed that workers within an organization sometimes have a code of honor that would forbid them to train new workers hired to do the same job for lower wages [1].

It is generally believed that workers favor leisure over effort and therefore they are predisposed to shirking [42]. Shirking refers to the avoidance of work or other responsibilities in a work environment [24]. Cyberloafing is a behavior in which an employee may engage as a form of shirking. A major responsibility of a firm’s management is to provide proper incentives to assure worker performance, especially when monitoring information on performance is costly [32]. In some cases, when properly incentivized, workers could even go against their own self-interests and provide extra value to their organization. Frey and Bohnet [14] conducted laboratory experiments and showed that people often act contrarily to their self-interest under an internal guidance of fairness and morality. This finding extends to the use of information systems and the decision making process for procuring effective IT resources used by organizations to optimize activities and reduce shirking.

Malhotra and Galletta [31] explored how users’ social norms moderate their adherence to system adoption and their usage behaviors. Their study provides a theoretical rationale for our inclusion of social norms as a potentially contributing factor to the acceptable use of Internet resources. Moreover, it has been shown that being allowed to take a break during work hours enables office workers to restore energies and mental capacities [49] . Scheduled breaks as a norm in turn have been shown to improve task vigilance by replenishing attention resources [18]. The core tenant of resource replenishment is that employees have a fixed amount of energy and they focus their energy and resources to complete tasks. When they are working on a task, this bank of energy and focus is continually being depleted. Research has indicated that by taking small breaks through the work day, this bank of energy and focus can be replenished, thereby increasing the productivity of an employee who takes breaks in contrast to one who does not [49]. In our context, studies have shown that cyberloafing activities during breaks time increase the rate at which the resource replenishment takes place [18].

## 3. FRAMEWORK AND HYPOTHESIS

Most organizations nowadays utilize some method to limit or monitor the Internet usage of their employees. The current industry standard for Internet usage monitoring software either resides on a stand-alone server or is incorporated into a service firewall.

#

Various software packages can be configured with different modules to block, inhibit, or influence a user’s access to the Internet.

At the root of cyberloafing is the existence of an agency conflict between the employees of an organization and its management. According to agency theory, the cause of the associated issues is the information asymmetry between the principal and agents [22]. To combat this information asymmetry inherent in cyberloafing, monitoring is a proven solution but monitoring alone with no subsequent consequences has been shown to be ineffective [44]. To augment a monitoring system to provide disincentives for inappropriate use, we develop a framework that combines monitoring with the theory of operant conditioning. In our framework, operant conditioning is applied to modify an employee’s behavior by utilizing methods derived from learning experiments [41]. In addition to operant conditioning, we also utilize the theories of procedural justice and social norms to develop our research framework and study the agency problem of cyberloafing. We first articulate the theoretical justifications for the act of cyberloafing in order to develop the Internet filtering framework for counteracting cyberloafing. In our model, the variable of appropriate use can be tied to the construct of productive Internet usage. The blocking module can be linked to measuring an employee’s intention to shirk, which refers to the avoidance of work or other responsibilities [24]. In regard to the quota module, research on attention resource replenishment has indicated that enjoyable breaks enable greater continued task vigilance than non-enjoyable breaks [8] [18] [49]. Extant research has also indicated that a positive correlation exists between employee empowerment and job satisfaction [43]. Our proposed framework includes a confirmation mechanism through which the employee is empowered to bypass the filtering system when the site being visited is not on the black, white or break list, and hence the system is unsure if the intended Internet use is for work-related purposes or not. Such empowerment to decide for one’s self can improve job satisfaction and productivity. In the following subsections, we discuss each module of Internet filtering and monitoring system and present the associated hypotheses.

## 3.1. Appropriate Use

The first component of the filtering system is an administrator-defined white list that enables employees to access all necessary sites to complete their normal job function. The white list is a mechanism for employers to define what is considered appropriate use of the network resources at work and therefore its access is used as the dependent variable for our analysis.

## 3.2. Blocking Module

The second component is a black list that contains the websites that are considered counterproductive for work or potentially harmful and are therefore blocked from access. When a user attempts to access these blacklisted sites and is blocked, the user is presented with a message explaining why it has been blocked (as shown in Figure 3), and he/she is asked to contact the administrator if the blocking is unwarranted. Consistent with operant conditioning and agency theory, when a user is presented with the blocking message, it conveys that the user is being monitored and is presented with a link to the organization’s policies. The policies explicitly state that employees will be held accountable for their actions. This blocking module along with the warning messages it generates as stimuli is expected to enhance a user’s awareness and appropriate use of the Internet access. Therefore, we formulate our first hypothesis as follows.

H1: The usage of a blocking module is positively associated with the appropriate use of Internet resources.

## 3.3. Confirmation Module

The next module is an active reminder mechanism to dissuade Internet usage abuse. When a user attempts to go to a site that is not on either white or black list, the user receives a prompt message asking if he/she intends to visit this possibly non-workrelated site (see Figure 4). Upon stating that the intended use of the target site is indeed work-related, the employee will be allowed to visit the requested web page and be able to request additional pages from that site for a period of 5 minutes before being prompted again about the work-related nature of the site. This confirmation module’s effect on a user’s behavior is attributable to both agency and procedural justice theories. In essence, when the system renders employees an opportunity to confirm the content classification of the target website as correct and work-related, a sense of fairness and openness is provided by the system. This confirmation mechanism empowers employees, prevents a feeling of conflict with the organization’s policies, and hence reduces an employee’s intent to cyberloaf [26]. A sense of fairness is engendered due to the consistency of the organization’s policies with the expectation of working when one is paid to provide work [13] [37]. We thus present our next hypothesis.

H2: The usage of a confirmation module is positively associated with the appropriate use of Internet resources.

## 4. METHODOLOGY AND DATA

We collected the actual usage data from a mid-sized diversified company over a 6-month period. The organization that was used for our study consists of three main business units located in one western state of the United States:

• Three manufacturing plants that grow, process and package fruits and vegetables

• 14 retail agricultural chemical distributors; and • One corporate office that includes an insurance agency, a private equity bank, and a transaction-processing department for accounts receivable and accounts payables.

The data set contains web filtering information for 275 employees over a period of 180 days from April 2011 to October 2011. The data includes over 34 million records of requests for web content.

The organization has a complex set of rules for filtering Internet usage. To enforce the Internet usage policy, four separate components are enacted via a Websense Internet Filtering Suite. This Internet-filtering product was purchased from Websense, Inc. (NASDAQ: WBSN), which is currently one of the market leaders in the web filtering industry [23]. Websense provides a complex hybrid filtering/monitoring system with the aim to make an organization’s employees more productive. The software package utilizes a web service and a proprietary database to dynamically categorize websites, and these categories are then assigned to the filtering modules used in this study.

In the organization, the white list includes all sites on the Internet that are deemed work-related as well as sites used for internal enterprise systems access. Because this organization utilizes a service delivery method for their business applications via a webbased system, the count of each user’s interactions with the websites on the white list is considered appropriate use for the purpose of this study and serves as a proxy for the amount of network resources employees access to complete their jobs. This whitelist is manually generated by the IT department of the organization and updated only when changes in IT infrastructure or job functions take place. None of these events occurred

during the period of our data collection. This means that the whitelist was fixed and remained unchanged throughout our study period.

## 5. ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

## 5.1. Data Description and Analysis

Due to the organization’s use of Websense’s Internet filtering product, the data collection process was straightforward as Websense stores all the data in a Microsoft SQL database. The original data set contained over 34 million user interaction records. The dataset was summarized by user for each of the 5 independent variables and 1 dependent variable. Over the course of the 180-day period from April through October in 2011, 275 different user accounts were active and recorded by the organization.

The independent variables for the regression model of user interactions with the filtering system include Blocked by Category (Block), Blocked with Work-related Confirm (Confirmation Block), Allowed with Work-related Confirm (Confirmation Allow), Allowed via Quota (Quota Allow), and Blocked via Quota (Quota Block). The dependent variable is the number of recorded appropriate use of Internet resources based on the white list (Appropriate Use). For this organization, the Appropriate Use variable covers all Intranet sites. This organization utilizes web delivery for the majority of their enterprise applications and therefore the Appropriate Use variable contains visits to work-related web sites and use of enterprise applications, making this variable a good proxy for work-related activities.

#

During the preliminary analysis of data, a significant variation in the number of appropriate usage of the websites that employees visited was found. After further analysis, it was determined that some user accounts existed outside of what would be classified as a “normal user.” After sorting the raw data by the number of accesses to the sites visited on the white list, two major gaps in the data were uncovered. On the low end, a gap between 135 and 201 interactions was observed. After looking into the details of the Internet traffic, all users below 200 interactions were believed to have been nonhuman user accounts that were used to update key enterprise systems and should therefore be removed from the sample.

In addition to the anomaly on the low end of the continuum, a similar irregularity occurred on the high end of the continuum. A gap existed between 287,444 and 314,534 interactions. ## 6. DISCUSSIONS

The current study builds on previous research of cyberloafing [27] [28] and utilizes data collected on employees attitudes towards cyberloafing to provide insights for both researchers and practitioners. We analyze archival real world data to help us examine the effectiveness of different Internet filtering modules of a leading commercial software system. Our framework is related to Lim and Teo’s study [28] of how employees view cyberloafing activities, and our analysis of successful countermeasures using monitoring data is consistent with their findings. Researchers can leverage this insight to develop and test innovative measures to counteract cyberloafing while practitioners can utilize the results to streamline their current Internet filtering implementations and identify the most effective countermeasures to address cyberloafing.

From the results, we find that the confirmation module is most effective in filtering and monitoring Internet usage. Possible topics based on this finding for future research include:

(1) Why is the confirmation module the most effectual? Our results suggest a through procedural justice has an impact on the effectiveness of the confirmation module. In order to address this question, a survey-based study on user perceptions and intentions would be required.

(2) Can user training, the Internet usage policies, and other factors be a substitute cyberloafing, other factors may influence employee's reaction to the Internet filtering and monitoring system. Examples include an organization’s Internet usage policy, user training, culture, and managerial orientation, among others. These factors, however, cannot be verified by our dataset. In order to answer this research question, a laboratory or field experiment would be required to collect both user responses and surveys to assess the efficacy of user training, Internet usage policy, and other factors in a controlled environment.

(3) Do users abuse the confirmation module? While the confirmation module relies on a pre-specified list of categories as listed in Figure 8 to inquire users if the website to be visited is work related, there is no procedure to automatically verify and evaluate their responses. Hence there exists the possibility that users may abuse the confirmation module. However, we noticed that in the past employees had been reprimanded by the organization for inappropriate web usage of the confirmation module. As such, we believe the abuse of the confirmation allow was not common during our study period. Still, it will be interesting to study if users abuse the confirmation module without reprimands and if so to what extent.

system, another interesting follow-up question is to study whether employees are simply being limited by the interactions with the Internet filter or if the Internet filter actually alters their behaviors permanently. One way to test this conjecture in future research is to determine if the conditioned behavior returns to the previous norm, called extinction, once employees are no longer blocked by the system. Extinction is the extent of the decrease in the reinforced behavior once the frequency of the reinforcer is reduced or removed altogether [11]. Understanding and operationalizing this construct will help pinpoint the root cause of the effect of cyberloafing reduction. In this study, we have taken the first step to show that an effect exists, but we are not certain whether the nature of relationships between the different stimuli of the system is permanent or temporary. More research on this topic is thus warranted. Another potential research topic is to look at whether filtering system warnings need to appear 100% of the time or if intermittent (random) warnings are just as effective.

The process of asking the user if the site visited is work-related and forcing them to utilize their quota time prevents extinction by promoting the notion that their actions are being constantly monitored by a sophisticated Internet filtering system. To study extinction in this setting, measuring how long an employee’s behavior remains changed since the last interaction with the system until their behavior returns to the previous

baseline level would be interesting. Prior research has shown that the realization of a system monitoring employee’s actions is powerful enough to alter their behaviors for a short duration [44].

By studying the rate of reoccurrence of inappropriate behaviors based on the different filter modules of the system, the learning process of using the system can be analyzed. One unanswered question is whether employees have actually learned that their behavior was unacceptable. A topic for future research thus is to study whether employees adjusted their behavior permanently or they just changed it temporarily to accommodate the filtering system. If the change in behavior is only temporary, then once the system stops blocking them, their unacceptable behavior would likely return.

Finally, the web filtering data can be aggregated into a user profile. The amount of time that passes between each triggering of the filtering modules can be analyzed. This analysis would provide evidence for the strength of the conditioning that takes place when the user is prompted to use quota time or mark a site as work-related. As user’s familiarity with the system increases, the amount of time between occurrences (i.e., appropriate use) is expected to increase. If initial interactions with warning pages are strong enough to affect the browsing habits of employees for an extended period, then having an interactive web filtering system would be unnecessary and a passive system with scheduled warnings alone would be sufficient. If there is a pattern of conditioning that is exposed by an increase in time between occurrences of cyberloafing, then it suggests that the system is more effective at training employees than reinforcing behaviors.

## 7. CONCLUSION

With the proliferation of connectedness in society, the separation of personal time from work time is becoming blurred. Employees now use computing resources of their concerns about security, privacy, and productivity loss. In this paper, we explored and analyzed the effectiveness of a countermeasure to this cyberloafing problem using a technical solution of Internet filtering and monitoring. Taking a multi-theoretical perspective, we employ operant conditioning as well as one’s psychological morals of procedural justice and social norms to study the effectiveness of such countermeasure in addressing this agency problem of cyberloafing and promoting greater compliance with the organization’s Internet usage policy. In addition to blocking, our results show that the confirmation and quota modules also can prevent shirking and promote better compliance through employee empowerment and attention resource replenishment. Implications for practice and topics for future research are identified from our findings.
