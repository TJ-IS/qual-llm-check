# Examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews on consumers' decision-making: A heuristic–systematic model

Kem Z.K. Zhang <sup>a</sup>, Sesia J. Zhao <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Christy M.K. Cheung <sup>c</sup>, Matthew K.O. Lee <sup>d</sup>

<sup>c</sup> Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong, China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 3 March 2013 Received in revised form 27 June 2014 Accepted 14 August 2014 Available online 23 August 2014

Keywords: Online reviews Electronic word-of-mouth Consumer decision-making Heuristic–systematic mode

## a b s t r a c t

Along with the growth of Internet and electronic commerce, online consumer reviews have become an important source of information that assists consumers to make purchase decision. However, theoretical development and empirical testing in this area of research are still limited, which greatly hinder us from understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews. Drawing upon the heuristic–systematic model from the literature of dual-process theories, we develop a research model to identify factors that are important to consumers' purchase decisionmaking. The model is empirically tested with 191 users of an existing online review site. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that argument quality of online reviews (systematic factor), which is characterized by perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness, has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on consumers' purchase intention. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews (heuristic factors) have direct impacts on purchase intention. The two heuristic factors further demonstrate positive in<sup>fl</sup>uences on argument strength. This result is consistent with the proposition of bias effect in the heuristic–systematic model, which elucidates the interrelationship between heuristic and systematic factors. Based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we discuss implications for both researchers and practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

The advancements of Web 2.0 technologies have greatly facilitated the proliferation of online consumer reviews on the Internet. These reviews express consumers' evaluations and opinions of products and services. Online reviews have been distributed among various Internet media, including online sellers' websites, discussion forums, blogs, social networking sites, and online review sites [e.g., 16,48,76]. Online reviews are increasingly treated as an importance information source that facilitates consumers to make purchase decision. A recent industry survey report from ChannelAdvisor [13] indicated that online reviews produce powerful in<sup>fl</sup>uences. They found that 90% of online shoppers read these reviews, and 83% of them believe that online reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence their purchase decision.

Research on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews has attracted great attention from scholars. According to Lee and Youn [48], this line of studies generally falls into two categories: market level and individual level. Research at the market level investigates whether review characteristics can affect market outcomes, such as product sales, revenues, and product strategies. For instance, Duan et al. [22] found that the rating of online reviews has no impact on movie revenues, whereas volume has. Forman et al. [28] showed that identity-relevant information of reviewers can improve community members' recognition of review helpfulness and increase product sales. Li and Hitt [49] contended that the self-selection bias of early online reviews can in<sup>fl</sup>uence market outcomes, such as sales and consumer surplus. Moon et al. [56] posited that review ratings are closely associated with movie revenues, and viewers' ratings are related to their own rating histories. On the other hand, research at the individual level investigates the relationship between online reviews and consumers' decisionmaking. A recent literature analysis from Cheung and Thadani [17] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 47 articles on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews from 2000 to 2010. Compared to market-level studies, they found that individuallevel studies (25 out of 47 articles) have been attracting growing attention. In this direction, scholars emphasize several key dependent variables, including the adoption of online reviews [e.g., 18,82], product attitude [e.g., 47,48], and purchase intention [e.g., 46,59,61]. Among these studies, dual-process theories (e.g., elaboration likelihood model and heuristic–systematic model) appear to be valid perspectives in explaining the impacts of online reviews at the individual level [17].

In this research, we attempt to extend our knowledge of individuallevel studies on online reviews. We aim at delving into the heuristic– systematic model to further enrich our understanding on how online reviews may affect consumers' behavioral intention. To achieve this objective, we will review prior literature on dual-process theories and how the theories are applied in the areas of online reviews and other broader Information Systems (IS) problems. We then refer to the heuristic–systematic model to provide new insights regarding: 1) what are the key systematic and heuristic factors for understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews and 2) whether bias effect exits between the two types of factors. The bias effect explains how heuristic and systematic factors may interact with each other [26]. It is an important theoretical extension in the heuristic–systematic model [10,15]. Examining the bias effect has potential to provide noteworthy implications for examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews on consumers' decisionmaking.

We will conduct this study in the context of online consumer review sites. According to Cheung and Thadani [17], previous individual-level studies on online reviews generally focus on two types of online contexts: online consumer review sites (e.g., Epinions.com and Tripadvisor.com) and online shopping sites (Amazon.com and eBay. com). Scholars also show that the impacts of online reviews may differ for different online contexts. For instance, Park and Lee [58] found that online reviews from well-established and reputable websites demonstrate stronger effect than from under-established websites. Lee and Youn [48] discriminated online reviews from marketer-generated and non-marketer-generated websites to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reviews on consumers' product judgment. Bickart and Schindler [6] contended that consumers will show greater interest in product topics if they receive online reviews from online consumer communities than from marketer-generated sources. Given these concerns, this study sheds light on non-marketer-generated online consumer review sites to better highlight the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of systematic and heuristic factors on consumers' behavioral intention. Such context enables us to avoid confounding effects from transaction activities and seller-created information that come from marketer-generated websites.

We expect that this study can provide several key contributions to previous studies. First, we identify the inconsistencies of conceptualization and operationalization in the literature of argument quality/ strength. We therefore re-conceptualize argument quality, the systematic factor here, as a second-order factor. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the two contentrelated dimensions of online reviews, namely perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness, are explicitly de<sup>fi</sup>ned to describe argument quality in this study. Second, we add to the existing literature by postulating why both source credibility and perceived quantity of online reviews are key heuristic factors that can affect consumers' purchase intention. Third, this study is one of the very <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to hypothesize and empirically examine the bias effect in the heuristic– systematic model. It shows that the two heuristic factors can demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on the systematic factor. The remainder of this study is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst present the theoretical background, followed by developing the research model to explain the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews. Then, we empirically test the model with 191 users of a popular Chinese online review site (i.e., Dianping. com). Finally, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, limitations, opportunities for future research, and summarize this research with implications to both researchers and practitioners.

## 2. Theoretical background

Dual-process theories provide comprehensive discussions on how individuals process information, establish its validity assessments, and later form decision outcomes [26]. As Moskowitz et al. [57] highlighted, the “duality” invoked by these theories indicates that 1) people may expend much effort in building beliefs and decisions, or 2) they may utilize little cognitive effort and rely on heuristics while looking at the social world. In the literature of dual-process theories, two of the most prevalent dual-process models are the elaboration likelihood model [64] and the heuristic–systematic model [9]. The two models provide similar mechanisms in explaining individuals' information processing strategies. For instance, the central route in the elaboration likelihood model and the systematic processing in the heuristic–systematic model indicate that individuals use high cognitive effort to elaborate information. By contrast, the peripheral route in the elaboration likelihood model and the heuristic processing in the heuristic–systematic model suggest that individuals adopt heuristic and simple decision rules to quickly form judgments.

## 2.1. Dual-process models in the IS literature

In the IS literature, the dual-process models have been applied to understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews and other broader IS problems. For online reviews, a number of scholars have adopted the elaboration likelihood model to investigate the phenomenon [17]. In Park et al.'s [61] study, they found that the quality (central route) and quantity (peripheral route) of online reviews positively affect consumers' purchase intention. They further showed that involvement moderates the effect from the quality of reviews, but not for the effect from the quantity of reviews. Cheung et al. [16] used four dimensions of information quality, namely relevance, timeliness, accuracy, and comprehensiveness, as central route factors and employed source expertise and source trustworthiness as peripheral factors. They found that only relevance and comprehensiveness demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on the adoption behavior of online reviews. Similarly, Park and Kim [59] applied the elaboration likelihood model to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of both the type of reviews and the number of reviews on purchase intention. Lee et al. [47] employed this model to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of both the quality and the proportion of negative reviews on purchase intention.

Meanwhile, only a few scholars mention or employ the heuristic– systematic model for understanding the impacts of online reviews. Gupta and Harris [35] adopted the two dual-process models interchangeably to explain how online reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' product consideration and choice. Zhang and Watts [82] preferred to use the heuristic–systematic model rather than apply the elaboration likelihood model based on two reasons: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the heuristic–systematic model is theorized to explain broader information processing activities [11]; second, this model holds more theoretical extensions. For instance, the model does not treat dual processes simply traded off (which is indicated by the elaboration likelihood model), but suggest that they can occur concurrently and affect each other in complex ways [26]. Considering all these points, Zhang and Watts found that argument quality (systematic processing) and source credibility (heuristic processing) can affect the adoption of online reviews in online communities.

The two dual-process models are applied to further examine other IS problems, including the adoption of email messages [73], adoption of information via videoconferencing [27], acceptance of information technology [4,5], acceptance of personalized offer on websites [74], web advertising effectiveness [79], attitude or perception toward websites [42,67], user perception of new information technology [45], likelihood to respond to a phishing email [78], trust building in electronic commerce context [34,43], agent-based online persuasion and bargaining strategies to consumers [40], collaborations among homeland security professionals [53], and data quality assessment performance [80]. Among these studies, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a few scholars employ the two dual-process models interchangeably and focus on the models' overlaps rather than differences [e.g., 53,67,80]. Angst and Agarwal [4] addressed the two models' differences and adopted the elaboration likelihood model given that it has received relatively more empirical supports. By contrast, Ferran and Watts [27] used the heuristic– systematic model and highlighted its capability in explaining a wider range of validity-seeking contexts.

In summary, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that dual-process models have received growing interest from IS scholars. Previous studies generally apply the models to 1) examine what factors (e.g., argument quality and source credibility) can be developed from the central route/ systematic processing or peripheral route/heuristic processing [e.g., 16], 2) investigate how these factors are moderated by other variables, such as expertise and involvement [e.g., 5,73,82] and 3) directly emphasize the extent of information elaboration and explore its determinants [e.g., 74,78].

## 2.2. Heuristic–systematic model

Compared to the elaboration likelihood model, prior IS research on the heuristic–systematic model is still limited. In this study, we attempt to employ the heuristic–systematic model to understand the impacts of online reviews. We refer to the social psychology literature to explicate the component and theoretical extensions of the model.

The heuristic–systematic model embraces three broad motives to explain why people perform information processing: accuracy, defense, and impression motivation [11]. This study focuses on accuracy motivation, which explains the persuasion context of validity-seeking (e.g., people can be persuaded through seeking for accurate online reviews). According to Todorov et al. [75], systematic information processing in the heuristic–systematic model indicates that “people consider all relevant pieces of information, elaborate on these pieces of information, and form a judgment based on these elaborations” (p. 196). The systematic mode shows that information recipients pay signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive effort in evaluating the arguments and assessing their validity for making judgment [9]. In the model, the sufficiency principle posits that people tend to perform systematic information processing when they have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient motivation, ability, and cognitive resources [15]. It implies that a highly motivated individual is more likely to evaluate the arguments of a message. Thereafter, high quality arguments can demonstrate greater impact on his/her decisionmaking [e.g., 73,82].

Heuristic information processing refers that “people consider a few informational cues – or even a single informational cue – and form a judgment based on these cues” [74, p. 196.] The heuristic view of persuasion speci<sup>fi</sup>es that the information recipients spend little effort and rely on accessible informational cues (e.g., characteristics of information sources) to gain conclusions [9]. The least effort principle of the model indicates that heuristic information processing is the default strategy because people are economy-minded processors, who prefer less cognitive effort and will spend much effort only when they have to [8]. People may automatically apply heuristic cues, which can be simple decision rules or rules of thumb, to reach quick judgment [10]. According to Chen and Chaiken [15], heuristic rules are associated with three elements: the availability, accessibility, and applicability of knowledge. For example, the heuristic cue “experts' statements can be trusted” will work only when people have certain knowledge structure or learned experiences about the cue (availability). People will perceive an expert's advice as helpful when they can remember or activate this heuristic cue at the time they need to make decisions (accessibility). Finally, people will not adopt this cue when they <sup>fi</sup>nd that it is not relevant or the expert has never provided correct advice on a particular judgmental task before (applicability).

Although the suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency principle suggests that the systematic mode of processing will be activated when motivation or ability to elaborate information is high, it does not rule out the possibility that heuristic information processing can continue to be adopted [8]. Indeed, the heuristic–systematic model posits that the two modes of information processing can occur concurrently [26]. The co-occurrence of processing modes can be described through three theoretical extensions, namely the additivity, attenuation, and bias effect [15]. The additivity effect asserts that systematic processing of information content and heuristic processing of non-content cues can produce independent impacts on judgmental decision [11]. Research supports have shown that both content-related thinking and cue-related characteristics can demonstrate impacts on one's judgment making [e.g., 7,12]. On the other hand, the additivity effect may often be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to discover for two reasons [8]. First, systematic information processing may provide much more issue-relevant information than heuristic processing, making the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the latter undetected. Second, rather than the additivity effect, the attenuation effect may <sup>fi</sup>rstly take place. The attenuation effect indicates that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the systematic mode of persuasion can attenuate the heuristic mode. That is, an elevated level of systematic processing may weaken the effects of heuristic processing on one's judgmental decision [11]. The attenuation effect will become stronger when people are highly motivated to process information. In this case, people may prefer to perform systematic processing than heuristic processing. Motivational factors may negatively moderate the effects of heuristic processing [82]. Another possible cause for the attenuation effect is that when the judgmental implications from two types of information processing are incongruent, people may rely on systematic processing to increase judgmental con<sup>fi</sup>dence [15]. Hence, the effects of heuristic processing may be reduced or even nulli<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Finally, the bias effect highlights the interdependence of the two mode of persuasion [10]. According to Chaiken and Maheswaran [12], the bias effect states that heuristic processing may alter individuals' judgment indirectly through biasing systematic processing. The rationale behind this effect is that heuristic cues may enable individuals to form expectations or inferences regarding the validity of arguments [52]. For example, advice received from a famous professor, rather than from an anonymous student, may be elaborated on more positively and perceived as more valid. The bias effect from heuristic processing to systematic processing describes a cognitive source of bias [12]. It will be more likely to occur if arguments are relatively ambiguous and amenable to varying interpretations [8,15].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

In this study, we develop the research model and hypotheses based on the heuristic–systematic model instead of the elaboration likelihood model for two primary reasons. First, the heuristic–systematic model points out that heuristic cues are associated with the availability, accessibility, and applicability of individuals' knowledge. It will be helpful to examine the three elements of heuristic cues in the present research context, because many non-content related cues of online reviews (e.g., number of reviews) exist on online review sites, and we need to discern whether a selected heuristic factor is valid or not in this situation. Second, and more importantly, the heuristic–systematic model highlights the co-occurrence of systematic and heuristic information processing. This is an important theoretical extension that differs from the elaboration likelihood model. In particular, we intend to empirically examine the bias effect of the theoretical extension. The bias effect is likely to occur in this research context because content-related and non-content related cues of online reviews often co-exist on online review sites, and consumers may refer to these cues simultaneously to make informed purchase decision. Examining the effect will enable us to derive more insights regarding how online reviews affect consumers' decision-making in a complex manner.

Fig. 1 depicts the research model of this study. We denote argument quality as the systematic factor that captures content-related perceptions derived from systematic processing of online reviews. Argument quality is conceptualized as a second-order factor with two dimensions: perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness. Source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews are heuristic factors that represent two types of non-content related perceptions developed from heuristic processing of online reviews. Argument quality, source credibility, and perceived quantity of reviews together predict behavioral intention. Moreover, source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews produce bias effects toward argument quality.

## 3.1. Behavioral intention and valence of online reviews

Consistent with prior research [e.g., 46,61], this study adopts behavioral intention to demonstrate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews at the individual level. Thus, one of our research objectives can be interpreted as examining what systematic and heuristic factors can determine behavioral intention. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne behavioral intention here as consumers' willingness to purchase products or services after they process issue-relevant online reviews. In the early studies of dual process models [e.g., 11,26], information processing is claimed to exert impacts on attitude formation/change or attitude-related judgments. Given that prior research has widely demonstrated the close connection between attitude and intention [e.g., 2,3], this study will emphasize the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of dual-process factors and examine the extent to which they can directly affect behavioral intention.

In this study, we will largely restrict the analysis on positive online reviews to achieve a simple design. This approach is similar to prior research [e.g., 4,60,61]. Scholars have shown that positively and negatively framed online reviews demonstrate asymmetric effects, such that negative reviews may exert stronger effects than positive reviews [58]. In addition, if negative online reviews are informative (i.e., high argument quality) and from trustworthy users (high source credibility), then consumers tend to be persuaded and refuse to purchase. By contrast, when positive reviews are informative (i.e., high argument quality) and from trustworthy users (high source credibility), consumers are more likely to purchase. Hence, to reduce the possible bias and perplexing effects from negative reviews, this study examines argument quality and source credibility of positive online reviews to highlight their positive power on behavioral intention.

## 3.2. Systematic factor: argument quality

Although argument quality is commonly used to manifest systematic processing in prior literature [e.g., 8,27,82], its conceptualization and operationalization remain inconsistent among scholars [4,72]. According to Eagly and Chaiken, argument quality refers to “the strength or plausibility of persuasive argumentation” [26, p. 325]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition is similar to Petty and Cacioppo's [64], which indicated that it pertains to perceptions of strong and convincing arguments rather than weak and unreal ones. Following this line of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, Bhattacherjee and Sanford [5] operationalized argument quality by emphasizing the persuasive strength of arguments, whereas Cheung et al. [18] employed argument strength to highlight whether received information can persuade a person to believe something or to perform a behavior. In addition, Lee and Xia [45] claimed that the quality of persuasive argument is indicated by two dimensions: argument valence and argument strength. Angst and Agarwal [4] referred to argument framing to show whether messages can elicit perceptions of behavioral outcomes and inferring causality. Kim and Benbasat [43] viewed strong content as those highlighting favorable thoughts, whereas weak content as those eliciting unfavorable thoughts.

Another line of scholars examines argument quality from a slightly different angle. In Ferran and Watts's study, they de<sup>fi</sup>ned argument quality of received information as “the factor on which information recipients base their validity assessments during systematic processing” [27]. Although Zhang and Watts [82] adopted Eagly and Chaiken's [26] de<sup>fi</sup>nition of argument quality, this lines of scholars commonly operationalizes it to capture the information quality of messages. To be more speci<sup>fi</sup>c, these studies measure argument quality through examining whether received information is complete, consistent, accurate, or adequate [e.g., 27,73,82]. Further, Cheung et al. [16] explicitly used information relevance, timeliness, accuracy, and comprehensiveness to account for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information quality on consumers information adoption behavior.

The inconsistencies of argument quality in existing studies may be attributed to a few reasons. First, early research on dual-process models widely adopted experimental approach to manipulate argument quality, without explicitly providing guidelines for its operationalization and indicating what constitutes a persuasive argument [43,64]. Second, some studies could not discriminate the valence and persuasive strength in argument quality [55,72]. Third, experienced users may be able to indicate the extent of information completeness, consistency, or accuracy, whereas inexperienced users may prefer estimating the strength of arguments [5].

To reconcile the inconsistent understandings of argument quality, this study <sup>fi</sup>rstly focuses on argument quality of positive online reviews. Then, we argue that argument quality may be a multidimensional construct, rather than unidimensional. We propose two dimensions of argument quality in this study: perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness. We refer to perceived informativeness as consumers' overall perceptions regarding the information quality related characteristics of online reviews [24], while perceived persuasiveness represents the general perceptions regarding the strength of persuasiveness embedded in online reviews [83]. We develop this point of view based on three primary concerns. First, the multidimensionality of argument quality consolidates the distinct conceptualizations in the two lines of studies discussed earlier. From this perspective, previous studies tend to shed light on only either one of the two dimensions. Second, the two dimensions of argument quality may be partially supported by studies in the marketing literature. For instance, prior research on advertising indicates the existence of information–persuasion dichotomy in advertisements [41,69]. Rosen and Olshavsky [68] proposed the dual role of informational social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. That is, consumers seek both attributevalue information and recommendations from information sources in their purchase decision-making process. From this perspective, online reviews may exert informational social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the form that both product attribute information and others' purchase recommendations in online reviews affect consumers' purchase behavior. Third, online reviews are actually consumer-oriented information that evaluates products or services. They are less likely to be narrated in a “standard” form, but rather in a form with certain level of objective product descriptions (i.e., informativeness), as well as in persuasive language with subjective or emotional expressions (i.e., persuasiveness). Consistent with this view, Park et al. [61] contended that online reviews may function as both informant and recommender roles for consumers. In summary, we expect that high argument quality of online reviews should be both informative and persuasive.

In this research, we propose that argument quality of online reviews may be an important predictor of consumers' behavioral intention. Prior research on advertising has shown that informative advertisements can lead to purchase behavior [24,54]. Park et al. [61] posited that high information quality of online reviews will affect consumers' purchasing intention. Similarly, persuasive arguments have also been found to contribute to favorable decision outcomes [4,26]. Bhattacherjee and Sanford [5] showed that if external information is persuasive, then individuals are more likely to adopt new technologies. In the context of online review sites, we therefore expect that if a consumer <sup>fi</sup>nds reviews about a particular product to be of high argument quality (i.e., high perceived informativeness and persuasiveness), then the consumer is likely to purchase that product. The following hypothesis is provided:

H1. Argument quality positively affects behavioral intention.

## 3.3. Heuristic factors: source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews

While asserting the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of review content on online review sites, we also postulate that heuristic cues can play an important role in affecting consumers' behavioral intention. This is consistent with the additivity effect of the heuristic–systematic model. In this study, we utilize source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews to manifest heuristic processing of online reviews. Source credibility has been widely studied as an important heuristic cue in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine context [e.g., 7,11,12]. Recent research on online reviews also shows that the quantity of reviews is an effective decision rule that helps consumers form quick decision [e.g., 60,61,71]. However, little research has been undertaken to examine both two factors and explicate their in<sup>fl</sup>uences through dual-process models. In this study, we therefore consider the two cues together and interpret their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance on online review sites through the availability, accessibility, and applicability dimensions of heuristic cues.

According to Chaiken [9], this research de<sup>fi</sup>nes source credibility as consumers' overall perceptions regarding the credibility of review sources, rather than the content of online reviews. It captures the perceived expertise and trustworthiness of review sources [64,66]. In prior literature, scholars have shown that “experts' statements can be trusted” and “experts know best” are commonly accepted heuristic cues by people [10,11,15]. It implies that the decision rule of source credibility can be assumed to be available in consumers' knowledge structure. In addition, online review sites generally allow registered users to create their personal pages to describe themselves. A comprehensive ranking system is often established to assign these users with different numbers of “starts” or “reputation level” based on their review quality and contribution frequency. By referring to such personal pages and ranking information, consumers become more accessible to infer the extent to which the sources of online reviews are credible. Finally, consumers often encounter situations like: being inconvenient to physically touch or feel some products, or feeling insuf<sup>fi</sup>- cient to make a good decision by simply relying on product information provided by sellers. Consumers can then seek online reviews to reduce uncertainty. Reviews from credible others are expected to provide reliable information that helps consumers learn more about products and improve their decision-making. Existing research further shows that source credibility exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on individuals' online information adoption behavior [82]. Consumers who receive recommendations from credible others are more likely to select corresponding products [70]. From this perspective, source credibility is a relevant and applicable heuristic cue for consumers. We posit that consumers adopt source credibility as an important cue to form their behavioral decision. Consumers will be more likely to purchase a product if s/he <sup>fi</sup>nds its online reviews are from credibility others. The following hypothesis is provided:

H2. Source credibility of online reviews positively affects behavioral intention.

This research denotes perceived quantity of reviews as another important heuristic cue. The large volume of online reviews is one of the distinct characteristics that differ from word-of-mouth information in of<sup>fl</sup>ine environments [14,21]. According to prior research [e.g., 61], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne perceived quantity of reviews as consumers' perceptions regarding the volume of reviews and the popularity of corresponding products within online review sites.

Previous studies have indicated that heuristic cues like “length or number of arguments equals argument strength” and “consensus opinions are correct” are useful and common decision rules for people to make judgments [15,75]. Perceived quantity of reviews appears to be a helpful cue that is available in consumers' knowledge structure. Thus, consumers can infer the extent to which whether other users have consistently purchased certain products before. On online review sites, platform designers widely use clear numerical indicators to inform consumers the number of reviews for certain products. By consulting such numerical information, consumers are more accessible to activate the heuristic cue of review quantity and then recognize the popularity of products on the websites. Finally, if consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd certain products have high review quantity, then their purchase con<sup>fi</sup>dence can be improved through observing or imitating other users' purchase behavior. Therefore, we expect that perceived quantity of reviews is also an applicable heuristic cue for consumes to support their purchase decision-making.

In this research, we do not explicitly distinguish perceived quantity of reviews into positive or negative ones. Previous studies show that the total number of online reviews can demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant marketing effects [e.g., 19,22]. Park et al. [61] found that perceived popularity of products positively affect individuals' decision-making. Similarly, Duan et al. [23] suggested that widely downloaded software products are likely to be downloaded by new adopters. These empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings further con<sup>fi</sup>rm that perceived quantity of reviews is applicable as a heuristic cue for consumers' decision-making. In addition, it is simple and easy for online review sites to provide numerical indicators to report the overall number of online reviews. By contrast, it may be challenging for review sites to automatically discriminate the valence of textual reviews. There may be little information provided to illustrate the exact number of positive and negative reviews. Then, the quantity of positive or negative reviews may not meet the condition of accessibility as heuristic cues. Based on these concerns, we expect that if consumers perceive a high quantity of reviews for a given product, then s/he is more likely to purchase it. The following hypothesis is provided:

H3. Perceived quantity of reviews positively affects behavioral intention.

## 3.4. Bias effect between systematic and heuristic factors

The co-occurrence of two information processing modes is an important theoretical extension for the heuristic–systematic model [11,26]. Building upon this extension, scholars further propose the bias effect to interpret the interdependence between the two modes. That is, heuristic processing can bias systematic processing by affecting individuals' expectations or inferences about the validity of arguments [10,15]. In the social psychology literature, the bias effect has received considerable support among scholars [e.g., 7,8,12]. However, little research has shed light on examining the bias effect in the IS literature. Though they did not hypothesize the bias effect explicitly, Zhang and Watts [82] recently found some support for the effect in a post-hoc analysis using survey data. In this study, we attempt to empirically test the bias effect through examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews. We propose that source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews (heuristic factors) can demonstrate bias effects toward argument quality (systematic factor).

On online review sites, we expect that when consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd online reviews are from credible review sources rather than from non-credible sources, they can develop expectancies regarding the argument quality of these reviews. As contended by Chaiken and Maheswaran [12], such expectation of the probable validity of arguments can strengthen the <sup>fi</sup>nal perceptions and evaluations of argument quality. Similarly, when consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd restaurant has many online reviews and is popular within review sites, they will favor such consensus from others, and their systematic processing of these reviews may be biased positively. Consumers will then infer that argument quality of these reviews is more likely to be informative and persuasive.

In prior literature, scholars also posit that the bias effect will be more obvious if arguments are relatively ambiguous and could be interpreted differently [15]. Indeed, this seems to be the case for online reviews. As for product reviews, they tend to contain subjective and emotional information from many heterogeneous consumers. Hence, compared to those direct, clear, and unambiguous product information provided by sellers, many online reviews may appear to be relative “ambiguous” and amenable to diverse interpretations. From this perspective, we further expect that the bias effect would occur on online review sites. In sum, this research develops the following two hypotheses.

H4. Source credibility of online reviews positively affects argument quality.

H5. Perceived quantity of reviews positively affects argument quality.

## 4. Research method

This study involves empirical testing of concrete hypotheses. We employ online review sites as the research context to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews. We adopt the survey method to gather real <sup>fi</sup>eld information and the natural state of the context. In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, we test the research model using data collected from an existing online review site: Dianping.com.

## 4.1. Online review site

Initiated in 2003, Dianping.com is one of the largest and most popular online review sites in China. By the fourth quarter of 2013, the review site has more than 90 million active users, a monthly visit of nearly 3.5 billion times, and approximately 30 million reviews on 8 million member merchants in 2300 cities of China.<sup>1</sup> Dianping.com has similar layout and functions to other international review sites like Tripadvisor.com and Yelp.com, where many online users have contributed a large number of reviews to evaluate a wide range of products or services. On Dianping.com, online reviews are mainly about restaurants. Each restaurant has a unique page on the review site where review messages are listed below the basic information of the restaurant. Besides providing a substantial amount of review content, Dianping.com also offer numerical information to show the number of reviews for each restaurant and use the numbers of “medals” and “contribution value” to demonstrate the ranking level of different review contributors. From this perspective, Dianping.com appears to be a suitable research site that enables subjects to develop perceptions regarding the content of arguments (i.e., argument quality) and different heuristic cues (i.e., source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews).

## 4.2. Questionnaire development and pilot study

We created an online survey questionnaire for this research. To better capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews, we focused on review site users who recently read online reviews in the past 3 days and considered visiting real restaurants listed on Dianping.com. Similar to previous studies [e.g., 18,82], we asked each respondent to recall the scene he/she processed such reviews of one restaurant. The respondents were required to provide the hyperlinks of these restaurants on the review site, which allowed us to examine the validity of restaurants and review messages retrospectively.

The instruments of this research were created by adopting previously validated measures in the literature, including perceived informativeness [24,25], perceived persuasiveness [18,83], source credibility [16,73], perceived quantity of reviews [61], and behavioral intention [63]. All items used 7-point Likert scales. We slightly modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the wording of these items to <sup>fi</sup>t the present research context. Appendix A depicts the details of the measures for this research.

To administrate the survey study in China, the original English instruments were translated into Chinese through the translation (i.e., from English to Chinese) and back-translation (i.e., from Chinese to English) process with the help of two Chinese doctoral students. The students were pro<sup>fi</sup>cient in both Chinese and English and familiar with online review sites like Dianping.com. Any disagreements or inconsistencies on the translation process were solved to ensure that the <sup>fi</sup>nal Chinese instruments had suf<sup>fi</sup>cient translation quality. Further, we conducted a pilot study to collect feedback about the questionnaire. We distributed the questionnaire to a convenient sample with 30 users of Dianping.com. The resulting feedback included the layout of the questionnaire and the wording of certain questions. The <sup>fi</sup>nal questionnaire was thus improved based on the feedback.

## 4.3. Data collection

We distributed URLs of the <sup>fi</sup>nal questionnaire among potential review site users. We posted invitation messages in a discussion forum that was af<sup>fi</sup>liated with Dianping.com. The forum covers various topics related to restaurants, foods, hobbies and interests, and website activities. Many users of Dianping.com participate in the forum to discuss with others of similar interests or to <sup>fi</sup>nd upcoming promotion events. Hence, the forum appears to be an effective channel to broadcast the questionnaire to review site users. To promote responses, we provided incentives of 10 RMB (around 1.5 US dollar) to each respondent in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 150 valid completions of the questionnaire. In total, 191 usable data were collected for this study. To estimate the nonresponse bias, we compared the composition of the <sup>fi</sup>rst 50 and late

50 responses. Results showed that no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found. Table 1 illustrates the demographic characteristics of the sample. As shown below, 67.5% were females and 32.5% were males. Most of the respondents were from ages 19 to 35. 97.9% had university or above degrees. 72.8% had incomes of above 2000 RMB per month.

## 5. Data analysis

In this study, we used Partial Least Squares (PLS) to analyze the research model. PLS is a robust and frequently adopted technique in the IS literature [1,30,77]. It is a component-based structural equation modeling approach that requires a relatively small sample size with few restrictions on normal distribution [20]. Given that this research is also theoretically developmental in nature, it is suitable to use PLS for the data analysis of the study. The two-step analytical procedure was followed: measurement model and structural model [36].

Convergent validity suggests that items of the same construct should be closely related to each other, as they are theoretically related. Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) are two indicators for measuring convergent validity. It is considered acceptable when CR values are higher than 0.7, and AVE values are higher than 0.5 [29]. We initially considered the impacts of possible control variables in the research model. These control variables included past experience (i.e., the experience of reading reviews on the review site and the experience of visiting restaurants after reading reviews), perceived price of the restaurant, and demographic variables, such as gender, age, education, and income. They were controlled on behavioral intention. The results indicated that only past experience of reading reviews $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 4 , \mathrm { t } = 2 . 4 7 0 )$ had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on behavioral intention. Thus, we considered this factor as a control variable in the following structural model.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, we examined the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, associated t-value of the paths (signi<sup>fi</sup>cant paths are indicated by asterisks), and the explanatory power of constructs in the structural model. Tests of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of all paths were based on the bootstrap resampling procedure. In the model, argument quality was assessed as a second-order factor with perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness. ## 6. Discussions and conclusions

The primary focus of this research is to gain an in-depth understanding of dual-process factors that explain the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews at the individual level. In particular, we examine what systematic and heuristic factors can affect consumers' decision-making and investigate whether the bias effect exists between the factors. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the view that argument quality (the systematic factor), source credibility, and perceived quantity of reviews (the two heuristic factors) are important determinants of behavioral intention. In general, consumers examine the content of online reviews to form perceptions about review informativeness and persuasiveness. Informative reviews act as important supplemental information that assists consumers to reduce uncertainty and improve their purchase decision. Consumers will also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from persuasive reviews, which can advise them with clear causality and help them follow convincing recommendations. Meanwhile, consumers will refer to heuristic cues that are associated with online reviews to facilitate their decision-making. Two types of important numerical cues include the ranking level of review sources and the number of review messages. If consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd review sources to be credible, their purchase intention can be increased. Similarly, consumers are more likely to purchase products with many online reviews rather than with few ones.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also provide strong empirical support for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews to argument quality in online environments. This bias effect uncovers the relationship between systematic and heuristic factors. That is, consumers' perceptions related to the content of online reviews can be strengthened by their perceptions related to non-content cues of online reviews. If consumers receive reviews from credible sources and <sup>fi</sup>nd the quantity of reviews to be large, then they will expect and further perceive that arguments in online reviews are more informative and persuasive.

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

Before addressing implications of this research, we have to point out several limitations and opportunities for future research. First, the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be limited given that this study only adopts a single online review site (i.e., Dianping.com) as the research context. Future research may consider extending our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other contexts of online reviews. However, care should be taken for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different online platforms, cultural backgrounds, control variables, and product types. In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, prior research suggests that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews may be subject to different platforms [17,48,58]. Consumers in collectivist cultures may show a higher information-seeking tendency in online communities than consumers in individualist cultures [38]. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of control variables (e.g., income and price) should also be tested to control any confounding effects on consumers' decision-making. Further, the impacts of online reviews may vary according to product types, such as quality vs. preference goods [46], and search vs. experience goods [58].

Second, we did not consider the impacts of negative online reviews on consumers' decision-making. On the other hand, we restrict the analysis of argument quality and source credibility to only positive reviews to achieve a simple design and highlight our research focus. We believe that this survey-based approach is viable given that online review sites largely consist of positive reviews to some extent (most reviews tend to be framed from neutral to extremely positive). Hu et al. [39] examined such phenomenon as the J-shaped distribution of online product reviews. The distribution is an asymmetric bimodal distribution illustrating high review ratings for most of the products. It can be explained by two biases: the purchasing bias, which states that products with low ratings are not purchased, thus no additional ratings are given; and the under-reporting bias, which describes that consumers are more likely to provide extremely high or extremely low ratings to products. Nevertheless, further studies, especially experimental studies, can extend our understandings by manipulating the valence of online reviews and investigating whether the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of dual-process factors may differ between positive and negative reviews. Prior research posits that negative reviews may exert stronger effects than positive ones [58]. Thus, it will be interesting to explore whether dual-process factors of negative reviews demonstrate stronger impacts than that of positive ones.

Researchers may try to explicate the moderating effects through the attenuation effects in the heuristic–systematic model. It is important to note, however, that previous studies generally examine these moderating effects to understand how people scrutinize only one piece of information [e.g., 73,82]. For the case of many online reviews, it is possible that these effects may not hold. For instance, Park et al. [61] recently found that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of review quantity on purchase intention remain the same between high-involvement and low-involvement subjects.

Finally, 40.9% of variances in behavioral intention are explained in the research model. It suggests that some other important predictors may be missing. These factors may include subjective norms of peers, perceived ease of use of review sites, and location of restaurants. Prior research also shows that consumers' familiarity with a <sup>fi</sup>rm may affect their searching behavior for reviews and then in<sup>fl</sup>uence their patronage intention [14]. If a consumer is familiar with certain products, s/he may not rely much on review messages, making the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews diminished. Further, Li and Hitt [49] pointed out that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews may be affected by self-selection bias. The self-selection bias indicates that early reviews may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from later ones if early reviews are posted by biased consumers (e.g., avid fans). From this perspective, online reviews of long running restaurants may affect consumers' behavior differently than that of newly opened restaurants. In sum, future researchers may incorporate the impacts of other factors into the model and better understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews.

## 6.2. Implications for research

We believe that this research can contribute to the prior literature with several important theoretical implications. First, we apply the heuristic–systematic model to investigate critical determinants of consumers' behavioral intention. Although dual-process theories are increasingly used to explicate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews at the individual level [17], little research has been undertaken to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine the potential relevance of the heuristic–systematic model in understanding the predictors of consumers' purchase decision. By extending previous studies that explore what are the factors related to two routs (i.e., the central and peripheral route) or two modes (i.e., the systematic and heuristic mode) of information processing [e.g., 16], the present study <sup>fi</sup>nds strong support for the effects from argument quality (the systematic factor), source credibility, and perceived quantity of reviews (the heuristic factors) on consumers' purchase decision. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with the additivity effect of the heuristic–systematic model, suggesting that both content-related thinking and cue-related characteristics can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence one's judgment making [7,11,12].

Second, the empirical evidence about the impact of argument quality on behavioral intention con<sup>fi</sup>rms the view that the content of online reviews constantly plays an important role in consumers' decision-making [62]. To explicate what is high argument quality and resolve its inconsistent understandings in prior literature, this research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst one that re-conceptualizes argument quality with two important dimensions: perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness. The two content-related dimensions of online reviews are consistent with Rosen and Olshavsky's [68] postulation about two types of informational social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (i.e., in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attributevalue information and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of recommendations) and Park et al.'s [61] proposition about two functional roles of online reviews (i.e., informant role and recommender role). Our re-conceptualization can also be linked to the claim-data-backing of trust-assuring arguments, which state that data provide informative grounds for a claim, and backing presents the reasons of why one should accept the data [43,44]. Thus, high argument quality can be interpreted by both high informativeness from suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data and high persuasiveness from strong backing and clear claim.

Third, the impacts of heuristic processing on consumers' behavioral intention are supported from the survey data. Perceived quantity of reviews and source credibility positively affect behavioral intention. Previous studies have shown that high review quantity can lead to market-level outcomes [e.g., 22,32,51]. In this study, we extend the <sup>fi</sup>nding of review quantity to individual-level outcomes [e.g., 61]. We conceptualize perceived quantity of reviews as another important heuristic cue on online review sites. Further, we elucidate the effects from both perceived quantity of reviews and source credibility through the availability, accessibility, and applicability of heuristic cues [15]. The three key elements of heuristic processing have shown to serve as a helpful vehicle that is conducive to examining whether a heuristic cue may work in a decision-making context. Researchers may apply these elements to explore more important heuristic cues for understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews on consumers' decision-making process.

Finally, the bias effect of the heuristic–systematic model is successfully tested in the context of this study. Source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews place positive impacts on argument quality. It provides empirical supports of the co-occurrence of systematic and heuristic information processing [12,52]. We expect that the evidence presented in this study will have several noteworthy implications for the information processing literature and research on online reviews: (1) the bias effect highlights the indirect effect of heuristic cues, which is largely uninvestigated in prior studies [82]. It uncovers the more complicated process of systematic and heuristic processing of online review. (2) It shows that heuristic cues can act as important predictors of argument quality. 66.6% of the variance in argument quality was explained in this study. Source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews demonstrate as two new and valid cognitive sources of bias that can make consumers more receptive to the content of online reviews. (3) It further shows that argument quality (systematic factor) had the strongest direct effect on behavioral intention, whereas source credibility (heuristic factor) had the strongest total effect. This <sup>fi</sup>nding extends prior studies [e.g., 73,82], which primarily contend that source credibility is less important and has a weaker impact than argument quality. It will be important for researchers to reconsider the overall in<sup>fl</sup>uence of heuristic factors by examining both direct and indirect effects.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

We believe that this research can also contribute to practitioners with some implications. First, designers of online review sites may leverage the impacts of review messages through the lens of our research model. To improve the impact of systematic processing, designers should be careful to the content of reviews contributed by users, especially monitoring the informativeness and persuasiveness dimensions of online reviews. Less informative or persuasive online reviews will be likely to hinder consumers' purchase behavior. Designers may establish certain kind of prosecution system, where users can voluntarily report any online reviews that are extremely not informative or persuasive, or with inappropriate content and advertisements. Second, based on the direct and indirect effects of heuristic cues, designers are suggested to strengthen the accessibility of source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews on online review sites. For instance, they could provide effective indications and technical tools to assist consumers in recognizing the credibility of review contributors. To increase the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived quantity of reviews, designers may clearly display and constantly update popular products on their online review website. In this regard, consumers are more likely to notice the products with many online reviews. Finally, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research can also contribute understandings to managers of <sup>fi</sup>rms. As Godes et al. [33] indicated, <sup>fi</sup>rms may get involved with the phenomenon of online reviews through four stages: observer, moderator, mediator, and participant. Although discussions of more aggressive stages (e.g., moderator, mediator, and participant) may be beyond the scope of this research, we believe that managers could still improve their understandings in the observer stage. We suggest that managers of <sup>fi</sup>rms could understand or even predict the impacts of online review through the lens of our research model.

## 6.4. Conclusions

Online reviews are now widely adopted to assist consumers in making informed decision. It has become critical for marketers to understand the power of these reviews and in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' behavior. Our research suggests that dual-process factors are essential to drive consumers' purchase decision. By re-conceptualizing argument quality, reasoning the impacts of source credibility and perceived quantity of reviews, and examining the bias effect, we provide a more detailed examination on the effects of systematic and heuristic factors. As such, this research can contribute to a better understanding of how online reviews exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence in both theory and practice.
