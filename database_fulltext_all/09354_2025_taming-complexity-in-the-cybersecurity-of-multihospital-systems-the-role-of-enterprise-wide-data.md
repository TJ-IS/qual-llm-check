# TAMING COMPLEXITY IN THE CYBERSECURITY OF MULTIHOSPITAL SYSTEMS: THE ROLE OF ENTERPRISE-WIDE DATA ANALYTICS PLATFORMS<sup>1</sup>

Hüseyin Tanriverdi McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin Austin, TX, U.S.A. {huseyin.tanriverdi@mccombs.utexas.edu}

Juhee Kwon Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {juhee.kwon@cityu.edu.hk}

Ghiyoung Im

College of Business, University of Louisville, Louisville, KY, U.S.A. {ghiyoung.im@louisville.edu}

A widely accepted notion in cybersecurity practice and research posits that complexity is detrimental to cybersecurity. While this notion mainly focuses on IT, the prevalence of complexity extends beyond IT into organizational domains. Whether the assertion that complexity is detrimental to cybersecurity holds true when applied to organizational domains remains largely unexplored. Moreover, “complexity” and “complicatedness” are often used interchangeably to refer to a large number of interconnected components. We build on complexity science to distinguish the two terms: complexity arises from ad hoc, nonlinear interactions among interconnected components, whereas complicatedness arises from structured, linear interactions. The field lacks sufficient understanding of these distinctions and their implications for cybersecurity risks and mitigations. We theorize how an organization’s complicatedness and complexity in both IT and organizational domains influence cybersecurity breaches. Our context of empirical inquiry is multihospital systems (MHSs), a complex organizational form that uses IT in complex ways. Drawing on complexity science, we posit that complicatedness in medical services, health IT, and the governance arrangements of an MHS increases cybersecurity breaches. We also posit that using enterprise-wide data analytics platforms (EWDAPs) to structure and control ad hoc information sharing practices of the complicated units can reduce breaches despite adding more technology components and dependencies to enterprise IT. We tested these ideas in a sample of 445 MHSs in the U.S. spanning from 2009 to 2017. Complicatedness in medical services, health IT, and governance stands out as the primary contributor to cybersecurity breaches in MHSs. Ad hoc, nonlinear interactions contribute to complexity and exacerbate the cybersecurity breach effects of complicatedness. In contrast, structured interactions, exemplified by patient data sharing through an EWDAP, achieve simplification and mitigate the cybersecurity breaches associated with complicatedness

Keywords: Cybersecurity, multihospital systems, complicated services, complicated IT, complicated governance mode, enterprise-wide data analytics platforms, complexity

## Introduction

The surge of cybersecurity attacks on healthcare organizations poses significant challenges for patients, healthcare entities, and society at large. Alarmingly, 77% of breached healthcare organizations have reported detrimental effects on patient care (Proofpoint, 2023). The breaches also have far-reaching consequences for the breached entities: e.g., financial loss, reputation impairment, operational disruptions, regulatory scrutiny and imposition of fines, and class action lawsuits. At a societal level, breaches of confidential health records erode trust in the healthcare system and contribute to a surge of medical identity theft. Breaches of availability render critical health services inaccessible to those in need.

This societal impact of healthcare cybersecurity breaches has been exacerbated by the rise of multihospital systems (MHSs). Given that MHSs own 67% of hospitals and 76% of hospital beds in the U.S. (American Hospital Association, 2023), they are valuable targets for cyberattacks. A breach in one hospital of an MHS can propagate to the interconnected hospitals and amplify the magnitude of the damages. MHSs are inherently complicated, both organizationally and technologically, as they are made up of multiple interconnected hospitals that offer a variety of medical services using diverse health IT systems and governance arrangements. They can also develop complexity if member hospitals exchange patient data and collaborate on patient care through ad hoc interactions. The objective of this study is to understand how the complicatedness and complexity of an MHS affect cybersecurity breaches and mitigations. With this understanding, MHSs will be better able to reduce the likelihood and impact of cybersecurity breaches.

Practitioners view complexity as a key antecedent of cybersecurity breaches. Bruce Schneier asserts: “Complexity is the worst enemy of security, and our systems are getting more complex all the time” (Schneier, 2015). Schneier’s explanations focus primarily on the complexity of technological components such as lines of code, hardware, and networks (Schneier, 1999, 2000). Complexity is also prevalent in enterprise-wide IT systems and organizational domains such as medical services and governance arrangements across member hospitals of an MHS (Kuhn & Le Plastrier, 2022). Whether the assertion that complexity is detrimental to cybersecurity also applies when generalized to enterprise-wide IT and organizational domains remains largely unexplored.

Practitioners often use “complicated” and “complex” interchangeably to refer to systems that are made up of a large number of interconnected components. According to complexity science, a large number of interconnected components are necessary but not sufficient for distinguishing whether a system is complicated or complex. The nature of interactions among the interconnected components should also be examined (Dekker et al., 2013). In a complicated system, interactions among the interconnected components are linear and well-structured. Such interaction characteristics allow for the analysis, testing, and understanding of cybersecurity risks, vulnerabilities, and controls (Dekker et al., 2013; Page, 2009). Although these activities are challenging, as explained by Schneier (1999, 2000), ultimately, they are feasible. On the other hand, in a complex system, interactions are ad hoc and nonlinear. These interaction features make it infeasible to fully analyze, test, understand, and control system-level cybersecurity behaviors and outcomes of a complex system (Cilliers, 1998; Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). We distinguish between the complicatedness and complexity of MHSs in both enterprise IT and organizational domains to understand how these characteristics affect technology, people, and process control effectiveness, and cybersecurity breaches.

Building on complexity science, we posit that an MHS’s complicatedness in (1) medical services, (2) health IT, and (3) governance weakens technology, people, and process controls to heighten the likelihood of cybersecurity breaches. To theorize mitigation mechanisms for the breach effects of complicatedness, we focus on how patient data is exchanged among an MHS’s complicated services, health IT, and governance arrangements across the constituent hospitals. We posit that ad hoc, nonlinear exchanges result in complexity and increase breaches. However, the use of enterprise-wide data analytics platforms (EWDAPs) to structure and control the exchanges achieves simplification and mitigates the breach effects of complicatedness although EWDAPs can add complexity to enterprise IT by adding more technology components and dependencies. Accordingly, we frame the following research questions:

R1: How does an MHS’s complicatedness in (a) medical services, (b) health IT, and (c) governance affect cybersecurity breaches?

R2: How do EWDAPs moderate the effects of these complicatedness measures on technical, people, and process controls, and cybersecurity breaches?

To answer these questions, we empirically examined 445 MHSs in the U.S. from 2009 to 2017. We employed Arellano-Bond system Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimation and two-stage least squares (2SLS) for data analyses. The findings reveal that complicatedness in medical services, health IT, and governance arrangements heightens cybersecurity breaches by compromising the MHS’s technical, process, and people controls. In contrast, the attainment of higher maturity levels for EWDAPs negatively moderates the relationships between all forms of complicatedness and breaches. The mechanisms through which EWDAPs achieve these breach reduction effects vary. EWDAPs strengthen process controls, thereby diminishing external cybersecurity breaches, but weakens technical and people controls, leading to an increase in internal cybersecurity breaches. Despite adding more technology components and dependencies to enterprise IT, higher maturity levels of EWDAPs achieve simplification and demonstrate a net beneficial effect of reducing the overall cybersecurity breaches of an MHS.

The study disentangles the intricate interplay between an organization’s complicatedness and complexity within both IT and organizational domains. It generates new theoretical and practical implications for antecedents and mitigations of cybersecurity breaches in multi-unit organizations such as MHSs. The research finds that complicatedness in both IT and organizational domains is the primary source of cybersecurity breaches. Challenging the conventional assumption, the study reveals that increasing the technological components and dependencies among complicated units does not universally create harmful complexity to pose cybersecurity threats. As the EWDAP interactions show, if the additional technological components and dependencies structure and control the previously ad hoc, nonlinear information exchanges of the complicated units, EWDAPs achieve simplification and mitigate the breach effects of complicatedness. If the information exchanges among the complicated units are left ad hoc and nonlinear, complexity can emerge to increase the risk of breaches.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Background

Information systems (IS) scholars have applied Schneier’s (2015) assertion about the cybersecurity implications of complexity to enterprise-wide IT in the context of mergers and acquisitions (M&A). Specifically, Liang et al. (2025) argued that IT M&A integrations between the acquirer and target can increase cybersecurity breaches by elevating IT complexity (Liang et al., 2025). However, their empirical tests focused only on the link between M&A and breaches. They did not include any measures of enterprise IT complexity, presumably due to data limitations. As a result, the asserted link between enterprise IT complexity and breaches has yet to be validated.

In a related study, Tanriverdi and Du (2020) found that organizational complexity reduced the effectiveness of IT controls in multi-unit firms, suggesting a potential link between organizational complexity and breaches. Their call for further exploration of the connection between organizational complexity and breaches motivated us to focus on the breach effects of not just enterprise IT complexity but also organizational complexity. Tanriverdi and Du (2020) also made a crucial distinction between complicatedness and complexity in the context of multi-unit firms. We follow their approach to build on complexity science in defining and explaining why a complicated MHS is not the same as a complex MHS (Dekker et al., 2013).

We define a complicated MHS as two or more constituent hospitals that refer patients to each other and share patient data through linear, well-structured interactions (Dekker et al., 2013). In a complicated MHS, standards and rules are used to enable structured, controlled patient data sharing, and rich, intensive collaborations on patient care. As Schneier (1999) argues, it is difficult to analyze the cybersecurity risks, vulnerabilities, and controls of the constituent hospitals and the cross-hospital interactions to understand the system-level cybersecurity behaviors and outcomes of a complicated MHS, but, ultimately, it is feasible to do (Page, 2009). Corporate-level behaviors and outcomes of a complicated MHS are knowable and controllable (Dekker et al., 2013) and they can be predicted from the properties of the constituent hospitals and their linear, well-structured interactions.

We define a complex MHS as two or more constituent hospitals that refer patients to each other and use ad hoc, nonlinear interactions to exchange patient data (Dekker et al., 2013). The cross-hospital interactions in a complex MHS go through feedback and feedforward loops to generate emergent behaviors and outcomes (McDaniel et al., 2003). For instance, a seemingly minor cybersecurity weakness in one member hospital in a complex MHS can become amplified through the positive feedback loops to produce a surprisingly large breach at the MHS level (Cilliers, 1998). Conversely, a major investment in the cybersecurity protections of a large hospital in a complex MHS can become attenuated through negative feedback loops and may yield only negligible mitigation effects at the MHS level. The cybersecurity behaviors and outcomes of a complex MHS are inevitably surprising and unpredictable due to the ad hoc, nonlinear interactions and self-organization of member hospitals, which dynamically produce emergent outcomes (McDaniel et al., 2003). We cannot fully know and control the system-level cybersecurity behaviors and outcomes of a complex MHS (Page, 2009).

As summarized in Appendix A, prior cybersecurity studies in the U.S. hospital industry have predominantly focused on hospital-level analysis. As noted, MHSs differ from individual hospitals not only quantitatively in scale but also qualitatively in cross-hospital interactions and corporate-level complicatedness and complexity attributes. By definition, all MHSs are complicated, as they are made up of a large number of connected components (e.g., a corporate center and two or more hospitals). However, there is variability in their levels of complicatedness: while some MHSs own only two hospitals, others own more than 200 hospitals. There is also variability in cross-hospital information sharing patterns of MHSs. An American Hospital Association (AHA) survey found that 81% of hospitals within an MHS share patient data (American Hospital Association, 2023). Some MHSs rely on ad hoc, nonlinear patient data sharing and collaborations, potentially increasing their complexity and cybersecurity risks. Other MHSs build IT platforms to structure and control patient data sharing (Tanriverdi et al., 2011). Structured and controlled interactions can lead to simplification, reduce complicatedness, restrain the emergence of complexity, and reduce cybersecurity risks. Thus, an MHS’s cybersecurity mitigations need to account not only for the risks of complicatedness in the IT and organizational domains of the constituent hospitals but also for the cross-hospital patient information exchanges and interactions.

## Three Types of Complicatedness

Drawing on Tanriverdi and Du’s (2020) generic concept of organizational complicatedness, we define the specific types of complicatedness that could affect patient cybersecurity breaches in an MHS. The major sources of an MHS’s complicatedness are medical services that produce and use patient data, health IT applications that manage patient data, and the governance of member hospitals that access patient data. Thus, we conceptualize three types of complicatedness in an MHS: (1) service complicatedness, (2) health IT complicatedness, and (3) governance complicatedness.

Service complicatedness: We define an MHS’s service complicatedness as the extent of medical services that the MHS’s member hospitals offer on a referral basis. When a member hospital does not have the medical expertise to diagnose or treat a patient, it refers the patient to another member hospital of the MHS that has the expertise. As the number and variety of healthcare services offered on a referral basis increase, member hospitals face the challenge of effectively coordinating their services, resulting in increased service complicatedness in the MHS. Caregivers from multiple member hospitals need access to the patient data generated by the services offered on a referral basis. Hence, the MHS’s cybersecurity attack surface enlarges with increased service complicatedness.

Health IT complicatedness: An MHS’s health IT complicatedness is defined as the scope of unique health IT systems used by the MHS’s member hospitals to support medical services and patient data exchanges with third-party physician groups, insurance companies, and other hospitals. If member hospitals use different brands of health IT applications to perform the same function, the MHS faces higher health IT complicatedness. For example, in the electronic health records (EHR) application category, if 10 hospitals each use a different brand of EHR, the MHS faces the complications of having to manage and secure all of these brands. If member hospitals adopt different brands within many categories of health IT applications, the MHS faces high levels of health IT complicatedness (Tanriverdi & Du, 2011). Designing, testing, and effectively operating IT controls thus becomes more challenging as the number and variety of the MHS’s health IT systems proliferate (Schneier, 2000).

Governance complicatedness: Governance complicatedness is defined as the extent to which the MHS decentralizes decisions about medical services, physician group arrangements, and insurance deals in its member hospitals (Bazzoli et al., 1999). If the MHS centralizes these decisions by having its corporate center make the decisions for all member hospitals, the MHS’s governance complicatedness remains low. If the MHS gives each member hospital full autonomy to make these decisions for themselves in a decentralized manner, the MHS faces high governance complicatedness. At increasing levels of governance complicatedness, it becomes more challenging for the MHS to align member hospitals’ cybersecurity protections across medical services, physician, groups and insurance relationships.

EWDAPs as a mitigation mechanism for cybersecurity risks of complicatedness: We define EWDAPs as an MHS’s use of common, enterprise-wide data warehouse and analytics platforms for enabling patient data sharing, integration, and analyses among member hospitals (Du & Tanriverdi, 2023; Tanriverdi et al., 2011). EWDAPs accept patient data in different formats from member hospitals’ incompatible EHRs, medical imaging systems, billing systems, and other health IT systems. EWDAPs also apply data management practices such as master data management, data quality management, data standardization, data integration, and consolidation across silos of hospitals. It translates the data into a common, structured format before storing the data in its central repository (PR Newswire, 2014a). By ensuring interoperability with various data formats, health information systems and standards, and EWDAP structures and controls, previously ad hoc, nonlinear patient data exchanges in the MHS. Its unified and comprehensive view of patient data enables care teams from different hospitals to use analytics to identify care gaps and opportunities for improvement across the MHS (PR Newswire, 2014b).

EWDAPs can potentially mitigate the cybersecurity risks of complicatedness in medical services, health IT systems, and governance of an MHS by structuring and controlling ad hoc patient data exchanges and achieving simplification to reduce complicatedness levels. EWDAPs can also impede the emergence of complexity in MHS by taming the wicked problems of complexity as follows (Clarke & Stewart, 1997): (1) digitize cross-hospital patient data flows to improve an

MHS’s quality of patient data exchange processes and controls; (2) align member hospitals’ cybersecurity goals; and (3) enable the member hospitals to coordinate MHS-level responses to emerging cybersecurity threats on the shared patient data.

## Hypotheses

We first justify how and why the three types of complicatedness increase the cybersecurity breaches of an MHS and then explain how EWDAPs can negatively moderate these relationships.

## Service Complicatedness and Cybersecurity Breaches of MHS

An MHS’s member hospitals offer a variety of integrated medical services along the continuum of care, each of which generates patient data. Caregivers from the respective hospitals and medical specialties have access rights to patient data through referrals. To secure patient data, an MHS has to institute controls over access points and caregiver behaviors. Higher service complicatedness implies a larger attack surface of the patient data (Theisen et al., 2018) because more caregivers can access the data and more diverse caregiver behaviors likely involve the data. More caregivers and diverse caregiver behaviors give unauthorized parties a larger scope of human behaviors to exploit to gain unauthorized access to the patient data. More access points also give unauthorized parties additional channels to exploit to gain unauthorized access to the data. Designing, testing, and effectively operating the cybersecurity controls of a larger scope of integrated medical services thus becomes more challenging (Schneier, 2000). Therefore, compared to an MHS that is low in service complicatedness, an MHS that is high in service complicatedness is likely to experience more cybersecurity breaches.

H1: An MHS’s service complicatedness increases cybersecurity breaches.

## Health IT Complicatedness and Cybersecurity Breaches of MHS

An MHS’s member hospitals use a variety of interconnected health IT applications to support their medical services (Shiver & Cantiello, 2016). The sheer number and variety of the interconnected health IT applications adopted by an MHS’s member hospitals increases its health IT complicatedness (Mocker et al., 2014; Schneier, 2000). The higher number and wider variety of health IT applications expand the MHS’s attack surface further (Theisen et al., 2018). With more health IT applications handling patient data, unauthorized parties have additional opportunities to search for and exploit health IT applications with the weakest IT controls to gain unauthorized access to patient data. To secure patient data, an MHS has to institute effective IT controls over a larger scope of health IT applications across the member hospitals. As Schneier (2000) argues, designing, testing, and effectively operating cybersecurity controls of a larger number and wider variety of health IT applications and hospitals is more challenging.

Furthermore, a complicated health IT environment could increase cybersecurity risks by reducing the quality of patient data flows among an MHS’s member hospitals. Compatibility and interoperability problems between the health IT systems of an MHS increase the difficulty of building structured, crosshospital data exchange capabilities. Member hospitals with different health IT systems face challenges in establishing and securing information sharing mechanisms (Majchrzak et al., 2015). Some member hospitals with less variety of health IT applications may find it easier to institute tight controls over their systems. Other member hospitals with a greater variety of IT applications may not be able to implement IT controls easily and may prefer to loosen the IT controls to ease the sharing of patient data across hospital boundaries. Disagreements on the protection-sharing trade-offs can lead to the failure of such cross-hospital data sharing initiatives (Cram et al., 2015; Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). In the absence of structured data exchange capabilities, the hospitals may have to resort to ad hoc data sharing practices in referring patients to each other. However, such ad hoc data sharing practices increase cybersecurity risks: e.g., theft or loss of media, human error, and unintended disclosure of patient data. Thus:

H2: An MHS’s health IT complicatedness increases cybersecurity breaches.

## Governance Complicatedness and Cybersecurity breaches of MHS

We define an MHS’s governance as the locus of decision rights between the corporate center and the member hospitals regarding medical services to offer, physician group arrangements to make, and deals with insurance companies (Alexander et al., 2003). In centralized governance, the corporate center has the legal or fiduciary authority to make these decisions for the member hospitals (Bazzoli et al., 1999; Shortell et al., 2000). In decentralized governance, the hospitals have the autonomy to make these decisions for themselves (Bazzoli et al., 1999). An MHS can configure the locus of decision rights differently for different member hospitals. Governance becomes more complicated and increases cybersecurity risks when the overall configuration of the decision rights favors decentralization.

Complicated governance configurations can increase the misalignment of an MHS’s cybersecurity goals and controls (Warkentin & Johnston, 2008). When member hospitals have full autonomy to decide which medical services to offer and how, they can choose how to set the cybersecurity goals and controls of the medical services. Some hospitals may prioritize cybersecurity while others may not. With member hospitals pursuing their own cybersecurity goals and controls, their MHS has to go through time-consuming negotiations to establish a unified cybersecurity framework across the medical services (Flores et al., 2014). This makes it challenging for the MHS to coordinate cybersecurity activities over medical services (Soomro et al., 2016) and to reduce potential conflicts among members (Bazzoli et al., 2000). The MHS may not be able to develop coordinated, system-level responses to cybersecurity threats. The variety of cybersecurity goals, policies, controls, and technologies can enlarge the MHS’s attack surface further and allow unauthorized parties to have more channels to target for gaining unauthorized access to the MHS’ patient data. Thus:

H3: An MHS’s governance complicatedness increases cybersecurity breaches.

## EWDAPs’ Moderation of the Breach Effect of Service Complicatedness

Some MHSs such as Trinity Health invest in EWDAPs so that their member hospitals can put their patient data into a common data warehouse and read, write, exchange, and analyze patient data through a single source of truth in the common data repository (Tanriverdi et al., 2011). The EWDAP connections replace ad hoc patient data flows across member hospitals with structured ones by implementing standardization in data types, data formats, data exchange protocols, and cybersecurity configurations. Standardization promotes the compatibility and interoperability of cybersecurity solutions among member hospitals (Shariati et al., 2011). This standardization of the access points of diverse medical services to EWDAPs reduces the variety of access points and shrinks the attack surface of the patient data (Theisen et al., 2018). While the standardization of access points makes an EWDAP into a single point of failure, an MHS can reduce this risk by hardening the cybersecurity controls of the single point of failure (Baskerville et al., 2018). With the standardization of access points, the design, testing, and operation of cybersecurity controls can be simplified (Schneier, 2000). With fewer access points and simplified and hardened cybersecurity controls, unauthorized parties become less likely to gain unauthorized access to patient data.

EWDAPs also enable an MHS to monitor how a diverse set of caregivers from different medical specialties and hospitals access patient data in the common patient data repository. For workers performing the cybersecurity controls of EWDAPs, standardization and simplification lead to a reduced workload (Schneier, 2000), which can increase the workers’ ability and willingness to analyze, understand, identify, and learn from unanticipated anomalies that cause cybersecurity failures (Say & Vasudevab, 2020). Having visibility into user behaviors can enable an MHS to institute behavioral controls that align the behaviors with the MHS’s cybersecurity policies.

In an MHS whose member hospitals rely on ad hoc, nonlinear patient data exchanges, service complicatedness is likely to evolve into service complexity and increase cybersecurity breaches. In comparison, an MHS that uses EWDAPs to systematically structure and control patient data exchanges among its member hospitals can achieve simplification in complicated services and restrain the emergence of complexity dynamics to mitigate the cybersecurity breach risks of service complicatedness.

H4: An MHS’s EWDAP negatively moderates the relationship between service complicatedness and cybersecurity breaches.

## EWDAP’s Moderation of the Breach Effect of Health IT Complicatedness

If an MHS has a complicated health IT landscape, with member hospitals using different health IT systems for performing the same medical procedures, cross-hospital collaboration on patient care may be challenging due to compatibility and interoperability problems. These problems weaken security and increase cybersecurity breach risks. For instance, member hospitals of the Community Health Network (CHN) had to resort to ad hoc patient data sharing to collaborate on patient care due to clinical data being isolated in three different electronic health record (EHR) applications: Epic, Cerner, and Meditech. As our theory notes, ad hoc patient data exchanges can also lead to the emergence of complexity in complicated health IT systems and heighten cybersecurity breach risks. To mitigate the risks, CHN invested in an EWDAP (PR Newswire, 2014a). Specifically, CHN used an EWDAP to accept patient data from the different EHRs in different formats and translated them into a common format before storing the data in its central repository. The repository served as the single source of patient data for the hospitals collaborating on patient care. Despite adding more technology components and dependencies to CHN’s enterprise IT, the EWDAP transformed the ad hoc data sharing practices among the complicated health IT systems of the member hospitals into structured and controlled patient data flows (Kozuch & Sienkiewicz-Malyjurek, 2015), thereby achieving simplification in the complicated health IT systems.

The EWDAP also restrained the emergence of complexity in the CHN’s complicated health IT systems by digitizing and structuring the previously ad hoc data-sharing practices (such as email, fax, physical shipment of digital media, etc.) and improving the quality of patient data exchanges. Structured electronic data interchanges between an EWDAP and the health IT systems of member hospitals require the sender and the recipient hospitals to agree on data types, data formats, data exchange protocols, data encryption methods, access rights, and so forth. Unlike in ad hoc data sharing, the likelihood of data loss, data theft, human error, and unintended disclosures is much lower in structured data interchange through EWDAPs. These benefits also justify the institution of additional cybersecurity controls at the interface points of an EWDAP and disparate health IT systems, helping to mitigate cybersecurity risks stemming from health IT complicatedness. Thus:

H5: An MHS’s EWDAP negatively moderates the relationship between health IT complicatedness and cybersecurity breaches.

## EWDAPs’ Moderation of the Breach Effect of Governance Complicatedness

Governance complicatedness, i.e., highly decentralized member hospitals autonomously governing their own medical services, physician group affiliations, and insurance company relationships, creates many cybersecurity challenges. An MHS is not able to gather threat intelligence from autonomously governed hospitals, have visibility into their cybersecurity vulnerabilities, monitor if the vulnerabilities are being exploited, and respond quickly to cybersecurity incidents (Say & Vasudevab, 2020). Ad hoc patient data exchanges among the complicated governance arrangements of the member hospitals can amplify these cybersecurity challenges and risks by leading to the emergence of complexity in the MHS’s governance arrangements.

By structuring the ad hoc patient data exchanges among the autonomously governed member hospitals, EWDAPs can achieve simplification and tame the cybersecurity challenges of complicated governance. As the autonomously governed member hospitals adopt a common EWDAP to govern patient data, the MHS gains the ability to observe behaviors and emerging threats on the patient data, analyze the data to generate threat intelligence, and share it with all member hospitals. An EWDAP can also enable the MHS to continuously monitor cybersecurity vulnerabilities at the interface points of the EWDAP with autonomously governed member hospitals. Thus, the MHS can detect cybersecurity incidents at the interface points of the EWDAP and take rapid action to stop intrusions and contain damages (Thompson, 2020). The use of a common EWDAP for patient data management can further align member hospitals’ cybersecurity goals. Even though the member hospitals have autonomy in making their own cybersecurity investments, achieving cybersecurity goal alignment can increase voluntary compliance with MHS’s enterprise-wide cybersecurity policies. Thus:

H6: An MHS’s EWDAP negatively moderates the relationship between governance complicatedness and cybersecurity breaches.

## Methods

## Sample and Data

Our sampling frame includes all U.S. MHSs that were in operation between 2009 and 2017. We constructed data on MHSs by combining multiple data sources. First, we used the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society Analytics™ (HIMSS) database to identify hospitals’ MHSs. The HIMSS database also provides information about their organizational and IT characteristics (i.e., bed size, facility type, service type, operating expenses, health IT applications, and security IT applications). Second, we utilized the “provider of services” files prepared by the Centers for Medicare Services (CMS)<sup>2</sup> to obtain data on hospital medical services. Third, we assessed the American Hospital Association (AHA) database to obtain data on the governance mode and information sharing practices of MHSs. Fourth, we used the Factiva news database to check if an MHS had announced investments in an EWDAP. Fifth, we merged and cross-checked two data sources on cybersecurity breaches: The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC) websites. For each breach, we also collected and contentanalyzed announcements, news articles, victim notification letters, and any court filings to code the types of control weaknesses that led to the breach.

We matched healthcare providers in the different data sources based on their Medicare identification numbers and linked them to MHSs based on their corporate center IDs. We included 445 MHSs whose member hospitals reported their data consecutively for more than five years. These MHSs were matched with the lists of breached hospitals based on hospital name, city, and state. The final sample included 445 MHSs, 3,814 observations, and 387 cybersecurity breaches between 2009 and 2017. The MHSs in our sample owned and operated a total of 4,061 hospitals, 43,730 ambulatory care facilities, and 2,093 subacute facilities. As Table 1 reports, MHSs in the sample had 7.92 hospitals, 55.44 ambulatory, and 3.37 subacute facilities, on average.

## Dependent Variables

While our dependent variables focus on the number of cybersecurity breaches in an MHS, it is important to note the variance in how cybersecurity breaches in an MHS are reported. In some MHSs, each affected hospital files a separate breach report. In other MHSs, the corporate center files the breach report on behalf of their members. This variance could potentially be due to the presence or absence of shared data centers and EWDAPs at the MHSs. In our sample, 322 of the 445 MHSs had at least one data center controlled by the corporate center of the MHS. About 25% of the MHSs had invested in an EWDAP. From a security viewpoint, a corporatelevel data center and an EWDAP represent single points of failure; if a breach happens in them, patient data from multiple member hospitals can leak simultaneously. Thus, it makes sense for an MHS with such systems to report its breaches centrally rather than having each member hospital report the same breach separately. To account for such variance in breach reporting practices, we employed two types of dependent variables: total MHS breaches and MHS breaches reported by corporate centers.

## Total MHS Breaches

As Table 2 shows, the main dependent variable for hypothesis testing is the total number of cybersecurity breaches at an MHS in a year. If a breach event affects multiple member hospitals simultaneously and each affected hospital files a separate breach report for the same event, we remove the duplications to count a unique breach event as one MHS breach. In addition, some breach events affect only the corporate center of an MHS, and the MHS files a single breach report with the HHS. We summed up the unique breach events reported by an MHS’s member hospitals and corporate center in a given year to obtain the MHS’s total breaches in that year.

## MHS Breaches Reported by Corporate Centers

For robustness analysis, we also added a second dependent variable to distinguish whether an MHS’s corporate center filed breaches affecting member hospitals (coded as 1, or 0 otherwise). We used the “Archive” section of the Wall of Shame website of the HHS to identify breach reports filed by MHSs’ corporate centers. Among the 387 cybersecurity breaches in our dataset, 123 breaches (about 32%) were reported by the corporate centers of MHSs.

## Control Weaknesses

A cybersecurity breach can happen due to weaknesses in technical, process, or people controls. To better understand the impact of our complicatedness measures on control weaknesses, we coded whether a breach at an MHS in a given year involved a technical control weakness (1) or not (0); process control weakness (1) or not (0); or people control weakness (1) or not (0). We then counted the number of weaknesses within each category of control across all cybersecurity breaches of the MHS in that year. Appendix B explains the development and validation of a coding guideline used by two independent coders to code these control weaknesses.

## Independent Variables

## Service Complicatedness

The service complicatedness (SVC Complicate) of an MHS is measured as the average number of medical services that the MHS’s member hospitals offer under arrangements/referrals. Since under some value-based arrangements, referrals would be directed within a network or continuum of preferred providers (based on quality and other legitimate considerations), the number of services offered under arrangements/referrals captures the connected medical services across an MHS’s member hospitals. Appendix C lists the medical services offered by MHSs.

## Health IT Complicatedness

The health IT complicatedness (HIT Complicate) of an MHS is measured as a reverse entropy weighted by bed sizes, reflecting the extent of diversity among vendors within the HIT categories that support the same types of medical services among member hospitals. When different vendors of HIT applications are adopted by member hospitals to support the same clinical processes, it poses challenges in data translation, bridging, and aggregation among the applications. As such, the reverse entropy of diverse vendors represents the level of health IT complicatedness. Appendix D lists the major HIT applications used in computing health IT complicatedness.

## Governance Complicatedness

The governance complicatedness (Gov Complicate) of an MHS is measured by calculating the weighted average of decentralization levels among an MHS’s member hospitals. The weight of a member hospital is the percentage of beds the hospital contributes to the total beds of MHS. The decentralization level of a hospital is measured using a taxonomy developed by Bazzoli et al. (1999), and it is annually computed in the AHA database. Annual AHA surveys ask whether decisions about a member hospital’s healthcare services, physician arrangements, and insurance arrangements are made by its MHS cooperate center, the hospital itself, or jointly:

1. Centralized health system, characterized by high degrees of centralization in hospital services, physician arrangements, and insurance products across member hospitals.

2. Partially centralized health system, characterized by relatively high levels of centralization for physician arrangements and insurance products, while allowing member hospitals discretion over the array of services they offer.

3. Moderately centralized health system, characterized by moderate levels of centralization and differentiation in hospital services, physician arrangements, and insurance products.

4. Decentralized health system, characterized by high degrees of decentralization of hospital services, physician arrangements, and insurance product development. There are high levels of differentiation in services/products among member hospitals.

5. Independent hospital system, characterized by little differentiation of hospital services, physician arrangements, and insurance product development. These systems primarily consist of horizontally affiliated autonomous hospitals.

## The Enterprise-Wide Data Analytics IT Platform

The enterprise-wide data analytics IT platform (EWDAP) of an MHS in a given year is measured as the number of years since the MHS’s first year of investment in an EWDAP for use across all of its member hospitals. To identify whether and when an MHS made an EWDAP investment, we performed systematic keyword searches in the Factiva news database.<sup>3</sup> We used variations of keywords such as shared patient data, data warehouses, data lakes, business intelligence and data analytical tools, analytics, data initiatives, information technology, information systems, patient records, etc. We focused on news articles that mentioned patient data (clinical data, medical services, or health services), and required the investment to be at the MHS level. If a member hospital announced a data analytical investment for its own use, we did not consider it to be an MHS-level EWDAP investment. Often, MHS-level EWDAP investments emphasized that the technology was used by multiple member hospitals. If an investment satisfied these criteria, we set EDWAP to 1 (0 otherwise) for that year. We recognized that it would take an MHS multiple years to achieve mature EWDAP capabilities after the initial investment. We incremented the EWDAP value by 1 in each new year after the initial investment. The number of years since MHS’s first EWDAP investment was used as a measure of the MHS’s EWDAP maturity level. We applied this procedure to all 445 MHSs from 2009 to 2017. The absence or lower maturity levels of EWDAP capture ad hoc patient data sharing whereas higher maturity levels capture structured patient data sharing within an MHS.

## Complexity Measures

As noted, complexity arises from ad hoc, nonlinear patient information exchanges among constituent hospitals of an MHS (Cilliers, 1998). According to the AHA surveys, 81% of MHSs report patient information sharing among their member hospitals (American Hospital Association, 2023). However, this measure does not distinguish if the information is shared in an ad hoc or structured manner. Using the AHA’s data, we controlled for the richness of patient information sharing in an MHS, as explained below. We used EWDAP to determine whether the patient information sharing was ad hoc or structured. We interacted EWDAP with the three complicatedness variables to measure if medical services, health IT, and governance arrangements remained complicated, became simplified, or became complex. If EWDAP was absent or had a very low maturity score, it meant that patient data sharing remained ad hoc and that complexity could hence emerge in medical services, health IT, and governance arrangements. At higher maturity levels, EWDAP transformed the ad hoc, nonlinear patient information exchanges into more structured, controlled ones. Although EWDAPs add more technology components and dependencies to enterprise IT, higher EWDAP maturity levels can achieve simplification, reduce complicatedness, and restrain the emergence of complexity dynamics in medical services, health IT, and governance arrangements.

## Control Variables

As shown in Table 3, we included controls to account for factors that can affect an MHS’s attractiveness as an attack target or factors that can make it easier or more difficult to protect patient data. First, to control for the extent of enterprise-wide standardization of IT security investments of an MHS (StandardSecurity), we used an entropy-based measure. This measure captured the degree to which member hospitals of the MHS used the same vendors/brands for each category of IT security solution in a given year. We took weighted averages of these entropy measures, using the member hospitals’ respective shares in the overall bed count of the MHS as the weights. Next, we used the logged number of healthcare applications (lnHealthIT) to control for an MHS’s health IT capability. We included a control for the richness of information sharing among an MHS’s member hospitals by utilizing the AHA survey question: “Which of the patient data (e.g., patient demographics, laboratory/radiology results, medication history, and clinical care record) does your hospital electronically exchange/share with one or more of the providers inside and outside of the MHS?”<sup>4</sup> If an MHS’s level of electronic information sharing was above the sample median, we set the richness of information sharing (InfoSharing) of the MHS to 1 (0 otherwise).

To control for the organic versus acquisitive growth of an MHS, we included the MHS’s logged number of hospital acquisitions (lnAcquisition) in a given year. Since some MHSs shrink their size by divesting poorly performing hospitals, we controlled for the MHS’s logged number of hospital divestitures (lnDivestiture) in a given year. To account for varying MHS sizes, we controlled for the logged revenues of an MHS (lnRevenue), the ratio of owned member hospitals to all hospitals in the MHS (RatioOwned), the logged number of different patient markets (CBSAs) served by the MHS (Coverage), and the logged number of member hospitals of the MHS (lnNofMembers). We further controlled for the numbers of medical school-affiliated academic teaching hospitals (NofAcademic), primary care hospitals (NofPrimary), for-profit hospitals (NofForProfit), and hospitals located in micropolitan areas—i.e., urban areas with a population of at least 10,000 but less than 50,000 (NofMicro).

## Identification Strategy

This study aimed to measure how an MHS’s complicatedness in services, health IT, and governance affects cybersecurity breaches and how EWDAPs can negatively moderate these effects. This context is subject to endogeneity issues. First, there may be selection bias from confounding unobservable factors. For instance, an MHS with well-established clinical workflows, skillful medical staff, and compliance culture may concurrently exhibit more streamlining and less complicatedness, EWDAP adoption, and fewer breaches. As such, complicatedness might be correlated with breach occurrence but might not directly affect it. Second, reverse causality could be an issue if a breach leads to changes in an MHS’s complicatedness levels in services, health IT, or governance, as well as the adoption or avoidance of EWDAPs.

To address these endogeneity concerns, we employ an Arellano-Bond system GMM estimation (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Blundell & Bond, 1998) and a 2SLS instrumental variable (IV) approach. More detailed information on constructing and validating our models is presented in the following sections.

## System GMM Model Specification

As our main model, we adopted the Arellano-Bond system GMM whose procedure controls for endogeneity and eliminates bias from unobserved heterogeneity. The system GMM estimator uses a system of two equations, which are the original equations and the transformed ones by firstdifferencing. The system GMM employs the lagged differences of an outcome as instruments to deal with the endogeneity of all input variables (Arellano & Bond, 1991, Blundell & Bond, 1998). This approach has been widely used in information systems studies (Aral et al., 2012; Bardhan et al., 2013; Bhargava & Mishra, 2014; Tambe & Hitt, 2012). Our panel data includes the 2009-2017 period with 445 MHSs, making it well-suited for the Arellano-Bond system GMM estimation. Our models are specified as follows:

$$

where i denotes an MHS; t denotes a year; $X _ { i t }$ represents a vector of control variables, with Θ being their corresponding estimated coefficients; $\eta _ { i }$ depicts unobserved and timeinvariant MHS effects; $\mu _ { t }$ reflects unobserved time effects; and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is an error term. However, these models have some difficulties obtaining robust estimates. First, the right-hand side variables are assumed to be endogenous. As causality may run in both directions—for example, from complicatedness to breaches or from breaches to complicatedness—the regressors may be correlated with the error term. Second, an MHS’s timeinvariant characteristics, as well as other unobserved heterogeneity, could bias parameter estimates, although the model includes MHS and time fixed effects.

Based on the equations of the original model—Equations (1)- (3)—the transformed system GMM estimation equations can effectively address these endogeneity issues for robust parameter estimates (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Arellano & Bover, 1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998). For instance, Equation (4) depicts the transformed form of Equation (3) by firstdifferencing. As first-differencing removes the $\eta _ { i }$ and $\mu _ { t } ,$ it eliminates potential bias from the effects of observed or unobserved MHS and time characteristics. The estimators address endogeneity with suitable lags of their own firstdifferences (Aral et al., 2012):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\Delta B r e a c h e s _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} \cdot \Delta S v c C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \cdot} \\ & {\Delta H I T C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {3} \cdot \Delta G o v C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {4} \cdot} \\ & {\Delta E W D A P _ {i t - 1} + \gamma_ {1} \cdot (\Delta S v c C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} \times} \\ & {\Delta E W D A P _ {i t - 1}) + \gamma_ {2} (\Delta H I T C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} \times} \\ & {\Delta E W D A P _ {i t - 1}) + \gamma_ {3} \cdot (\Delta G o v C o m p l i c a t e _ {i t - 1} \times} \\ & {E W D A P _ {i t - 1}) + \Theta \cdot \Delta X _ {i t - 1} + \Delta \varepsilon_ {i t}} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

For instance, in service complicatedness (SVC Complicate), each additional medical service under referrals/arrangements from member hospitals raises cybersecurity risks by 1.01% (0.0101 at $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . ## Validity of System GMM Estimations

To ensure the validity of our system GMM estimations, we confirmed that two conditions were met by two tests: (1) a test of whether the differenced error terms $( \Delta \varepsilon _ { i t } )$ in Equation (4) were serially correlated; and (2) the Hansen test of overidentifying restrictions, which checks for overall validity of the instruments (Hansen, 1982). Failure to reject the null hypotheses of both tests provided support for our system GMM models. First, the first-order serial correlations for the differenced error terms in the Arellano-Bond method were all significant across all models (AR (1) test); see Tables 5 and 6.

## Fixed Effects Models and 2SLS

We further validated our main results using fixed effect models and 2SLS. First, we included the MHS and year fixed effects in Equations (1)-(3). As presented in Table 7, the results are consistent with those of our main system GMM models. This reinforced the robustness of our findings. Second, as an alternative method for addressing endogeneity, we employed 2SLS estimation which allowed us to obtain causal estimates for the effects of independent variables on outcomes. In our context, the potential endogenous variables were three types of complicatedness and EWDAP. For these potential endogenous variables, valid IVs must satisfy two conditions: (1) be relevant for explaining variation in complicatedness or EWDAP, and (2) be unrelated to the unobservable factors captured in the error term (??<sub>????</sub>)—that is, not affect our outcome measure, cybersecurity breaches (Wooldridge, 2012).

Previous studies have demonstrated that hospitals’ strategies in offering medical services and adopting healthcare IT systems are affected by those of their competitors or peers in their local healthcare service area (HSA) (Karshenas & Stoneman, 1993; Lu et al., 2018; Miller & Tucker, 2009). Based on the institutional and learning effects, Lu et al. (2018) employed the adoption rates of the computerized physician order entry (CPOE) in rival hospitals within a local HSA as IVs to investigate the effect of the focal hospital’s CPOE adoption rate on nursing home staffing decisions. The exclusion restriction in their IVs was explained by the fact that the CPOE adoption rate of rival hospitals affected the focal hospital’s CPOE adoption but not the hospital’s nursing home staffing decisions. Similarly, Miller and Tucker (2009) adopted the preexisting IT privacy policies of other hospitals in an HSA as IVs to investigate the relationship between privacy policies and the diffusion of EHR adoption.

## Empirical Extensions

We additionally examined whether the mitigation effect of EWDAP varied with the motivation and intent for cybersecurity breaches. Cybersecurity breaches are typically categorized into two types: internal threats (e.g., negligent insiders) and external threats (e.g., hacking or theft) since they vary in source, motive, and intensity, depending on who is behind the breach (Kwon & Johnson, 2013). We separately added external and internal breaches as dependent variables to Equation (4). The results in Table 9 indicate that the complex HIT created by EWDAP in a complicated HIT landscape (i.e., EWDAP × HIT Complicate) significantly reduced external breaches (-0.0071 at p < 0.01) but significantly increased internal breaches (0.0082 at p < 0.01). Regarding service complexity (EWDAP × SVC Complicate) and governance complexity (EWDAP × Gov Complicate), they both significantly reduced both external (-0.0023 and -0.0110 at p < 0.01, respectively) and internal breaches (-0.0014 and -0.0064 at p < 0.01, respectively). Table 6 shows that complex HIT (EWDAP × HIT Complicate) increased technical and people control weaknesses. The results in Table 9 explain that these control weaknesses were driven by insiders’ mistakes or lack of skills in handling technical controls (0.0082 at p < 0.01).

## Discussion and Conclusion

Multi-unit organizations such as MHSs are characterized by a dynamic interplay of complicatedness and complexity in their IT and organizational domains. This study develops theoretical and practical insights into cybersecurity risks and the mitigations of these characteristics.

## Contributions to Research

First, in contrast to cybersecurity practitioners and researchers who interchangeably use IT complicatedness and IT complexity, we distinguish between the two concepts by grounding them in complexity science. We found that IT complicatedness (HIT Complicate) increases cybersecurity breaches. On the other hand, utilizing an EWDAP to interconnect the complicated health IT of an MHS (EWDAP × HIT Complicate) can elevate IT complexity by introducing additional technologies, interconnections, and dependencies. Counterintuitively, however, this represents a beneficial type of IT complexity that reduces cybersecurity breaches (Table

5) by strengthening process controls (Table 6) and reducing external cybersecurity breaches (Table 9). However, overlaying an EWDAP onto a complicated health IT landscape can weaken technical and people controls, reducing an MHS’s ability to prevent insider breaches, which often stem from unintentional human errors. These results provide more nuanced insights into the relationship between IT complexity and cybersecurity than are currently assumed in practice (Schneier, 2015) and academia (Liang et al., 2025).

Second, unlike cybersecurity practitioners who focus only on IT, we also focus on organizations. The results indicate that both service complicatedness (SVC Complicate) and governance complicatedness (Gov Complicate) significantly increase cybersecurity breaches. Initially, using an EWDAP to interconnect complicated services for patient data sharing might seem to heighten service complexity (EWDAP × SVC Complicate). Similarly, establishing EWDAP links among hospitals with decentralized governance structures for patient data sharing could be seen as introducing additional governance complexity (EWDAP × Gov Complicate). However, EWDAP negatively interacts with SVC Complicate and Gov Complicate to reduce cybersecurity breaches within an MHS by reducing technical, process, and people control weaknesses. By digitizing and structuring previously ad hoc patient data flows, EWDAPs bring order to complicated services and governance arrangements. Moreover, EWDAPs enable MHSs to design and effectively operate technical, process, and people controls over patient data flows. EWDAPs act as a deterrent to the emergence of service and governance complexities and reduce the likelihood of breaches. EWDAPs also reduce breaches directly by promoting goal alignment, coordination, and joint responses across the varied services and governance arrangements of the interconnected hospitals.

The results address the study’s research questions as follows: Complicatedness, distinguished from complexity, in medical services, health IT, and governance stands out as the primary contributor to control weaknesses and cybersecurity breaches in MHSs. The impact of complexity on cybersecurity breaches depends on whether the complexity arises from ad hoc or structured interactions among the complicated units. Ad hoc interactions, like emailing patient data across hospitals or transporting patient data on electronic media (disk drives, flash drives, laptops, etc.), contribute to a detrimental form of complexity that exacerbates the cybersecurity breach effects of complicatedness. In contrast, structured interactions, exemplified by patient data sharing through EWDAPs, foster a beneficial form of complexity that mitigates the cybersecurity breaches associated with all three types of complicatedness.

Third, to our knowledge, this is the first study to theorize and test antecedents and mitigations of cybersecurity breaches at the MHS level of analysis. As detailed in Appendix A, preceding research has primarily concentrated on investigating factors influencing cybersecurity breaches at the individual hospital level. Notably, only one study acknowledged the possibility of individual hospitals existing within a larger multihospital health system. However, it controlled solely for health system size in its examination of hospital-level cybersecurity breaches (Angst et al., 2017). The fact that 67% of hospitals and 76% of hospital beds in the U.S. are operated by MHSs (American Hospital Association, 2023) underscores the significance of understanding MHS-level antecedents and mitigations of cybersecurity breaches. By acquiring community hospitals, specialty hospitals, tertiary care hospitals, and post-acute services, MHSs seek to offer integrated health services across the continuum of patient care (Du, 2015; Du & Tanriverdi, 2023). These initiatives increase the complicatedness and complexity of MHSs and heighten their cybersecurity risks. Standardizing and unifying the IT systems and processes of the acquired entities can address these challenges. However, such initiatives often take decades (Tanriverdi & Du, 2011; Tanriverdi et al., 2011). EWDAPs can serve as a workaround to tame cybersecurity risks of complicatedness and complexity in MHSs.

Fourth, this study contributes to the existing body of research examining the relationship between complicatedness and IT risks. Tanriverdi and Du (2020) discovered that organizational complicatedness was associated with increased material weaknesses in IT controls related to financial reporting. Our study refines and extends the understanding of this relationship by proposing and empirically examining three specific types of complicatedness relevant to cybersecurity control effectiveness within MHSs. Namely, we introduce service complicatedness (related to medical aspects of care delivery), governance complicatedness (linked to administrative aspects of care delivery), and health IT complicatedness (associated with technological aspects of care delivery). The results show that each of these complicatedness types weakens an MHS’s technical, process, and people controls, providing a more granular insight into the factors influencing IT control effectiveness. Furthermore, our study addresses the broader impact of complicatedness on cybersecurity breaches, revealing that these three types of complicatedness not only disrupt IT control effectiveness but also elevate the risk of cybersecurity breaches in MHSs.

Fifth, the study has implications for IS research building on complexity science. Current IS research assumes that a pattern of rich, intense interactions among interconnected components would create system complexity (Liang et al., 2025; Nan & Tanriverdi, 2017; Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Our theory and results require refining this assumption by explaining that it is the form of information sharing (ad hoc versus structured) rather than the richness of information sharing that determines whether a system is complicated or complex. The control variable for the richness of information sharing is associated with a reduction in the overall breaches within MHS—interestingly, however, it increases breaches announced by the corporate center (Table 5). The inconsistency of these results could be attributed to the variable’s failure to distinguish between ad hoc and structured information sharing. EWDAP makes this distinction and shows consistent breach reduction effects with both dependent variables. At high EWDAP maturity levels, breach effects of the complicatedness measures decline after controlling for the richness of information sharing. These results suggest that structuring rich and intense interactions among complicated units can lead to simplification and act as a deterrent against the emergence of complexity, thereby mitigating the breach effects of complicatedness. Future IS research building on complexity science could distinguish whether a system is complicated or complex by measuring how information sharing among constituent components of the system occurs—via ad hoc or structured methods.

Sixth, this study theorizes how enterprise-wide patient data analytics initiatives affect cybersecurity breaches. Some MHSs invest in an EWDAP to establish a common IT platform over which member hospitals can share patient data and conduct patient data analytics. There are divergent views on how such initiatives might affect cybersecurity. On one hand, EWDAPs may increase cybersecurity risks by creating a single point of failure. If attackers breach the EWDAP of an MHS, a large amount of patient data may be compromised. On the other hand, EWDAPs have the potential to strengthen cybersecurity by minimizing distributed failure points across member hospitals, enabling streamlined, structured, and controlled data exchange in the MHS. This study provides evidence that both EWDAP and its interactions with the three types of complicatedness measures significantly reduce cybersecurity breaches in MHSs.

## Contributions to Practice

This study highlights to business leaders that the complicatedness of services, IT, and governance significantly elevates cybersecurity breaches in multi-unit organizations. However, complicatedness is essential for business. Leaders embrace complicatedness to address dynamically changing customer demands, competition, and regulation (Mocker et al., 2014). While pursuing the benefits of complicatedness, business leaders should implement controls to mitigate the heightened breach risks it poses. For example, since increases in service complicatedness amplify process and people control weaknesses, business leaders should invest in more effective process and people controls when pursuing a larger scope of medical services. Governance complicatedness increases weaknesses in technical and people controls. Thus, if business leaders choose to decentralize the locus of decision rights to member hospitals, they should invest more in technical and people controls. Health IT complicatedness increases weaknesses in technical, process, and people controls. Business leaders should thus invest in strengthening all three types of controls when they take on more health IT complicatedness.

In multi-unit organizations made up of autonomously managed units, each with its own disparate IT systems and governance arrangements, cross-unit data exchange is a major problem and a key source of breach risks. EWDAPs offer a useful workaround for addressing this problem. However, EWDAPs also introduce complexities to multi-unit organizations by establishing interlinkages and dependencies among the disparate IT systems and governance arrangements of the autonomously managed units. The resulting IT and governance complexities can disrupt technical and people controls. Business leaders investing in EWDAPs should therefore strengthen the technical and people controls with alternative ways (e.g., cybersecurity policies, cybersecurity training, etc.).

## Limitations and Future Research

One limitation of the study is its focus on a single industry. Future research is required to test if the theory and findings could apply to multi-unit organizations in other industries. Another limitation is that this study focuses on hospitals as the central actors in an MHS. Other actors in an MHS, such as nursing homes, senior living homes, and physician networks, could also be major sources of control weaknesses and cybersecurity breaches. Due to data limitations, we were not able to study such actors in detail. This limitation provides future research opportunities on how different members of an MHS might affect the system’s control effectiveness and cybersecurity breaches. Lastly, the study focused on MHS governance arrangements with third-party business partners such as physician groups and insurance companies. We did not theorize about MHSs’ connections to patients and care providers at home. The COVID-19 pandemic has forced MHSs to establish telehealth links to patients and caregivers working from home. Those links can further increase the complicatedness and the complexity of MHSs. Cybersecurity breach implications of individual telehealth links are also a promising future research direction.

## Appendix B

## Development and Validation of Hospital Data Breach Coding Guidelines

We reviewed both the academic and practitioner literature on IT controls and data security controls to develop guidelines for coding hospital cybersecurity breaches (IIA, 2008; IIA, 2009; IIA, 2012; ITGI, 2006; ITGI, 2007; Li et al., 2012; Masli et al., 2016). To validate the coding guidelines we first trained two independent coders using test samples of non-hospital cybersecurity breaches listed in the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC) website. We assessed intercoder agreement rates and revised the coding guidelines based on lessons learned in each iteration of coding until agreement rates met or exceeded the recommended threshold of 90%. In the first test sample (50 cybersecurity breaches), the intercoder agreement rate ranged from 82% to 88% across the coded variables. In the next test sample of an additional 50 cybersecurity breaches, agreement rates ranged from 88% to 93%. In the third test sample of an additional 50 cybersecurity breaches, agreement rates ranged from 90% to 95%.

We then provided the two coders with the sample of hospital cybersecurity breaches. We also provided them with detailed breach descriptions obtained from HHS, PRC, hospitals’ own announcements, news coverage of the breaches, victim notification letters, court filings, etc. The intercoder agreement rates in the hospital breach sample ranged from 90% to 98% and averaged 95%. All disagreements were jointly discussed and resolved between the two coders. The guidelines for the coding of technical control weaknesses, process control weaknesses, and people control weaknesses are listed in Table B1. If any member hospital of an MHS had a given type of control weakness, we coded the MHS as having that type of control weakness

Coders also classified the types of cybersecurity breaches using the following categories: hacking, insider, third party, theft or loss, and unintended breach. Hacking was set to 1 if an external attacker gained unauthorized access to patient data, 0 otherwise. Insider was set to 1 if an employee of the hospital misused patient data maliciously in unauthorized ways, 0 otherwise. Third party was set to 1 if the breach of a member hospital’s patient data occurred in the custody of a third-party business partner of the hospital, 0 otherwise. Theft or loss was set to 1 if the patient data was leaked to unauthorized parties due to theft or loss of tangible, physical media such as electronic devices (e.g., laptop, desktop, server, flash drive, etc.); or nonelectronic media (e.g., paper files, X-rays, storage and trash bins, etc.), 0 otherwise. Unintended breach was set to 1 if employees of a member hospital accidentally or unintentionally made patient data available to the public or to unauthorized parties, 0 otherwise.

## Antecedents and Mitigations of Cybersecurity Breaches (2SLS-Second Stage)

2SLS requires IVs that are not determined by the outcome (e.g., cybersecurity breaches) but are correlated with endogenous variables (e.g., complicatedness and EWDAP). Previous healthcare studies demonstrated that hospitals’ strategic decisions are influenced by their rivals in local markets. Building on these institutional or learning effects demonstrated by the previous studies, for our potentially endogenous independent variables (i.e., an MHS’s complicatedness in service, health IT, and governance; and EWDAP), IVs were computed by averaging the complicatedness levels of rival MHSs competing with a focal MHS in the same healthcare market (MK), known as HSAs: i.e., MK SVC, MK HIT, and MK Gov Complicate. Likewise, the IV for EWDAP (MK EWDAP) is the average EWDAP adoption/maturity level of rival MHSs in the same HSAs. The summary statistics of the IVs are provided in Table E1.

Using these IVs, we conduct 2SLS models including weak instrument and Durbin–Wu–Hausman (DWH) tests. First, weak instrument tests demonstrate the validity of our IVs. As described in Table E2, the Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F statistics are above the maximal Stock-Yogo critical values of 16.38, suggesting that all our IVs are relevant (Stock and Yogo 2005; Stock et al. 2002). Second, DWH tests identify what variables are actually endogenous. As shown in Table E2, the results indicate that the null hypothesis of exogeneity is rejected for Gov Complicate (Dubin $\chi ^ { 2 } = 4 . 3 5 7$ and Wu-Hausman F statistics =4.326 at p < 0.05), suggesting that Gov Complicate is endogenous. However, the tests fail to reject the null hypothesis for the other variables.
