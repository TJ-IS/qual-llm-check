# Information Technology Investment Strategies Under Declining Technology Cost

DIDEM DEMIRHAN, VARGHESE S. JACOB, AND SRINIVASAN RAGHUNATHAN

ABSTRACT: Investments in information technology (IT) have become crucial for firms to improve the quality of their products and services. Typically, IT cost for the same performance level declines over time. In a competitive market, a decline in IT cost over time provides a cost advantage to the later entrant, making the early entrant’s investment decision problem challenging. In this paper, we study the problem of strategic IT investments in the declining cost scenario using a sequential duopoly model. Our results show that declining IT cost intensifies or relaxes competition between firms depending on whether they are serving quality- or price-sensitive markets. In both cases, the average price per unit quality decreases when the IT cost declines,

which benefits consumers. We also show that if the first entrant is uncertain about the extent of its cost disadvantage, the first entrant overinvests (underinvests) in a pricesensitive (quality-sensitive) market as the degree of uncertainty increases.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cost advantage, declining IT cost, economic analysis, game theory, IT investment, IT strategy.

FIRMS IN INFORMATION-INTENSIVE INDUSTRIES such as communications and financial services are increasingly recognizing the importance of information technology (IT) [42, 70]. Almost two-thirds of the IT budget in organizations is spent on IT infrastructure that consists of computer and communications hardware and software [57, 67]. Investment in IT infrastructure has a direct impact on the quality of the product offered by firms [52] in information-intensive industries. For instance, Internet service providers (ISPs) have to invest substantially more in IT infrastructure if they plan to offer their customers a digital subscriber line (DSL), a higher-quality connection, compared to dial-up, a lower-quality connection. Even within dial-up ISPs, connection quality varies in dimensions such as initial modem speed, average time to log on, average download time, and evening and business hour call failure rate, depending on the capacity and capability of the ISP’s IT infrastructure that includes modems, media bandwidth, Ethernet switches, and remote servers [65].

The distinguishing characteristics of IT are the rapid price declines and dramatic performance improvements over time. Computing power for the same cost has been doubling and the cost for the same power has been declining by half every 18 months, an observation known as Moore’s Law [24]. The performance of microprocessors has improved over 25,000 times since their introduction 30 years ago [43]. Similar trends are observed in the communications technologies. Process improvements in fiber fabrication and advances in wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) have led to increased throughput capacity and lower cost [61]. The decline in price and improvement in quality over time is not restricted to hardware alone. Liebowitz and Margolis [46] document a similar trend in software when price is adjusted for quality (or functionality offered by the software).

The rapid pace of development in IT poses significant problems to firms that heavily invest in IT to improve the quality of their products and services. In a competitive market, steep declines in IT cost result in a lower cost of investment (for offering a product with the same quality) for firms that enter the market later. The technology literature offers several anecdotal examples as well as empirical data about the cost advantage of the late entrants [12, 13]. For instance, in the CAT scanner market, the later entrant, GE, exploited its lower manufacturing cost to displace the first entrant, Electrical Musical Industries (EMI) [66]. Similarly, in the DSL market, SBC Communications—a late entrant to the market—took advantage of as much as 40 percent price declines in DSL equipment in 2001 and lower cost of DSL installation [10]. The competition from SBC hurt the start-up DSL company, Covad Communications Group Inc. [16]. In 2004, Covad has less than 10 percent of the DSL market despite its leading position before the competition from SBC [62].

The examples from the DSL and CAT scanner market suggest that a firm that enters a market later with a cost advantage can be a threat to incumbents. This makes the investment decision a challenging problem for the firm that enters the market first. One of the questions that the first entrant faces is, when faced with a potential future competitor with a lower cost, whether it should compete aggressively (increase its investment) or defensively (reduce its investment) in response to a decline in IT cost. Related to this is the question of whether a decline in IT cost benefits or hurts the early entrant and the late entrant. Because firms’ investment strategies affect the quality of products they offer, a decline in IT cost also affects consumers. An important question from the perspective of consumers is whether a decline in IT cost increases or decreases the quality of products offered and the price per unit quality they pay. Finally, we are also interested in how the firms’ strategies change when they cannot estimate the future decline in IT cost accurately.

To answer these questions, we develop and analyze a game-theoretic model of a duopoly with sequential entry. Our model and results are applicable to situations where firms invest in IT infrastructure to improve the quality of their products or services. Our model assumes that investments are irrevocable onetime decisions, and the differences in cost structures between firms depend only on the timing of these decisions. This assumption is consistent with the commonly accepted view that IT infrastructure investments are long-term commitments [14]. The assumption regarding the onetime nature of investments restricts the applicability of our model and results to specific IT investment scenarios. Although both IT hardware and software exhibit the declining cost phenomenon, as we indicated earlier, our model is more appropriate when the required IT investment is primarily for hardware because of two reasons. First, cost for the same piece of hardware declines over time. However, the cost of the same piece of software may not change much over time, but newer versions may offer more features for the same price. Second, adopting a new hardware typically requires abandoning old hardware and investing in new hardware. However, software can be upgraded. Consequently, neither the pioneer nor the follower firms may have a cost advantage over the other with respect to investment in software. Our research particularly studies the problem faced by firms that adopt IT rather than firms, such as IT vendors, that produce (introduce) IT. Consequently, product design and introduction-related issues, such as network externalities [19, 32, 39] and technological compatibility [38, 39, 48], are excluded from our model.

Our research differs from the earlier related work in that firms, with potentially different cost structures, choose investment decisions sequentially in our model as opposed to simultaneously, as assumed by earlier studies on quality and price competition [3, 40, 50]. Sequential investment is required in our model because our goal is to analyze the effect of a decline in IT cost on firms’ relative investment strategies. Moreover, we are unaware of any study that explicitly studies the effect of the extent of decline in IT cost over time on firms’ investment strategies. By modeling the extent of decline in IT cost as a parameter, we are able to study the sensitivity of firms’ strategies with respect to this parameter. The most significant result of our study relates to how the market type, whether the market is price-sensitive or quality-sensitive, affects the firms’ strategies as well as their profit in a declining IT cost environment.

## Related Research

IT HAS LONG BEEN CONSIDERED a critical factor that creates sustained competitive advantage for firms [5, 55]. Researchers in the information systems (IS), industrial organization, and management areas have addressed different aspects of IT investments. One major stream of the IS literature has examined the impact of IT investments on organizational performance and productivity [35, 51, 59] by analyzing empirical data collected at the economy, industry, firm, and application levels, and has shown mixed results, giving life to the term productivity paradox [15]. This research stream focuses on postinvestment results as opposed to firms’ investment decisions. In an attempt to understand these mixed empirical findings, Thatcher and Oliver [63] developed an analytical model by formalizing the effect of IT investments on production efficiency, product quality, and productivity. In particular, Thatcher and Oliver found that the impact of technology investments on firm productivity depends on the type of technology implemented, size of the market, and extent of the fixed costs of the firm. Another stream of research in IS has focused on real options pricing models to analyze technology investment decisions [7, 8, 9, 29, 41]. However, these models do not consider competition even though Clemons [22] reported that IT investment decisions are influenced by possible actions by competitors.

A few studies in the IS area have analyzed firms’ IT investment decisions in a competitive setting. These studies considered two different settings. In the first setting, firms were assumed to make their investment decisions simultaneously [3, 40, 50]. Kim et al. [40] found that lower technology prices lead to higher quality together with higher prices. Banker et al. [3] analyzed investments in quality by n firms that invest simultaneously. They showed how the industry quality level is affected by the competitive intensity measured by number of firms and the degree of substitutability between competing products. In the simultaneous quality and price competition literature, the work of Quan et al. [53] is particularly relevant to our research. They studied the effects of IT investments on firm performance and productivity. Both Kim et al. [40] and Quan et al. [53] categorized the market as price- and quality-sensitive and found that the effects of IT investment were contingent on market sensitivities. We also find that firms’ strategies depend on whether the market is price- or qualitysensitive. However, the literature that considered simultaneous investment decisions by firms did not address the impact of declining IT cost over time, a key characteristic of our problem context.

In the second setting, firms were assumed to make investment decisions sequentially. The main focus of these studies [4, 37, 49] was predation and market entry deterrence aspects of competition. Most of the studies that used sequential competition models considered identical cost structures for the firms in the market. Moorthy [49] developed a model of two identical firms competing on quality and price. He showed that each firm’s equilibrium strategy was to differentiate its product from that of its competitor. The firm that enters the market first could defend itself from later entrants by preempting the most desirable product position. Jones and Mendelson [37] showed that the value of being first in the information goods market was substantially higher compared to entering later. The stream of research most relevant to ours in quality and price competition context includes the studies of Barua et al. [6], Bohlmann et al. [11], and Demirhan et al. [27], which considered different cost structures for the competing firms. Barua et al. [6] studied the influence of the firms’ relative IT efficiency on their strategic IT investments that can improve service quality. They found that the IT-inefficient firm prefers to be a later entrant into the market, whereas the investment decision of the IT-efficient firm depends on the difference in IT cost structures and on the degree of substitutability between the services of the two firms. Our model is similar to the model of Barua et al. [6], with one crucial difference. Their model assumes that price is exogenous and is independent of quality. Consumers in the Barua et al. model base their decision to choose the product only on the quality since the service is delivered free, whereas, we consider in our model, both price and quality as factors affecting the demand for the product. Our research also relates to the recent work of Bohlmann et al. [11], who analyzed the impact of vintage effects on firm performance and showed that the lower costs of late entrants could lead to higher market shares for them and lower market shares for the early entrants.

The industrial organization literature on strategic investment in technology addressed technology introduction or technology adoption issues. Technology introduction literature (e.g., [1, 28, 30, 32, 38, 39]) focuses on product introduction and pricing strategies for technology vendors. These studies investigate the network externalities and technology compatibility aspects of the competition, whereas technology adoption literature (e.g., [3, 6, 11, 34, 40, 47, 53]) deals with the strategic investment decisions on available technologies. Since our paper focuses on adoption of the existing technologies by firms for the purpose of supporting and improving their businesses to offer high-quality products or services, technology adoption research within the context of strategic investment is more relevant.

The management literature has focused on first-mover advantages and disadvantages when firms enter a market sequentially. First-mover advantages can arise from technological leadership, preemption of scarce assets such as geographic location and product characteristic space, and buyer switching costs [44]. Marketing, economics, and IS researchers have constructed theoretical models relating to first-mover advantages [17, 26, 58, 60]. Lieberman and Montgomery [45] have identified several sources of first-mover disadvantages such as the late entrant’s ability to free ride on first-mover investments, resolution of technological and market uncertainty favoring the late movers, technological discontinuities, and pioneer’s difficulty in adapting to environmental changes. Most of these papers provided empirical evidence of first-mover (dis)advantages using market share and sales data. This stream of work is related to ours as we consider the decline in IT cost as a source of first-mover disadvantage and analyze how cost disadvantage affects IT investment decisions of the competing firms.

The differences between our research and that of the prior literature can be summarized as follows. Our focus in this paper is on the IT investment decision a firm faces when it knows that a competitor that enjoys a lower cost may enter the market in the future. Unlike the technology introduction literature, we assume that the firms do not have control of the technology trajectory and are simply users of the available technologies. Also, unlike the earlier research (empirical and theoretical), our focus is on the impact of the extent of decline in cost on strategic investments. Hence, the issues previously described using the DSL and scanner examples required us to develop a model with the following characteristics: the customers base their decision to choose a product on both its price and quality—that is, decision on investment in quality cannot be made in isolation of price. In other words, in our model, investments depend on both quality and price that can be charged. Furthermore, quality level chosen by the firm determines the investment required as well as the demand faced by the firm. And, finally, we assume that the firms set their investment decisions at different times, rather than at the same time, and hence they have different cost structures.

## The Basic Model

OUR MODEL IS BASED ON THE WELL-KNOWN vertical differentiation model of R&D investment competition [25]. In this model, firms differentiate their products from those of their rivals on quality and price, and these are the only variables of interest to consumers. Table 1 provides a list of notations used in our basic model and analysis. We make the following assumptions in our model.

1. We consider a duopoly for analytical tractability—labeled Firm 1 and Firm 2. However, it is possible to generalize the model to n > 2 firms. The model with n firms results in more complex expressions. Since the complexity of expressions precludes gaining any insight into the problem, we have stayed with a duopoly model for the analysis. However, we do discuss models with n firms in the Conclusion.

2. Each firm offers one product and chooses a price level p and a quality level k of its product. Both price and quality assume nonnegative values. The product quality determines the level of IT investment I required. A higher-quality product will require a higher level of investment. The IT investments are assumed to be primarily in IT hardware, which exhibits a declining cost over time. We could incorporate investments, both IT and non-IT, that do not affect product quality into our model without any change to the results. Consequently, we normalize such investments to zero.

3. Because our goal is to understand how a decline in the cost of IT over time affects the investment decisions, we assume that the firms enter the market sequentially. This assumption is consistent with empirical observations that few technology innovations are adopted instantaneously and simultaneously since firms may expect a decrease in the cost or uncertainty about acceptance of technology over time [64]. We utilize a three-stage sequential entry game as in Barua et al. [6], Bohlmann et al. [11], and Tyagi [66] for analytical tractability. Without loss of generality, we assume that Firm 1 enters the market first:

As the assumed sequence of moves indicates, Firm 1 allows and anticipates entry by Firm 2. Since our goal is to understand the potential ability of a late entrant enjoying declining IT cost to overtake the pioneer, we do not explicitly model Firm 1’s period of monopoly profit as an entry deterrence mechanism, which is common in sequential games [11, 64]. Monopoly profit before competition is negligible, which is especially reasonable in IT-intensive industries where new product introduction is rapid because of rapid advances in technology.

The sequence that price is set after quality is consistent with that of other models of quality and price competition [4, 11]. The rationale for this sequence is that quality (investment) level is a strategic decision that cannot be changed as easily as prices.

In order to analyze the effect of declining IT costs, we assume that either (i) Firm 2 also uses the same technology as Firm 1, but Firm 2 obtains it at a lower cost, or (ii) Firm 2 uses a new technology, but Firm 1 does not adopt the new IT in stage (ii). The latter assumption holds when IT investments are long-term commitments to specific technologies [14], or adoption of new technology is very costly for the early entrant because of the cost and organizational commitment made to the technology that was available when it made its decision to enter the market. This assumption is also consistent with the recommendation of many technology analysts to put off buying new equipment until the older network equipment is creating a bottleneck [56]. Lieberman and Montgomery [44] also observed that firms might prefer harvesting the initial investments rather than replacing these investments radically.

5. Following prior research in this area [4, 24, 39], we assume that the aggregate (market) demand functions are linear in price and quality:

$$
q _ {i} = a - p _ {i} + b p _ {j} + k _ {i} - c k _ {j} \mathrm{for} i, j \in \{1, 2 \} \mathrm{and} i \neq j,\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { i }$ is the price charged by firm $i , k _ { i }$ is the quality level of firm i’s product, $p _ { j }$ is the price charged by competitor firm $j , k _ { j }$ is the quality level of competitor firm j’s product, and $a > 0$ is the demand intercept. If firm i reduces its price or improves its product quality, ceteris paribus, demand for its product increases and the demand for the product from firm j decreases. The parameters $0 <$ $b < 1$ and $0 < c < 1$ represent the price and quality cross-sensitivity of demands, respectively. We assume that the demand for a firm is more responsive to its own price and quality levels than those of the competitor. This condition is necessary to show that both firms should experience a decrease in sales if they all raise their prices by \$1 or decrease their quality by one unit [63]. Therefore, b and c are less than one.

6. A firm’s IT investment level I is determined by the quality of its product. The cost represents the investment made by a firm in its IT infrastructure to offer its product. We assume standard quadratic functions for the cost of investment. This assumption guarantees that the quality levels offered by firms are bounded.<sup>1</sup> It also models the declining return from IT investments in improving quality. The total cost of investment on quality improvement functions for Firm 1 and Firm 2 are given as

$$
I _ {1} = \frac {k _ {1} ^ {2}}{2}\tag{2}
$$

$$
I _ {2} = \delta \frac {k _ {2} ^ {2}}{2}.\tag{3}
$$

The coefficient $\delta > 0$ captures the difference in cost between firms. $\delta < ( > )$ 1 implies that Firm 2 has a cost (dis)advantage over Firm 1. In this paper, we assume $\delta < 1$ to model the declining cost of IT. In subsequent sections, we will specify additional restrictions on $\delta$ to ensure that both firms do enter the market. The above cost functions assume, for simplicity, that the marginal cost of production is zero for each firm. This is especially true for the digital products with zero or negligible marginal cost of production. However, our analysis holds for any marginal production cost that is independent of product quality level and that is not influenced by IT investment that is purely for quality improvement. We discuss relaxing this assumption in the Conclusion.

In our basic model, the parameters are deterministic and are common knowledge. Later, we extend this model by introducing uncertainty of the first firm about δ.

## Analysis of the Basic Model

WE LOOK FOR THE SUBGAME PERFECT Nash equilibrium of the game and obtain the results by backward induction. That is, we derive the price equilibrium followed by the quality equilibrium.

In the pricing game, both firms maximize their profit functions simultaneously given the quality levels. Solving fo $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { p } _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ simultaneously from the first-order conditions for the equilibrium prices, we obtain the following:

$$
p _ {i} ^ {*} \left(k _ {i}, k _ {j}\right) = \frac {a (2 + b) + (2 - b c) k _ {i} + (b - 2 c) k _ {j}}{4 - b ^ {2}} \text {   for   } i, j \in \{1, 2 \} \text {   and   } i \neq j.\tag{4}
$$

The price charged by a firm increases or decreases with the quality level of its competitor depending on whether $b < 2 c$ or $b \geq 2 c ,$ respectively. Substituting ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * }$ and ${ p _ { 2 } } ^ { * }$ into Equation (1), we get the following:

$$
q _ {i} ^ {*} \left(k _ {i}, k _ {j}\right) = \frac {a (2 + b) + (2 - b c) k _ {i} + (b - 2 c) k _ {j}}{4 - b ^ {2}} \text {   for   } i, j \in \{1, 2 \} \text {   and   } i \neq j.\tag{5}
$$

Note that in the equilibrium, a firm will increase its price by b/2 percent for a 1 percent increase in the competitor price and will increase its quality by c percent for a 1 percent increase in the competitor product’s quality. As we discuss in the next section, the relative sensitivity to change in price and quality defines the market type.

Firm 1 will set its quality by anticipating Firm 2’s optimal quality. In other words, Firm 1 determines $k _ { 2 }$ as a function of $k _ { 1 }$ by maximizing $\Pi _ { 2 } ( k _ { 1 } , k _ { 2 } )$ with respect to $k _ { 2 }$ and then maximizing its own profit with respect to $k _ { 1 } .$ . Consequently, the equilibrium quality levels, $k _ { 1 } ^ { * }$ and $k _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ , are derived as

$$
\begin{array}{l} k _ {1} ^ {*} = \left[ 2 (b c - 2) (2 a (1 + c) (b c - 2) - a (b - 2) (b + 2) ^ {2} \delta) (2 - 2 c ^ {2} + (b ^ {2} - 4) \delta) \right] / \\ \left[ 4 (b c - 2) ^ {2} ((((b c - 2) ^ {2} - 2 (c ^ {2} - 1) ^ {2}) - (b ^ {2} - 4) (b ^ {2} - 2 (1 + c ^ {2})) \delta) \right. \\ \left. + (b ^ {2} - 4) ^ {2} ((b ^ {2} - 4) ^ {2} - 2 (b c - 2) ^ {2}) \delta^ {2} \right] \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

$$
k _ {2} ^ {*} = \frac {2 (2 - b c) [ a (2 + b) + (b - 2 c) k _ {1} ^ {*} ]}{- 2 (2 - b c) ^ {2} + (4 - b ^ {2}) ^ {2} \delta}.\tag{7}
$$

For a pure strategy equilibrium to exist, we require that the reaction functions for the two firms intersect at only one point [64] and obtain the following sufficient conditions.

$$
\left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \Pi_ {2}}{\partial k _ {2} ^ {2}} \right| \geq \left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \Pi_ {2}}{\partial k _ {1} \partial k _ {2}} \right|
$$

$$
\Rightarrow \delta \geq \frac {2 (2 - b c) (1 + c)}{(2 - b) (2 + b) ^ {2}} \text {   when   } b <   2 c\tag{8}
$$

$$
\Rightarrow \delta \geq \frac {2 (2 - b c) (1 - c)}{(2 - b) ^ {2} (2 + b)} \text {   when   } b \geq 2 c.\tag{9}
$$

We can show that for δ less than the above lower bound, the market breaks down because Firm 1 does not enter the market at all. As a result, in our analysis, we consider only δ values that satisfy the above conditions.

## Market Sensitivity

WE ARE PRIMARILY INTERESTED IN the impact of δ on firms’ investment (or quality) and profit levels. Our analysis reveals that firms respond differently to a decline in IT cost depending on whether $b < 2 c \thinspace { \mathrm { o r } } b \geq 2 c$ . Following Kim et al. [40], we denote the market in which $b < 2 c$ as the quality-sensitive market and the market in which $b \geq$ $2 c$ as the price-sensitive market. The relative sensitivity of firms’ reactions to competitor’s price and quality changes distinguishes the two market types. If firms strategies differ significantly for each of these markets, it is important to understand how firms compete in each of these markets. In the following subsections, we present results for each of these markets separately. For each of these markets, we primarily discuss the impact of δ on firms as well as consumers.

## Price-Sensitive Market

In a price-sensitive market $( b \geq 2 c )$ , the competitor’s price affects the demand for a firm’s product significantly more than the competitor’s quality. In this type of market, consumers are more sensitive to price than quality when they evaluate competing products. The discount retailing industry reflects such behavior [31]. Since consumers value price more than quality in this type of market, it is not clear whether firms still invest heavily in IT to improve the quality of their services. The following proposition presents the results of the analysis of the impact of a decline in IT cost on firms investment strategies and profits in a price-sensitive market.

P1: In a price-sensitive market, the quality and profit of both firms are higher when the decline in IT cost is higher.

The proof is omitted.

P1 shows that, in a price-sensitive market, as the cost of IT declines, the profits of both firms increase. Anticipating a positive effect of the declining IT cost for a future entrant, Firm 1 assumes an aggressive position and offers a high-quality product to be able to compete. This finding is consistent with the traditional explanation—that is, firms attempt to protect their market shares in a competitive market. A decline in IT cost induces Firm 2 to also invest more in quality. Thus, both firms are investing in higher quality, and, as seen from Equation (5), this results in higher demand for both firms and, consequently, higher total industry demand. Our results for the price-sensitive market appear to support anecdotal observations and comments from industry experts regarding the pattern of IT investments in a price-sensitive industry such as discount retailers. Experts frequently observe that IT investment by a firm induces similar investments by the competitors in this sector to improve product quality [31].

Given the above investment behaviors, we can expect higher profits for both firms in a price-sensitive market. This result is different from the finding of an empirical study by Bohlmann et al. [11], which showed that the pioneer’s profit decreases, whereas late entrant’s profit increases with a cost advantage for the late entrant. However, Bohlmann et al. [11] did not take into account the market type in their analysis. Our results suggest that if the market is price-sensitive, the pioneer also obtains a higher profit due to its aggressive competitive behavior when a decline in IT cost is anticipated.

P1 states that both firms offer higher-quality products when the cost of IT decreases over time. However, the higher quality comes with a higher price tag. So, from the consumers’ perspective, it is not clear whether a decline in IT cost is beneficial. In order to gain some insights into the effect of declining IT cost on consumers, we use average of price to quality ratios for both firms as a measure of average price per unit quality in the market. That is, we define average price per unit quality, $p / k ,$ as

$$
\overline {{p / k}} = \left(\frac {p _ {1}}{k _ {1}} + \frac {p _ {2}}{k _ {2}}\right) / 2.
$$

A lower value for $\overline { { p / k } }$ is better from the consumers’ perspective.

P2: In a price-sensitive market, the price paid per unit quality offered decreases as IT cost declines.

The above result shows that in a price-sensitive market, consumers, on average, are better off when the cost of IT declines. Recall from P1 that both the pioneer and the late entrant offer higher-quality products with a higher decline in IT cost. While consumers get higher-quality products as a result of a decline in IT costs, the prices do not increase as much as the quality. The higher intensity of price competition compared to the quality competition in the price-sensitive market limits the extent of price increase, so that the consumers get higher-quality products per unit price.

## Quality-Sensitive Market

In a quality-sensitive market (b < 2c), firms’ demands are more sensitive to changes in competitor’s quality than price. The ISP market is an example of a quality-sensitive market as reported by various surveys [18, 68, 69]. The findings from these surveys suggest that ISP quality measured using connection availability, uptime percentage, and network performance are the top criterion when consumers choose an ISP. As Equation (5) shows, the demand for a firm’s product decreases when the competitor’s quality increases in a quality-sensitive market. Thus, unlike the price-sensitive market in which both firms obtain higher profits with higher quality levels offered, the firm that offers a higher-quality product hurts the firm that offers the lower-quality product in a quality-sensitive market. Consequently, quality plays a significant role in how firms compete in a quality-sensitive market.

When firms compete on quality, a decrease in IT cost will favor Firm 2 significantly because Firm 2 will be able to take advantage of its lower cost and offer a higherquality product for the same cost. A key issue here is how Firm 1 competes with Firm 2 on quality. We are also interested in the effect of such competition on Firm 1’s profit. The following proposition presents the effect of a decline in IT cost on Firm 1’s profit.

P3: In a quality-sensitive market, Firm 1’s profit decreases as IT cost declines, and the decrease in Firm 1’s profit is higher for a higher decline in IT cost.

P3 shows that Firm 1 is hurt by a decline in IT cost in a quality-sensitive market. The result can be attributed to the quality competition between firms. A decline in IT cost allows Firm 2 to aggressively compete with Firm 1, which hurts Firm 1’s demand and profit. This result is consistent with Bohlmann et al. [11], who finds that in a market in which the early entrant has a cost disadvantage, the early entrant’s profit decreases as consumer valuation of quality increases. These competition dynamics are observed in the DSL market where the quality of the Internet access services offered by the competitors is cited as the top reason for the consumers to switch [69]. SBC, a late entrant with superior quality and lower costs from advanced technology, hurt the early entrant Covad Communications [36, 62].

The effect of a decline in IT cost on Firm 2’s profit as well as the quality levels is unclear. While Firm 2 has a lower cost of investment when IT cost declines, Firm 2 is still a follower to Firm 1. Since Firm 1 sets its strategy in anticipation of a decline in IT cost first, Firm 2 may not be able to exploit the cost advantage fully. Thus, one can argue that the cost advantage might be mitigated by the disadvantage of being a follower. The interplay of declining IT cost and order of entry into the market raises the interesting questions of how Firm 2 competes with Firm 1 with respect to quality and how its profit is affected by a decline in IT cost. Our analysis reveals that Firm 2’s profit as well as both firms’ investment strategies in response to a decline in IT cost is mixed. We can show the following results.

P4: In a quality-sensitive market: (a) when IT cost declines, if Firm 1 reduces its quality, then Firm 2 increases its quality and realizes higher profit, and (b) there exists a cutoff value for decline in IT cost, δ<sup>c</sup>, such that for $\delta < ( > ) \delta ^ { \mathrm { c } }$ , Firm 2’s quality and profit are higher (lower) than those of Firm 1.

P4 highlights the nature of competition between Firm 1 and Firm 2 when they compete on quality. If Firm 1 reduces its investment in response to a decline in IT cost, then Firm 2 increases its investment to provide a higher-quality product and earns a higher profit. Lower IT cost as well as a reduction in quality by Firm 1 in response to the decline in IT cost helps Firm 2. Our findings related to quality-sensitive market support the results of Foster [33], who showed that late entrants enjoy an “attacker’s advantage” over the incumbent when they can exploit the new technologies.

P4b provides insights into the trade-off between the first-mover advantage and the cost disadvantage of Firm 1. If Firm 1 does not suffer from any cost disadvantage— that is, $\delta = 1$ —our result shows that Firm 1 invests more and offers a higher-quality level than Firm 2. It also enjoys higher profits than Firm 2. However, as Firm 2’s cost advantage increases so that δ becomes less than δ<sup>c</sup>, Firm 1 loses the first-mover advantage—that is, it offers a lower quality level than Firm 2. Firm 1 acts as the quality leader or follower depending on whether $\delta > \delta ^ { c }$ or $\delta < \delta ^ { c }$ . Christensen [20] and Christensen and Bower [21] draw a similar conclusion, stating that disruptive technological changes (which might lead to high cost decline, i.e., $\delta < \delta ^ { c } )$ results in victory for entrants over established companies in the market.

In a quality-sensitive market, Firm 1 may pursue a defensive or aggressive investment strategy when faced with a decline in IT cost. If it follows a defensive strategy and produces a lower-quality product, then it is hurt by a decline in IT cost because it allows Firm 2 to be the quality leader. If it follows an aggressive strategy and produces a higher-quality product, then it is also hurt, because competing head-on with the lower cost rival requires too much investment. Whether Firm 1 reduces or increases its investment in response to a decline in IT cost depends on the relative sensitivities of demand to price and quality. Since the analytical expression for the condition that determines Firm 1’s investment strategy with respect to a decline in IT cost is complex, we use graphical illustrations as detailed in the following paragraphs.

We describe results from two sets of quality-sensitive markets. In both sets of markets, quality cross-sensitivity, c, is equal to 0.5. The value of price sensitivity, b, is set to 0.01 and 0.99 to represent two extreme cases of a quality-sensitive market. When b is significantly smaller than 2c—that is, $b = 0 . 0 1$ and $c = 0 . 5$ —the degree of quality sensitivity is high relative to price sensitivity. When b is very close to 2c—that is, $b =$ 0.99 and $c = 0 . 5$ —the degree of quality sensitivity is low relative to price sensitivity. The ISP market, characterized as a quality-sensitive market, exhibits both of these behaviors. Surveys [18, 68, 69] suggest that service quality is the top criteria for consumers when they choose an ISP. However, depending on whether the product is a dial-up service or a DSL service, the degree of quality sensitivity relative to price sensitivity varies. The surveys reveal that the customers of high-speed DSL providers and cable modem providers are more willing to pay for reliability and performance compared to the customers of dial-up ISPs. To this end, high-speed ISPs reflect the high quality-sensitive market, whereas dial-up ISPs reflect the low quality-sensitive market. Figures 1 through 8 show the numerical results for the valid ranges of decline in IT cost for these two different quality-sensitive markets.

As seen in Figures 1 and 2, Firm 1’s quality level $k _ { 1 }$ increases with a decline in IT cost when the quality sensitivity relative to price sensitivity is high and decreases with a decline in IT cost when quality sensitivity relative to price sensitivity is low. On the other hand, Figures 3 and 4 show that Firm 2’s behavior is opposite that of Firm 1. We explain the above findings as follows. The firm with a lower quality stands to lose more demand, revenue, and, consequently, profit in a market in which the quality sensitivity is high, as compared to a market in which the quality sensitivity is low. To this end, anticipation of high-quality products from Firm 2 causes Firm 1 to increase its quality level in an attempt to reduce the difference in quality levels between the products. Thus, Firm 1 responds to a decline in IT cost by investing heavily. However, as Figure 5 shows, this overinvestment leads to lower profit for Firm 1. This rationale might explain what happened to start-up DSL providers [36, 62] compared to SBC, which was able to take advantage of steep cost declines in the highly quality-sensitive high-speed ISP market.

The higher quality level offered by Firm 1, in turn, reduces the demand that can be realized by Firm 2. Faced with a smaller demand, Firm 2 reduces its investment level and its product quality, which, in turn, result in a decrease in its profit (Figure 7). This is a scenario where a decline in IT cost intensifies quality competition between firms and hurts both firms.

When the degree of quality sensitivity is low, the incentive for Firm 1 to reduce the difference in quality levels decreases because the increase in the demand and profit from a higher quality level is smaller. In this case, Firm 1 attempts to use price instead of quality to compete with Firm 2. Since both firms set prices simultaneously, neither enjoys any special advantage in the price competition. Thus, Firm 1 assumes a defensive position by decreasing its investment when faced with a decline in IT cost. As shown in Figures 5 through 8, whereas Firm 1 is always hurt by a decline in cost in a quality-sensitive market, Firm 2 benefits only when quality sensitivity relative to price sensitivity is low.

Firms in the low-speed ISP market, a market in which price sensitivity dominates quality sensitivity, exhibit behaviors consistent with the explanation given in the previous paragraph. AOL, an early entrant in the dial-up ISP market, launched a service that is cheaper than its regular dial-up service, but with fewer features, to compete with United Online, a late entrant, on prices for low-end quality services [2].

Next, we study the effect of declining IT cost on consumers in a quality-sensitive market. Again, we use average of price to quality ratios for both firms as a measure of average price per unit quality in the market.

## Impact of Uncertainty and Error in the Estimate of the Decline in IT Cost on Quality

IN THE PREVIOUS SECTION, WE ASSUMED that Firm 1 has perfect knowledge of Firm 2’s cost advantage. Accordingly, it can increase or decrease its investment depending on the nature of the market and the extent of cost advantage. Although it is reasonable to assume that Firm 1 will know that the IT cost will decline over time, Firm 1 may not know the extent of cost advantage with certainty. In this section, we incorporate firm 1’s uncertainty t about the decline in future cost when it chooses its investment level. Specifically, we consider the case in which, when the actual decline in cost is δ, Firm 1 believes that the decline is $\delta + t$ with probability 0.5 and $\delta - t$ with probability 0.5, where $\delta + t < 1$ and $\delta - t > 0 .$ <sup>2</sup> In other words, while Firm 1 believes that Firm 2 will enjoy a cost advantage of δ on average, Firm 1 can over- or underestimate the decline in cost. We choose this probability distribution to capture the feature that Firm 1 is equally likely to overestimate the cost decline as underestimate. Later in this section, we also analyze the cases when Firm 1 either underestimates or overestimates the cost decline. The parameter t measures the degree of uncertainty in Firm 1’s belief. As t increases, Firm 1 is more uncertain about the decline in cost. For example, Firm 1 has perfect knowledge about $\delta$ when $t = 0$ , and has maximum uncertainty about δ when $t = \delta$

Firm 1 now maximizes its expected profit given by

$$
E \left(\Pi_ {1}\right) = \frac {1}{2} \left[ \Pi_ {1} (\delta + t) + \Pi_ {1} (\delta - 1) \right].
$$

Our primary interest is in understanding how t affects the optimal quality levels and the profits of each firm in both price-sensitive and quality-sensitive markets. The results are summarized in the following proposition:

P6: In a price-sensitive market, the quality levels of both Firm 1 and Firm 2 increase, the profit of Firm 1 decreases and the profit of Firm 2 increases when uncertainty t increases.

Intuitively, an increase in uncertainty about the decline in IT cost implies that Firm 1 would end up with either a lower IT cost disadvantage or a higher IT cost disadvantage. When Firm 1 anticipates a lower IT cost disadvantage, Firm 1 relies on its firstmover advantages and invests less in quality in a price-sensitive market. As Firm 1 expects to end up with a bigger cost disadvantage, it assumes an aggressive position and offers a higher-quality product to compete on quality with a lower-cost future entrant. Overall, the impact of a decrease in low-cost disadvantage on investment strategies is outweighed by the impact of an increase in high-cost disadvantage. Therefore, a higher degree of uncertainty makes Firm 1 more aggressive in quality competition. Firm 1’s actual profit decreases because the firm invests more than what it would have had it known the decline in IT cost precisely.

Since Firm 2 observes the actual quality level of Firm 1 and the decline in IT cost, it can set its investment level that maximizes its profit. As seen in Firm 2’s quality reaction function (7), any increase in Firm 1’s quality due to the increase in uncertainty, on the other hand, forces Firm 2 to increase its investment to offer high-quality products so that the firm can compete with its competitor on quality.

As in the analysis of the basic model, we find that, in a quality-sensitive market, if Firm 1 is uncertain about the decline in cost, then its strategy depends on the relative intensity of price and quality competition. Thus, we have the next result.

P7: In a quality-sensitive market, when uncertainty increases, if Firm 1 decreases its investment with a decline in IT cost, then the quality and profit of Firm 1 decreases, and the quality and profit of Firm 2 increases.

When quality competition outweighs the price competition, the uncertainty about the cost decline has opposite effects on Firm 1 and Firm 2. The imperfect knowledge about future costs negatively (positively) affects Firm 1 (Firm 2), especially when Firm 1’s strategy is to decrease its investment with a decline in IT cost. The uncertainty reduces the competition in favor of Firm 2, further weakening Firm 1’s competition. A higher degree of uncertainty about the extent of decline in IT cost leads Firm 1 to pursue a less aggressive investment strategy and decrease its investment. A lowquality product from Firm 1 and a decline in IT cost benefit Firm 2. Therefore, while Firm 2 offers a higher-quality product, Firm 1 underinvests and offers a much lowerquality product. Increase in uncertainty that results in underinvestment causes profit for Firm 1 to decline. Firm 2’s profit, on the other hand, increases as the demand for its product increases with a high-quality product offering.

In summary, even though Firm 1’s strategy in response to uncertainty shifts depending on the nature of the market, uncertainty hurts Firm 1 but benefits Firm 2 irrespective of the nature of the market. In this sense, uncertainty on the part of Firm 1 provides an advantage to Firm 2 similar to that provided by cost decline.

## Effect of Overestimation and Underestimation of the Decline in IT Cost

Firm 1’s forecast of the future IT cost decline may lead to either overestimation or underestimation. Firm 1 will underestimate (overestimate) the decline in IT cost when it expects the decline to be lower (higher) than the actual decline. Firm 1’s forecast of the decline in IT cost can be expressed as δ + t, where t is the forecast error term. Allowing t to be either positive or negative enables us to model the cases of underestimation and overestimation, respectively. |t| represents the magnitude of the forecast error.

The results when Firm 1 incorrectly estimates the cost decline are summarized in the following proposition:

P8: In a price-sensitive market, if Firm 1 overestimates (underestimates) the decline in IT cost, then Firm 1 and Firm 2 invest more (less) when the magnitude of the error is higher.

The intuition behind Firm 1’s investment strategy can be explained through the positive effect of the decline in IT cost on product quality of firms in a price-sensitive market (P1). A higher decline in IT cost anticipated by Firm 1—that is, overestimation—will lead Firm 1 to overinvest because it will anticipate Firm 2 to increase its investment, which, in turn, will benefit Firm 1. A higher investment by Firm 1 induces Firm 2 to also increase its investment. The opposite is true for both Firm 1 and Firm 2 when the decline in IT cost is predicted to be lower than the actual decline— underestimation.

P9: In a quality-sensitive market, when Firm 1 decreases its investment in anticipation of a decline in IT cost, if Firm 1 overestimates (underestimates) the decline in IT cost, then Firm 1 invests less (more) and Firm 2 invests more (less) when the magnitude of the error is higher.

Firm 1’s focus is to minimize the expected loss of profit when a decline in IT cost negatively affects its investment. Hence, the strategy of loss minimization outlines the investment behavior of Firm 1 in the presence of unknown cost decline. A higher decline in IT cost forecasted by Firm 1—that is, overestimation—will lead Firm 1 to underinvest anticipating Firm 2 will offer high-quality products to increase its demand by taking advantage of a decline in IT cost, thus hurting Firm 1. As quality is the most crucial factor determining consumer’s product choice, the low-quality level of Firm 1 creates an incentive for Firm 2 to offer high-quality levels.

## Discussion

OUR MAIN INTEREST IN THIS PAPER IS TO EXPLORE the role of a decline in IT cost on firms’ IT investment strategies and performance. We derived important and interesting implications of a decline in IT cost on firms by analyzing a quantitative model of a duopoly market with sequential order of entry and differential cost structures. In particular, we showed that the impact of declining technology cost on IT investment strategies and firm performance is contingent upon the market sensitivity to product price and quality. Consequently, managers must take into consideration the market characteristics in terms of price and quality sensitivity when investing in IT under declining IT costs.

Our results highlight that firms should increase IT investments when they expect a decline in IT cost if consumers are more sensitive to price than quality when they make purchase decisions. Although prior research on the cost advantage of late entry [11] suggested that the pioneer’s profit decreases and the late entrant’s profit increases when the later entrant’s cost advantage increases, we found that the pioneer may also benefit from a cost advantage of late entrants if the market is price-sensitive. The discount retailing industry reflects such market behavior. IT investment by a firm induces similar investments by the competitors in this sector [31].

Unlike the price-sensitive market, if quality is more crucial for consumer product choice, the pioneer will be hurt by the cost advantage of late entry. In such a market, the pioneer’s strategy should be to minimize the expected loss of profit. According to Clemons [22], minimization of profit loss because of future competition is one of the factors considered by firms when they make investment decisions. Our results show that the pioneer’s strategy of loss minimization depends on the market sensitivity to quality relative to price. If market quality sensitivity is sufficiently high and the cost of IT is expected to decline, the first firm should minimize its loss by increasing its investment level from the base level when there is no decline in cost of IT. Such phenomenon can be observed in an investment banking sector, a quality-sensitive market. For instance, when Web services technology was in the initial stages of development and standards had not been finalized, JPMorgan, a pioneer of the real-world Web applications for end users in investment banking, heavily invested in Web services technologies to limit the possibility of future losses [23]. On the other hand, if market quality sensitivity is sufficiently low, the pioneer should minimize its loss by decreasing its IT investment. Dial-up ISPs that offer relatively inexpensive and lowspeed Internet access to less–quality-sensitive consumers demonstrate this competition dynamic. For instance, AOL, an early entrant in dial-up ISP, has launched a service that is cheaper than its regular dial-up service, but with fewer features, to compete with United Online, a late entrant, on prices for low-end quality services [2].

Our results also showed that consumers benefit from a decline in IT cost in both price-sensitive and quality-sensitive markets. Consumers get high-quality products with relatively low prices in the presence of declining IT cost. In discount retail chains, such as Wal-Mart and Target, which compete mostly on price, the benefits of intensive IT infrastructure investment accrue to consumers who enjoy low prices with relatively more convenient shopping [31].

As supported by previous literature that focused on uncertainty [54, 66], our results highlight that when the first mover has imperfect knowledge about its cost disadvantage over the later entrant, it is in a worse position than the later entrant. Our findings suggest that the first mover should acquire sophisticated and detailed information that can help to reduce the uncertainty about future cost.

## Conclusion

DECLINING COST OF IT OVER TIME poses significant challenges to competing firms that depend on IT investments to improve the quality of their products. In this paper, we analyzed the impact of a decline in IT cost on firms that enter the market at different times and hence have different cost structures. We showed that a decline in IT cost has different effects on firms depending on whether the market is price-sensitive or quality-sensitive. In a price-sensitive market, a decline in IT cost benefits both firms. Consumers also benefit because they pay a lower price per unit quality with an increasing decline in IT cost. In a quality-sensitive market, a decline in IT cost intensifies the competition between firms. A decline in IT cost always hurts the first entrant; however, the second entrant benefits from a decline in IT cost only when the first entrant assumes a defensive approach and reduces its investment in response to a decline in IT cost.

Although our analysis provides several important insights about the impact of a decline in IT cost on firms, our research has several limitations that can be addressed in the future. First, our model did not include optimal timing of introductions. If the second mover enters too soon, it may not have a significant cost advantage over the first mover. If it enters too late, it has to wait to realize its profit. An interesting extension of our research is to consider the timing of introductions explicitly.

Second, we assumed that variable cost is constant and normalized to zero. However, one might reasonably argue that many products have positive variable costs of production, and that high-quality products are produced at a higher variable cost than the low-quality products [50]. Furthermore, IT advances may reduce the variable costs as well as fixed costs of production. Hence, a more general model would include the effect of decline in IT cost on fixed costs of investment as well as variable costs of quality. That is, IT investment can be both quality improving and cost reducing.

Third, future research on extending our two-firm model to a more general problem of quality and price competition among n firms entering the market sequentially is likely to provide more general results. The competition intensity will increase as more firms are competing for a limited number of consumers. Furthermore, in an infinite horizon setting, as technology develops over time, an early entrant’s cost disadvantage becomes more severe over time. Thus, it would be interesting to further study how the firms are affected by increasing competition and whether the firms that enter the market in the early periods can sustain their profitability in the presence of a decline in IT cost.

Fourth, we assumed three-stage model in which firms make a onetime, up-front, long-term IT investment. This assumption is reasonable because a large percentage of IT investments is in the long-term IT infrastructure [14, 57], and upgrading to the new technology is very costly because of the organizational and financial commitments (organizational inertia) [56]. Another interesting extension of our research is the study of the case where the firms may have an opportunity to adjust their initial IT investment in subsequent periods. This opportunity might have a major impact on the investment decisions of each firm. Anticipating the future cost decline and the new competition, the early entrant might prefer to invest less initially and adjust its IT investment later when the IT cost declines. The early entrant’s flexibility in adjusting its initial investment is likely to reduce the cost difference between the two firms and the cost advantage of the late entrant.

Fifth, we assumed that consumers could freely switch from the first to second product when it is introduced. In this respect, our results would apply to markets, such as ISPs, where the turnover rates are very high, indicating very low or zero switching costs. However, often, for instance, in the telecommunications market, consumers face switching costs imposed by compatibility, long-term contracts with the first firm, and other considerations. A more general model of IT investments that includes such switching costs is another important extension to our research.

Finally, we model the demand at the aggregate level (market) and use constant b and c as the price and quality cross-sensitivity of the aggregated demand, respectively. Consequently, we could not study the problem where consumers have different cross elasticities. Modeling the problem at the individual consumer level may offer insights about the impact of consumer heterogeneity on the price- and qualitysensitivity dimensions. We have also assumed that price and quality sensitivity of the markets are common knowledge to both firms. However, firms might have imperfect knowledge about the market type at the time of the entry. Hence, the question of how firms’ investment strategies change when firms are uncertain about the market type deserves further attention.
