# Evaluating the effects of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t in multi-DSS models context: A two-phase view

Yucong Liu <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Younghwa Lee <sup>b</sup>, Andrew N.K. Chen <sup>c</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

We investigate the effects of individual difference with the framework of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t under a multi-DSS models context using a two-phase view. Our research question is: in addition to task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t, does individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t or individual–task <sup>fi</sup>t matter in users' attitude and performance in the multi-tasks and multi-DSS models context? We <sup>fi</sup>rst divide the concept of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t into three dimensions – task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (IT F), and task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (T IF) – so that we can explore mechanisms and effects of interaction among these factors (i.e., task, individual difference, and technology). We then propose a two-phase view of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (i.e., preparadigm phase and paradigm phase) based on Kuhn's concept of revolutionary science. We conducted a controlled laboratory experiment with multiple DSS models and decision-making tasks to test our hypotheses. Results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed our arguments that in the paradigm phase, the effects of individual differences on user attitudes toward DSS models can be ignored and that in the pre-paradigm phases individual differences play an important role. In addition, we found that effects of individual difference can be a twoblade sword: IT F can enhance but T IF can diminish users' attitude to DSS model (i.e., technology). Our results also suggested that different dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t may affect performance directly or indirectly.

## 1. Introduction

Throughout the history of information systems (IS) development, there has remained great interest in developing accurate insights into how individuals interact with information technology (IT) to complete varying tasks [8,25,32,34]. Myriad studies have proposed a variety of “<sup>fi</sup>t models” to describe and explain these complex interrelations including various forms of cognitive <sup>fi</sup>t [59,60], task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t [18,19], user style–task structure–information support <sup>fi</sup>t [46], agent–task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t [11], and capability–task–strategy <sup>fi</sup>t [16,56]. While these models help our understanding of the “IT <sup>fi</sup>t” phenomena, they commonly have underlying limitations. For example, previous <sup>fi</sup>t models have been criticized for: (1) applying only to low-level spatial and symbolic tasks, (2) having decidedly rational perspective, (3) having not touched core individual differences, and (4) being without suf<sup>fi</sup>cient empirical support [e.g., 18,56]. We consider various shortcomings of the literature to date which, together, suggest that there remains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant need and opportunity for researchers to advance the knowledge in the area of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t.

We begin by noting a major limitation to date in the literature on human–technology interaction which has been the use of an oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed focus on what factors should be included or excluded while ignoring how factors interact with each other. A rather old, but still valid example is the unresolved debate over whether individual differences should be considered for IS design [see 26,45]. We suggest that this debate is more salient in the context of decision support systems (DSS), where researchers question whether we should design a DSS to <sup>fi</sup>t needs of each type of decision makers [30] or we should not [26]. We conjecture that a better starting point to address this issue is to carefully investigate how the individual difference factor interacts with other relevant factors from a <sup>fi</sup>t perspective. As some authors have pointed out: “the quality of interaction, […] between human and computer […] is affected very slowly, if at all, by technological advance” [5]. Thus, in this study we investigate the effects of individual difference with the framework of task–individual– technology <sup>fi</sup>t using a two-phase view. Because DSS models are in the core of decision support systems, we investigate the phenomenon under a multi-DSS models context. Our research question is: in addition to task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t, does individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t or individual–task <sup>fi</sup>t matter in users' attitude and performance in the multi-tasks and multi-DSS models context?

In order to answer this question, we <sup>fi</sup>rst divide the concept of task– individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t into three dimensions – task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (IT F), and task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (T IF) – so that we can explore mechanisms and effects of interaction among these factors (i.e., task, individual difference, and technology). While prior <sup>fi</sup>t studies [e.g., 18,46] considered interactions among task, individual difference, and technology at the same level, we propose a two-phase view and contend that those above-mentioned <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions take effect differently in different phases. The two-phase view is based on Kuhn's concept of revolutionary science [33]. Kuhn de<sup>fi</sup>ned a paradigm as what members of a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community, and they alone, share. According to Kuhn, there are pre-paradigm and paradigm phases. In the pre-paradigm phase, there are several incompatible and incomplete theories and there is no consensus on any particular theory. If the actors in the pre-paradigm community eventually reach a consensus, then the phase, paradigm phase (or normal science), begins. Basically, this paradigm revolution view postulates that science activities present different characteristics in different phases. In the paradigm phase, the focus is problem-solving with accepted paradigm; in the pre-paradigm phase, however, problem-solving is less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and the major focus is paradigm competition.

Traditionally, tasks can be characterized as structured, semistructured, and unstructured based on whether we can clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne a task and identify a process to complete the task [20]. Our two-phase view of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t refers to the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase as the preparadigm phase where tasks are not clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and problem-solving processes are hardly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed (so called semi- and unstructured task setting). We refer to the second phase as the paradigm phase where tasks are structured and problem-solving processes are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed so that technologies can be designated to carry out speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks (so called structured task setting). We suggest that the two-phase view can facilitate a better lens to examine con<sup>fl</sup>icting arguments and provide new insights on the effects of individual difference on IS design. We further elaborate on the mechanisms under each phase in the next section.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents research background and theoretical foundation for our work. Section 3 introduces a variety of speci<sup>fi</sup>c research hypotheses. Sections 4 and 5 discuss methodology and experiment results, respectively. Section 6 indicates limitations of this research and directions for future research. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section concludes this study.

## 2. Research background and theoretical bases

## 2.1. Task characteristic, individual difference, and DSS models

Task characteristic and individual difference are two factors widely studied in DSS research. Scholars investigated their interaction with DSS from varying perspectives, including technology use [14,17,29,36], decision-making effectiveness [10,38], performance [3,37,47,52], implementation [2], model formulation [4], and decision-making process [58]. The effects of interaction between task and DSS (i.e., technology) are generally consistent, as can be seen in the research stream on task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t [13,19,31,34]. But the impacts of interaction between individual difference and DSS, and between individual difference and task remain elusive with both signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported [see 2 for a review].

DSS can be used to solve problems with varying degrees of structuredness: structured [55], semi-structured [30], and unstructured problems [6]. Although many authors argue that DSS should focus on semi- or unstructured tasks, empirical evidence appears to provide little support for this argument [48]. Therefore, we argue that all different task characteristics should be taken into account for investigating their effects on users' attitude and performance.

One commonly noted category of individual difference is cognitive style, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “characteristic models of functioning shown by individuals in their perceptual and thinking behavior” [64, p. 967]. Cognitive style identi<sup>fi</sup>cation has been assessed as “a huge forward step in the understanding of the relations of personalities to their environment” [63, preface]. There was a wave of cognitive style research in psychological science from 1950s to 1970s, and the area still receives signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention in IS research. In fact, understanding of human–computer interaction has become even more important as user friendliness and customization have become widely cited factors that impact individuals' information technology utilization [8,16,36,40].

DSS models and related research have a long history [see 15,44 for a review]. Yet unlike other applications, such as word processing, accounting software, email and web-browsing, our understanding of interaction between human and DSS model is very limited [5]. There seems to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant lag and imbalance between research of DSS model applications (such as proposing a new DSS model for problemsolving) and research on user interactions with DSS models. For example, there have been studies proposing broad ranges of DSS models including normative analyses (e.g., multi-attribute utility, decision-tree analysis, and Bayesian networks) and descriptive analyses (e.g., heuristics and biases, fast and frugal heuristics, and naturalistic decision making) [49]. However, there have been only a few interaction studies including some focusing on the acceptance of the models [36] and some on how users formulate DSS models to solve problems [4]. To begin to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap, this study investigates a variety of DSS models and examines the interactions between differing individuals, task characteristics, and DSS models.

## 2.2. Concept of fit

According to Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary [42], the verb form of <sup>fi</sup>t means “to conform correctly to the shape or size of”. From our point of view, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition is especially suitable for studying the interaction between individual differences and DSS models. As cited in Keen and Morton [30], Mason and Mitroff [39] argued the following:

“What is information for one type will de<sup>fi</sup>nitely not be information for another. Thus, as designers of MIS, our job is not to get (or force) all types to conform to one, but to give each type the kind of information he is psychologically attuned to and will use most effectively” (p. 478).

In our interpretation, the concept of <sup>fi</sup>t indicates a “match” or a “contingent” perspective between individual differences and DSS models. That is, there are not “always good” characteristics of individuals and a DSS model. In a context of decision making, individuals possess different knowledge and diverse experiences on different topics. They are likely to have distinct needs for decision making and likely to use decision making aids differently.

There have been two key approaches for operationalizing <sup>fi</sup>t. The <sup>fi</sup>rst focuses on category match between individuals, tasks, and technologies [e.g., 59,60], such as “analytic individuals matching structured tasks and tabular format” and “intuitive individuals suiting unstructured task with graphic format” [46]. That is, <sup>fi</sup>t matches unique capabilities and needs of decision makers with appropriate DSS models/ decision aids for varying task types. The second approach focuses mostly on support fit from IS, including aspects such as accessibility, ease of use, and system reliability [18,19]. An obvious issue with this view of support <sup>fi</sup>t is that the focus is on factors related to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, such as systems reliability and ease of use, but it says nothing about effectiveness (such as better understanding of decision problems), one of the cores of DSS [30]. Given that we are interested in different types of decision-making tasks, we focus and investigate the phenomena of <sup>fi</sup>t as a category match.

## 2.3. Two-phase view of task–individual–technology fit

We consider <sup>fi</sup>t as consisting of three two-way interactions. Task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) refers to the degree to which characteristics of technologies <sup>fi</sup>t the needs for carrying out certain tasks. Task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (T IF) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which characteristics of individuals <sup>fi</sup>t the needs of certain tasks. Individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (IT F) refers to the degree to which characteristics of technologies <sup>fi</sup>t the needs of individuals to solve problems. Our two-phase view of task–individual– technology <sup>fi</sup>t follows Kuhn's [33] paradigm evaluation process (see earlier discussion). In the pre-paradigm phase, a variety of models/ opinions/hypotheses of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c activity are competing with each other without any dominant one. Sooner or later, some model(s) prevails and take a dominant position with an enduring group of adherents. In the paradigm phase, the major focus of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c activity is using these commonly accepted models to solve problems.<sup>1</sup> Nevertheless, certain problems (existing or newly appearing) will not be solvable within the chosen paradigm. These problems are termed “anomalies”. For anomalies, the chosen paradigm phase is now a pre-paradigm phase. The anomalies can only be solved when a satisfactory paradigm is developed later. Thus, the pre-paradigm and paradigm phases tend to continuously evolve and constitute the process of problem solving.

In the context of this study, the pre-paradigm phase involves semiand unstructured tasks. In this phase, there is no commonly accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nition of tasks or problem-solving processes. Decision makers have to depend on their experience and intuition to carry out tasks. In this situation, individual differences tend to play an important role in decision-making process. That is, task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t is unachievable but individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t as well as individual–task <sup>fi</sup>t should be two important factors for problem solving. In contrast, the paradigm phase is the structured task setting. In this phase, tasks are usually well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned and problem-solving processes have been speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. Decision makers do not have much <sup>fl</sup>exibility to choose how to complete tasks. Therefore, individual differences are ignored in this phase. Here task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t is feasible and is the main consideration for technology development, whereas individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t and individual–task <sup>fi</sup>t are deemed less relevant. Instead of debating the effect of individual difference between the “doesn't matter” view [26] and “bases of IS design” view [45], we conjecture that this two-phase view better identi<sup>fi</sup>es situations where individual difference either can be ignored or have an important role to play and must be considered.

## 3. Hypotheses

We approach the concept of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t with three dimensions – task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (IT F), and task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (T IF) – and investigate their effects on user attitude and performance in different phases under the twophase view outlined above. Fig. 1 presents our conceptual model.

In the paradigm phase, tasks are structured and problem-solving processes are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed so that sophisticated applications can be developed to solve problems. In this phase, TTF is the major concern of developing technology for assisting users to complete tasks. Meanwhile, there is little variation of understanding tasks and choosing problem-solving processes among users. Evaluation of performance can be process-oriented and users are expected to follow pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned processes to solve problems. Regardless of individual difference in perceptual and thinking behavior, the focus is on compliance with commonly accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and processes. The impact of individual difference is not preferred nor encouraged. Therefore, IT F and T IF are not important in this situation. This argument is consistent with Huber's [26] view that individual differences do not matter on IS/DSS design and is supported by empirical evidence. For example, Taylor [53] found that cognitive style affects usage of some types of IS, such as data mining, knowledge repository, and collaboration (related to low level task structuredness), but not for other types, such as email and yellow page directory (related to high level task structuredness). Benbasat and Dexter [3] found that there was no performance difference of cognitive style with relatively structured tasks. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 1:

Hypothesis 1. In the paradigm phase (structured task setting), TTF (but not IT F or T IF) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects users' attitude to DSS models.

In the pre-paradigm, because tasks are semi- or un-structured and no speci<sup>fi</sup>ed problem-solving processes are available, people must rely on their own personal experience and knowledge/skills to solve problems. That is, how technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the needs of decision makers (i.e., IT F) and how the characteristics of decision makers <sup>fi</sup>t the capabilities necessary to carry out the speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks (i.e., T IF) become two important issues in this phase. This argument is consistent with the current trend in “individual difference” research [e.g., 7,24,40]. For example, Chakraborty and his colleagues [8] found that cognitive style has a positive impact on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and subjective norm. Taylor [53] found that cognitive style is positively related to usage of certain knowledge management systems. Lee et al. [34] found that cognitive style contributes to cognitive <sup>fi</sup>t of using technology. Thus, we posit the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a. In the pre-paradigm phase (semi- and un-structured task settings), IT F signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects users' attitude to DSS models.

Hypothesis 2b. In the pre-paradigm phase (semi- and un-structured task settings), T IF signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects users' attitude to DSS models.

Past studies proposed the positive linkages among TTF, attitude to technology, and performance [8,13,19,34], arguing that the TTF model and technology acceptance model (TAM) [12] can be combined to explain information system performance. We untangle the task– individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t into three separate dimensions (TTF, IT<sub>e</sub>F, and T IF) and analyze relationships among TTF, attitude, and performance and explore possible relationships along with IT F and T IF dimensions.

Existing literature has suggested a diversity of measurements of DSS performance [see 27 for a review]. We adopt two types of measurement of performance: decision agreement (perceived performance) and decision time (objective performance). Decision agreement is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which decision makers accept recommendations from DSS models when there is inconsistency between their initial intuition and the outcomes from DSS models. Prior studies suggested that a decision maker's initial, unaided solution (intuition) has a profound in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the generation of other solutions [52,54]. Empirical study also indicated that decision makers may rely on their intuitive judgment even when there is clear inconsistency between their intuition and recommendations by DSS models [36]. To improve understanding of this phenomenon, we include decision agreement in this study. Moreover, the abovementioned relationships among TTF, attitude to technology, and performance have received scant attention in the context of DSS model with objective measurements of performance. We thus include decision time, a widely used objective measurement of DSS performance [48], in our analysis.

The following hypotheses re<sup>fl</sup>ect the intention to test the direct effects of <sup>fi</sup>ts on performance in addition to the indirect effects through attitude:

Hypothesis 3a (1). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, TTF has direct effects on decision agreement.

Hypothesis 3a (2). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, TTF has direct effects on decision time.

Hypothesis 3b (1). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, T IF has direct effects on decision agreement.

Hypothesis 3b (2). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, T IF has direct effects on decision time.

Hypothesis 3c (1). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, IT F has direct effects on decision agreement.

Hypothesis 3c (2). In addition to the indirect effects through attitude, IT F has direct effects on decision time.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Manipulation of task, individual difference, and DSS models

We manipulate task, individual difference, and DSS models based on literature review of existing DSS studies [e.g., 21,36,48,52]. First, three decision tasks were selected, including structured, semistructured, and unstructured tasks [20,30]. Each task was used in one or more previous DSS studies. We selected Gorry et al.'s [20] warehouse location task as the structured task because factors that affect the warehouse location decision are clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and decision models are available to provide optimal solutions. We selected Skyttner's [51] new business of<sup>fi</sup>ce selection task as the semi-structured task and Cooper's [9] new manager recruitment task as the unstructured task because factors that may affect those decisions are not well identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and decision makers may have different understandings of the importance of various factors. Table 1 provides the list of the selected tasks and Appendix A provides details of each type of task.

For testing individual differences, we measured a well-known individual cognitive style called “<sup>fi</sup>eld dependence/independence”.

Field dependence/independence describes “the ability to separate an objective or phenomenon from its environment” [35, p. 409]. The <sup>fi</sup>eld dependent individuals tend to prefer intuitive approaches of problemsolving, while the <sup>fi</sup>eld independent persons are inclined to use rational, detail-based approaches. We used Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) [62] to measure this construct since it is widely accepted in the DSS literature [2] and has received the strong support in psychology literature [35].

Past research adopted many categories of DSS models to aid decision making, including normative and descriptive analyses models [49], decision analysis models, optimization models, and simulation techniques [44]. We adopt categories of “optimal”, “rational”, and “behavioral” DSS models based on their rationality-level [36,44]. We use these three categories to better capture the <sup>fi</sup>ts with the task characteristics and the individual difference used in this study.<sup>2</sup> The optimal category DSS models are the models providing optimal results under the situation where factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence results are clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and measurable. The rational category DSS models are the models that support individuals' decision making under circumstance where they have relatively complete information on situations where decisions need to be made, but have lack of clarity with how that information can be used for decision making. Individuals have to make assumptions and try to derive an optimal solution under those assumptions while using the rational category of DSS models. Finally, the behavior category DSS models are applied where the bounded rationality of individuals is evident and thus individuals apply rules of thumb in an attempt to get a satisfying solution [36]. ## 4.2. Operationalization of TTF, IT F, and T IF

We operationalize three dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>ts among task, individual difference, and DSS models at different levels (see Table 3). We specify <sup>fi</sup>t levels based on the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of <sup>fi</sup>t and the “user style–task structure–information support” <sup>fi</sup>t model proposed by Robey and Taggart [46]. For example, a structured task is said to be highly <sup>fi</sup>tted with an optimal model. This type of task is well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned and the corresponding problem-solving processes can be clearly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed so that an optimal DSS model can provide outputs that enable completion of the task. A <sup>fi</sup>eld independence individual is said to be highly <sup>fi</sup>tted with a structured task. Such tasks require a rational, detail based approach which is the preference of the <sup>fi</sup>eld independence individual. On the other hand, carrying out unstructured tasks tends to rely more on individual intuition and general rather than speci<sup>fi</sup>c information from DSS models. A <sup>fi</sup>eld dependence person prefers intuition-based approaches and behavioral models apply rules of thumb to problemsolving. Therefore, we suggest that there is a higher level of <sup>fi</sup>t among unstructured task, <sup>fi</sup>eld dependence, and behavioral models. In addition, as we argue in our two-phase view, the <sup>fi</sup>t between semi- and unstructured task and technology cannot be achieved. How technology can support these two types of tasks is contingent on how individuals use it. Thus, we assign a low level of TTF to settings of semi- and un-structured tasks. We summarize the levels of <sup>fi</sup>t among tasks, individual difference, and DSS models for this study in Table 3.

## 4.3. Other measurements

In Simon's [50] pioneering book, task structuredness is judged by whether tasks can be programmed or not. This judgment is close to the analyzability dimension of task complexity, which refers to the extent to which tasks can be conceptualized and analyzed [43]. Thus, in this study we use the measurement of task analyzability to verify structuredness of tasks. Task analyzability is measured by four items adopted from Haerem and Rau [22].

The measurement of attitude to DSS models (<sup>fi</sup>ve items) is developed based on Hartwick and Barki [23] and Lu et al. [36], which refers to user's attitude while using the DSS models. Decision agreement tests the extent to which users will accept results of DSS when there is an inconsistency between their initial intuition and results of DSS. This measurement is developed from Lu et al. [36]. All measurements are on a seven-point scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (7) strongly agree. The survey questions are detailed in Table 4. Decision time was clocked and automatically captured by the software utilized in this study.

## 4.4. Experiment design and procedure

We conducted a controlled laboratory experiment with our subjects being business school students at a large mid-western university in the U.S. The rationale for using student subjects is that we want to focus on human–technology interaction while avoiding the “overriding” of cognitive style preference that tends to come from experience and from requirements within a work organization [2]. In addition, the student subjects were exposed to tasks similar to those used in this study during their business school coursework. Subjects were recruited through campus advertisements and class announcements. Extra credits from instructors were awarded to participants as incentives. To further motivate active participation, we also provided \$15 cash reward for subjects who achieve top 20% performance. We <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted a pilot study with 58 subjects to verify our task descriptions, questionnaire, and DSS models. Based on the results, minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made for task descriptions and questionnaire. We then conducted a formal study with 230 subjects. The detailed demographics of the subjects are listed in Table 5.

To examine our two-phase view, we designed 10 experimental setting groups. Groups 1 and 2 were designed to test Hypothesis 1 with a size of 40 subjects in each group. Because we'd like to test whether individual differences matter in the paradigm phase, we examined two extreme situations. That is, Group 1 was involved in a situation where TTF, IT F, and T IF were all at the high level while Group 2 was involved in a situation where only TTF was at the high level, but IT F and T IF were both at the low level. The other eight experiment groups (i.e., Groups 3 to 10) were used to investigate potential different levels of <sup>fi</sup>t among task, individual, and technology. For Groups 3 to 10, each group consisted of 20 subjects for the investigation of the pre-paradigm phase where we tested whether IT F and T IF signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect users' attitude to DSS models. The group/experiment details are summarized in Table 6.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Validity and reliability

An exploratory factor analysis with Varimax rotation was conducted to investigate convergent and discriminant validity and reliability. Convergent validity indicates “whether the items comprising a scale behave as if they are measuring a common underlying construct” and discriminant validity refers to “the ability of a measurement item to differentiate between constructs being measured” [12, p. 327]. As shown in bold numbers in Table 7, all factor loadings are above .6, thus supporting convergent validity. At the same time, all items correlated much higher with items used to measure the same construct than with items for different constructs. Thus, discriminant validity was con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Finally, reliability was examined using Cronbach's Alpha. The Cronbach's Alphas for task analyzability and attitude to DSS model are .602 and .923 respectively. Thus, the reliability of task analyzability is acceptable at low level and that of attitude to DSS is preferred [41].

## 5.3. Results

Our results showed that high TTF positively and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects users' attitude to DSS models in the paradigm phase (structured task setting). At the same time, we would like to see whether $\mathrm { I T } _ { \mathrm { e } } \mathrm { F }$ and $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F }$ play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role. We compared Groups 1 and 2 in the paradigm phase with an ANOVA test. As indicated in Section 4.4, Group 1 is designed to have high <sup>fi</sup>t in all three dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t, but Group 2 only has high <sup>fi</sup>t in task–technology dimension. Results from this ANOVA test are shown in Table 10 which indicate that even though the two groups are different in the levels of both $\mathrm { I T _ { e } F }$ and T IF, there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference on group means of attitude to DSS model (p-value=.585). That is, our results suggest that in the paradigm phase (structured task setting) IT F and T IF do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects users' attitude to DSS models. Therefore, Hypotheses 1 is supported.

We conducted regression analysis to test Hypothesis 2 in the preparadigm phase (including Groups 3 to 10). As expected, the results show that both the impacts of T IF and IT F on users' attitude to DSS model are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p-values are .030 and .041 respectively) (Table 11). Therefore, Hypotheses 2a and 2b are supported. It is especially worth noting that our results showed that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of $\mathrm { \dot { T } _ { a } I F }$ is negative. This indicates that the low level of T IF is related to the high level of attitude to DSS model and the high level of $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F }$ is related to the low level of attitude to DSS model. This result is against the prior studies' implications that high <sup>fi</sup>ts are related to high performance or high attitude to DSS model [e.g., 11,28]. In other words, our results show that individuals have lower attitudes to DSS models when their cognitive styles are <sup>fi</sup>t with the tasks, but have higher attitudes to DSS models when their task–cognitive style <sup>fi</sup>t is low. We believe that this <sup>fi</sup>nding is important and interesting since it challenges the current view of treating the task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t as one single construct. That is, this <sup>fi</sup>nding supports the two-phase view where the three dimensions of the task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t should be considered and pursued separately. Further implications from this result are discussed in the next subsection.

## 5.4. Discussions

We utilized a two-phase view and investigated the effects of the three dimensions of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF, IT F, and T IF) on users' attitude to DSS model and performance. In the paradigm phase IT F and T IF do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect users' attitude to DSS model. This implies that the effects of individual difference on users' attitude to DSS model can be ignored in this phase. On the other hand, in the preparadigm phase, $\mathrm { I T _ { e } F }$ and $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F }$ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect users' attitude toward DSS models, suggesting that the effects of individual difference on users' attitude toward DSS model should be considered in this phase. We further examine the <sup>fi</sup>t–attitude–performance relationships proposed in task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t literature [19,34]. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm the proposed relationships among TTF, attitude and performance of decision agreement, but not for performance of decision time. Moreover, results on the other two <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions $( \mathrm { I T _ { e } F } ,$ and $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F ) }$ are not as expected. We also found that there is a negative relationship between $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F }$ and users' attitude toward DSS models.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are interesting for several reasons. First, in contrast to the prior task–individual–technology models [e.g., 11,28,46] that <sup>fi</sup>t may be related to better performance and attitude to technology, we found that high task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t is related to low attitude to DSS models and low task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t is related to high attitude to DSS models. This suggests that when decision makers' individual characteristics (e.g., cognitive style) <sup>fi</sup>t the needs for completing tasks, they may have little interest in the technology available to provide decision aids. But when the individual–task <sup>fi</sup>t is low, they have quite different attitudes to the technology. This <sup>fi</sup>nding begins to <sup>fi</sup>ll the lack of results relating to whether DSS designs should $^ { * } ( 1 )$ conform to the user's cognitive style or (2) complement the user's cognitive style” [26, p. 571]. The results provide evidence that DSS designers may need to do both conformance and complementation in different circumstances. In the dimension of task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t, DSS design should complement the user's cognitive style because low task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t is related to high attitude to DSS models. In the dimension of individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t, DSS design should conform to the user's cognitive style because high individual– technology <sup>fi</sup>t is related to high attitude to DSS models. Thus, our study consolidates the arguments of the varying situations and captures a blind spot relating to task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t that has been overlooked by past research.

Second, we examine two measurements of performance, perceived performance (decision agreement) and objective performance (decision time). Our results show that the <sup>fi</sup>t–attitude–performance relationships proposed in the task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t literature [19,34] hold for decision agreement but not for decision time. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, TTF has both direct and indirect effects (through attitude to DSS model) on decision agreement, but only direct effects on decision time. The implication of this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that in order to improve decision agreement (perceived performance), both TTF and attitude to DSS models can be important. On the other hand, to shorten decision time, we need to focus on improving TTF and not worry about users' attitude to the technology. This <sup>fi</sup>nding provides clues for IS practitioners of how to adopt different strategies to pursue different dimensions of performance concerning TTF.

Third, we examine $\mathrm { T _ { a } I F }$ and $\mathrm { I T _ { e } F }$ in the proposed <sup>fi</sup>t–attitude– performance relationships. The results show that even though T IF and $\mathrm { { I T _ { e } F } }$ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' attitude to DSS models, they do not directly or only marginally contribute to performance. This <sup>fi</sup>nding seems to imply that $\mathrm { T } _ { \mathrm { a } } \mathrm { I F }$ and $\mathrm { { I T _ { e } F } }$ are less important than TTF for improving performance. We note and caution readers to keep in mind that TTF is achievable only for a small portion of tasks (structured tasks). T IF and IT F are still the feasible goals to pursue for most other types of tasks (semi- and un-structured tasks) to improve users' attitude to DSS models.

The results of this study have important managerial implications to company practices. First, for routine and structured decision-making tasks, companies should focus on obtaining appropriate technology that can <sup>fi</sup>t the needs of these decision-making tasks, such as providing ease of access to high quality data [18,19]. By doing so, companies can pursue better task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t and in turn directly improve both performance (e.g., decision time and decision agreement) and user attitude to technology.

Second, for semi- and un-structured decision-making tasks, companies should pay more attention to using technology to enable and leverage human capital (i.e., decision makers) instead of <sup>fi</sup>tting tasks. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest when companies use technology to enable human capital for semi- and un-structured decision-making tasks, they should focus more on <sup>fi</sup>ts between decision makers and both tasks and technology. Organizational literature has demonstrated that human capital is related to superior <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [e.g., 57] and empirical study has revealed that IT and human capital can complement each other [1].

Third, for semi- and un-structured decision-making tasks, companies should pay more attention to <sup>fi</sup>rst improving user attitude to the technology which in turn will improve performance. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that user attitude to technology is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mediator for both T<sub>a</sub>IF and IT<sub>e</sub>F and performance. In other words, companies might not directly achieve better indicator of performance measurement (e.g., decision time and decision agreement) by ignoring user attitude towards the technology provided to decision makers.

Finally, however, in some extreme situation when decision time is the most important concern, companies may discard user attitude as an indicator of successful technology use and focuses on improving task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t and individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t. A possible example for this situation is decision making on buying or selling stock. When stock traders pursue numerical pro<sup>fi</sup>t and quickly respond to marketing changes, they may not care too much about whether the technology is easy to learn but use any technology that can help them gain pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 6. Limitations and future research

This study suffers from certain limitations. First, we used student subjects and conducted an experiment in a lab environment. Although student subjects can successfully represent knowledge workers to complete the tasks by using DSS models and a lab experiment is pertinent to precisely examine the effect of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t on users' attitude to DSS model and performance, it can be important to use knowledge workers in real business settings to provide comparative results. In this work, however, the use of student subjects was expected to limit the impact of experience (“locked in approach”) and organizational constraints/restrictions (“only one way to do it”). We expect future research can examine our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in various business contexts.

Second, one factor loading of the construct of task analyzability is only .601 and the reliability of this construct is only .602. However, the factor loadings of this construct in this study are totally comparable with existing similar studies [e.g., 22,61]. Moreover, the lower limits of acceptability are generally considered around .50–.60 [41]. Thus, we think our operationalization of this construct is valid but caution readers to keep the limitation in mind when interpreting the results of this study.

For future research, we encourage researchers to conduct replication studies to con<sup>fi</sup>rm (or possibly dis-con<sup>fi</sup>rm) <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study. First, task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t is negatively related users' attitude to DSS model. We believe this <sup>fi</sup>nding is important because it seems to be totally missed in prior <sup>fi</sup>t studies. It will be interesting to see if this result holds in different contexts with different tasks, individual differences, and technologies. Second, types of measurement of performance may affect the paths through which <sup>fi</sup>t contribute to performance. In this study, we found that users' attitude to DSS models mediates the effects of <sup>fi</sup>t on perceived performance but not on objective performance. We believe this <sup>fi</sup>nding is important because it adds new insights to the task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t research stream. Other measurements of perceived and objective performance can be designed in future studies. Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not support the direct effect of T IF and IT F on users' performance. Future research can focus on seeking what “technology” can be designed and provided for high T IF situation so better user performance results. Similarly, future studies can be directed to identify what speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks can be applied in high IT F situations so better user performance occurs.

## 7. Conclusion

In this study, we propose and examine a two-phase view of task– individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t in a lab environment. The results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed our arguments that in the paradigm phase, the effects of individual differences on user attitudes toward DSS models can be ignored and that in the pre-paradigm phases, individual differences play an important role. We believe that this view has both theoretical and practical implications for DSS research. First, it con<sup>fi</sup>rms the effects of individual difference in the pre-paradigm phase and provides a clearer view for the old debate of individual difference on DSS [26,45]. Second, it indicates the complexity of interaction among task, individual, and technology and provides valuable insights on how those factors interact. Third, it suggests future directions for IS practitioners. In the structured task setting, the efforts should contribute to task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t. In the semiand unstructured task setting, however, individual difference has to be taken into account.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also challenge the popular view on task–individual– technology <sup>fi</sup>t, which implies that more <sup>fi</sup>t is better [e.g., 11,28,46]. The results of this study show that effects of individual difference may be a two-blade sword: IT F can increase but T IF can decrease users' attitude to DSS model (i.e., technology). Finally, this study indicates that different dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t may affect performance through different paths. We look forward to future research on these topics and additional insights on these important issues.
