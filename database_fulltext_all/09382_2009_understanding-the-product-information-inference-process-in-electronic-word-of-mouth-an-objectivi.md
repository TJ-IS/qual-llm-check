# Understanding the product information inference process in electronic word-of-mouth: An objectivity–subjectivity dichotomy perspective

Jung Lee, Jae-Nam Lee 米

Korea University Business School, Anam-Dong Seongbuk-Gu, Seoul, 136-701, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 23 April 2008 Received in revised form 16 March 2009 Accepted 15 May 2009 Available online 7 June 2009

Keywords: Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) Customer’s purchase intention Quality Preference Product type Product information PLS

## A B S T R A C T

We examined the actions of a customer when inferring product information from electronic word-of mouth (eWOM) material at a website. We developed a customer purchase intention model and simulated various eWOM levels within this, adopting an objectivity–subjectivity dichotomy, and considering quality and preference as the major antecedents of customer purchase intention. We inferred the information that the customers had obtained from the eWOM by categorizing the customers’ responses. The eWOM was parameterized using mean and variance; products that were categorized into quality and preference goods. We considered four cases in which customers infer different product information and exhibit different reactions. Items for quality and preference goods were developed by using a card-sorting method. An experimental survey was conducted on 121 Korean Internet shopping mall users. The hypotheses were partially supported using a Partial Least Squares path comparison method. Overall, our study should provide guidance to firms in their managing eWOM systems by identifying how customers react to them at various levels.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, online shopping has been shown to provide customers with a convenient and efficient purchasing opportunity. However, online shopping has also resulted in some disadvantages, such as product uncertainty and transaction risk [5]. Since customers cannot always experience the true features of a product purchased via the Internet, there are difficulties in making the correct purchasing decision. Online customers need accurate product information when they are not familiar with a product, and thus must assess it.

For this reason, eWOM or online rating systems are an important product information source: they provide potential customers with an extensive source of customer opinions about the product. Such a process supports impartiality. In addition, since opinions are gathered from many customers, the process supports truthfulness and information richness [8].

Not surprisingly, a number of studies of eWOM-related effectiveness have been conducted [18]. These may be classified into two research types: market- and individual-level. The difference between these two lies in how the information is viewed. If it was seen as a market-level parameter, eWOM was considered to be related to other market parameters, such as price and sales and generally analyzed using an econometric approach and the eWOM was measured as a number, such as the average rating and dispersion of ratings [7]. On the other hand, if eWOM was viewed as an individuallevel parameter, it assumed to be related to other individual-level parameters, such as trust and purchase intent, researchers would measure eWOM as notions and adopt a survey approach to finding its impact and location in the behavioral model [1]. Thus, the customer’s perception of the product is more important than the actual rating levels in the eWOM systems.

EWOM research stems from complicated customer activities in the eWOM systems. As illustrated in Fig. 1, there are three major parts needed in explaining eWOM activities. Firstly, a group of post-purchase customers must rate the target product information as integers representing their quality. As eWOM systems mature and more customers participate, ratings are accumulated. Second, from these, the pre-purchase customer infers product quality as ‘‘this product is good’’ or ‘‘this product is not reliable.’’ Third, with this information, the customer estimates the value of the product and makes a purchase decision.

From this model, it is easy to see why prior eWOM research efforts fell into either:

\- Market-level, identifying the product information process by viewing eWOM as accumulated customer opinion, and its relationship with other market-level signals, or,

\- individual-level, identifying the customer’s decision-making process by viewing the eWOM as informational, focusing on how the information affects a customer’s decision-making process.

While many studies focused on one of these two, a few focused on the product information inference process; understanding this is essential in explaining how rating levels change customers’ decisions. In these studies, the process was regarded as motivational [12], while the inferred information was seen to be an exogenously given factor. Customers are strongly affected by the online customer ratings of their purchase decisions; yet, we have no knowledge of how this changes a customer’s purchase intentions.

To overcome this drawback, we tried to determine the product information inference process when a customer uses eWOM systems. However, since this process is purely mental, it cannot be validated directly. Therefore, we developed a general customer purchase intentions model that simulated eWOM changes within it. By considering the customer responses to this eWOM simulation, we could determine the information inferred by the customer, and thus, confirm the process.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Quality and preference based on objectivity–subjectivity dichotomy

A customer’s purchase intention is determined by his or her estimated value of the product. In estimating product value, a customer must carefully examine the specific attributes of importance; some customers have general standards for their evaluation, while others do not. For example, when a customer evaluates an MP3 player, he or she may examine its weight, capacity, design, and warranty. If the prices were equal, an MP3 player with a 2G capacity would rate higher than one with only 1G. However, in terms of color and texture, some customers may evaluate a red MP3 player higher than a yellow one.

The basic difference between these two types of attributes lies in whether there is an accepted, ranking-based standard for evaluation. If the attributes are all objectively measurable, there is a low possibility of misunderstanding, or bias, in the evaluation process. In contrast, when a customer examines the style, color, and shape of the product, he or she may elicit different preferences and a high level of subjectivity is assumed [19]. These two types of product attributes produce an objectivity–subjectivity dichotomy, as illustrated in Table 1.

From this we can derive two types of product evaluation criteria, quality and preference. In the dichotomy, we have two domains: subjective and objective. When these domains are considered at the variable level, quality is derived from the objective domain, while preference is derived from subjective domain. Thus, our study defined ‘quality’ as the product evaluation based on the product attributes which are objectively measurable and ‘preference’ as a subjective judgment of the product quality and other attributes that depend on the customer’s taste.

## 2.2. Customer purchase intention model

Since a customer’s purchase intention depends on the estimated value of the product, the two product evaluation criteria, quality and preference, need to be considered as major antecedents to the purchase intention.

In practice, however, preference is formulated in two ways:

\- By evaluation of the product or service attributes that can only be examined subjectively, such as color, style, and shape.

\- By the perceived quality of the product or service, which changes over time [11] and is continuously affected by outside stimuli such as satisfaction and cost [9]. It also can result from an exogenous factor given to the customer and affecting his or her individual preference. Therefore, we propose that a customer’s perception of quality also influences his or her perception of preference.

## 3.1. Conceptualizing eWOM by using mean and variance

We conceptualized the eWOM statistically. When specified values, say the rating from 1 to 5 in the eWOM system are accumulated on a large scale, they form a distribution, which can be abstracted as mean and variance if it follows a normal distribution pattern. In the eWOM context, mean is the expected product value for a potential customer. A customer’s expected value of the product would be the average of all customer ratings. This naturally explains why most customers are likely to buy products with higher ratings.

However, variance is the average scatter level around the mean. In the eWOM context, this determines the probability of obtaining a product with the expected value. For example, if there are two products with the same average ratings but with different variances, a customer’s expected values for them would be the same, but the opportunity of getting the expected rating would be lower for the product with the higher variance, since it represents the distance from the mean value, a high variance implies a higher risk to decision makers.

Even though both mean and variance are important and are the essential parameters in the eWOM ratings, many researchers conceptualize the eWOM with either a central point (mean) [3] or the level of dispersion (variance) [13]. In our study, to fully abstract the information in the eWOM, mean and variance were considered equally important parameters needing to be examined.

## 3.2. Product categorization

Product type differentiates the relationship among variables and the eWOM’s impact. If the product is mainly evaluated on quality, it is termed a quality good. If it is mainly evaluated on preference, it is a preference good. For example, a copy machine would be a quality good if it is mainly evaluated by its copy speed, warranty, and weight rather than its color and style. On the other hand, a book would be a preference good because it is typically evaluated by its plot, genre, and story rather than its size and weight. Table 2 summarizes the product categorization descriptions.

In reality, however, categorizing a product is not easy because the majority of commodities are mixture goods. For example, a 19- in. Sony TV would be a quality good if it was compared to a 21-in. Sony TV, but it would also be a preference good if it is compared to a 19-in. Samsung TV. Width is objectively measurable, whereas brand is not. In this case, it is not easy to decide which attribute is more important. Nonetheless, product categorization is still effective because it shows the general customers’ assumptions, criteria, and standards for products. Even though customers do not have the same assumptions and criteria for all products and their attributes, online businesses can briefly categorize products by identifying the perceptions of most of their customers.

We developed hypotheses for four eWOM changes: (1) average impact on quality goods; (2) average impact on preference goods; (3) variance impact on quality goods; and (4) variance impact on preference goods.

## 3.3. Impact of eWOM average on quality goods

In determining the impact of the eWOM, we consider a marginal increase of eWOM ratings, rationalizing customer responses to the change. The increased eWOM average represents the increased estimated product value. In quality goods, since value is mainly determined by quality, the increased estimated value indicates an increase in estimated quality. In addition, since quality satisfies the objectivity assumption, the estimation is equal to the expectation; thus, expected quality increases.

When the expected quality is high, the customer is assured of the quality and concern for it decreases. If a customer thinks the quality of a product is high enough, there is no need to know more, and concern for it decreases. This is similar to the effect of the law of diminishing marginal utility, where the marginal utility from one unit of quality will decrease as quality increases.

However, if a potential customer is assured of quality, he or she will consider other attributes, such as style and color in determining preference for the product. Increased concern and care for such attributes are interpreted as the increased impact of preference on purchase intention. From these considerations, we formulated the following hypotheses:

## 3.4. Impact of eWOM average on preference goods

The increased eWOM average in preference goods must be processed in a more complicated manner. Not only does it change the estimated value of the product, but it also alters the underlying assumption. As previously, the increased average indicates the increased value of the product. However, in preference goods where the value is mainly determined by preference, the increased value indicates an increase in the estimated preference. Moreover, since preference does not satisfy the objectivity assumption, estimation does not equal expectation and expected preference does not necessarily increase. For example, even though a movie has high ratings, it does not follow that you will rate it highly. Accordingly, since the individual’s expected preference level is not directly affected by the change in average rating, we hypothesized that there were no changes in its impact.

However, the increased average indicates that many customers evaluate the product higher. For a customer, this can be interpreted as increased objectivity because other customers’ collective behaviors for a preference can only be explained by an objectivity assumption relaxation. When the level of objectivity for the product increases, a customer considers quality as more important than previously, thinking that the product is more likely to be a quality than a preference good. As a result, the increased consideration of quality results in the increased impact of quality on preference and purchase intention. Hence, we have the following hypotheses.

In preference goods, as the average of eWOM ratings increases,

H4. the impact of quality on the customer’s purchase intention increases;

H5. the impact of quality on preference increases;

H6. the impact of preference on the customer’s purchase intention does not change.

## 3.5. Impact of eWOM variance on quality goods

An increased variance indicates inconsistent opinions of different customers. For a potential customer, it is the ‘variation of product value’. In quality goods, the product value is mainly determined by quality, the variation represents a change in the estimated quality. Therefore, when the variance increases, the purchasing decision becomes riskier, and to avoid risks, the customer becomes more concerned with, and sensitive to, product quality, carefully examining the quality attributes. These show an increased impact of quality on preference and the customer’s purchase intention.

Furthermore, the customer becomes less concerned with preference because of increased concern over quality. In quality goods, preference was an unimportant and considered secondary. If the concern for quality increases, the concern for preference decreases as the variance increases. In our study, this was interpreted as decreased impact of preference on purchase intention. From these arguments, we hypothesized.

In quality goods, as the variance of eWOM ratings increases,

H7. the impact of quality on the customer’s purchase intention increases;

H8. the impact of quality on preference increases;

H9. the impact of preference on the customer’s purchase intention decreases.

## 3.6. Impact of eWOM variance on preference goods

Increased variance represents changes in the estimated product value. In preference goods, where the product value is mainly determined by preference, it results from variation in estimated preference. As previously, preference possesses the subjectivity assumption and expected preference does not correspond to the estimated preference. Therefore, even though estimated preference fluctuates, expected preference is not affected.

However, increased variance indicates that customers have more diversified opinions about the product. This results in an increased level of subjectivity assumption for the product. Thus a customer may consider preference more important than before, thinking that product value is more likely to be determined by preference. As a result, the importance and impact of preference in the product evaluation process increases, which results in the increased impact of preference on purchase intention. In addition, since there is no ground for changing the concern for quality, the impact of quality on preference and purchase intention does not change. From these:

In preference goods, as the variance of eWOM ratings increases,

H10. the impact of quality on the customer’s purchase intention does not change;

H11. the impact of quality on preference does not change;

H12. the impact of preference on the customer’s purchase intention increases.

## 4. Experimental survey design

## 4.1. Case design

To validate the hypotheses, we developed a paper-based experimental survey in which we showed respondents a product with various levels of eWOM, and asked them questions about their perceived level of quality, preference, and purchase intention.

Windows Vista was selected as a sample quality good because within the MS Windows series, each product has a ranking based on quality. Customers may however wish to upgrade to a newer version because it is assumed to be better. Versioning goods are typical examples of quality-differentiated goods [14]. A wellknown movie, Indiana Jones 4 (prior to theater release), was also selected as a sample preference good because it is differentiated mainly by the subjective preference of the individual customer.

Each product was shown to subjects with four different levels of eWOM ratings: low average (Case 1), high average (Case 2), low variance (Case 3), and high variance (Case 4). The first two were used in validating the eWOM average impact (H1–H6), while the last two were intended for eWOM variance impact (H7–H12). In total, we had eight cases for one set of survey sheets (2 products  4 eWOM levels). Table 4 and Fig. 3 show their differences.

For the subjects to make their comparative decisions, we used a booklet of survey sheets, so that, when the pages were opened, two contrasting cases (high mean versus low mean and high variance versus low variance) were shown at the same time to the subject (see Appendix A). Each product was provided with a picture and product name, but no price information was given (in order to eliminate any price effect). The subjects were told to suppose that the price was lower than their budget. Each case had 30 ratings per page and all the comments provided with the rating consisted of neutral words, such as ‘‘I’ve read a lot of reviews’’ and ‘‘I’ve been using.’’

## 4.2. Scale development

We developed questions for quality and preference using the card-sorting method [20]. From the management and economic literature, we derived eight and nine candidate nouns and verbs, as shown in Table 5. With each card containing one word from the table, we asked 10 graduate students majoring in IS from a Korean university to sort the cards in the order of the meaning closest to quality and preference as a first pilot test. Using the result of the pilot test, the top five and six words were selected and converted into full sentences, such as ‘‘I would opt for this product,’’ and ‘‘This product would fit my taste.’’ For quality items, we were especially careful in the conversion because they should reflect the order within the sentences. For example, if the product was described as ‘‘it is very good’’ from an absolute perspective, comparing it with other ‘‘very good’’ products is not easy. However, if the product was described in relative terms such as ‘‘this is better than that,’’ we could rank the statements. Therefore, we used relative forms.

## 5.3. Hypothesis testing

Since the collected data was not sufficiently large for the use of SEM, a PLS path comparison test was conducted; it has strong statistical validity for a relatively small sample. This test usually requires a sample size of 10 times the most complex relationship in the model, and prior research has shown its statistical significance for even less than 50 samples [15,21]. Our model has four items for one construct at most; thus, the PLS method was deemed appropriate with a sample of 121 respondents.

Among the 12 hypotheses, nine came in the form of alternative hypotheses, $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 } ,$ while three came in the form of the null hypotheses, $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } .$ To validate $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 } ,$ we estimated the t-values to examine the significance level. Statistical interpretation of this process evaluates whether we could or could not reject $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } .$ To validate $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } ,$ t-values were estimated to determine whether or not we could reject $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } .$ . In this case, the result may have a lower level of significance, but it is an appropriate way to validate the three hypotheses (H6, H10 and H11).

## 6.1. Summary of findings

We attempted to identify the customer’s product information inference process by proposing a purchase intention model based on the subjectivity–objectivity dichotomy. Our model included quality and preference as major determinants of the customer’s purchase intention, which represented the objective and the subjective product evaluation criteria, respectively. ## 6.2. Contribution and implications

Our study made several contributions to knowledge. First, it identified the customers’ product information inference process in the eWOM context. The process, though it provides a concrete rationale for customer behavior regarding eWOM, had not previously clearly understood because it is an intrinsic and an implicit mental operation. The inferred information is not revealed until a customer exhibits a decisive behavior [purchase it or not]. Most prior studies have not distinguished between the inference and response processes [17]; they generally assumed that customers already had inferred information from eWOM, and explained their behavior without identifying what information was inferred. We, by focusing on the preceding inference process, enriched the discussion of customer response to eWOM.

Second, we proposed and validated a general product evaluation framework of the customer’s purchase intention model, which could be applied in other contexts. We adopted a subjectivity– objectivity dichotomy to derive the antecedents of purchase intention and proposed quality and preference as the two most important criteria. This framework was effective because the two determinants are collectively exhaustive, preventing confusion and overlapping. This simple purchase intention model is applicable to other IS contexts such as customer value creation [6] and trust building.

Finally, we proposed a new product categorization: quality and preference goods; it is meaningful because it emphasizes the existence of rankings among the products within categories. Ranking within the product category is important because it determines market prices and the willingness of customers to pay. This could be used when customers consider objective rankings on price.

The results have two implications that are of practical importance. First, by integrating the analysis levels of numeric and symbolic eWOM, we have provided meaningful guidelines for firms on the use of eWOM in making customers’ purchasing decisions. We treated eWOM as having an impact on purchase intention in an intact numeric form, integrating different views of eWOM and providing strategic guidelines for businesses.

Second, the product categorization can support firms when they choose their strategic positions in a competitive market. Their customer behavior is critical because it determines expected profits. Theoretically, high quality will directly increase the firm’s profit and thus the firm has an incentive to invest in quality. Thus, unit cost for high quality is an important decision factor. However, if the current product is close to a preference good, increasing quality does not guarantee high profit. Thus, the firm should investigate their target customers’ preferences and develop a plan before improving the product.

## 6.3. Limitations

The study, however, had several limitations. First, in the quality conceptualization, we did not consider quality in a subjective manner. Second, owing to the survey screen limitation, the eWOM levels were not sufficiently differentiated to reveal the different customer responses. The impact of eWOM might not be as large as we intended because we provided only 30 ratings per product. This, in part, led us to validate some hypotheses heuristically (H1, H4, H7 and H10). Third, we conducted a paper-based survey for measuring online product purchase intention; this might have introduced unexpected biases even though we imitated the sheets from the computer screen and fully explained the items in their context. Lastly, the modest sample size was a concern; it may have caused instability in the parameter estimates, such as low t-values in the hypothesis tests. In similar studies on path comparison, over 500 [16] and 150 [10] respondents were used. Our sample size may, thus, not be sufficient for path comparison.

## 7. Conclusion

We examined the impact of eWOM on customer purchase intention to determine the effect of product information on the customers’ online purchasing process. By considering the different effects of eWOM on purchase intention for several product types, we were able to identify the decision process, which provided a rationale for the customers’ purchasing behavior. The process involves a mental operation of the customer which can only be revealed by the purchasing action. By adopting a subjectivity– objectivity dichotomy as a conceptual foundation, we were able to make a significant contribution to the literature by providing a rationale for customer’s varying behavior to different product types.

His published research articles appear in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of MIS Journal of the AIS Communications of the AIS IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, European

Journal of Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, and others. He has presented several papers at the ICIS, HICSS, ECIS DSI and IRMA Conferences, and serves on the editorial boards of MIS Quarterly Information Systems Research, and Journal of the AIS.
