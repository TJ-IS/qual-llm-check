# WEBSITE LOCALIZATION STRATEGIES TO PROMOTE GLOBAL E-COMMERCE: THE MODERATING ROLE OF INDIVIDUALISM AND COLLECTIVISM<sup>1</sup>

Department of Information Management, School of Medicine and Health Management, Huazhong University of Science and Technology Wuhan, CHINA {wutailai@hust.edu.cn}

Chih-Hung Peng Department of Information Management, College of Management, National Taiwan University, Taipei, TAIWAN ROC {ischpeng@ntu.edu.tw}

Choon Ling Sia Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, HONG KONG SAR {iscl@cityu.edu.hk}

Yaobin Lu Department of Management Science and Information Management, School of Management, Huazhong University of Science and Technology Wuhan, CHINA {luyb@hust.edu.cn}

Website localization plays an important role in guiding firms on how to customize websites across countries in which they have a local presence. However, few studies on website localization have systematically examined this topic from a theoretically grounded perspective. Drawing upon the theoretically driven consumer-company identification perspective, we propose that three website localization strategies (web similarity strategy, web distinctiveness strategy, and web prestige strategy) have positive effects on local users’ perceived website localization, which, in turn, is related to local users’ website loyalty. We further investigate the effects of these website localization strategies in a crosscultural setting, focusing on the individualism-collectivism dimension of culture. Based on our online experiments in both the United States and China, we find that these website localization strategies have significant impacts on consumers’ perceived website localization, which is related to consumers’ website loyalty. We also find that web distinctiveness and web prestige strategies are more effective for people from collectivistic societies than for those from individualistic societies, whereas web similarity strategies do not differ across societal types. These findings highlight the importance of website localization strategies for customizing websites for global e-commerce.

Keywords: Website localization, web similarity strategy, web distinctiveness strategy, web prestige strategy, behavioral intentions, individualism-collectivism

## Introduction

Global e-commerce has become increasingly important for both firms and consumers; global retail e-commerce market sales amounted to \$4.28 trillion in 2020 and are expected to reach \$6.39 trillion in 2024 (Chevalier, 2021). Thus, firms that target global e-commerce must determine not only how to customize websites across the countries in which they have a local presence but also how their websites— especially if they are new or relatively unknown—invoke consumers’ behavioral intentions. A key issue with respect to website localization (i.e., customizing a website for a country) is the extent to which global e-commerce firms localize their websites by taking into account the local context of consumers. <sup>2</sup> Companies design websites with varying degrees of localization for different countries. Lesslocalized websites (i.e., standardized websites) may contain local language and content but typically have uniform design elements. For example, Juniper, a U.S. manufacturer of networking products, uses the same design elements for both its U.S. and Chinese websites. The only difference between the two websites is content language.<sup>3</sup> In contrast, highly localized websites fully customize both content and design elements for a particular country. To localize websites, companies implement customized design elements that are familiar to local consumers. For example, Coca-Cola, the leading U.S. beverage corporation, designed its Chinese website using images of Chinese people, food, and holiday celebrations (e.g., mooncakes during the annual mid-autumn festival). <sup>4</sup> Localization makes websites appear natural or local to users<sup>5</sup> and is especially beneficial to multinational corporations (Singh et al., 2009). Website localization has been shown to improve consumers’ intentions toward websites, purchase intentions, and companies’ online sales (Singh & Baack, 2004; Tixier, 2005).

Although the importance of website localization is salient, little research has been devoted to localized websites and their design elements from a theoretically grounded perspective focused on the consumer. Prior studies on website localization have focused on categorizing website elements based on cultural frameworks (Baack & Singh, 2007; Benmamoun et al., 2019; Singh & Baack, 2004).<sup>6</sup> For example, Baack and Singh (2007) found that websites in collectivistic countries tend to use elements highlighting community relationships, traditions, and family. Furthermore, a number of studies have considered the importance of website elements in different cultures, finding that elements such as human images, portals, and peer customers had different effects on trust in different cultures (Cheng et al., 2019; Cyr et al., 2009; Sia et al., 2009).<sup>7</sup> Thus far, there has been relatively little research on designing and examining website elements for website localization that theoretically accounts for how foreign companies can attract local consumers—specifically, how foreign companies can more systematically focus localization efforts to shape perceptions held by local consumers toward their companies. Therefore, in this paper, we seek to develop a theoretically grounded examination of the design elements and strategies that can be used to localize websites in a way that bridges foreign companies with local consumers.

Some classical information systems theories (e.g., technology acceptance model, unified theory of acceptance and use of technology) have explained users’ behavioral intentions toward using IT artifacts (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Xu et al., 2017). However, these theories tend to focus on IT adoption/acceptance, rather than being specifically contextualized to website localization. Drawing upon the theoretical perspective of consumer-company identification (Bhattacharya & Sen, 2003), which is itself grounded in social identity theory and organizational identification (Bednar et al., 2020; Tajfel & Turner, 2004), we argue that when a foreign company uses website localization strategies rooted in establishing consumer-company links, local consumers increase their identification with that company and perceive the company’s website as localized. Localization thus not only pertains to the social group to which an individual belongs but also to the focal person. Website localization strategies leverage website design elements to transfer positive attitudes from in-group entities (e.g., local consumers, local companies, local websites) to out-group entities (e.g., foreigners, foreign companies, foreign websites) (Sia et al., 2009; Song & Zahedi, 2005). Based on consumer-company identification, we propose three types of website localization strategies: the web similarity strategy, the web distinctiveness strategy, and the web prestige strategy.

The web similarity strategy conveys the similarity between a foreign company and local users by displaying local elements on websites. Examples of web similarity strategies include presenting images of local employees, using local language and slogans, and displaying local symbolic cultural artifacts and patterns on the foreign company’s website. The web distinctiveness strategy highlights the distinctiveness of a foreign company by emphasizing how the products or services of a foreign company can be distinguished from those of other foreign or local competitors. Examples of web distinctiveness strategies include indicating the value-added uniqueness of a product/service through implicit or explicit comparison with local competitors or using the website to directly highlight the distinctiveness of the company by displaying the special value offered (e.g., social responsibility to the local community). Finally, the web prestige strategy leverages recognition from local users and countries to convey the prestige of the company on its website. Examples of web prestige strategies include using the company website to display well-known awards received from local industry associations and governments, highlight positive comments and feedback from local consumers, and display prominent local industry rankings.

We hypothesize that three types of website localization strategies grounded in the consumer-company identification perspective cause local users to perceive a foreign company’s website to be localized, which may lead local users to increase their intention to promote or revisit the website (i.e., consumers’ website loyalty). In this research, we further extend the theoretically grounded investigation of website localization effectiveness across different national cultures. To validate and compare website localization across cultures, we further consider the differential effects of website localization strategies in different countries. Specifically, we consider the moderating effect of a cultural dimension—individualism vs. collectivism—one of the most consequential cultural dimensions underlying an individual’s social thinking and behavior (Triandis, 2001) and a foundational “deep structure” responsible for many major differences between cultures (Greenfield, 2000). To support these contentions, we conducted between-subject online experiments in both the United States and China.

This paper makes three primary contributions. First, we introduce a novel theoretical perspective, the consumercompany identification perspective, into the literature on website localization. This new perspective helps to better evaluate the effectiveness of website localization strategies. Second, we enrich the literature and offer deeper insights into website localization by proposing various practical yet theoretically grounded design strategies (i.e., web similarity strategies, web distinctiveness strategies, and web prestige strategies) for website localization. Third, we extend our investigation of website localization across different national cultures. This is achieved by examining the moderating effect of a cultural dimension on the relationship between website localization strategies and local users’ perceived website localization. Our investigation facilitates identifying the boundary conditions associated with the effects of website localization strategies and explores similarities and differences across various cultures. Given the importance of a localized website for a foreign company, we believe that our findings have significant practical implications.

## Theoretical Background

## Social Identity Theory and Organizational Identification Perspective

As the basis of the consumer-company identification perspective, social identity theory and organizational identification are articulated first. According to social identity theory, people tend to engage in intergroup social comparison, differentiate between in-groups and out-groups, and perceive in-groups more favorably than out-groups (Hogg & Abrams, 1988). The social groups that people belong to are in-groups, while other social groups are out-groups (Billig & Tajfel, 1973). To understand their social environment and reduce uncertainty about their attitudes, beliefs, feelings, and perceptions, as well as their attitudes about themselves and other people, which are subjectively important, people categorize themselves and others as members of various social groups according to social membership categories such as gender, sports clubs, fraternities, companies, etc. (Hogg, 2000; Tajfel & Turner, 1985). Subjective uncertainty about one’s relationship with others is aversive, as it implies less control over one’s life. People seek to reduce such uncertainty, particularly in certain aspects that matter to them (and are thus subjectively important) through a self-categorization process in social groups.

Through such means, individuals form a social identity, which refers to an individual’s self-image derived from the social groups one belongs to. Social identity comprises one part of people’s self-concept, which refers to people’s beliefs about themselves (Baumeister, 1999). Once social identity is formed based on social groups, individuals strive to achieve a positive social identity to achieve better self-esteem and tend to prefer in-groups over out-groups, which is referred to as in-group favoritism. They then take actions to maximize the positive distinctiveness of their social groups, compared with other social groups, even at the expense of their own self-interest (Turner et al., 1979).

Applying social identity theory to the organizational context, researchers have studied the concepts of organizational identity and organizational identification (Ashforth & Mael, 1989). Organizational identity, which contains the core, distinctive, and enduring characteristics and values of organizations, is reflected in the organizational image and promotes employees’ organizational identification (Dutton et al., 1994; Gioia et al., 2000). Once employees identify with their organizations, they begin to integrate their organizations as a part of their own self-concept. Thereafter, employees will prefer their own organizations to other organizations and begin to internalize their organizations’ goals and values. Research has shown that organizational identification has many consequences, including in-role and extra-role behaviors, job satisfaction, and the intention to stay in or leave an organization (Riketta, 2005). Although the research on organizational identification has primarily focused on elucidating employees’ relationships with their companies/employers, organizational identification can also be applied to the consumer-company context (Bhattacharya & Sen, 2003). In the following section, we elaborate the consumer-company identification perspective, which is our theoretical basis.

## Consumer-Company Identification Perspective

Drawing on social identity theory and the organization identification perspective, Bhattacharya and Sen (2003) conceptualize consumers’ identification with companies. Similar to social identity theory, the consumer-company identification perspective posits that when a company’s identity satisfies consumers’ self-definitional needs, consumers are likely to evaluate the company’s identity as an attractive identity. The attractiveness of the company’s identity causes consumers to identify with the company and its products. Examples include fans of Tesla, Louis Vuitton, and Apple products, among others. Consumers then begin to psychologically attach themselves to the company and perceive the company more positively, compared to other companies.

Self-definitional needs comprise an individual’s need for selfcontinuity, self-distinctiveness, and self-enhancement (Dutton et al., 1994). Specifically, the need for self-continuity refers to an individual’s need for a continuous self-concept across time and situations (e.g., being a consistently refined person), the need for self-distinctiveness concerns the need to highlight one’s personal distinctiveness vis-à-vis others (e.g., cultivating an image of wearing bow ties rather than standard ties in formal situations), and the need for self-enhancement relates to the need to enhance one’s own self-esteem and develop positive self-evaluation (e.g. taking self-improvement courses, pursuing higher qualifications, etc.).

Similar to the three self-definitional needs described above, Bhattacharya and Sen (2003) propose that consumer-company identification depends on three antecedents: identity similarity, identity distinctiveness, and identity prestige. Identity similarity refers to the similarity between an individual’s and a company’s identities. People are more likely to identify with a company that they perceive as having an identity that is similar to their own or an identity to which they aspire. Kunda (1999) suggests that people have a need to maintain a continuous sense of self over time and across situations, and the need for self-continuity represents a key motive in forming identities (Vignoles et al., 2008).

Individuals can fulfill their need for self-continuity by identifying with a company whose identity matches their own sense of who they are.

Identity distinctiveness indicates to consumers that a company is different from its competitors in terms of its mission, structure, processes, climate, etc. Although individuals have a need for similarity with others in their social categories, they also have a need to distinguish themselves from others in social contexts (Tajfel & Turner, 1985). The need for distinctiveness can be satisfied when individuals identify with a company whose identity is perceived to be more unique and distinct than that of other companies.

Identity prestige refers to consumers’ perceptions that a company is well-regarded (Smidts et al., 2001). Kunda (1999) suggests that individuals need self-enhancement in order to maintain and affirm positive self-perceptions and enhance selfesteem through the process of social comparison (Festinger, 1954; Hogg, 2000; Wheeler, 1991). One way to satisfy the need for self-enhancement is to identify with a prestigious company, which can allow individuals to see themselves in the positive light of the company’s reflected glow.

In this study, we conceptualize three website localization strategies—the web similarity strategy, the web distinctive strategy, and the web prestige strategy—based on the three antecedents of consumer-company identification. We use local consumers’ perceived website localization to represent local consumers’ identification with a foreign company’s website.

## Cultural Dimensions

It has been shown that culture plays an important role in people’s different responses to various website elements (e.g., portal affiliation, human images) in a trust-building context (e.g., Cyr et al., 2009; Sia et al., 2009). It would thus be interesting to investigate whether culture also plays a role in website localization. In this study, we adopt Geert Hofstede’s cultural dimensions theory to deepen our understanding of the effect of website localization strategies. According to cultural dimensions theory, several dimensions exist in national cultures: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism/collectivism, femininity/masculinity, longterm/short-term orientation, and indulgence/restraint (Hofstede, 1980; Hofstede & Bond, 1988; Hofstede et al., 2010). Power distance describes the degree to which individuals in a society accept the inequality of power distribution; uncertainty avoidance represents the degree to which individuals feel threatened by uncertain situations; femininity/masculinity represents the degree to which individuals adhere to masculine or feminine values; long-/short-term orientation indicates the extent to which individuals in a society make choices that focus on the past, present, or future; and indulgence/restraint refers to the degree to which individuals gratify or restrict their desires to enjoy life. While many of these dimensions may have an impact on website localization, in this study, we focus particularly on the cultural dimension of individualism/collectivism, which refers to the degree to which individuals in a society separate themselves into groups, because this dimension encompasses the most significant differences among cultures (Triandis & Gelfand, 1998). Individualism/collectivism is one of the most consequential cultural dimensions underlying an individual’s social thinking and behavior (Triandis, 2001) and is a foundational “deep structure” responsible for many major differences between cultures (Greenfield, 2000). Therefore, individualism and collectivism may link to website localization directly because this dimension might help consumers distinguish “local” from “foreign” (in-group vs. out-group) websites. Further, individualism/collectivism has been shown to overlap substantially with femininity/masculinity and power distance (Schwartz, 1994) and to have high levels of correlation with uncertainty avoidance (Lim et al., 2004). Also, long/short-term orientation and indulgence/restraint have less pertinence to consumercompany identification and thus are less aligned with the focus of the present study.

Our study integrates the consumer-company identification perspective with cultural dimensions theory to support the theorizing of website localization strategies and their effects. The consumer-company identification perspective has roots in social identity theory and articulates the process of consumers’ identification with a company. Cultural dimensions theory suggests that people in different cultures emphasize the identification of social groups differently. Therefore, these two theoretical perspectives share similar identification underpinnings, which provide a basis for their integration (Whetten, 1989). Furthermore, the combination of these two theoretical perspectives enhances our understanding of website localization. Specifically, the consumer-company identification perspective helps us to develop and understand the effects of website localization strategies and their relative effectiveness, while cultural dimensions theory enables us to investigate the effects of the strategies across different cultures.

## Website Localization Strategies and Perceived Website Localization

The web similarity strategy is based on identity similarity and may be used to increase the perceived similarity between the identity of a foreign company and the identity of local users by displaying local elements on the company’s website. Local elements represent the projection of a local identity, which can help increase perceptions of identity similarity. Examples of web similarity strategies include presenting images of local employees, using local language and slogans, and displaying symbolic cultural artifacts and patterns on the foreign company’s website.

We argue that web similarity strategies increase local users perceived website localization. Because individuals are motivated by the need for a continuous sense of self-concept, a foreign company’s website that uses web similarity strategies can enable local users to fulfill their self-continuity needs by aligning the company with their own identities, making the foreign company and its website potentially more attractive to local users (Dutton et al., 1994). This could then lead local users to develop an attachment to and identify with the foreign company and its website (Kunda, 1999). Furthermore, web similarity strategies could enhance local users’ familiarity with a foreign company and thereby reduce uncertainty among local users regarding their relationship with the foreign company (Antheunis et al., 2010). Reduced uncertainty may cause local users to treat the foreign company positively and identify with it (Balliet et al., 2014), also leading local users to identify with the foreign company’s website. Based on the above reasoning, we hypothesize:

## H1: The web similarity strategy is positively associated with local consumers’ perceived website localization.

The web distinctiveness strategy is derived from identity distinctiveness and serves to highlight the distinctiveness of a foreign company by emphasizing the product or service differentiation between a foreign company and its local or foreign competitors. For example, PayPal, an American financial technology company that provides digital payment services, uses its Chinese website to emphasize the uniqueness and distinctiveness of its services (e.g., crossborder payments) compared to other local companies (e.g., Tencent and Ant Group). Other examples of web distinctiveness strategies include using the company website to emphasize the company’s advantages or highlight the special values of the company (e.g., social responsibility, commitment to environmental sustainability, support of the local community, etc.).

Using web distinctiveness strategies on a foreign company’s website may increase perceptions among local users that the company’s identity is unique and distinct in comparison to other competitors. Because people are motivated to be distinctive, web distinctiveness strategies used on a foreign company’s website can fulfill users’ self-distinctiveness needs (part of self-definitional needs during consumer-company identification), thus increasing the appeal of the website to local users, potentially leading local users to identify with the foreign company’s website. In addition, distinguishing the foreign company from its competitors through distinctiveness strategies can increase the saliency and uniqueness of the company’s identity (Mael & Ashforth, 1992) vis-à-vis other local competitors, making the company accessible, comprehensible, and highly visible to local users (Chan et al., 2012), which may reduce local users’ uncertainty about their relationship with the foreign company. Thus, reduced uncertainty and alignment with users’ self-distinctiveness needs could drive local users to identify with a foreign company and its website (Wiesenfeld et al., 2001). In summary, we hypothesize:

## H2: The web distinctiveness strategy is positively associated with local consumers’ perceived website localization.

The web prestige strategy. The foundation of this strategy is identity prestige, and the purpose of this strategy is to convey the prestige of a foreign company. Examples of web prestige strategies, particularly for new or relatively unknown companies, include using the company website to exhibit awards from local governments or industry associations, display positive comments and feedback received from local consumers, or highlight high positions in local industry rankings.

We argue that the web prestige strategy could increase local users’ perceived website localization. Using web prestige strategies on a foreign company’s website may increase perceptions among local users that the company is reputable and prestigious, which can fulfill users’ self-enhancement needs through association with a prestigious company (Ashforth & Mael, 1989), making the foreign company more attractive to them and potentially elevating local users identification with the foreign company and its website (Dutton et al., 1994). Furthermore, association with a prestigious foreign company can increase users’ self-esteem, thus facilitating the formation of positive social relationships between local users and the foreign company and potentially further heightening identification with the foreign company and its website (Mael & Ashforth, 1992). Summarizing this reasoning, we hypothesize:

## H3: The web prestige strategy is positively associated with local consumers’ perceived website localization.

## Perceived Website Localization and Consumers’ Website Loyalty

Prior literature has defined consumer loyalty as the intention to repeatedly use or support the same product/service (Oliver, 1999). Consumer loyalty involves two dimensions: attitudinal and behavioral (Dick & Basu, 1994). The attitudinal dimension of loyalty refers to consumers’ psychological propensity to engage with a product or service, while the behavioral dimension of loyalty captures consumers’ repeated behaviors toward a product or service (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001). Prior literature has suggested that consumer loyalty is related to actual purchase behaviors and company performance (Liu, 2007; Morgan & Rego, 2006).

In the context of website localization, we define consumers website loyalty as local users’ intention to promote or revisit the same foreign company’s website. Following prior literature (Chiu et al., 2013; Deng et al., 2010), we use word-of-mouth intention to represent the attitudinal dimension of consumers’ website loyalty and continuance intention to reflect the behavioral dimension of consumers’ website loyalty. Specifically, word-ofmouth intention focuses on local users’ intention to state positive things about a foreign company’s website (Choi et al., 2016), which is consistent with the attitudinal dimension. Continuance intention focuses on the repetitive use of a foreign company’s website by local users (Bhattacherjee, 2001), which is consistent with the behavioral dimension.

We posit that perceived website localization is associated with consumers’ website loyalty. Regarding the effect of perceived website localization, once local users identify with a foreign company’s website, they would categorize the foreign company’s website as an in-group website and perceive the website more favorably than other companies’ websites. The ingroup favoritism then motivates them to commit to the website (Bhattacharya & Sen, 2003). This commitment could make local users seek to increase the well-being of the website. Such local users would have positive views of the website and thus engage in positive word-of-mouth regarding the company’s website, which covers the attitudinal dimension of loyalty. This commitment could also stimulate local users’ desire to establish longer-term relationships with the foreign company and its website (Marin & Ruiz, 2007). Thus, local users would continue to use the foreign company’s website, reflecting the behavioral dimension of loyalty. The relationship between commitment and loyalty is also supported in prior literature (Fullerton, 2003; Pritchard et al., 1999). Summarizing the above reasoning, we hypothesize:

H4: Perceived website localization correlates positively with two dimensions of local consumers’ website loyalty: (a) word-ofmouth intention and (b) continuance intention.

## Moderating Effect of Individualism-Collectivism

We propose that cultural dimensions moderate the effects of website localization strategies on local users’ perceived website localization. As discussed in the theoretical background section above, we focus on one critical cultural dimension: namely, individualism-collectivism, which is a country-level measure (Clark, 1990; Hofstede et al., 2010). We theorize that web similarity strategies work more effectively in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures. Web similarity strategies may lead local users to perceive a foreign company’s website as localized by increasing the perceived similarity between local users and the foreign company. People from collectivistic cultures tend to exhibit interdependent selfconstrual and value the opinions and behaviors of referent others, especially similar others, whereas people from individualistic cultures tend to exhibit independent selfconstrual and value consistency and stability (Cialdini et al., 1999). Furthermore, people from collectivistic cultures would be more likely than people from individualistic cultures to emphasize similarity among in-group members (Triandis et al., 1988). Therefore, we expect that similarity would be more important for collectivistic cultures than for individualistic cultures and that web similarity strategies would thus be more effective in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures. Summarizing the above reasoning, we hypothesize:

H5a: The web similarity strategy has a greater impact on perceived website localization for local users from collectivistic cultures than for those from individualistic cultures.

Given that web distinctiveness strategies are associated with local users’ perceived website localization, we further argue that web distinctiveness strategies would work better in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures. Compared with people from individualistic cultures, people from collectivistic cultures tend to view themselves as part of large social units and seek greater connection with others (Markus & Kitayama, 1991). The implication here is that people from individualistic cultures tend to use others in their social group as reference points, whereas people from individualistic cultures emphasize their own individuality. Furthermore, compared to individualistic cultures, collectivistic cultures tend to engage more in social comparison (Chung & Mallery, 1999). Prior studies have identified three types of distinctiveness: difference, separateness, and social position (Vignoles et al., 2002). Difference implies distinctiveness in individual qualities such as ability, traits, and physical characteristics (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002); separateness describes distinctiveness as physical or symbolic distance from others; and social position concerns distinctiveness in terms of social relationships related to social ties, roles, and friendships.

Compared with the other two types of distinctiveness, social position distinctiveness is more pertinent to web distinctiveness strategies related to the localization of a foreign company’s website because this strategy compares a foreign company with its competitors. Social position distinctiveness not only involves the foreign company’s cooperative and competitive relationships with other local and foreign companies but also addresses possible ties with social groups of local users. Social position distinctiveness tends to be more highly valued by people from collectivistic cultures than by people from individualistic cultures since this type of distinctiveness can reflect social status within a social community (Becker et al., 2012). In contrast, individualistic cultures tend to emphasize individuality more and place less emphasis on others in a social group (Clark, 1990; Hofstede et al., 2010). We thus expect that web distinctiveness strategies are more likely to cause local users from collectivistic cultures (versus local users in individualistic cultures) to identify with a foreign company’s website. Summarizing this reasoning, we hypothesize:

H5b: The web distinctiveness strategy has a greater impact on perceived website localization for local users from collectivistic cultures than for those from individualistic cultures.

Similarly, we expect web prestige strategies to also be more effective in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures. Whereas people from collectivistic cultures tend to focus more on their community and others, seeking positive assessments from social groups to enhance their self-esteem, people from individualistic cultures tend to seek positive attributes related to their self-concept to boost self-esteem (Heine et al., 1999). Therefore, compared to people from individualistic cultures, people from collectivistic cultures tend to place more emphasis on the prestige of the social groups they belong to. Since web prestige strategies seek to convey the stature and reputation of a company, we expect that this would have a greater impact on people from collectivistic cultures because the reputation of entities that individuals associate with would presumably be more important in collectivist cultures than in individualistic cultures. To summarize the above reasoning, we hypothesize:

H5c: The web prestige strategy has a greater impact on perceived website localization for local users from collectivistic cultures than for those from individualistic cultures.

## Methodology

To validate our research model, we conducted a betweensubjects experiment using a 2 (web similarity strategy: with vs. without) by 2 (web distinctiveness strategy: with vs. without) by 2 (web prestige strategy: with vs. without) factorial design. In total, we established eight experimental groups for subjects in the U.S. and China. We describe the experimental website design, measurements, subjects, and experimental procedure below.

## Experimental Website Design

To design website similarity, distinctiveness, and prestige strategies, we searched U.S. Fortune 1000 company websites<sup>8</sup> for the Chinese market and Fortune 500 Chinese company websites<sup>9</sup> for the U.S. market. We found that 227 of the U.S. companies had local websites in China and 319 of the Chinese companies had local websites in the U.S. To ensure the validity of our analysis results, we recruited two bilingual Ph.D. students to analyze the websites and identify and categorize website design strategies according to the definitions of the three proposed website localization strategies. Any inconsistency of analysis results between the two analyzers was resolved through discussion with the authors. Based on the definitions of the three proposed website localization strategies, we identified 12 website design strategies, including five web similarity strategies, three web distinctiveness strategies, and four web prestige strategies. Appendix D presents detailed descriptions of the 12 website design strategies.

We recruited and interviewed 20 individuals to prioritize and select specific design strategies to represent each of our three web strategies. Based on the interview results, we selected (1) displaying local symbols as the web similarity strategy, (2) displaying products/service differences between a foreign company and its competitors as the web distinctiveness strategy, and (3) displaying awards received from local industry associations and governments as the web prestige strategy.

Finally, we chose snacks as the focal product for our experiment for the following reasons. First, in recent years, this product category has had the highest online sales growth in both the U.S. and China (Blazyte, 2019; Clement, 2019), and the COVID-19 pandemic has further accelerated online snack sales (Poll, 2020). Second, this product category has been adopted in many e-commerce studies (Ho & Lim, 2018;

Tam & Ho, 2006). Third, compared with other product categories (e.g., clothing, beauty, and toys), snacks seem to be a more neutral product in terms of gender preference (Sabanoglu, 2020). The experimental websites used for both the Chinese and U.S. subjects appear in Appendix E.

## Measurement

We adapted measurable items from the extant literature. Specifically, we adapted items measuring perceived website localization from Singh et al. (2006), word-of-mouth intention from Choi et al. (2016), and continuance intention from Bhattacherjee (2001). All the original scales are from the cited research. Items from original scales are adapted to the website localization context, and all items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Details of all these items appear in Appendix F. To assess the validity and reliability of these items, we conducted a pre-test by interviewing eight experts knowledgeable in human-computer interactions and electronic commerce. We revised our questionnaires based on their comments and suggestions. In addition to the English version of the measurement, we developed a Chinese version for the experimental subjects from China. Specifically, we first developed the questionnaire in English and then translated it into Chinese by using the back-translation method for Chinese subjects. We then compared the two versions for consistency and accuracy (Brislin, 1970). Inconsistencies and inaccuracies were resolved during comparison by two of the authors who are bilingual speakers.

## Subjects

We recruited experimental subjects from the United States and China because these two countries (1) differ according to cultural dimension (i.e., individualism vs. collectivism) (Hofstede, 1980), (2) have a large population of internet and e-commerce users (UNCTAD, 2018), and (3) were ranked as the top two in the world for merchandise trade value in 2016- 2017 (World Trade Organization, 2019) and for global ecommerce sales in 2019 (Lipsman, 2019). To collect responses from the U.S. subjects, we posted a recruitment call on Amazon Mechanic Turk (MTurk). We believe that MTurk serves as an adequate forum for recruiting our experimental subjects because MTurk “workers” have diverse backgrounds and experience using different companies’ websites (Jia et al., 2017). We required all recruited MTurk workers to be U.S. citizens at birth. To collect responses from subjects in China, we hired a company called WJX<sup>10</sup>. WJX has a large sample pool of more than 260 million Chinese subjects with diverse demographics. Prior studies have demonstrated the quality of data collected from WJX (Chen et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2019).

In total, we collected 756 responses from U.S. subjects and 729 responses from Chinese subjects. We excluded responses from subjects that did not answer all screening questions correctly. Specifically, we asked questions to evaluate the extent to which participants paid attention to the experimental website, including: “What is the main color of the company logo on the website?” and “How many products are displayed in the product list section on the home page?” Furthermore, this study focused on a website designed for desktop and laptop computers rather than mobile devices. Therefore, we asked: “Did you use a computer or a mobile phone to do this study?” In the end, we had 670 valid responses from U.S. subjects and 665 valid responses from Chinese subjects. Table 1 shows the demographic information for U.S. and Chinese samples and Table 2 shows the number of subjects in each experimental group. The demographic characteristics of both the U.S. and Chinese samples are reasonably consistent with e-commerce users in the U.S. and China (Statista, 2019a, 2019b).

## Experimental Procedure

To begin, we presented a cover story to participants, explaining that the researchers were working with a foreign company to design its website. In each country, the participants were randomly assigned to one of the eight experimental websites and were told to navigate the assigned website carefully. Each participant was then asked to complete a questionnaire that included the measures and manipulation checks.

To ensure the quality of our online experiment, we took several actions at different stages of our experiment, according to recent guidelines (Aguinis et al., 2021; Karahanna et al., 2018; Lowry et al., 2016). First, we provided a detailed description of our project to both MTurk and WJX subjects about our experimental purpose, experimental procedure, estimated time commitment, and compensation rules. Second, we screened invalid responses by checking the answers to our attention-trap questions and reverse-coded questions included in the questionnaire. Third, we responded in a timely manner to questions and concerns about our experiment from MTurk workers and WJX respondents. Finally, we approved or denied the compensation for completed responses and explained every rejection to the subjects. Subjects who successfully completed our screening questions were paid around US\$3.00 for their participation.

## Data Analysis and Results

## Manipulation Checks

To check our experimental manipulation, we developed corresponding manipulation check items based on definitions of the three website localization strategies, which are presented in Appendix G. Through ANOVA analysis, we found that subjects exposed to the website using the web similarity strategy were more likely to agree that some elements of the website represented their U.S./Chinese identity (U.S.: F = 87.944, p < 0.001; China: F = 141.729, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 ,$ ), and that the website included some elements that were similar to their U.S./Chinese identity (U.S.: F = 76.165, p < 0.001; China: F = 118.565, p < 0.001). Those exposed to the website using the web distinctiveness strategy were more likely to agree that the website showed that the foreign company was distinctive (U.S.: F = 16.66, p < 0.001; China: F = 11.798, p < 0.01), and that the design of the website illustrated the distinctiveness of the foreign company (U.S.: F = 5.541, p < 0.05; China: F = 15.014, p < 0.001). Those exposed to the website using the web prestige strategy were more likely to agree that the website displayed elements that conveyed the prestige of the foreign company (U.S.: F = 8.740, p < 0.01; China: F = 27.541, p < 0.001) and that the website contained elements that reflected the prestige of the foreign company (U.S.: F = 12.815, p < 0.001; China: F = 10.642, p < 0.01). Therefore, we concluded that the manipulation of all website localization strategies was successful.

We also tested whether the subjects on each research site differed in terms of age, online shopping experience, internet use experience, experience using the official websites of companies, and gender across eight experimental conditions. We found that there were no significant differences among subjects in terms of age (U.S.: F = 0.791, p = 0.595; China: F = 1.296, p = 0.250), online shopping experience (U.S.: F = 1.046, p = 0.397; China: F = 1.214, p = 0.292), internet experience (U.S.: F = 1.96, p = 0.364; China: F = 1.571, p = 0.141), and experience using the official websites of companies (U.S.: F = 1.537, p = 0.152; China: F = 1.195, p = 0.303). We also conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests, finding that there was no significant difference in gender across the eight experimental conditions (U.S.: chi-square = 2.483, p = 0.928; China: chi-square = 9.072, p = 0.248).

## CBSEM Analysis

We employed a covariance-based structural equation modeling (CBSEM) technique using AMOS 21.0. The CBSEM technique compensates for more measurement errors and estimates path strengths more accurately than other techniques like regression and partial least squares techniques (Goodhue et al., 2012). Following the two-stage methodology suggested by Anderson and Gerbing (1988), we sequentially developed and evaluated the measurement model and analyzed the structural model to test our hypotheses for both the U.S. and Chinese samples. In this study, we used the manipulation check items of website localization strategies as the measurements in our analysis to capture the effects of website localization strategies (Sia et al., 2009).

## Measurement Model

The comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) extracted for U.S. (CFI = 0.946, TLI = 0.923, GFI = 0.918, RMSEA = 0.077) and Chinese models (CFI = 0.939, TLI = 0.912, GFI = 0.919, RMSEA = 0.075) indicate an acceptable level of model fit (Gefen et al., 2011). These two sets of indices offer support for construct validity. For the convergent and discriminant validities of constructs for both the U.S. and Chinese models (see Appendix H), the value of the average variance extracted (AVE), item loadings, and internal composite reliability (ICR) are all above the suggested threshold, indicating an acceptable level of convergent validity and reliability (Bagozzi et al., 1991; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Thatcher et al., 2018). Discriminant validity is also demonstrated since the square root of each construct’s AVE is greater than the correlation coefficients between the construct and other constructs (Fornell & Larker, 1981).

To assess any potential multicollinearity, we calculated variance inflation factor (VIF) scores. Given that we used two dependent variables (i.e., word-of-mouth intention and continuance intention) in our research model, we analyzed the entire model as two basic regressions on the two dependent variables for the U.S. and Chinese samples, respectively (Mason & Perreault, 1991). As shown in Appendix I, none of the variance inflation factor values are higher than 10 (Hair et al., 1995). Therefore, we determined that multicollinearity is not a major concern for our study.

We carefully checked for the potential of common method bias. No single factor was found to account for the majority of the variance by using the Harman test and no correlation coefficients of any pair of constructs were very high (r > 0.90) (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Therefore, we determined that common method bias was not a major concern for our study. To summarize, we found the quality of our measurement model to be adequate to test our hypotheses in both the U.S. and Chinese models.

## Structural Model

By using maximum likelihood estimation, we analyzed the hypothesized effects in our research model for the U.S. and Chinese samples. The means and standard deviations of all the variables for U.S. and Chinese samples are shown in Table 3. Standardized coefficients and the significance of each path in our research model are depicted in Figures 2a and 2b. The results reveal that the web similarity strategy (U.S: β = 0.492, p < 0.001; China: β = 0.235, p < 0.001), web distinctiveness strategy (U.S: β = 0.264, p < 0.001; China: β = 0.545, p < 0.001), and web prestige strategy (U.S: β = 0.146, p < 0.05; China: β = 0.200, p < 0.001 ) had significant effects on perceived website localization, while perceived website localization had significant impacts on word-of-mouth intention (U.S: β = 0.887, p < 0.001; China: β = 0.924, p < 0.001 ) and continuance intention (U.S: β = 0.914, p < 0.001; China: β = 0.978, p < 0.001 ). To confirm the effect of website localization strategies on perceived website localization, we conducted ANOVA tests and found the ANOVA results to be similar to the results of the SEM analysis. The mean values of the key variables across the experiment groups also provide additional empirical support (see Appendix J for ANOVA results and the mean values). Therefore, H1-H4 are all supported for both the U.S. and Chinese models.

## Moderating Effects Analysis of Cultural Dimension

According to Hofstede et al. (2010), the U.S. ranks higher in individualism (U.S.: 91 vs. China: 37) than China. We also measured the individualism of Americans and Chinese participants in our sample directly and found that they do have significant differences in this cultural dimension (F = 52.631, p < 0.001). Therefore, we tested the moderating effect of individualism-collectivism by comparing the effect of website localization strategies on perceived website localization in the U.S. and China, respectively. To leverage the multiple group analysis in CBSEM (Qureshi & Compeau, 2009), it is necessary to first establish the invariance of the measurement model and then compare the paths across the different models. According to Steenkamp and Baumgartner (1998), it is necessary to confirm the invariance of metric and factor variance for coefficient comparison. To decide whether the measurement is invariant for different groups, previous literature has typically used the chi-square difference test (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). However, Cheung and Rensvold (1999) found that the difference in the comparative fit index (CFI) is superior to the difference in chi-square for measurement invariance testing since the chi-square difference test is sensitive to sample size. In particular, they claim that changes in CFI of -0.01 or less may indicate invariance. The invariance test results are shown in Table 4. Although the omnibus invariance of the measurement model was not confirmed in our testing, the invariance of the factor variance and metric was demonstrated. Therefore, we proceeded to compare the effect of website localization strategies on perceived website localization. We performed a resampling bootstrap procedure in AMOS and present the effects comparison results in Table 5.

According to the path comparison results, the effects of the web similarity strategy on perceived website localization in the U.S. and Chinese models, respectively, did not differ from each other $( p \mathrm { - v a l u e } = 0 . 3 1 7 > 0 . 1 0 )$ . Therefore, H5a is not supported. For the web distinctiveness strategy, our analysis results show a significantly stronger impact on perceived website localization for the Chinese model than for the U.S. model (p-value = 0.001 < 0.01). We also found that the effects of the web prestige strategy on perceived website localization were significantly stronger in the Chinese model than in the U.S. model (p-value = 0.018 < 0.05). In other words, the web distinctiveness and web prestige strategies worked better in the collectivistic culture we studied. We further illustrate two significant moderating effects in Figures 3a and 3b. Our findings indicate support for H5b and H5c.

## Discussion and Implications

Based on the theoretically driven consumer-company identification framework, we propose and validate three website localization strategies: the web similarity strategy, the web distinctiveness strategy, and the web prestige strategy. We found that all proposed website localization strategies had significant effects on local users’ perceived website localization. We also found that website localization enhanced local users’ website loyalty (i.e., word-of-mouth intention and continuance intention). The findings are consistent with the literature on consumer-company identification (Bhattacharya & Sen, 2003). The findings also show the significance of investigating website localization from the perspective of consumers’ identification and echo the motivation of our study.

We also compared the effects of the three website localization strategies across cultures. We found the effects of the web distinctiveness and prestige strategies on perceived website localization to be stronger in China than in the U.S., which verifies our hypotheses that the web distinctiveness and prestige strategies have greater impacts on perceived website localization for local users from collectivistic cultures than for those from individualistic cultures. These results also imply that because people from collectivistic cultures emphasize social comparison more strongly (Chung & Mallery, 1999), the web distinctiveness and prestige strategies, which provide a significant and feasible basis for local users to make comparisons with other foreign companies’ websites, are more important to local users from collectivistic cultures than they are to users from individualistic cultures. Our findings indicate that the effect of the web similarity strategy on perceived website localization was not significantly different in the Chinese and U.S. samples. One possible explanation is that cultural symbols used in the web similarity strategy may work equally well in both collectivistic and individualistic cultures—in this case, the effect of the web similarity strategy may not differ significantly across individualistic and collectivistic cultures.

## Theoretical Implications

This paper makes several theoretical contributions. First, we introduce a novel theoretical perspective, the consumercompany identification perspective, into website localization research to better understand the effectiveness of website localization strategies from a consumer perspective. Designing and evaluating localized websites from a consumer perspective is an important but underinvestigated topic (Xu et al., 2010). Prior literature on website localization has generally focused on categorizing website strategies based on cultural frameworks (Benmamoun et al., 2019; Singh & Matsuo, 2004). However, drawing on the consumer-company identification perspective, our study proposes the concept of local consumers’ identification with a foreign company’s website, which supports the explanation and prediction of the effectiveness of website localization strategies. Therefore, our study bridges the gap between a foreign company’s website and local consumers by theorizing the links between the identity of local consumers, organizational identity, and consumer-company identification.

Second, we propose and identify various practical yet theoretically grounded design strategies for website localization. We contextualize the consumer-company identification by adapting its core antecedents, including identity similarity, identity distinctiveness, and identity prestige, to the web similarity strategy, the web distinctiveness strategy, and the web prestige strategy in a website localization context. Conceptualizing and harnessing the antecedents of consumer-company identification allowed us to better understand the website localization context. Furthermore, these three proposed website localization strategies are the theory-driven antecedents of website localization. These theory-driven antecedents not only provide actionable theoretical insights regarding website localization but also offer opportunities to validate our theoretical perspectives. Therefore, we contribute to the literature on website localization by adding new knowledge about theoretically derived antecedents of consumer-company identification for website localization (Singh et al., 2010; Singh et al., 2006).

Third, we investigate the impact of culture, specifically the moderating effect of a cultural dimension on the relationship between website localization strategies and local users perceived website localization. The investigation of the moderating effect of the cultural dimension facilitates identifying the boundary conditions associated with the role of website localization strategies. The identification of boundary conditions helps to define and improve the scope of application of website localization strategies. Since our research model is established based on the consumercompany identification perspective, our study also conveys implications regarding the boundary conditions of the consumer-company identification perspective (Busse et al., 2017). Specifically, our study shows the web distinctiveness strategy and web prestige strategy to be more effective for website localization in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures.

Fourth, we contribute to the literature on website localization by demonstrating not only why but also when website localization strategies are effective. Why and when are both critical components for theory development and theory testing (Colquitt & Zapata-Phelan, 2007). Moreover, we integrate the consumer-company identification perspective and cultural dimensions theory to provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis for understanding website localization strategies that span cultures. These two theoretical perspectives are proximal to the phenomena explained because both theories concern individuals’ identification with and relationship to social groups (Brewer & Yuki, 2007). These two perspectives are also compatible in their theoretical assumptions because they both assume that social groups lie at the basis of people’s social behaviors and are therefore amenable to theoretical integration (Okhuysen & Bonardi, 2011). We blend these two theories to establish a comprehensive and compatible theoretical viewpoint from which to understand website localization (Oswick et al., 2011).

Finally, our study contributes to design science research by implementing a theory in the context of designing a localized website. Gregor and Hevner (2013) suggested that justificatory knowledge from any descriptive theory could be utilized to inform IT artifact construction. The consumercompany identification perspective has been rarely applied in design science research. We adopt and demonstrate the consumer-company perspective as an appropriate kernel theory to derive the three website strategies and to guide the design of localized websites. Adopting an external mode of design theorizing makes it possible to analyze, describe, and predict the effect of designed website localization strategies (Baskerville et al., 2018). Therefore, according to the knowledge contribution framework (Gregor & Hevner, 2013), our work contributes to the knowledge of design science as improvement research by applying a theory that enables the design of effective and novel solutions to website localization.

## Practical Implications

Our study offers several implications for practitioners. First, this study provides guidance for designing localized websites. We demonstrate that website localization strategies are effective and that website designers could thus employ such strategies to achieve website localization. Given the effectiveness of web similarity strategies, web distinctiveness strategies, and web prestige strategies, website designers could develop variations of these strategies to localize a foreign company’s website by enhancing similarities between local consumers and the foreign company, highlighting the distinctiveness of the foreign company, compared with its competitors, and advertising the prestige or reputation of the foreign company.

Second, our results suggest that local consumers’ perceptions of website localization play an important role in their loyalty intentions toward foreign companies’ websites (i.e., word-of-mouth intention and continuance intention). Therefore, website designers of foreign companies should pay more attention to evaluating the extent of website localization in order to elicit more positive responses from local consumers. The instruments we used in our study could complement other indices used by foreign companies to assess and evaluate their website localization efforts.

Third, our study demonstrates that culture plays a critical role in website design. The use of specific web localization strategies to enhance perceived website localization likely depends on the cultural traits of potential users. In this study, we focus on one critical cultural trait: individualismcollectivism. Our study shows that web distinctiveness and prestige strategies would be expected to be more effective in collectivistic cultures (e.g., China, Japan, and Korea) than in individualistic cultures (e.g., United States, Germany, Australia), and web similarity strategies would be expected to be equally effective in both types of cultures. In other words, when a foreign company, particularly a new or relatively unknown foreign company, displays web distinctiveness strategies (e.g., the highlights of differences between a foreign company and its competitors) and web prestige strategies (e.g., awards from local industry associations ) on their website, users in collectivistic cultures will perceive the website to be more natural and local than users in individualistic cultures; However, when a foreign company’s website displays web similarity strategies (e.g., national symbols), their website will be perceived to be natural and local to users in both cultures. Therefore, we suggest that website designers should consider cultural traits when designing websites for foreign companies.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has some limitations that could motivate future research. First, we only focus on individualism-collectivism in this study since it is one of the most salient cultural dimensions. However, other cultural dimensions, such as uncertainty avoidance, masculinity vs. femininity, and power distance, may nevertheless have important impacts on website localization design (Singh & Matsuo, 2004; Singh et al., 2009). Therefore, future research could examine and compare the moderating effects of other cultural dimensions in light of the individualism-collectivism effects that we have identified. Such a comparison could enrich the understanding of website localization design.

Second, beyond culture, other contingent factors of website localization strategies could also be explored. Although the extant literature discusses some contingent factors of website localization (e.g., strategic priority, translation management, customer acquisition) (Singh et al. 2010), additional factors could be discovered through the examination of organizational and industrial perspectives, such as global orientation, international experience, market competition, and media availability (Zou & Volz, 2010). Therefore, future studies could investigate additional contingent factors and their effects on corresponding website localization strategies.

Third, we focus specifically on snacks as the target product in this study. Our results might offer more direct implications for foreign companies selling nondurable products like snacks than for those selling durable products or services. Also, beyond the company’s official website, consumers might access information about brands or products through other digital channels or even omnichannels. In this study, we focus on desktop websites because research has shown that desktop websites are likely to be the first channel that local users use to search for and explore information about a foreign company, particularly a new or relatively unknown company (Singh & Jang, 2020). Furthermore, a recent report has shown that conversion rates are higher for desktops than for tablets and smartphones (BroadbandSearch, 2020). However, different digital channels (e.g., mobile websites) may involve other localization elements and may have different impacts on consumers’ behaviors (Verhoef et al., 2015). Future research on website localization might take the channel or other product or service characteristics into consideration.

Fourth, our experimental design focuses on the home page rather than the whole website. The scope of this research addresses website elements and features based on the consumer-company identification perspective. There could be other website elements, such as navigational structure, the content of other pages, etc., that impact perceptions of website localization. Our experimental website was designed to focus on the main localization features, and we purposefully decided not to design an entire website because of potential differences in how subjects navigate websites, which could have confounded whether the subjects noticed the website localization features. We thus designed the website as the home page to control for possible differences in navigating and noticing different features or information presented on multiple pages. Thus, future research on website localization should take different design elements into consideration.

Finally, we tested our developed website localization strategies and their effects using online experiments rather than laboratory experiments. To ensure the quality of our online experiments, we conducted several actions in different stages of our experiment following recent guidelines (Aguinis et al., 2021; Karahanna et al., 2018; Lowry et al., 2016). However, based on experimental conditions, further studies could consider some additional measures, such as adding qualitative open-ended questions in the planning stage or monitoring MTurk worker communities in the implementation stage. Moreover, laboratory experiments may have advantages in terms of controlling confounding effects from the user’s environment, recognizing the identity of participants, and ensuring greater internal validity (Lowry et al., 2016). Therefore, future studies should consider testing our proposed website localization strategies by using other methods such as laboratory experiments or field experiments.

## Conclusion

This study investigates website localization from a novel theoretical perspective: the consumer-company identification perspective. Based on this perspective, we propose and validate three website localization strategies: the web similarity strategy, the web distinctiveness strategy, and the web prestige strategy. In line with cultural dimensions theory, we also identified the differential effects of these three website localization strategies on local consumers’ perceived website localization for different cultural settings. The integration of the consumer-company identification perspective with cultural dimensions theory provides a more comprehensive basis for the study of website localization.
