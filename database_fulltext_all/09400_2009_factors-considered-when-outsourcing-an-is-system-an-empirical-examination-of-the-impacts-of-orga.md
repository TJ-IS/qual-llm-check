# Factors considered when outsourcing an IS system: an empirical examination of the impacts of organizational size, strategy and the object of a decision

Bjarne Berg and Antonis C. Stylianou\*

Department of Business Information Systems & Operations Management, The Belk College of Business Administration, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, NC, U.S.A.

## Abstract

As IT expenditures have been growing over the last few years, organizations have started to scrutinize them more closely and some are deciding to outsource parts of their Information Systems (IS) operations. Unfortunately, there is a lack of research on the impact that the object of an outsourcing decision may have on the factors considered when making such a decision. The impact of organizational size and the firm’s strategy has also not been conclusively established in the literature. This paper examines and compares the different supplier, internal, technology and cost factors considered when outsourcing Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) or Decision Support Systems (DSSs) (the object of a decision). It also examines the divergent decision factors for large, medium and small organizations, and the competitive strategy’s impact on the factors that are considered. The paper is based on a study with samples from four large SAP Conferences and includes 1889 individuals working in organizations that use enterprise resource planning software. This research found that the object being outsourced, the firm’s competitive strategies, and the organizational size are factors that significantly influence the outsourcing decision process. We found that the relative importance of decision factors for the outsourcing of OLTP is significantly different from those for a DSS and that, where the outsourcing object is of strategic importance, there is a closer attention to internal factors. Our findings confirm that outsourcing strategies are aligned with organizational strategies. For example, cost factors dominate in the outsourcing decision among organizations that employ a low-costs strategy as compared to those following a differentiation or niche strategy. Also, compared to firms pursuing other competitive strategies, for the outsourcing of DSS, differentiators place a significantly higher emphasis on supplier factors. Regarding the role of company size, we found significant differences in the importance given to supplier, internal, technology, and cost factors by organizations of different sizes. For example, compared to smaller organizations, larger organizations gave less importance to supplier and technology factors and more importance to cost factors.

## Introduction

Expenditures on IT over the last decade have remained high and are expected to grow 4.5% to over 3.4 trillion dollars in 2008 (Gartner, 2008). Given the resulting pressures on the overall budgets, organizations have started to scrutinize such expenditures more closely. In an effort to remedy perceived inefficiencies in internal IS operations, reduce costs, and have easier access to new technologies, some organizations are deciding to outsource parts of IS operations. At the same time the outsourcing model has matured and providers have increased their capabilities and became more stable, internal IS departments have retooled and refocused in order to become more competitive with outside alternatives. As a result, outsourcing decisions continue to be important as they have become more common.

We define outsourcing as ‘the use of external agencies to process, manage, or maintain internal data and to provide information related services’ (Smith et al., 1998), whether offshored or operated domestically. IS outsourcing has been studied extensively. Some studies focused on the implementation of the outsourcing decision, implications to nations, employees, and organizations (Dibbern et al., 2004). Others have examined the success factors related to industry process standardization (Wullenweber & Weitzel, 2007) and some have created predictive models for outsourcing success. A distinct track of research in the outsourcing field examines the decision-making process of the outsourcers. With regards to this track, Dibbern et al. (2004) identified a gap in the literature in the area of comparative studies that include the object that is to be outsourced. This paper attempts to fill some of this gap by exploring how the object of an outsourcing decision (what to outsource), organizational size, and strategy lead companies to emphasize different decision factors (i.e., supplier, internal, technology, and cost factors). This insight could have significant benefits to service providers. Armed with this information, sellers may target the value proposition of their services based on firm size, strategy and the object of the outsourcing decision, instead of merely relying on cost as the overriding factor. Beside the practical benefits of understanding these links, the results of this study could also benefit future outsourcing research by highlighting the importance of appropriate research scope, that is, whether future research should be more narrowly focused on organizations of similar sizes, competitive strategies or grouped around the object being considered for outsourcing.

To determine what object to select in our study, we examined the software offerings of the largest enterprise resource planning (ERP) software provider in the world, SAP AG. With revenues of \$15 billion in 2007, the German company has two distinct software offerings. The Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) software known as R/3, and the Decision Support System (DSS) software known as NetWeaver. Each of these solutions are marketed, sold and licensed separately by the company and the software offerings are, therefore, distinct in scope and purpose and have a significant market size. In our survey, the term ‘OLTP’ refers to the R/3 system and the term ‘DSS’ refers to the NetWeaver system. As the NetWeaver software runs on separate servers, has a completely different user interface, different trade magazines and conferences, the distinction in the SAP community between OLTP and DSS is very clear.

For data gathering, we surveyed 1889 employees of SAP customers at national SAP conferences over a period of 8 months. Our large-scale survey focuses on how outsourcing decisions are made. We do not examine if the subsequent actions of the companies are successful or not. Instead we focus on the factors used in the decisionmaking process.

The next section contains a review of the literature leading to the development of hypotheses. That is followed by a section describing the sampling and instrument development procedures. Then the findings are presented and discussed and the paper closes with the conclusions section.

## Literature review and hypothesis development

## Firm strategy and outsourcing decisions

A fundamental principle of business is that a firm’s strategy should drive its actions. Porter outlines three fundamental competitive strategies that a firm can undertake: a low-cost provider of products and services, a niche player, or a differentiator (Porter, 1985). These strategies can either drive the organization’s core competencies or are sometimes created based on the core competencies already in place (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990). Traditionalists have argued that for IS outsourcing to be effective, it should only be considered when what is outsourced is not a core activity of the organization and does not provide a strategic differentiation (Lacity et al., 1996). Others have argued that the first step in outsourcing is to create a strategic objective of the transaction (Stark et al., 2006). There are also several proposed guidelines for when to outsource IS functions based on a firm’s strategic positioning (Apte & Mason, 1995; Cronk & Sharp, 1995; Earl, 1996) or a strategy’s possible need for dynamic capabilities (Eisenhart & Martin, 2000) that may be provided through outsourcing.

Unfortunately, the impact of a firm’s competitive strategy on the IS outsourcing decision is weakly supported in the literature. Despite proposed models, a link between core strategies and outsourcing has been hard to establish (Smith et al., 1998). For example, a link between the strategy types, such as prospector, defender or analyzer, and the outsourcing decision has not been conclusively established (Teng et al., 1995). Also, researchers found only partial support that an organization’s strategy may have some moderating effects on outsourcing decisions (Grover et al., 1994a). Others have found conflicting empirical support for a link between strategy and outsourcing decisions when examining the negative impacts of outsourcing core competencies (Currie & Wilcox, 1998; Hancox & Hackney, 1999).

When viewed in the context of the object being outsourced we see that more consistent results have emerged. Links were found between computer firms’ core competencies and a tendency to keep these in-house (Slaughter & Ang, 1996) and between strategic goals and outsourcing contract extensions (Nam et al., 1996). However, in view of the overall conflicting findings of a link between strategy and outsourcing, we have yet to see conclusive evidence for a strong relationship between a firm’s strategy and the decision to outsource.

## Supplier factors

Supplier factors are often post hoc a decision to outsource (i.e., considered during vendor selection). However, supplier factors are also an important part of the decision whether to outsource at all. If few realistic choices exist, companies are unlikely to be early adopters of new processes and technology services. Organizations may look at established success models to determine what factors they should emphasize when making the decision to outsource, as well as during the vendor selection process after the decision has been made.

Predictive models for outsourcing success based on empirical studies include measures such as: the supplier’s expertize with the client’s IS operations, IS operations in general, and outsourcing; the supplier’s investment in the outsourcing process; supplier’s human resources specificity; and the external relatedness of the organizations (Gable & Sharp, 1992). An area of contention is the impact of the number of available suppliers on an outsourcing decision. Nam et al. (1996) did not find support for this measure, whereas Bahli & Rivard (2003) found that the existence of only a small number of suppliers affected the success of an outsourcing effort. Others have found that the number of suppliers may not be as important as the mere presence of one in the relevant technology or process area (Ang & Cummings, 1997).

As the number of available suppliers increases, the feasibility of outsourcing increases and this could have an impact on the decision-making process and the likelihood that organizations will engage in outsourcing activities (Levina & Ross, 2003). Our research explores the relative importance given to this measure by organizations.

Additional supplier factors validated by outsourcing studies include the supplier’s demonstrated understanding of organizational needs, apparent trustworthiness of the supplier’s employees, and the supplier’s prior experience with the object being outsourced (Gable, 1996). In addition, companies have reported that obtaining access to a country’s skilled workforce and taking advantage of a supplier’s host country’s universities rank among the top five reasons for outsourcing (Palvia, 1995). Our research includes all these measures individually and also groups them as an overall average score of all supplier factors. Although heuristics and fuzzy logic models exist that suggest ways to provide weights to the vendor selection process (Yao & Hongli, 2007), the use of averages is frequent in outsourcing research (Nam et al., 1996; Bahli & Rivard, 2003; Charalambos & Nakatsu, 2008). Actually, in much of the research published, the use of averages is not directly stated, but manifests itself by not weighting the factors used. To provide insights into our findings, we report scores for all individual measures (Table 10) as well as the group averages (Table 7). The measures obtained from the literature and used in our study are summarized in Table 1.

## Internal factors

In addition to supplier factors, organizations are also looking at internal factors when considering outsourcing. These include the need to sustain 24/7 operations, perceived improvements in IS flexibility and agility (Djavanshir, 2005) and the internal relatedness of the processes being outsourced (Gable & Sharp, 1992; Bahli & Rivard, 2003). Processes that are outside the normal operations of a company, or that are not deemed a good fit with the core operations of a business model are more likely to be outsourced. Other internal factors may include an organization’s expertise with outsourcing in general, or in a specific related area, as well as the organization’s expertise in IS operations (Djavanshir, 2005). It is more likely that organizations that have little familiarity with IS operations will outsource those functions. Our research includes all these measures individually and also groups them as an overall average score of all internal factors (Tables 7–10).

## Technology factors

Some organizations choose to outsource IS due to uncertainty around technology choice, or task complexity (Djavanshir, 2005). Large-scale ERP systems, such as SAP R/3, have a high degree of complexity and an increasing number of organizations have, therefore, decided not to acquire these systems. Instead some are relying on outsourcing as a means to get access to the ERP technology without making formal software or hardware purchases. This lack of commitment to a specific system is frequently motivated by perceptions regarding the frequency of technology changes (Nam et al., 1996; Ang & Cummings, 1997) and uncertainty regarding the longterm IS strategic direction of the organizations due to events such as mergers and divestitures. The technology factors in our research, therefore, include the perceived uncertainty associated with a long-term DSS and OLTP technology choice, and the perceived complexity of these systems.

Justifications include beliefs that the outsourcer has ‘better economies of scale, tighter control over fringe benefits, better access to lower cost labor pools and a more focused expertise in managing IS’ (Smith et al., 1998). This economic perspective is echoed in many studies (Arnett & Jones, 1994; Alpar & Saharia, 1995; McFarland & Nolan, 1995; Palvia, 1995). In this economic perspective, we find two consistent measures: labor costs reduction and/or overall costs reduction.

As labor costs are a function of an organization’s overall cost structure, outsourcing is sometimes used to change the overall labor cost structure for the whole organization. In these cases, outsourcing may be used as a tool for an overall reduction in compensation even for those jobs that remain (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992).

The distinct importance of labor costs was confirmed in a recent survey of 114 senior IS managers who ranked labor cost as the most important item considered when outsourcing IS (Djavanshir, 2005), well ahead of other factors such as flexibility and agility and a set of supplier factors. In this paper, we do not consider implicit costs or non-tangible costs, but make a distinction between labor costs and overall financial costs (measured as the total reduction in spending on those activities being outsourced).

## The object of the outsourcing decision

SAP suppliers in the outsourcing market have increased substantially. A 2005 survey of 872 outsourcing vendors identified over 115 companies providing SAP outsourcing services (Brown & Wilson, 2005). With the increase in suppliers and outsourcing activity, companies using SAP face increasing peer pressure to outsource. However, they have two core decisions to make. First, should they outsource at all, and second, should they outsource the transaction processing system (SAP R/3) and/or the DSS (NetWeaver). In general, outsourcing of OLTP allows management to focus available IS talent on important and strategic IT applications (such as DSS), rather than the mundane and routine transaction processing activities (Wang & Yang, 2007).

We suggest that DSSs are at the heart of the firm’s ability to differentiate their product and services and they are, therefore, more important to those who employ that strategy. As DSS are seldom highly standardized (SAP NetWeaver is only partially standardized). organizations that are differentiators, and consider outsourcing these systems, may be more exposed to the risk that the supplier may not have the ability to support them. We would, therefore, expect that organizations that are differentiators are placing a higher emphasis on factors relating to the suppliers. Such factors include trust, experience (Gable, 1996), supplier’s expertize, employees, investments in technology and facilities, prior experience in IS operations (Bahli & Rivard, 2003), the number of suppliers available (Levina & Ross, 2003) and access to a skilled labor force and educational system (Djavanshir, 2005). The link between strategy and the object of the outsourcing decision is important, as it is likely that the object may be closely linked to a firm’s strategy in certain cases, while unimportant in others (Teng et al., 1995). We, therefore, propose the following hypothesis:

H1 When outsourcing a decision support system, supplier factors are more important to organizations that employ a differentiation strategy than those employing other strategies.

A survey of 357 firms, suggested that a strategy of total outsourcing, without consideration of what is being outsourced, can be very dangerous due to the inflexibility it creates, and that managers should be very selective in deciding which IT functions are outsourced (Gonzalez et al., 2005). We agree with option theory that suggests that DSSs add a longer time horizon between problem/ opportunity discovery and the need for actions, and, therefore, have a high intrinsic value to managing an organization (Simon, 1960). Transaction processing systems, on the other hand, may be viewed more as common infrastructure often standardized by ERP software packages such as SAP and Oracle. Although it is likely that, given these differences, different decision criteria will be used depending on the object of the outsourcing decisions (what to outsource), we found no research demonstrating that such differences actually exist, which leads us to the following null hypotheses:

H2a There is no difference between the importance given to supplier factors considered in a DSS outsourcing decision and that of an OLTP outsourcing decision.

H2b There is no difference between the importance given to internal factors considered in a DSS outsourcing decision and that of an OLTP outsourcing decision.

H2c There is no difference between the importance given to technology factors considered in a DSS outsourcing decision and that of an OLTP outsourcing decision.

H2d There is no difference between the importance given to cost factors considered in a DSS outsourcing decision and that of an OLTP outsourcing decision.

## Firm size and outsourcing decisions

Research has been more successful in establishing a link between a firm’s size and outsourcing decisions. In a study of 226 banks, Ang & Cummings (1997) found that firm size affected factors such as supplier presence, technology uncertainty, task complexity, and asset specificity (object of decision) on IS outsourcing. They also found that these factors did not affect small banks as much as large banks in their conformity to peers. As resources were constrained, both large and small banks were induced toward conforming to peer influence on outsourcing. However, for large banks, factors such as production cost advantages and supplier presence significantly strengthened, while factors such as asset specificity and task complexity weakened. A follow-up study in 1998 of 243 banks confirmed these findings (Ang & Straub, 1998). In their discussion, Ang and Straub state that ‘no doubt there are other elements to firm size that would also explain why this relationship appears so consistently as a factor associated with outsourcing in the IS literature’ (Brynjolfsson et al., 1994; Grover et al., 1994a, b). To examine these relationships, we propose the following set of null hypotheses:

H3a In an IS outsourcing decision, there is no difference between the importance given to supplier factors by organizations of different sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations).

H3b In an IS outsourcing decision, there is no difference between the importance given to internal factors by organizations of different sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations).

H3c In an IS outsourcing decision, there is no difference between the importance given to technology factors by organizations of different sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations).

H3d In an IS outsourcing decision, there is no difference between the importance given to cost factors by organizations of different sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations).

In their bank study, Ang & Straub (1998) found a consistent strong negative relationship between firm size and the degree of outsourcing. Another study by Sobol & Apte (1995), however, found a significant positive relationship between firm size and overall outsourcing. Given the inconsistency in the past literature, we hypothesize a relationship but not a direction.

H4 There is no difference in the percent of IT budget that firms of different sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations) plan to spend on outsourcing.

As there are several pros and cons to measuring a firm’s size by its revenues (Mitra & Chaya, 1996), in this paper we classified companies as Fortune-500, 1000 and smaller organizations by both revenues and the number of employees (Fortune, 2006).

## Competitive strategy

When examining the literature we found an implied agreement that a relationship exists (or should exist) between the various strategies and the relative importance of the factors considered. Based on Porter’s (1998) work, we would expect that organizations that employ a low-cost strategy will need to rely heavily on being highly efficient in their transaction processing. This indicates that firms following a low-cost strategy may regard their OLTP systems as more crucial to their long-term success

On the other hand, firms following niche and differentiation strategies rely more on market information and may regard their DSSs as more crucial to their long-term success.

H5 Organizations that employ a niche or a differentiation strategy assign less weight to cost factors in an IS outsourcing decision than organizations that are primarily competing using a low cost strategy.

## Sampling and instrument

Based on the set of established measures and the instruments used in the literature (see Table 1), a survey instrument was created. The instrument was reviewed by a group of five IT professors from two institutions as well as a set of graduate students. Following this input, a pilot survey was conducted (n ¼ 23) with the preliminary instrument to examine it for clarity, terminology, and organization. The published revenues and number of employees for the world’s largest 1000 organizations were used as measures of company size (Fortune, 2006). The survey results were analyzed using correlations for convergent and divergent validity. Strong correlations within the measures were established indicating good convergent validity and expected low correlations were found between measures, indicating divergent validity of the instrument.

To examine the outsourcing factors being considered by companies of different sizes and competitive strategies, we then administered the surveys at four national SAP conferences over a period of 8 months. The conferences were attended by Chief Information Officers, General Managers, Vice Presidents and managers of Business Systems, Consulting, Finance, ERP, Financial Planning, Global Projects, and IT and by functional professionals such as ERP Program Managers, BI Team Leads and ERP Directors (WIS Publishing, 2007). The combined attendance at these conferences was over 15,800 individuals from corporations in the U.S.A. and the rest of the world. The convenience sample resulted in 1889 returned surveys of 2381 distributed. Of the 1889 respondents, 44 did not identify company size, and 36 did not identify a strategy (response ¼ ‘n/a’). Also, 270 respondents did not engage in any form of outsourcing and were not planning to do so (within 3 years) or had no responses. The overall usable sample size for most research questions was, therefore, 1575 (see Table 2).

After administrating the survey at the first two conferences, we did a comparison of the first two survey results and found no significant differences between the survey results when measuring the constructs, indicating a good test–retest reliability. Cronbach alphas were also calculated for each of the scales on the instrument and scores ranged from 0.69 to 0.83. Although the alpha coefficients for the sub-scales were not extremely high, Liu & Zumbo (2007) state that the magnitude of the coefficient is directly related to the number of items in the scale and note that, for shorter scales, alpha coefficients in the high 0.60s or 0.70s are acceptable. Separately, descriptive statistics of each factor based on firm size and competitive strategy were calculated (see Table 3).

We also performed factor analysis with the extraction method of principal component analysis and Varimax with Kaiser normalization as the rotation method to test the survey instrument. The rotation converged in six iterations and identified four distinct components (Table 4). Further analysis of the component matrix demonstrated a clear separation of the four components with rotation sums of squared loaded eigenvalues of 5.71, 3.17, 2.86, and 2.04 respectively, and a cumulative 72.5% of the variance explained (Table 5). This supports prior research (see Table 1) that indicated that supplier, technology, internal and cost factors are distinct in an outsourcing decision-making process and also confirms convergent and divergent validity of the constructs.

## Competitive strategies

To determine the respondents’ relative competitive strategies, we asked three questions related to their organizations (see Appendix A) and used clustering of responses to determine their predominant strategy types (acknowledging that few organizations engage in a competitive strategy that is one-dimensional). The clustering was performed using group average and Minikowski as a distance measure (P ¼ 3). The WPGMC method (Lance & Williams, 1967) yielded three useful clusters, each representing a niche, a low-cost, and a differentiation strategy, using a 5-point Likert scale. As the cluster centroids were established, each response was classified based on its Minikowski distance. Twenty-one non-anonymous surveys were classified based on their distance to each of the centroids, confirming the classification validity. This supports the hypothesis that companies engaged in a differentiation strategy may recognize the unique strategic importance of DSSs and, therefore, place a higher importance on supplier factors than organizations engaged in other strategies (Table 8).

## Finding – H2a–d

H2a–d stated that the importance of the factors (cost, technology, supplier, and internal) considered in a DSS outsourcing decision is not different from that in an OLTP outsourcing decision. Using a two-factor ANOVA with replication, we tested the overall hypothesis that the four factors being considered are different for each object type (DSS and OLTP). We found this to be the case at a 99% confidence level (F = 270.37. n = 1575). This overall test is important, as it provides an adjustment for the Bonferroni effect of multiple t-tests. We also tested each hypothesis (H2a–d) individually. The results in Table 9 indicate that the object being outsourced results in significant differences in the weighting of all factors. Based on the results shown in Table 9, all four null hypotheses (H2a–d) can be rejected.

When exploring our data set further, we also found that there were statistical differences for 15 of the 19 factors considered depending on the object of the decision (Table 10). This indicates that the object of the outsourcing decision has an impact on what factors are being considered important in a decision-making process. This also implies that future research exploring the outsourcing decision-making process should not group a variety of IS systems, but instead should be very specific in grouping similar purpose systems.

## Finding – H4

H4 was that the percent of IT budget that firms plan to spend on outsourcing is not significantly different between firms of various sizes (Fortune 500, Fortune 1000, and smaller organizations). Due to the inconsistent results of previous studies, the hypothesis was not predicting a direction for this relationship. Based on the results of the t-tests in Table 12, we can reject the null hypothesis. Our analysis leads to a conclusion that there are significant differences in the percent of IT budget that firms of different sizes plan to spend on outsourcing. Furthermore, there are indications that we may be dealing with a non-linear relationship.

To further explore the relationship between outsourcing expenses and firm size, we computed the percent of IT budget currently spent on outsourcing as well as the planned annual growth in this expense and compared the results across the three firm size classes. In the case of current expenses, there was a non-linear relationship, which was also not consistently significant. Also, there were differences in the current expenses between the Fortune 500 and 1000 and between smaller organizations and the Fortune 1000. No significant differences were found in the comparison between current expenses for smaller organizations and those of Fortune 500 firms (Table 12).

## Finding – H5

Our last research hypothesis was that organizations that employ a niche or a differentiation strategy are less likely to consider cost factors, than organizations that are primarily competing using a low-cost strategy. Using a test of sample means assuming equal variance in the population (based on equal variance in sample), we found this hypothesis supported by our data. Specifically, we found that costs factors were rated significantly higher by firms that employ a low-costs strategy. Also, no differences were found between firms that employ a niche or a differentiation strategy (see Table 14).

This indicates that a firm’s strategy appears to impact the factors that are used when a firm is making a decision to outsource an information system. When we analyzed the data without respect to the object of the outsourcing decision, we found that the relative importance of costs factors for firms with a low-costs strategy was 3.18, whereas firms with a different core strategy attached significantly lower scores to the cost measures. Additional research into various industries that have different cost pressures and cost structures may yield further insights into this relationship.

## Conclusion

In spite of the substantial research effort in the area of outsourcing, there are still gaps in our understanding of organizations’ decision-making process and their decision factors. Most past studies have approached the outsourcing decision from a generic perspective without regard to the object of the decision. As demonstrated by this research, there can be significant differences in the relative importance attached to key decision factors for the outsourcing of different types of systems. For example, we found significant differences between the outsourcing of OLTP and DSS in 15 of the 19 decision factors we examined.

While past studies offered conflicting support for the impact of competitive strategy and company size on the decision factors, we found support for the importance of both. Our findings confirm that outsourcing strategies are aligned with organizational strategies. For example, cost factors dominate in the outsourcing decision among organizations that employ a low-costs strategy as compared to those following a differentiation or niche strategy. Also, compared to firms pursuing other competitive strategies, for the outsourcing of DSSs, differentiators place a significantly higher emphasis on supplier factors. Regarding the role of company size, we found significant differences in the importance given to supplier, internal, technology, and cost factors by organizations of different sizes. For example, compared to smaller organizations, larger organizations gave less importance to supplier and technology factors and more importance to cost factors.

From a practitioner’s point of view our findings may be particularly interesting. Recent research from IBM’s Global Services examined how to build scorecards and predictive models to identify the best opportunities to sell outsourcing services based on primarily financial measures (Mojsilovic´ et al., 2007). Using the results of our study to expand these models to include the factors considered by the buyer based on what is being outsourced, the buyer’s firm size, and strategy type, is likely to improve the accuracy of such models and also reduce the costly negative time window (time from targeting to contract written) through a more focused sales effort.
