# The effect of service employees’ technology readiness on technology acceptance<sup>§</sup>

Rita Walczuch <sup>a,\*</sup>, Jos Lemmink <sup>b</sup>, Sandra Streukens b

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accounting and Information Management, Faculty of Economics and Business Administration, Postbus 616, Maastricht University, The Netherlands

<sup>b</sup> Department of Marketing, Faculty of Economics and Business Administration, Maastricht University, The Netherlands Received 3 March 2004; received in revised form 31 July 2006; accepted 31 December 2006 Available online 16 January 2007

## Abstract

As much of the work of service employees is supported by IT, the quality of the service often depends on how the technology is used. Most employees only use a fraction of the functionality available on their desk-top. This may also depend on their personality We performed research on the relationship of personality and technology acceptance.

In our study we combined the technology readiness index (TRD) and TAM into one model. Specifically, we measured the relation between TRIs personality trait dimensions – optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity – and the cognitive dimensions of TAM. Data was collected from 810 employees of a multi-site financial service provider. Analysis revealed that personality traits had the expected impact on user perceptions. Surprisingly, Innovativeness was negatively related to usefulness. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Technology readiness index; Technology acceptance model

## 1. Introduction

Much research has focused on investigating the determinants of technology use; TAM (based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [10]) has been widely applied to explain IS usage behaviour [9]; in TAM, the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of an IS has been shown to affect the use of IT.

Another stream of research posits an individual’s personality at the centre of his or her acceptance of technology. One such model is the technology readiness index (TRI) [28]. TRI measures an individual’s readiness to use new technology in general using four personality traits: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity. According to Parasuraman, a person with optimism and innovativeness and little discomfort and insecurity is more likely to use a new technology.

However, little effort has so far been made to combine personality-based and cognitive antecedents to technology use in one model. Psychological traits are antecedents of the cognitive dimensions of TAM [2,17]. We therefore hypothesized that the personality traits of TRI were antecedents to technology acceptance asking: does a person’s general attitude towards technology have an effect on his or her perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of a given software system.

Our model was tested using data collected at a large Belgian financial service provider.

## 2. Literature research

## 2.1. Technology acceptance model (TAM)

TAM adopted the causal relationship of Fishbein’s and Ajzen’s TRA model [4]. Davis included perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) as the two beliefs that determine the attitude towards using an IS. Davis concluded that the relationship between PU and user acceptance was stronger than that of PEU and user acceptance.

## 2.2. Technology readiness index (TRI)

Research by Rogers [32] suggested that there are differences in peoples’ disposition towards using technology. He split people into five groups describing their character; ranging from innovators to laggards.

The technology readiness index (TRI) is a framework that relates to technology in general. The traits differ among people and therefore their beliefs about various aspects of technology differ. The relative strength of each trait indicates a person’s openness to technology. TRI thus reflects a set of beliefs about technology but is not an indicator of a person’s competence in using it.

TRI is multifaceted. It defines four groups of users separated by their prevailing personality trait with two factors being motivators of new technology use and another two being inhibitors; they are:

\- Optimism: a positive view of technology. Belief in increased control, flexibility, and efficiency in life due to technology.

\- Innovativeness: a tendency to be the first using a new technologies.

\- Discomfort: having a need for control and a sense of being overwhelmed.

\- Insecurity: distrusting technology for security and privacy reasons.

The stronger a trait, the better the person fits into one of the groups and the more significantly he or she is influenced in the use of high-technology products and services. An analysis by Parasuraman and Colby [29] identified five clusters based on these four dimensions. Tsikritsis [39] replicated the research in the UK and found support for four of the five clusters [31]. People with high TRI levels score high on optimism and innovativeness. They feel comfortable using technology and only call for little proof of its performance. People with lower levels are more critical, they ask for help more often and feel uncomfortable with new technologies.

## 3. Research model

Chen et al. [7] showed that consumers are increasingly aware of the risks and liabilities of online shopping as they become more Web-knowledgeable. However, it seems that this should promote consumer confidence rather than increase scepticism. Indeed, early adopters apparently think more optimistically and are less concerned with risks or are more willing to accept risk in order to improve personal performance. Their willingness suggests a clear difference from later adopters, as stressed by Carroll and Thomas [6], who introduced the term ‘‘subjective ease of use’’, which relates to the user’s decision to use a system that may not agree with simple ease of use. Pocius [30] stated that ‘‘human-computer interaction is mediated by both the computer system design and by the characteristics of the user.’’

Buss [5], who suggested that personality affects the environment that people choose and that it also influences the speed with which they adapted a new technology. This also supported Roger’s suggestion that differences in character lead to several categories of technology adopters.

Kleijnen et al. [19] adapted the initial TRI into a more specific ‘‘Mobile Technology Readiness’’ scale. Olson and Olson [27] found that ‘‘Readiness for Collaboration Technologies’’ has a positive effect on the success of remote group work.

Fig. 1 shows our proposed model. Using TRI and TAM as starting points, we attempted to analyse the influence of the four dimensions of TRI on the two levels of TAM to shed more light on technology acceptance and to understand the importance of personality differences.

## 3.1. The effect of optimism on perception of technology

Optimism is ‘‘the tendency to believe that one will generally experience good versus bad outcomes in life’’ [35, p. 203]. Optimists use more active coping strategies than pessimists and these strategies are more effective in achieving positive outcomes. It is inversely related to emotional distress, worry and concern about bad experiences as well as perceived risk, and perceived control [38].

Optimists are less likely to focus on negative events and thus confront technology more openly. They are more likely to accept their situation and less likely to be escapists. Therefore, optimists are more willing to use new technologies [34].

Thus optimism leads to more positive attitudes and will help bring out more positive attitudes towards computers [21,23]. Therefore, we assumed that an optimist perceives a technology as being more useful and easier to use because he or she worries less about possible negative outcomes.

H1a. High personal optimism about technology in general leads to higher perceived ease of use of a specific technology.

H1b. High personal optimism about technology in general leads to higher perceived usefulness of a specific technology.

3.2. The effect of innovativeness on perception of technology

Personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) is the ‘‘willingness of an individual to try out any new information technology’’ [22,11]. It is considered to be a trait; i.e., a relatively stable descriptor of an individual and invariant across situations (uninfluenced by environmental or internal variables) [1]. Karahanna et al. [18] showed that more innovative individuals, the early adopters, have less complex belief sets about new technology. Therefore we hypothesized:

H2a. High personal innovativeness towards technology in general leads to higher perceived ease of use of a specific technology.

People scoring high on innovativeness tend to think that they might miss certain benefits when not trying out a new technology. They have a positive impression of its usefulness in general. Early adopters use innovations even when their potential value is uncertain and their benefits are not obvious. Thus:

H2b. High personal innovativeness towards technology in general leads to higher perceived usefulness of a specific technology.

## 3.3. The effect of insecurity on perception of technology

Apprehensiveness, as described by Kwon and Chidambaram [20], results in individuals avoiding the use of computers due to their innate fear of technology. The reasons for this might lie in the scepticism people have to new technologies. In 1995, about one fourth of Americans felt ‘‘very insecure’’ when setting their digital alarm clock [33]. We thus posit that people with a sense of insecurity will have lower PEU of a technology:

H3a. High personal insecurity with regard to technology in general leads to lower perceived ease of use of a specific technology.

Chen et al. established that some obvious obstacles of technology acceptance were due to security and privacy concerns. This can result in suspicion and reduced PU of a technology and thus lower use. We thus hypothesized.

H3b. High personal insecurity of technology in general leads to lower perceived usefulness of a specific technology.

## 3.4. The effect of discomfort on perception of technology

Persons scoring high on the discomfort scale suffer from a perceived lack of control and a sense of being overwhelmed by technology. This might be improved through informative feedback and augmented ease of use [8,25]. People who score high on the discomfort trait perceive technology as more complex and thus less easy to use; thus:

H4a. High personal discomfort with regard to technology in general leads to lower perceived ease of use of a specific technology.

We expected no direct relation of discomfort with perceived usefulness and hypothesized:

H4b. High personal discomfort in using technology in general does not lead to higher or lower perceived usefulness of a specific technology.

## 3.5. The effect between easy of use and usefulness

Ample empirical studies have demonstrated that PEU significantly and positively influences perceived usefulness [37,40,41]. Given that PU is defined as the prospective user’s subjective probability that using a specific technology will increase his or her job performance, PEU and PU will be positively related. Consequently we hypothesized:

H5. There will be a positive relationship between perceived ease of use and usefulness.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Sample

The research was conducted in co-operation with a Belgian multi-site financial service provider, which is one of the major players in the Belgian market. The subjects were the employees of this company. Questionnaires were administered to all branches of the company, which is located in both the Dutch and French speaking parts of Belgium. The questionnaires were sent in the respective language by internal mail to all employees, 2764 at 937 branches. In total 1126 people responded, however due to missing data and outliers we obtained an effective response rate of n = 810 (29.3%).

## 4.2. Questionnaire

The survey instrument included the tested and validated instruments developed by Parasuraman and

Davis (see Appendix A). The items were clear and understandable, had already proven to be reliable and had been validated in former studies. The translations were performed by native speakers and were back translated to remove and reduce any translation errors.

Participants were not asked to rate a specific technology on its PU and PEU. Instead, the participants were asked to select the software application they use most and to complete the questionnaire considering their feelings about that application (not all employees used the same software). We assumed that this was the technology with which they had the most experience. However, this did not to mean that the technology was easier or more comprehensive to use. Each item question was scored on a Likert scale from 1 to 7, with a 1 rating indicating strong disagreement and a 7 rating indicating strong agreement.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Preliminary analyses and estimation method

Before starting our empirical analysis we conducted a thorough examination of the data; including checks for missing values, outliers, and characteristics of the distribution of the variables used in our study. Next, we determine the best estimation method.

Cases with missing values where deleted listwise [14] (all these cases contained multiple missing values on individual constructs and thus their use in imputation methods would not be feasible). Furthermore, we scanned our data for univariate outliers using z-scores [36]. Finally, as Tabachnick and Fidell suggested, Mahalanobis’ D was used to check for multivariate outliers. Inspection of these revealed inconsistent and/ or illogical response patterns. Overall, this preliminary examination of the data led to a reduction to a set of 810 useable responses.

In order to gain insight into the distributional characteristics of the data, Table 1 was generated, it summarized a variety of descriptive statistics. The distributional shape of the data, as reflected by its skewness and kurtosis, determined, to a large extent, the conditions under which the use of various estimation techniques were appropriate. For most structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques the presence of multivariate normality is needed. As a necessary condition for multivariate normality is the presence of univariate normality [16], inspection of the significance of the skewness and kurtosis of each individual variable provided us with information about the choice of the appropriate statistical technique. Based on the information in Table 1 we concluded that most variables used deviated significantly from normality, thereby limiting the validity of maximum likelihood estimation techniques. Given these distributional characteristics we opted for a partial least squares (PLS) estimation technique as this method makes no distributional assumptions. Hence, both the measurement model and the structural model were estimated by means of partial least squares (PLS) [12,15,43]. Examples of recent studies that use PLS include [24,44], and [42].

## 5.2. Estimation results measurement model

Inspection of the empirical results revealed that the scales possessed favorable psychometric properties in terms of reliability and validity. Based on the loadings of the items on their respective constructs, we decided to delete two items for ‘insecurity’ (they had loadings $< 0 . 1 5 )$ . In all other cases a high degree of individual item reliability was evidenced as all loadings exceed 0.40 which conforms with the requirement of Hulland. Furthermore, the constructs under study exhibited a high degree of internal consistency as all composite reliability values exceeded the 0.70 guideline suggested by Nunnally and Bernstein [26]. Details of the internal consistency of each construct are summarized in Table 2.

Discriminant validity was evidenced in two ways. First, the square root of the average variance extracted was greater than all corresponding correlations [13]. Second, discriminant validity was good as each correlation coefficient was less than 1 by an amount greater than twice its respective standard error [3].

## 6. Conclusion

## 6.1. Discussion

We were able to show that personality makes a difference in the adoption process of IT and this may help to explain how its adoption may be influenced by the personality of the user as well as the characteristics of the technology; personality characteristics as measured in the TRI have a significant effect on technology adoption.

Employees’ optimism has the strongest impact on PEU and PU of the provided and most frequently used IT; they seem to confront IT more openly and positively and are less likely to focus on its negative aspects.

Innovativeness negatively impacts perceived usefulness. This finding was not expected. A possible explanation is that innovative people are more critical towards technology since they are aware of the newest developments and possibilities end expect all technology to fulfil highest demands.

Discomfort had a negative impact on PEU, as predicted. Employees scoring high on this dimension felt overwhelmed by the complexity of technology. Discomfort had no impact on PU as predicted. Insecurity had a negative effect on PU and PEU. As predicted, insecure employees perceived IT as less useful and less easy to use.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

The implications of our findings for management are important: personality influences technology use. Thus, a manager should be aware of this relationship when initiating IS. Simply providing employees with IT plus standard training sessions may not be sufficient to gain full benefit from the investment.

A manager can adopt his or her strategy on how to stimulate use of the IS by employees, based on their personalities.

A manager would be well-advised to be aware of the technology readiness of his or her employees and adjust the training schedule and management activities to optimise IS use; e.g., a highly innovative person could be asked to be the champion for a new technology or a person with a high insecurity level could be directed to design security systems to safe guard the firm from security leaks. We strongly believe that each personality type could have positive as well as negative effects on the use and adoption of IT in an organization. It is the task of the manager to use these differences between people to the advantage of the firm.

## 6.3. Limitations

The sample for this research was taken from one organization (a financial service provider) in one country and thus deals with only one organizational culture. This may make our results less generalizable to other organizations and countries. Also, the use of a financial service provider may have had an impact: a more innovative company may show different results especially for innovativeness.

Another point that needs attention is that TAM was intended to deal with a single technology, while our experiment was for the individual’s own choice of a software system. By selecting the most used software instead of one specific software we ensure that the evaluation of the usefulness of the software was not related to its degree of use in daily operations.
