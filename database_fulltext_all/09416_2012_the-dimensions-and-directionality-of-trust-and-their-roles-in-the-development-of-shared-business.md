# The dimensions and directionality of trust and their roles in the development of shared business–IS understanding

M. Dale Stoel <sup>a,</sup>\*, Waleed A. Muhanna <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accountancy, Farmer School of Business, Miami University, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Accounting & Management Information Systems, Fisher College of Business, Ohio State University, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 20 June 2011 Received in revised form 11 April 2012 Accepted 31 May 2012 Available online 18 June 2012

Keywords: Shared understanding Trust IS governance Dyadic relationships

## A B S T R A C T

Prior work suggests that mutual trust is an important antecedent to knowledge transfer and shared understanding between the IS and business units; however, the findings have been based on a conceptualization of trust as a one-dimensional construct. Using a richer dyadic conceptualization and operationalization of trust, we empirically investigated the degree to which the importance of trust varies across two members of a dyadic relationship as well as the different role of distinct facets of trust. Analysis of data collected from two informants (the IS manager and a business-unit counterpart) in a sample of manufacturing firms showed that perceived business-unit trustworthiness was significantly more important in fostering shared business–IS understanding than perceived IS-unit trustworthiness. We attributed this to the inherent power asymmetry that characterizes the relationship between IS and business units. We further hypothesized and found that the importance of the three key dimensions of trust (benevolence, integrity, and competence) depended on the relationship age and organizational position of the IS unit. Our findings show how to manage this critical dyadic relationships more effectively and also where to focus trust-building practices in order to ensure success.

## 1. Introduction

Creating an effective partnership between business and IS units is a critical factor in successful utilization of IT in the support of business objectives [6,11]. One measure of an effective partnership is the level of shared business–IS understanding; this has been shown to be a key performance differentiator [12]. While prior work suggested that mutual trust was an important antecedent to shared understanding, this finding was based on a conceptualization of trust as a one-dimensional aggregate feature of the relationship. However, trust has been considered a multidimensional construct encompassing beliefs about competence/ ability (a group of skills, and characteristics that enable a party to have influence in a specific domain), integrity (adherence to a set of principles that the trustor finds acceptable), and benevolence (the extent to which a trustee is believed to want to do good to the trustor, not including a profit motive) [10]. Yet, the importance of these dimensions of trust has not been examined in the context of the IS–business relationship. Additionally, research within the IS domain has ignored the question of which aspect of trust plays the more significant role, business unit trust of IS or vice versa. We hoped to fill this important gap in the literature.

More specifically, we extended our understanding of the role of trust in three ways. First, we argue that focusing on mutual trust (shared expectations) may not fully explain trust when there is power asymmetry, and that trust should be considered bidirectional and possibly asymmetric. The nature of the relationship between the IS and the business units may be more of a function of power and control than trust. Therefore, trust may differ between the two parties (the IS manager and a business-unit counterpart); indeed we found that perceived trustworthiness of the business unit was significantly more important in fostering shared business–IS understanding than perceived trustworthiness of the IS unit.

Second, drawing on the richer conceptualization of trust, as a multi-dimensional construct encompassing beliefs about competence, integrity and benevolence, we explored whether those facets were equally influential; each captures distinct aspects of trust which may individually impact the development of shared understanding (e.g., perceptions about competence may be task specific and related to experience on similar activities; while perceptions about benevolence may be related to all prior work between the IS and business units). Therefore, each facet may have unique affects in developing relationships and transferring knowledge required in the formation of shared understanding.

Third, we hypothesized and tested a contingency perspective to the relative importance of the three trust dimensions. While trust is likely to have a direct effect on organizational outcomes such as knowledge sharing and performance, this does not consider the potential moderating role of organizational factors related to the specific IS–business. In particular, we posited that the importance of the three dimensions of trust depends on the relationship length and organizational position of the IS unit.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Shared understanding

Shared understanding, the dependent variable of interest in this study, refers to the knowledge that the IT organization has about its business process, the knowledge that it possesses about the opportunities to apply IT to improve the business activities, and the common understanding between the IT organization and the business unit about the role of IT in improving business performance [12]. It may be achieved through repeated information exchange, and agreement between the business and IS units that each needs to understand the capabilities and limitations of the other. Shared understanding has been linked to several organizational outcomes, including the perceived performance of the IS group, assimilation of IT within the organization and strategic alignment between business and IT. Prior research has indicated that the primary factors that affect the level of knowledge exchange include social factors, with trust between the two parties being a factor essential for joint problem solving [2].

## 2.2. Trust

Trust is central to social interactions and successful relationships between organizations [5]. Trust affects a person’s willingness to seek and provide new information and absorb new knowledge [8]; a lack of trust is deleterious to information exchange, diminishes the effectiveness of joint problem solving, and is likely to reduce the parties’ ability to resolve disagreements.

Here, consistent with the TRA, we build on the integrative models of trust of McKnight et al. [9] which posited that trusting beliefs (perceptions of one party about the trustworthiness of another) lead to trusting intention (willingness to depend on or engage in trust-related behavior with another), which then results in trust-related behavior. Viewing the actual sharing and assimilation of knowledge as the focal behavior, we believe that trust should be conceptualized as a set of trusting beliefs. Thus, we define trust {or trustworthiness} as a set of specific beliefs that one party holds about the integrity, benevolence, and ability of another.

In the IS area, mutual trust is assumed to lead to the sharing of ideas and a common appreciation of information, and this results in shared understanding. We therefore started by positing a link between mutual trust and shared understanding in order to test this under our broader conceptualization of mutual trust as a set of shared cognitive/affective beliefs that each party has about the trustworthiness of the other.

H1. Mutual trusting beliefs between the IS and business units will be positively associated with the level of shared IS–business understanding.

This conceptualization of trust may not fully identify the role of trust in knowledge sharing as it assumes that trust is equally valuable for each member; however, factors relating to organizational structures may substitute for trust and trust may be more salient for one of the parties. In particular, differences in the organizational power of the business and IS units may result in discrepancies in the perceived vulnerability of one party to the other’s action. Organizational power generally includes the concepts of decision making authority and influence over other parties, including the ability to reward and punish others within the organization [4]. Prior studies have shown that the IS unit often plays a support role and tends to have lower levels of organizational power than the business units. This power asymmetry may elevate the importance of the business unit being perceived as trustworthy by the IS unit. If the business unit is not perceived as trustworthy, the IS unit may limit the information provided as it may be concerned that the business unit will use their power to limit resources or punish the IS unit. However, the trustworthiness of the IS unit may be less important if it has limited power. We therefore hypothesized that the business unit trustworthiness will be most important in fostering greater shared business–IS understanding.

H2A. Perceived business unit trustworthiness will be positively associated with the level of shared IS–business understanding.

H2B. Perceived IS unit trustworthiness will be positively associated with the level of shared IS–business understanding.

H2C. Compared to the perceived IS unit trustworthiness, perceived business unit trustworthiness will be more strongly associated with shared IS–business understanding.

Research comparing a single dimensional construct to a multidimensional construct of trusting beliefs (trustworthiness) in other contexts has suggested that the multi-dimensional approach yields improved model fit characteristics and explains more variance in proposed organizational outcomes [13]. Therefore, we explored the potential relationship between each facet of trust and shared understanding.

The trustor’s perceptions of ability may come from information provided by other parties or from prior interactions between the trustor and trustee. Therefore, prior success may increase the interest of the business unit in determining how IT may help improve their operations. Additionally, the literature has argued that the perception of benevolence-based trust may increase both a unit’s willingness to offer information and their desire to absorb it. Competence- and benevolence-based trust have been found to be significantly related to effective knowledge transfer; however, integrity has not been examined as prior work has tended to focus on the receipt of useful information and argue that the usefulness of the information should not be impacted by the provider’s perceived integrity [7].

Our conceptualization of shared understanding requires repeated interchange of information and a willingness to consider the perspectives and experiences of both parties. We believe that the ability to accept the information and share ideas over time would be based on the perception of the integrity of the other party as well as the perception of the other party’s competence and benevolence. We therefore posited that each dimension of trust was positively associated with the level of shared understanding:

H3A. The degree of competence-based trusting beliefs between the IS and business units will be positively associated with the level of shared IS–business understanding.

H3B. The degree of integrity-based trusting beliefs between the IS and business units will be positively associated with the level of shared IS– business understanding.

H3C. The degree of benevolence-based trusting beliefs between the IS and business units will be positively associated with the level of shared IS–business understanding.

These hypotheses consider the average association of each dimension of trust with shared understanding; however, there may be contextual factors that impact the relative importance of specific dimensions. Early in relationships, much of the information thateachparty gathers abouttheother may beobtained through third parties without any direct involvement. As the observing party is not directly involved, the information gathered will be primarily related to the integrity of the other party. As relationships continue over time, the parties will continue to interact and provide an opportunitytoassesstheotherparty’scompetencyandbenevolence. This may result in a change to the importance of each trust’s dimension, with relationships moving from integrity-based toward competence- and benevolence-based trust.

H4. Relationship tenure (length) moderates the relation between the dimensions of trustworthiness and the level of shared IS–business understanding.

Organizational and governance mechanisms impact the form and function of business relationships which in turn impact the knowledge exchange leading to shared understanding. The frequency and level of interaction between the two parties significantly affects effective knowledge transfer. In business–IS relationships, governance mechanisms which foster joint work may provide both parties with the ability to influence each other’s policies and decisions, leading to shared knowledge.

We focused on the reporting position of the IS unit within the organization as one such mechanism. Specifically, we considered whether the IS unit directly reported to the CEO, and argued that this reporting structure moderated the impact of the dimensions of trust on shared understanding to the extent that it influenced the desire or need for each unit to share knowledge. The position of the IS unit within social networks and the level of connections in the business and IS planning processes have been shown to provide additional formal and informal opportunities for shared knowledge between business and IS organizations. Specifically, an elevated organizational position of the IS unit may provide the IS unit a role in determining the resources available to the business unit; therefore, the business unit may attach greater weight to the benevolence dimension of the IS unit’s trustworthiness. These observations lead to:

H5. The direct reporting position of the IS organization moderates the relation between the dimensions of trust and the level of shared understanding.

## 3. Research methodology

A two-part survey instrument was designed to elicit information about the variables. In each firm, a matched-pair of informants within the IS and manufacturing organizations was asked to provide information about the units, their relationship, and the level of shared understanding. This dual survey approach was similar to that performed recently in the areas of top management, shared understanding, and IS performance. We decided to focus on the manufacturing industry in order to control for industryspecific factors, and we concentrated on the relationship between the IS and manufacturing units as manufacturing processes have generally been large users of IS.

## 3.1. Questionnaire development

The key constructs to be measured in the survey were shared understanding and trustworthiness. Both constructs were measured using previously developed scales and requiring information from the IS and manufacturing organizations. Table 1 summarizes the measures of the study variables.

## 3.3. Sampling frame

Based on prior literature, we required firms to have minimum sales revenue of \$100 million. We believed that this threshold was significant and would ensure that we had a sample of organizations concerned with the interaction between IS and business units. The manufacturing industry contained a variety of product segments and we wanted to identify a subset of the manufacturing industries to achieve a sample with similar characteristics. We used the Bureau of Economic Analysis input–output tables and identified the SIC code ranges of 3400 through 3999 as the manufacturing sub-industries which were the heaviest users of IT relative to other expenditures. We then utilize Hoovers and Dunn & Bradstreet databases to select firms which met our revenue and industry criteria. A total of 1503 firms qualified and we surveyed this entire population for our study. Table 2 provides a breakdown of the sampling frame by sub-industry and revenue.

Hoovers’ database provides general contact information, including the name of the CEO; however, we felt that the response for our survey would be improved by identifying senior manufacturing or IS contacts. Therefore, the information from Hoovers was supplemented by using data from Dunn & Bradstreet, Top Computer Executives, and corporate websites to identify respondents for our survey. Our strategy focused on contacting the senior member of the organization responsible for IS to request his or her participation in our study and asking the recipient to forward the manufacturing component of the study to the senior manufacturing manager. If we could not identify the senior member of the IS organization, we attempted to identify the senior member of the manufacturing business unit and ask them to sponsor the study and solicit the IS manager’s participation. If neither the IS or manufacturing contact could be identified, we addressed our two-parts survey instrument to the CEO and requested his or her assistance in forwarding the surveys to the respective IS and manufacturing managers.

## 4. Data analysis and results

## 4.1. Survey response

The survey was initially targeted at all 1503 companies; however, 111 companies were dropped due to returned surveys or their stated policy against responding to surveys, reducing the overall sample to 1392 companies. We received responses from 171 companies, representing a response rate of 12.3%. Of these 171 companies, 75 provided both the IS and manufacturing responses, 54 companies provide the IS component only and 42 companies provided the manufacturing component only. Thus the usable response may be lower than that required in general survey research; however, this level is similar to other surveys which have employed a matched-pair design. In spite of the modest sample size, we believed our sample was sufficient for our analysis using PLS, for which rules of thumb suggest that the sample size be five to ten times the larger of: (1) the number of indicators for the scale with the largest number of formative indicators, or (2) the largest number of structural paths directed at a particular construct in the structural model [1]. In the main-effects models (H1 through H3), the worst case scenario is the H3 model which contains eight paths into shared understanding. This would indicate the need for a sample of between 40 and 80 responses and our 75 responses is near the high end of this range. Our models which contain interaction terms have 14 paths into shared understanding, thus the rules of thumb would indicate a sample between 70 and 140 responses. Again, our sample is within the acceptable range.

## 4.2. Data analysis

We focused our analysis on organizations that provided complete and paired responses. The characteristics of these firms are shown in Table 3.

We performed several analyses to check for potential biases. First we checked for common method variance, though we believe that our research design mitigated this as we required separate responses from the manufacturing and IS units. We performed a Harman single factor test to ensure that information did not load on a common factor and we found separate factors for our defined constructs. Second, we investigated non-response bias by using wave analysis to compare the responses of early and late responders (assuming that late responders represent the nonresponder group). We split the sample and performed Kolmogorov–Smirinov tests on each item in the questionnaire as well as each construct to determine if there were differences between the early and late responders. We did not find any statistically significant differences between them. Also, as our research design required paired responses, we assumed that organizations which provided only a response from one part of the dyad represented non-responders and thus compared responses from organizations with paired responses to those that provided only manufacturing or IS information. Again, Kolmogorov–Smirinov tests did not find differences that might indicate non-response bias. Third, a possible source of bias could exist between responses that were completed online and those using the paper survey and mailed, or where the survey package was sent to the CEO rather than one of the business unit senior executives. We again separate the responses and perform the Kolmogorov–Smirinov tests at the item and construct levels but did not find any statistically significant differences. We used PLS\*GRAPH to estimate the latent variable and test the path significance. In evaluating each latent variable there are two primary concerns, reliability of indicators and the convergent and discriminant validity of the latent variable [3]. Table 4 provides descriptive statistics of the latent variables. As measures of indicator reliability, the Cronbach alphas and the composite reliabilities for each latent variable were well above the typical 0.7 threshold. We also found the item loadings for each latent construct to be strongly significant. We thus concluded that the scales were reliable. In the first and second model, the mutual trust, IS trustworthiness and manufacturing trustworthiness variables were constructed as second-order factors based on the first order factors. For each of the second order factors, we created an alternative first order version of the factor using the underlying items. In each case, the Cronbach alpha and reliability measures were similar to those of the six first-order factors. Also, the use of these alternative calculations within their respective models did not qualitatively change our inferences.

Beyond examining reliability, we also examined convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was confirmed by verifying that the correlations between the indicators and the latent variable that they represent were greater than the correlation between the items and any other latent variable. The item loadings were also examined and we found that the items were loading higher on their intended latent variables than on any other latent variable. Discriminant validity in PLS modeling is assessed by examining whether the square root of the average variance explained by a latent variable is greater than the correlation between that latent variable and any of the other latent variables in the model. Table 5 provides a correlation table of the latent variables with the value on the diagonal being the square root of the average variance explained. We found that the square root of the average variance explained was greater than the correlation between the latent variable and any other latent variable, demonstrating discriminant validity. Collectively, the evidence suggests that the constructs demonstrate adequate measurement properties.

## 5. Results

Fig. 3 shows the results for our analyses of model 1 examining the association between mutual trustworthiness and shared understanding, model 2 examining the directions of trust, and model 3 assessing the differential effects of dimensions of directional trust.

The first model indicates that mutual trust had a positive and significant relationship with shared understanding. Thus, H1 received strong support. The second model was used to separate the effect of IS trustworthiness and manufacturing trustworthiness and we found that both were positive and significant determinants of shared understanding, as posited in H2A and H2B. To test H2C, we use the bootstrapping option within PLS\*Graph to develop multiple samples and regression results. Based on this set of regression results we tested the series of estimated IS and manufacturing trustworthiness coefficients for statistical difference. We found that the coefficients were statistically different at the p < 0.01 level, providing strong support for H2C.

The third model was used to consider affects from each of the dimensions of trustworthiness for the IS unit and the manufacturing unit separately. We found that there were significant path coefficients within the trust dimensions for both the IS and manufacturing units; however, not all path coefficients were significant. This analysis indicated that as IS units assess whether to share information with the manufacturing unit they may be considering all of the dimensions of the manufacturing unit’s perceived trustworthiness (competence, integrity and benevolence) as each dimension of manufacturing trustworthiness are positive and significant, albeit at different levels. However, the integrity dimension of IS unit trustworthiness was the only dimension of IS unit trustworthiness that was significant. One possible explanation is that it may be more difficult for the manufacturing unit to determine the competence of the IS unit and their organizational power may limit the manufacturing unit’s concern about the benevolence of the IS unit.

Fig. 4 presents results from the two interaction models designed to test H4 and H5. The top model includes interactions between the dimensions of trust and relationship length, and the low model includes interactions between the dimensions of trust and the reporting position of the IS group. As the relationship length grows, the importance of the integrity dimension of manufacturing trustworthiness was reduced and more weight was given to the competence dimension of manufacturing trustworthiness. This result provided empirical evidence that organizations may initially consider the integrity dimension to be important, given the availability of general information; however, later the IS unit may assess and consider the competence of the business unit. Similarly, the lower model indicated that when IS units report directly to the CEO, the interaction between the reporting position and the benevolence dimension of IS trustworthiness became positive and significant. ## 6. Discussion

Overall, we find support for our hypotheses. We find that mutual trust, even with our extended definition to include its three facets, is strongly associated with shared understanding. We also find that IS and manufacturing trustworthiness are positively associated with shared understanding, and that perceived manufacturing trustworthiness is more important because of the power asymmetry between manufacturing and IS units. Only the competence and benevolence dimensions of perceived manufacturing trustworthiness and the integrity dimension of perceived IS trustworthiness were found to be positively associated with shared understanding. We found that increased relationship length resulted in greater importance of competence dimension of manufacturing trustworthiness and a reduced importance of the integrity dimension. We also found that direct reporting to the CEO positively moderated the benevolence dimension of perceived IS trustworthiness.

As with all research, our study had some limitations, including our survey response rate and consequent small sample size. However, we believe that our sample was adequate based on the well-known rules of thumb. The models were rerun dropping items that were insignificant to account for potential power issues, and the results were generally similar. To examine possible power issues we performed a power analysis on the main paths and found results above accepted thresholds. Based on the results of these tests and the power analysis we conclude that though the sample size was modest, statistical power was not a limitation in our analysis. A second limitation was that our cross-sectional analysis did not allow us to consider how trust or shared understanding developed over time. The data allowed us to show evidence of association but did not allow us to consider causality. Additionally, the survey dealt only with the manufacturing industry and this may limit the generalizability of our results to other industries.

## 7. Conclusion

Trust is a key antecedent to many social and commercial interactions, including shared business–IS understanding which has been linked to improved assimilation of IT within organization and improved IT-enabled performance of the business unit. The results of our study confirmed that mutual trust is related to shared understanding; however, we also found that it is the business unit’s trustworthiness which is most salient in fostering shared understanding. While trust building exercises are often symmetrically balanced across the two business units, our results indicated that the organizational power held by one business unit requires them to be perceived as being more trustworthy for other units to fully engage in knowledge sharing. Therefore, disparities in organizational position between the two business units should be considered before designing trust building exercises.

We also found that while all three of the trust dimensions may be important, some dimensions appeared to be more salient. Moreover, the effects of certain dimensions of trust on the degree of shared understanding were moderated by some contextual factors. We also found that direct reporting to the CEO positively moderated the benevolence dimension of perceived IS trustworthiness, with a higher reporting position for the IS unit increasing the importance of benevolence as perceived by the business unit. These results highlight the need to consider all facets of trust as well as the situational factors that can change the importance of each facet as an antecedent to shared understanding.

## Appendix A

The following questions were used to measure the level of shared understanding, joint IS governance and the dimensions of trust. Participants provided their response to the following questions based on a scale of 1 (not at all) to 5 (very greatly).

## A.1. Shared understanding (based on [12])

The following questions were included on the manufacturing unit survey:

 The IS unit understands the improvement objectives of the manufacturing unit.

 The IS unit understands the operational procedures and practices of the manufacturing unit.

 There is a common understanding between the IS unit and the manufacturing unit regarding the role of IS in meeting new manufacturing objectives.

The following questions were included on the IS unit survey:

 The manufacturing unit recognizes IS as a tool to increase their productivity (efficiency).

 The manufacturing unit recognizes IS as a tool to increase their product and process quality.

 There is a common understanding between the IS unit and the manufacturing unit regarding the role of IS in meeting new manufacturing objectives.

A.2. Trust (based on [7,9])

The following questions were included on the manufacturing (IS) unit surveys:

 The IS (manufacturing) unit is a capable and proficient unit

 The IS (manufacturing) unit possesses the appropriate skills to work effectively.

 The IS (manufacturing) unit works with other units openly and honestly.

 The IS (manufacturing) unit displays ethical behavior and integrity.

 We trust that decisions made by the IS (manufacturing) unit wil be in the manufacturing (IS) unit’s best interest.

 The IS (manufacturing) unit is concerned about the well-being of the manufacturing (IS) unit, not just its own.
