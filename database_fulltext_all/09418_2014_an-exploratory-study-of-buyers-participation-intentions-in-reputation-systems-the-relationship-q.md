# An exploratory study of buyers’ participation intentions in reputation systems: The relationship quality perspective

Qian Huang <sup>a,1</sup>, Robert M. Davison <sup>b,2</sup>, Hefu Liu <sup>a,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, Jinzhai Road 96, Hefei, Anhui, China <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 11 September 2012 Received in revised form 12 May 2014 Accepted 15 September 2014 Available online 23 September 2014

Keywords: Reputation systems Relationship quality Conflict handling styles Social conformity Perceived value of knowledge

## A B S T R A C T

Drawing upon a relationship quality framework, this study identifies how satisfaction, trust and different styles of handling conflict influence online buyers’ participation intentions in reputation systems associated with a C2C online shopping platform. Furthermore, we investigate how these effects are moderated by social conformity and the perceived value of knowledge. The results of a survey of 269 online buyers indicate that satisfaction, trust and an accommodating conflict handling style positively impact the intention to submit positive ratings, whereas an avoiding conflict handling style negatively affects the intention to submit positive ratings. Implications and suggestions for future research are provided.

## 1. Introduction

Online reputation systems have become an important component of online shopping because they help build trust [6], increase profits [79], and increase the efficiency of market performance as a whole [71]. Previous failures to deliver products or services on the part of sellers with weak reputations implies that those sellers will not be chosen by buyers as trade partners [21]. Many buyers have formed the habit of reviewing sellers’ reputation records before making purchase decisions. This ensures that reputation is a crucial indicator of a seller’s trustworthiness. iResearch, a leading organization that focuses on in-depth research in China’s Internet industry, indicates that reputation, after-sales service, and buyers’ comments are the three most influential factors in buyers’ purchasing decisions [35].

However, even though the importance of reputation systems has been recognized in previous research, there is a paucity of literature that focuses explicitly on the participation conditions of these systems. Dellarocas suggests that sufficient participation can render these systems more effective because eliciting sufficient feedback is deemed a new opportunity and challenge for research into the study of online systems [21]. Previous research has proposed that satisfied buyers are more likely to post feedback, indicating that reputation systems do not reflect the true feelings of all previous buyers, particularly buyers who are dissatisfied. Scholars further contend that the risks associated with trading in the C2C context are higher than suggested by reputation systems [23]. This means that buyers may not have sufficiently taken advantage of the opportunities offered by reputation systems. Dellarocas et al. [22] consider feedback to be a form of public good and analyze online feedback contributions in terms of selfish and altruistic motives in a manner that does not differentiate between buyers’ and sellers’ contributions. However, iResearch’s report indicates that buyers’ contributions receive the most attention from potential buyers who are making purchase decisions [35].

Both practitioners and researchers have found that in practice, reputation systems suffer from several forms of reporting bias [23,33,37], notably the overwhelmingly positive nature of ratings and the insufficiency of textual feedback. Dellarocas and Wood argue that reputation systems’ usefulness in both deterring fraud and informing buyers can be diminished if an overwhelmingly positive set of ratings has a bias of adequate severity [23]. Furthermore, dissatisfaction with unsuccessful shopping experiences diminishes buyer loyalty, with the result that new buyers in particular may not continue to use the same shopping platform [56]. Thus, an inefficient or ineffective reputation system may damage the long-term development of an online market. Consequently, it is meaningful to know why people intend to provide a positive rating and what determines their intention to leave textual feedback because an online reputation system cannot be successful without sustained contributions of voluntary feedback [22].

Extending this trend, our study aims to address the following research questions: what types of factors will determine buyers’ intention to give positive ratings and textual feedback in online reputation systems? How do these factors affect buyers’ participation intentions in reputation systems? We decided to focus on intentions instead of actual behavior for the following reasons. First, the majority of ratings (more than 99%) are positive, and thus, it is meaningful to investigate why people intend to submit a positive rating. Second, we believe that the real percentage of satisfied buyers is far lower than 99% [23]. That is, actual rating-submission behavior is badly distorted for many reasons. Thus, intention can better show buyers’ genuine intentions than their actual behavior.

This research adopts a relationship quality framework to investigate the antecedents of buyers’ participation intention in reputation systems. Relationship orientation has been suggested as a key operational mode in Chinese social life [72]. Scholars also indicate that in the Chinese C2C market, social relationships are more important than transaction service because the Chinese C2C market is socially oriented and ‘‘social relations play a crucial role in maintaining customers in China’’ [12]. We also analyze the antecedents of intentions to provide textual feedback because many buyers choose to write nothing, although textual feedback contains useful information that supplements numerical ratings [49]. At the same time, previous research has paid little attention to the perspective of real users: the actual buyers who participate in and use the reputation system in a C2C online shopping platform. However, all systems must first be used by buyers before their needs can be fulfilled. Thus, this research will target real online buyers as our research respondents and establish what drives them to participate in reputation systems in online C2C markets.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Reputation systems

A reputation system is a mechanism that can be used to reduce the number of problems caused by information asymmetry between sellers and buyers in an online shopping context [80]. Information asymmetry is one of the major reasons for market failure [2]. Buyers tend to have less information than sellers about products, which results in buyers’ inability to evaluate product quality before making a purchase [42]. This condition is especially serious in the online shopping context because nearly all of the information that buyers can obtain is from a seller’s description on its web page; unlike in the offline shopping context, buyers cannot touch the product or judge its quality directly. Thus, some sellers might take advantage of this opportunity to deliver a product of lower quality than claimed in their description of the product. To avoid the potential for online shopping deception, reputation systems have been introduced to encourage sellers to be honest. It has also been found that a seller’s good reputation can help to reduce a buyer’s risk perception [4]. Based on Shapiro [57], a seller with a good reputation will not behave opportunistically using a price premium. According to this logic, a seller will obtain more profit in the long term from his good reputation because a good reputation will bring sellers both higher prices and more customers. A seller who engages in opportunistic behavior not only damages his reputation but also incurs losses that exceed long-term gains. Thus, a good reputation not only ensures sellers long-term profits but also ensures sellers’ honest behavior. In the end, both sellers’ and buyers’ benefits would be ensured by this mechanism.

Because of the prosperity of online shopping markets, reputation systems (also known as online feedback mechanisms) have been the focus of considerable research effort. Research confirms the contribution of reputation systems from the perspective of outcomes such as trust building [5], higher profits [43] and improved market performance [71]. In addition to the positive functions brought about by reputation systems, considerable research has begun to explore participation and communication issues related to those systems.

Resnick and Zeckhauser [53] summarize several findings from their investigation of the reputation systems used by online auction website eBay: (1) More than half of transactions receive feedback; (2) an extremely high percentage of that feedback is positive; (3) when Pollyanna assessments (disproportionately positive ratings that make newcomers overestimate the website’s performance) are encouraged, the system is likely to be less effective or convincing. Dellarocas [21] regards online feedback mechanisms as a digitized form of word-of-mouth (WOM) behavior. Having conducted a case study on eBay’s feedback mechanism, he proposes several ways to design, evaluate and use these feedback mechanisms more effectively. Dellarocas and Wood confirm that satisfied customers are likely to give feedback, and satisfactory outcomes are more likely to be reported by online buyers, which could account for the overwhelming proportion of positive ratings on eBay [23].

Dellarocas et al. [22] investigate participation conditions in online reputation systems in the context of eBay and demonstrate that altruism, self-interest and reciprocity all lead to buyers participation. That research finds that voluntary online feedback contributions are primarily motivated by self-interest rather than pure altruism. It also finds that feedback submission is positively correlated to reputation scores. However, Dellarocas et al. do not differentiate between sellers’ and buyers’ contributions to reputation systems [22]. Thus, it is primarily sellers that have self-interested motives: buyers’ self-interest is not implicated by their contributions [77]. Furthermore, as iResearch shows [35], buyers’ comments receive the most attention when purchase decisions are considered. Thus, it is necessary to investigate participation in reputation systems from the buyers’ perspective.

## 2.2. Relationship quality

The concept of relationship quality arises out of the areas of service management and relationship marketing. It refers to the customer being ‘‘able to rely on the salesperson’s integrity and having confidence in the salesperson’s future performance because the level of past performance has been consistently satisfactory’ [20]. Although some researchers have recently applied the concept to interorganizational relationships [32], it was originally applied to interpersonal relationships, which is our current context. Relationship quality is a critical indicator of a healthy relationship between a buyer and seller and has been used to measure interpersonal relationships (customer-salesperson) [20]. Relationship quality has been defined as a high-order construct comprised of several components. However, so far, there is no consensus on what these components are. As Hennig-Thurau [31] claims, authors ‘‘tend to assume that everyone has some type of intuitive understanding of what it involves’’. For instance, Crosby et al. [20] first indicate that trust and satisfaction are the basic components of relationship quality. They propose that relationship quality can be ‘‘achieved through the sales person’s ability to reduce perceived uncertainty’’. Sanchez-Franco, Ramos and Velicia [55] suggest that relationship quality should include trust, satisfaction, and commitment.

Skarmeas and Robson [59] consider trust, commitment, satisfaction and conflict to create relationship quality. Zhang et al. [78] include factors of satisfaction and trust.

The preceding review of the literature on relationship quality indicates that it is regarded as a high-order construct composed of several different—but related—dimensions. Because relationship quality has numerous components, its definition can vary according to the research context. However, satisfaction and trust are the most common factors across the research studies we examined. Trust refers to ‘‘a confident belief that the salesperson can be relied upon to behave in such a manner that the long-term interest of the customer will be served’’, and satisfaction refers to the emotional state that is created as a result of an evaluation of the interactions between buyers and sales people [20]. In addition, Crosby and his colleagues focus on the perspective of a salesperson’s ability to reduce customers’ perceived uncertainty. This is salient for our research context, C2C online shopping, in which online buyers are confronted by higher uncertainty than are offline buyers; consequently, relationship quality composed of satisfaction and trust also makes sense in our research model.

## 2.3. Conflict handling theory

Conflict, which has been defined as the ‘‘perception of incompatibility between values, needs, interests or actions’’ ([76], p. 167), occurs in people’s daily lives in many different ways. It is believed that different strategies in handling conflict will lead to different outcomes and different relationships [60]. Conflict handling styles have been the focus of research in many disciplines, notably those of management, negotiation and leadership. Moberg ([44], p. 147) presents conflict styles as ‘‘specific behavioral patterns that one prefers to employ when addressing conflict situations’’. Shell [58] complements this concept and adds that conflict styles can be viewed as people’s predispositions related to their behavior in conflict situations, which are formed due to the influence of personality.

Blake and Mouton [9] propose a dual concern model, describing five styles of interpersonal conflict management that are derived from two dimensions: ‘‘concern for people’’ and ‘‘concern for production’’. Blake and Mouton [8] indicate that these dimensions are the two major factors that would cause a person concern during a conflict situation. Based on Blake and Mouton’s model, other two-dimensional models have been developed to explain the choice of different conflict styles when confronted with interpersonal conflicts [30,50,51,63]. In these models, researchers—despite having given different names to the dimensions—agree that conflict handling choice is based on two dimensions: ‘‘concern for self’’ and ‘‘concern for others’’ [61]. Five conflict handling styles have been developed from these two dimensions [61]: ‘‘competing (high concern for self and low concern for others); collaborating (high concern for both self and others); compromising (moderate concern for both self and others); accommodating (low concern for self and high concern for others); and avoiding (low concern for self and for others)’’. Under this framework, when determining styles of cognition and thinking it is crucial to understand how people respond to conflict.

Previous research [64,68] indicates that the avoiding and compromising conflict handling styles, which can coexist, are the most likely to be adopted by Chinese people who encounter conflict situations. Munduate [46] contends that people are likely to combine different patterns of conflict handling styles rather than using each style independently. Van De Vliert, Euwema and Huismans ([67], p. 272) propose the concept of conglomerated conflict handling behavior to describe the ‘‘aggregation of various degrees of several modes of conflict handling’’.

## 3. Research hypotheses

## 3.1. Preliminary interviews

Before developing hypotheses, and as part of our preliminary research, we conducted a small-scale set of interviews with online buyers from TaoBao to understand the research context more clearly. The TaoBao website was chosen as our research context because of its leading status in China’s C2C online shopping market. According to a report from iResearch [36], TaoBao has more than 90% of the Chinese C2C market. After each transaction on TaoBao is complete, buyers are given the opportunity to rate sellers with a simple ternary evaluation: positive, negative or neutral. Buyers are also invited to contribute up to 500 words of text describing their feelings about the transaction. Neither the evaluation nor the textual feedback is compulsory. However, if a buyer does not rate a seller’s performance, then the seller is automatically given a positive evaluation by the reputation system. These system-generated positive evaluations can be distinguished from positive ratings that buyers give deliberately. A seller’s reputation is closely tied to buyers’ evaluations. One reputation point is awarded for a positive evaluation, no points are awarded for a neutral evaluation, and one point is subtracted for a negative evaluation. The more reputation points a seller accumulates, the higher its rank. Generally speaking, highly ranked sellers find it easier to attract buyers [37]. After a rating has been submitted, TaoBao operates a review process that can change a negative rating to a positive one if the seller can provide persuasive evidence that the negative rating is unreasonable. Ratings may also be changed if they are the result of actions such as an inappropriate manipulation of the reputation system (e.g., an action taken by a seller to negatively affect a competitor’s reputation).

We interviewed 30 buyers, each of whom had previously engaged in at least three successful transactions on TaoBao. Each interview lasted 10–30 min. The interviews were conducted online using WangWang, a proprietary instant messaging tool designed for and primarily used by sellers and buyers on TaoBao. We asked open-ended questions about participation in reputation systems. Online buyers’ answers were coded using open coding techniques [18], classifying textual answers from buyers as corresponding to different factors. After the open coding, we used axial coding to group the concepts into similar themes. The results of the interviews are listed in Table 1.

The results of the interviews indicate that a buyer’s intention to participate in a reputation system can be directly affected by satisfaction and conflict handling styles. After conducting the interviews, we organized an online group discussion to triangulate the data collected in the interviews. During the online discussion, we confirmed the interview results and identified a new factor: trust in the seller. We clarify that if buyers trust a seller, they will be more willing to leave textual feedback.

## 3.2. Hypothesis development

A high quality buyer-seller relationship is primarily due to previous satisfying interactions and has a significant impact on future seller-buyer interactions [8]. Buyers who exhibit high relationship quality are inclined to be more cooperative and confident with sellers, and thus, they are more likely to offer positive ratings and feedback to enhance sellers’ reputations. In addition, positive rating and textual feedback have the characteristic of a very large scale, exercising control (power) through the introduction of automated feedback mediators by information technology, and offering trustworthy information through online interaction [21]. Information offered through reputation systems can be regarded as a form of digital WOM behavior [21], and elements of relationship quality, including satisfaction and trust, have been found to have a positive impact on WOM behavior in the Chinese context [13].

On C2C online shopping platforms, ratings and textual feedback submitted by buyers are based on a single transaction with a particular seller. Thus, satisfaction with the seller exerts a significant impact on positive rating and feedback submission intention. It has been found that satisfied buyers have a higher propensity to post positive feedback [23] because in the business context, satisfaction with a transaction is mostly consistent with satisfaction with the service provider [19].

Satisfaction is an important determinant of spreading information through WOM behavior [3,11]. Casalo et al. [11] indicate that satisfaction with previous interactions on a bank website have a positive influence on positive WOM behavior. Ranaweera and Prabhu [52], after conducting a large-scale survey of fixed-line telephone users, report a similar result, showing that satisfaction is the primary driver of positive WOM. In an analysis of more than 50,000 eBay transactions, Dellarocas and Wood [23] conclude that satisfied traders are more likely to offer feedback, whereas dissatisfied traders are more likely to choose to remain silent. Thus, we hypothesize as follows:

H1. Satisfaction with a seller has a positive effect on the intention to submit a positive rating.

H2. Satisfaction with a seller has a positive effect on the intention to submit textual feedback.

Trust has been defined as ‘‘existing when one party has confidence in an exchange partner’s reliability and integrity’’ [45]. There has been little research on the relationship between trust and participation in reputation systems. However, we find some clues from past research on trust. Previous research indicates that trust can lead to cooperative behavior in both virtual and real contexts [48]. In the online shopping context, submitting a positive rating is a typical cooperative behavior because a positive rating can enhance a seller’s reputation. Prior research also shows that trust leads to positive WOM behavior [52]. Ranaweera and Prabhu [52] propose that trust is an antecedent of customer loyalty and that positive WOM is a typical characteristic of a loyal customer. It also has been demonstrated that buyer trust in an seller leads to positive WOM intentions [28]. Accordingly, we propose as follows:

H3. Trust in a seller has a positive effect on the intention to submit a positive rating.

H4. Trust in a seller has a positive effect on the intention to submit textual feedback.

Our pre-survey interview results provided hints of antecedents of the intention to submit positive ratings from the perspective of conflict handling style. Many buyers adopt either avoiding or accommodating conflict handling styles when they are in conflict with sellers. A buyer with a high accommodating conflict handling style expresses a low degree of concern for self and a high degree of concern for the seller. If these buyers receive an unsatisfactory product, they are not likely to openly blame the sellers; indeed, they tend to think that a negative or neutral rating will harm the seller’s reputation, which in turn could influence future transactions with that seller.

A buyer with a high avoiding conflict handling style expresses a low concern both for self and for the seller. If such a buyer receives an unsatisfactory product, he or she is likely to offer the seller a positive rating to avoid an embarrassing experience or conflict. Alternatively, they allow the system to automatically give the seller a positive rating.

These actions are consistent with prior research demonstrating that traditional Chinese culture may influence Chinese negotiating behavior [38]. For instance, research indicates that people such as the Chinese, who are influenced by a long-term orientation culture, are inclined to suppress conflict [54]. Thus, Chinese people are inclined to adopt avoiding and accommodating conflict handling styles rather than a competing style. Ting-Toomey et al. [65] investigate the relationship between face maintenance dimensions and conflict styles in five countries, including China. Their research shows that Chinese people are more likely to use a high degree of both accommodating and avoiding conflict handling styles. Furthermore, Ma [40] proposes that the Chinese culture of collectivism might lead to nonconfrontational styles, including the accommodating and avoiding styles. Thus, we propose the following two hypotheses:

H5. An accommodating style of conflict handling will positively affect a buyer’s intention to submit positive ratings.

H6. An avoiding style of conflict handling will positively affect a buyer’s intention to submit positive ratings

According to Ford and Staples [26], a person’s perceived value of knowledge (PVK), which reflects ‘‘the value that the individual places on his/her knowledge’’, is another critical factor that impacts his/her knowledge-sharing intention. Ford and Staples [26] propose three dimensions of PVK—the usefulness, the benefits and the source of knowledge—and then suggest that these three dimensions are critical determinants of a person’s intention to share knowledge. They also suggest that PVK could indirectly affect knowledge sharing by functioning as a moderator of existing knowledge sharing intentions. For example, individuals could be inclined to share high-value knowledge with close colleagues and low-value knowledge with distant colleagues [26]. In the context of reputation systems, textual feedback does not fit the dimension of benefits because online buyers who have purchased a particular type of product cannot receive direct rewards or benefits when they offer useful and credible views in reputation systems. Therefore, a buyer’s knowledge-sharing behavior might be more directly related to interpersonal factors such as trust rather than to her/his PVK. This suggests that the influence of a buyer’s PVK on her/his intention to submit textual feedback is mediated through a process that involves leveraging the impacts of relationship quality.

Some scholars have suggested the importance of exploring the moderating effects of PVK in knowledge sharing. For instance, Ye et al. [73] suggest that PVK positively moderates the relationship between the enjoyment of helping others and knowledge contribution intention in virtual communities. Consequently, we propose that higher PVK can strengthen the relationship between satisfaction and the intention to submit textual feedback. According to Anderson [3], altruism is the most important underlying reason that explains the positive relationship between satisfaction and WOM. Therefore, for a buyer whose textual feedback submitting intention is primarily motivated by satisfaction, higher PVK means a greater perceived contribution of her/his submission that consequently fits her/his altruistic stance, eventually resulting in a stronger intention to submit textual feedback.

Conversely, the relationship between trust and the intention to submit textual feedback can also be positively moderated by PVK. Developing cooperative behavior is a basic consideration of a trusted buyer when submitting textual feedback [48]. Because a higher PVK means that the perceived contributions of a buyer’s textual submissions are more valuable, feedback submission from a buyer with a high PVK could ensure that such trust-based cooperative behavior becomes stronger and more meaningful. Thus, we propose that PVK can positively moderate two relationships: the relationship between satisfaction and the intention to submit textual feedback; and the relationship between trust and the intention to submit textual feedback. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows:

H7. Perceived value of knowledge positively moderates the relationship between satisfaction and the intention to submit textual feedback.

H8. Perceived value of knowledge positively moderates the relationship between trust and the intention to submit textual feedback.

Scholars indicate that an individual’s behavior is likely to be affected by social influences in uncertain situations, and therefore, an individual would tend to follow the majority’s preferences and engage in mimetic behavior; in this way, herding behavior occurs [7]. Social conformity, which refers to ‘‘the alignment of people’s thinking or behavior with a societal or group norm’’ ([17], p. 77), has been investigated in a variety of contexts that potentially relate to people’s behavior. Social influences reflect both normative and informational social influences. Normative social influence is ‘‘an influence to conform with the positive expectations of another’’ and informational social influence refers to ‘‘an influence to accept information obtained from another as evidence about reality’’ [24]. Scholars suggest that in the online shopping context, social conformity can cause herding behavior. For instance, Dholakia et al. [25] note that online buyers are more attracted by products that have existing bids. Huang and Chen [34] suggest that herding behavior caused by sales volume and customer reviews exerts an influence on buyers’ product choices. In this way, a buyer’s sense of social conformity might play an important role in the process of influencing relationship quality and conflict handling styles related to a buyer’s intention to submit a positive rating.

High levels of social conformity indicate that buyers are likely to be strongly influenced by normative social influences from sellers and informational social influences from other buyers during their participation in reputation systems. In TaoBao’s reputation system, for example, more than 99% of ratings are positive, which constitutes a strong informational social influence. Consequently, sellers have a strong expectation for positive ratings from buyers, which in turn forms a strong normative social influence. In this situation, buyers characterized by high social conformity might feel that submitting a positive rating is an implicit routine. Conversely, an individual’s social conformity will impact his or her behavior in conflict situations [16]. ## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

Due to the paucity of prior literature, a small-scale set of presurvey interviews was conducted to explore the research scope more thoroughly. Based on our analysis of the interview data, we then proposed a research model that incorporates both a relationship quality perspective (including satisfaction and trust) and conflict handling styles. The interview data suggest that buyers are inclined to choose both accommodating and avoiding conflict handling styles when they disagree with sellers. Thus, we include both these two conflict handling styles in our model. In this study, according to the real process that a buyer needs to complete, participation in a reputation system has been divided into two segments: indicative ratings (numerical scores) and qualitative textual feedback.

We adopted the survey method to test our hypotheses because this method enabled us to gather real information from online consumers, enhancing the realism of our study. TaoBao’s website (www.taobao.com) was chosen as the research context given its large share of China’s C2C online market. We selected eight categories (as classified by the TaoBao website) of tangible products: digital products, cosmetics, clothes, accessories, food, sports and stationery, mother and child, and household. For each of TaoBao’s product categories, we chose the first ten sellers through the search engine without any sorting criteria. We then used the reputation system to identify buyers who had transacted with these sellers. This contact information provided us with access to those buyers and we invited ten buyers from each seller to participate in our survey. In this way, we randomly identified 800 online buyers (i.e., 100 buyers per category).

We also prepared a text version of the questionnaire for those who failed or did not want to open the hyperlink but remained willing to participate in the survey. An important inclusion criterion was that a survey respondent must have had at least three transaction experiences on the TaoBao website. In this way, we could be sure that the respondents were familiar with the website’s transactional processes. The data collection lasted for one and one-half months. To motivate participation and ensure a higher response rate, we organized a drawing for respondents who completed the survey. When the data collection was complete, a prize ranging from 30 to 100 RMB (US\$4–14) was sent to randomly selected respondents through TaoBao’s online payment system (Alipay). We received 269 valid responses (a 33.63% response rate). Table 2 shows the respondents’ demographic information.

## 4.2. Measures

Following Churchill’s [15] suggestions, most of the items used in the survey were developed from the literature. We adapted some of the items to better suit our research context. For some constructs that could not be found in the literature, we developed new questions based on a literature review combined with the key words and content mentioned by interviewees during the online interviews. All of the questions in the questionnaire were measured using seven-point scales anchored from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’. Three Ph.D. students who had online buying experience with TaoBao reviewed the questionnaire items and suggested improvements. All of the items are listed in Appendix 1.

Because the research was to be conducted in China, all of the instrument items were translated into Chinese. In the translation process, we followed the translation committee approach [66]. ## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement model

Using two-stage analytical procedures [29], we examined the measurement model and structural relationships. We examined content validity, convergent validity and discriminant validity to validate the measurement model. Content validity was assessed by reviewing the literature and pilot testing the instrument.

We examined the value of factor loadings, Cronbach’s Alpha, composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE) to assess convergent validity. The results of CFA (confirmatory factor analysis) show that all loadings were above 0.6. As shown in Table 3, Cronbach’s Alphas ranged from 0.619 to 0.969, which were well above the benchmark value of 0.60, and the composite reliability ranged from 0.795 to 0.976, which was higher than the recommended value of 0.7 [47]. Discriminant validity could be tested by comparing the relationship between correlations among constructs and the square root of the AVEs [27]. Table 4 indicates that the square root of the AVEs of each construct is greater than the correlations between constructs, suggesting full discriminant validity. In addition, as shown in Appendix 2, all items load on their own constructs.

## 6. Discussion

The primary effects of relationship quality on participation in reputation systems offer some new findings along with the confirming arguments expressed in prior studies. Satisfaction with sellers was found to have a significant positive relationship with the two types of participation in reputation systems: positive rating and textual feedback submission. However, we found that trust is positively related to the intention to submit a positive rating but did not find any significant relationship between trust and the intention to submit textual feedback. This indicates that although trust in a seller can lead to the submission of a positive rating, it is insufficient to lead to the submission of textual feedback, which contradicts previous research in offline environments [52]. It has been argued that consumers will recommend a trusted provider to their acquaintances primarily because previous experiences might give them the confidence to do so [41]. However, providing textual feedback online is different from providing feedback offline because online consumers do not know who will read their textual feedback. Thus, they might feel that they do not need to take responsibility for those strangers by sharing their online shopping experiences even if they do trust a certain online seller. In addition, our results indicate that satisfaction is the only antecedent with a positive effect on the intention to submit textual feedback, which is consistent with the finding of Dellarocas and Woods [23] that dissatisfied online buyers are inclined to remain silent. In addition, this result confirms that individuals may not want to express the true extent of their dissatisfaction to people with whom they have weak ties [70].

In accordance with our hypotheses, satisfaction, trust and an accommodating conflict handling style are found to be positively related to the intention to submit positive ratings. However, our data analysis indicates that an avoiding conflict handling style is negatively associated with the intention to submit positive ratings, which is contrary to our expectations. From the definition of avoiding conflict handling style, we can find that this style reveals a low degree of concern for both self and others [61]. Therefore, people who adopt an avoiding conflict handling style do not consider anyone else’s interest but simply avoid conflict situations. In addition, we measured intention rather than the actual behavior of submitting positive ratings. Online buyers who adopt an avoiding conflict handling style might not intend to submit positive ratings, but still choose to avoid possible arguments with sellers and therefore allow the reputation system to assign a positive rating by default. In this way, the existence of an avoiding conflict handling style can also explain why some buyers stopped giving ratings altogether and simply allowed the system to assign positive ratings automatically.

The positive relationship between an accommodating conflict handling style and the intention to submit positive ratings indicates that an accommodating conflict handling style is an important factor that influences participation in reputation systems. The primary reason for buyers to adopt an accommodating conflict style may be due to a high level of empathy with the seller developed during the buyer-seller interaction process [10]. TaoBao’s website offers a platform where buyers and sellers can communicate with each other easily. One consequence of this communication can be an increased level of empathy, which results in buyers behaving more considerately toward sellers. Thus, even if buyers are dissatisfied with a product, they may still take the seller’s effort into consideration and submit a positive rating as a form of encouragement, especially if the service quality was good. This act of consideration is very important to a novice seller who desperately needs to develop and enhance his/her reputation.

With respect to the moderating influences of perceived value of knowledge, we found only that PVK strengthens the relationship between satisfaction and the intention to submit textual feedback. Because we do not find a significant relationship between trust and the intention to submit textual feedback, this study supports our hypothesis that PVK can only catalyze existing intentions. The results also indicate that social conformity at a general level strengthens the relationship between an avoiding conflict handling style and the intention to submit positive ratings. One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that there are many default positive ratings in an online reputation system, which means that buyers who adopt an avoiding conflict handling style are less willing to give a positive rating themselves, instead letting the system assign it by default. These default positive ratings not only help buyers avoid potential conflict with sellers but also help buyers to avoid giving a positive rating that would be the opposite of their original intention. However, for the factor that has a positive relationship with the intention to submit positive ratings, a moderating effect has not been found. One possible explanation is that if online consumers are inclined to adopt an accommodating conflict handling style toward sellers, they are likely to be less influenced by social conformity because they believe that their decision is appropriate in the context of the TaoBao website and thus is more socially acceptable. This indicates that when making decisions to submit ratings, especially neutral or negative ratings, people with high levels of social conformity are likely to be affected by other buyers’ prior ratings.

This research confirms that satisfied online buyers are more likely to submit textual feedback and positive ratings. Previous research shows that ‘‘reluctance to transmit bad news’’ [23] is a possible explanation. In addition, there are other possible explanations for this phenomenon. Because many online buyers choose to adopt an avoiding conflict handling style, this phenomenon indicates that they might not want to remind themselves of an unpleasant experience and so choose to remain silent. According to Wetzer, Zeelenberg and Pieters [69], there are eight motivations that can lead to WOM behavior: comfort search, venting, advice search, bonding, entertaining, self-presentation, warning and revenge. Due to limitations in a reputation system’s functions, not all of the goals of WOM—such as comfort search and advice search—can be realized. Thus, buyers have less motivation to submit their negative textual feedback after a dissatisfying shopping experience. Moreover, some buyers indicated that they did not fully trust the reputation system because their own negative ratings and negative textual feedback something disappeared without any appropriate explanation. Thus, they felt that submitting feedback was useless, and they stopped doing so.

## 7. Limitations and extensions

This study has some limitations that should be addressed in future research. This research measures and investigates buyers’ perceptions only. However, both sellers and buyers can participate in reputation systems, resulting in an interactional process. Dellarocas and Wood [23] propose that sellers’ ratings and textual feedback can also affect buyers’ participation. Future research could take the sellers’ perspective into consideration to render our understanding of participation process more complete.

We did not collect sufficient data describing buyers who submitted negative or neutral ratings. Consequently, we do not have a deep understanding of the antecedents of negative or neutral ratings nor of how these negative or neutral sentiments are expressed. What would buyers do with negative or neutral information obtained from reputation systems and how would such information affect their interactions with sellers? What would motivate them to submit negative or neutral ratings? How else might they express negative or neutral feelings about their transaction experiences? These questions could usefully be studied in future research.

In addition, more research is necessary in the following directions. First, textual feedback has its own valence similar to that of WOM, i.e., it can be negative, positive or neutral. However, the valence of feedback has been ignored in this research, even though negative WOM and positive WOM might have different antecedents and thus be motivated differently. Therefore, in future research, negative, neutral, and positive WOM could be differentiated and different motives could be explored. Second, research on online knowledge contribution has emphasized the quality of knowledge, suggesting that high-quality knowledge could keep an online community alive. In this study, we have only investigated participation intentions. Yoo, Suh, and Lee [75] find that knowledge quality can influence participation in online communities. Thus, the quality of feedback is critical for the future development of reputation systems.

## 8. Implications

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study makes significant contributions to both research and practice. This study advances theoretical development in the area of reputation systems in the Chinese C2C online shopping context by integrating a relationship quality framework into our research model. We further enrich the study of reputation systems by examining factors from the relationship quality framework and the effect of conflict handling styles on the intention to participate in reputation systems. This contributes to a better understanding of why online buyers from C2C websites are willing to participate in reputation systems. Indeed, research that focuses on the antecedents of participation in reputation systems is quite limited. Researchers studying reputation systems primarily emphasize the functionality of the system itself but neglect the participation process. Dellarocas [21] encourages researchers to conduct empirical research that can consider the perspectives of systems users. This study responds to his call. From the perspective of relationship quality, our research extends our current understanding of reputation systems by refocusing attention from the system perspective to the user perspective. A high value of R square (0.528 for the intention to submit positive ratings and 0.393 for the intention to submit textual feedback) for each of the dependent variables indicates that we have captured the right orientation in this area.

Another contribution to the extant literature is that participation in reputation systems can also be explained through different conflict handling styles. This finding breaks new ground by introducing conflict handling styles into reputation systems research and confirms that an accommodating conflict handling style has a positive effect on positive rating intentions, whereas an avoiding conflict handling style has a negative effect. This indicates the need for future research by adding a more detailed and comprehensive examination on conflict handling styles into reputation research.

Our findings related to the moderating effect of PVK enhance our understanding of the function of PVK in both the reputation systems context and the knowledge sharing area. Although the previous literature suggests that PVK has a direct effect on sharing intentions, we believe that PVK can also be regarded as a moderator that strengthens existing positive relationships. Accordingly, this study sheds new light on the role of PVK in affecting individuals’ intentions both to share and to participate in online reputation systems.

The results of this study lead us to offer several suggestions for encouraging online buyers to participate in reputation systems. Sellers must be honest and frank with buyers, keeping them updated with transactional information. They also need to develop friendly relationships with buyers. For example, to offer buyers better options, sellers can use online communication to understand buyers’ personal preferences. This can lead to buyers having higher levels of satisfaction and therefore a stronger intention to participate in reputation systems.

There are also some implications for websites to improve their management of reputation systems. Currently, there is an atmosphere in which sellers cannot tolerate neutral or negative ratings. E-commerce platforms such as TaoBao should encourage buyers to submit honest feedback related to transactions instead of being accommodating and giving positive responses. Excessive positive feedback could mislead future buyers and create distrust in reputation systems. Finally, in the end, excessive positive feedback could even damage the platform’s reputation.

Not all buyers realize that their textual feedback is useful to future buyers. Platform managers could emphasize this point by adding some hints when asking buyers to write a comment, making them realize that they can make a valuable contribution to novice buyers by writing several words or sentences.

Buyers who have an avoiding conflict handling style are likely to let the system automatically leave a positive rating. Clearly, these positive ratings cannot reflect buyers’ real evaluations of transactions. Moreover, these positive ratings can mislead future buyers, especially novice buyers. Thus, if buyers do not want to participate in reputation systems, they should have the right to quit instead of allowing the system to give a positive rating by default.
