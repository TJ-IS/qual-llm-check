# The value of product presentation technologies on mobile vs. non-mobile devices: A randomized field experiment

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Product presentation Information processing theory Internet commerce Mobile devices Channels Field experiment

## A B S T R A C T

A maior limitation for e-commerce retailers remains the lack of physical touch and feel of products before purchase. Potential buyers may be reluctant to purchase such products online because of the limited ability to evaluate physical product attributes and to infer their performance in digital media. Retailers have been addressing this limitation by implementing visual product presentation technologies such as zoom and alternative photos. In addition, the widespread adoption and use of smartphones and tablets for online shopping poses another challenge for e-commerce retailers because of the devices' smaller displays and thus limited ability for visual product presentation. In this paper, we empirically investigate how the combination of visual product presentation technologies – via zoom and alternative photos – on mobile as well as non-mobile devices afect sales in online shopping. By utilizing a randomized field experiment, we find that alternative photos are associated with an increased purchase likelihood, while zoom is associated with a decreased purchase likelihood. For the devices, we find that the highest purchases likelihoods can be observed on PCs and tablets – potentially acting as substitutes for each other – followed by smartphones, which may be explained by their smaller display sizes. However, there is no significant interaction between devices and product presentation technologies. We also demonstrate the robustness of our results and discuss implications for e-commerce retailers

## 1. Introduction

A major limitation for e-commerce retailers remains the lack of physical touch before the product purchase [1]. Potential buyers with strong ex ante preferences to touch, feel and try products might shy away from buying products online because of product uncertainty [2]. As a consequence, the presence of product uncertainty might lead to lost sales opportunities for e-commerce retailers.

To address the lack of physical touch, e-commerce retailers – including companies such as Amazon, Alibaba, eBay and Rakuten – have been implementing visual product presentation technologies that go beyond the usage of static product photos [3]. Despite the goal to improve the perceived product presentation and to thereby reduce product uncertainty via product presentation technologies [4], it is not entirely clear whether diferent product presentation technologies will have the same positive efect on product preferences.

However, the presence of diferent underlying information processing modes can be responsible for driving diferences in perceptions of visual product information. To fill this gap, we make use of information processing theory [5], which diferentiates between global and local processing of visual information. Local processing has been shown to diminish the distinctiveness of products, leading to a lower visual appeal and less purchases [6]. This may indicate that only those presentation technologies that are able to convey a global level of distinctive product information, will lead to a higher probability of purchase.

E-commerce has additionally been afected by the widespread adoption and use of mobile devices for online shopping. For example, it is now crucial to optimize shopping websites and apps across diferent devices in order to enable a seamless usage experience [7]. From a behavioral perspective, we also have to understand how the usage of such devices for shopping is correlated with device-specific diferences. One key factor that might afect consumers' shopping behavior is the (smaller) display size, that was found to increase the cognitive load and search costs on mobile compared to non-mobile devices [8].

The purpose of the paper is to experimentally investigate how different visual product presentation technologies on mobile as well as non-mobile devices afect the probability of purchase in online shop ping by answering the following research questions:

RQ1. How does the zoom technology in contrast to alternative photos afect the probability of purchase?

RQ2. How does the interaction between zoom technology and alternative photo afect the probability of purchase?

RQ3. How does device type (i.e., PCs, tablets or smartphones) influence the efects of visual product presentation technologies on the probability of purchase?

To address these research questions, we collaborated with a large European online afiliate platform for lifestyle and fashion products and one of their largest partnerships, a leading European fashion online retailer to conduct a large randomized field experiment with 15,942 unique products and 22,051 unique visitors.

This paper advances the literature in various ways. First, we in tegrate information processing theory to explain how the diferent visual product presentation technologies can lead to diferent information processing levels and thus diferences in perceived utility of visual product information. We combine this with vividness theory to explain the diferences in perception between visual stimuli on the same processing level, which allows us to show diferences in presentation-specific sales outcomes. Second, we extend the impact of product pre sentation technologies on sales by including, theorizing – via cognitive fit – and testing the impact on mobile as well as non-mobile devices. In this regard, related literature in information systems and operations management has not studied the role of device-specific diferences [9,10] or mainly focused on product returns [4]. Third, compared to the majority of these studies, our analyses are based on a randomized field experiment. This allows us to disentangle the efects of diferent visual product presentation technologies in order to measure their causal impact. As a method, randomized field experiments have become popular in the economics, information systems, and marketing literature (e.g., [11]) as they allow for drawing clean inferences by mini mizing the presence of confounded variables [12].

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss previous literature on channel diferences and device-specific characteristics as well as derive our hypotheses from information processing, vividness and cognitive fit theory. In Section 3, we describe the setting and design of the field experiment, using four diferent treatment groups, conducted in collaboration with an online afiliate platform. Section 4 in cludes the descriptive results, which investigate the purchase rates between diferent experimental groups, device types and product categories, as well as the estimation results, which are based on diferent choice models. We additionally incorporate the presentation of different robustness checks in Section 4. Section 5 discusses the results and Section 6 discusses the theoretical and managerial implications as well as limitations. We end with an overall conclusion in Section 7.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

Our paper is related to previous literature on the diferences between online and ofline channels [13]. This includes the concept of product uncertainty [4], as well as device-specific search and shopping behavior [7,8]. Specifically, we rely on information processing theory [5], vividness theory [14] and cognitive fit theory [15] to develop our hypotheses.

## 2.1. Previous literature

2.1.1. Product uncertainty, product presentation technologies and information processing

Literature on the diferences between online and ofline channels dates back to the late 90s, when Lal and Sarvary [13] found that the lack of physical touch before product purchase creates search frictions for non-digital product attributes. This finding is connected to the concept of search and experience attributes, originally introduced by Nelson [16]. Digital (i.e., search) attributes, such as the product price and its textual description, can be relatively easily communicated via websites. However, non-digital (i.e., experience) attributes, such as the feel, texture or fit – in the case of fashion products – are harder to communicate online. This may introduce a relatively higher degree of uncertainty for products that are more dependent on non-digital attributes (e.g., the rather subjective component of fashion trends and styles) [13,17].

The underlying theoretical problem related to buying products on line refers to product uncertainty [2]. Product uncertainty is defined as “the buyer's dificulty in evaluating product characteristics, and predicting how a product will perform in the future” ([18], p. 395). Hong and Pavlou [4] further conceptually diferentiate between product fit uncertainty (i.e., regarding the match of product attributes and consumer preferences) and product quality uncertainty (i.e., regarding a product's fabric). In addition, they investigate the role of digital product photos and online forums on both facets of product uncertainty and find forums to moderate the efects of fit uncertainty; photos reduce the degree of quality uncertainty instead. Similarly, we assume that our study implicitly addresses both types of product uncertainty. Specifi cally, we assume that both types of product uncertainty afect consumers' purchase decisions.

Recent literature started to explore how website-specific technologies can be used to increase online product sales and returns. For example, Kim and Krishnan [19] investigate the efect of product videos on online purchase behavior. They find higher online purchase rates for products which were presented using video commercials. In a similar study, De et al. [20] explore how visual product presentation technologies can mitigate product returns in online shopping. In their study, they find that zoom technology reduces the likelihood of product returns while alternative photo technology increases the return likelihood. In line with De et al. [20], we argue that product presentation technologies beyond static pictures can lead to heterogeneous (i.e., positive and negative) efects on sales.

Our work extends those findings by integrating information processing theory [5] as theoretical concept to better understand the heterogeneous efects of visual presentation technologies on the sales of apparel, shoes and accessories. Visual information processing diferentiates between global and local processing modes. By default. in. dividuals are assumed to process information at the global level (“forest before threes”), which can be explained by the greater perceived importance of the “gestalt” over individual parts [5]. In the case of products, global processing is indicated by observing products' shape, style and design, which are shown to drive the distinctiveness of products [6]. Local processing instead focuses on examining more specific product details such as patterns, texture and stitching.

Consistent with these findings, we suggest that presentation technologies, such as zoom, can shift the processing level. This change to local processing is assumed to reduce the perceived distinctiveness and thus the preference for those products. Besides information processing, we make use of vividness theory as a description of visual presentation formats [14]. This allows us to distinguish between diferent presentation technologies on the same processing level. We also broaden the scope of previous analyses by theorizing and investigating the combined impact of diferent product presentation technologies on mobile as well as non-mobile devices on sales.

## 2.1.2. Mobile vs. non-mobile devices and product presentation technologies

We build upon previous literature on the diferences between mobile (i.e., smartphones and tablets) and non-mobile devices (i.e., laptop and desktop PCs) to get a better understanding about the dimensions and attributes to compare diferent devices. In this regard, devices can be distinguished along the dimensions of usability and ubiquity [7,21]. Ubiquity refers to instant internet access anywhere and anytime.

Usability on the other hand refers to device-specific characteristics that may afect its usability, including the display size [8] and portability [22], as well as the touch interface [23]. Specifically, Ghose et al. [8] examine how internet browsing difers between smartphones and per sonal computers. They find that consumers incur higher search and cognitive costs on smartphones, indicated by less scrolling and a preference for higher ranked content. While the display size might limit the usability of smartphones for online shopping compared to tablets, larger displays on tablets may reduce their portability and thus decreasing the perceived mobility [22]. Moreover, the coverage and bandwidth constraints of mobile networks, for example the quality of the internet service in more rural areas, might also afect the ubiquity and device usability [24]. In this regard, smartphones could be more afected by the quality of mobile networks, as tablets are rather used at home via Wi-Fi networks [25]. Another unique aspect of mobile devices refers to its touch interface. There is some evidence that touch interfaces increase perceived psychological ownership of products that are inspected on mobile touch devices [23]. However, it is not clear whe ther this finding is generalizable, as laptop trackpads also provide some form of touch and more touch-optimized laptops are being sold [26].

In summary, the usability of smartphones, tablets, and PCs (i.e., desktops and laptops) difers based on the display size and port ability as well as the mode of interaction (i.e., mouse/trackpad/non touch vs. touch). Nevertheless, we consider the display size to be the key diferentiating factor based on more established empirical evi dence via field data (e.g., [7]). Accordingly, when comparing tablets to smartphones, display size and navigational convenience might lead to diferent usage and shopping behaviors on smartphones compared to tablets, which might be rather similar to PCs in this regard. This can be summarized as diferences in cognitive fit [15]. Larger displays are thus more suitable – cognitively less taxing – for the decision making in the context of shopping tasks. In addition, the portability-specific diference may also lead to less mobile usage of tablets (e.g., at home as compared to the daily commute), which is again rather similar to PCs.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

The following hypotheses focus on the diferences between product presentation technologies in the context of information processing and vividness theory, additionally combined with the expected diferences in cognitive fit between PCs, tablets and smartphones, and investigate their impact on e-commerce sales for apparel, shoes and accessories.

2.2.1. Product evaluation technologies, information processing and vividness

Compared to static photos, zoom allows consumers to inspect more product details; alternative photos enable consumers to view products from diferent angles and in multiple contexts. However, when comparing zoom to alternative and static photos, information processing theory suggests that the distinction of global and local processing of visual information might lead to diferent benefits of information types [27].

Individuals typically process visual information at the global level (“forest before trees”) [5]. In spite of the default processing of globallevel information, the processing level can be shifted to local informa tion processing by the presentation of diferent visual stimuli [6,27]. More specifically, zoom technology may activate local information processing by providing more detailed product information. Consumers may then be able to observe the fabric, craftsmanship or ornaments of products via zoom.

However, previous research has also shown that the provision of more detailed visual product information can be disadvantageous, because the activation of local information processing might reduce the distinctiveness of products [6]. Specifically, zoom then makes key differentiating product features such as style and design less salient. This suggests that detailed product information via zoom might shift the processing level to focusing on local information, which is assumed to decrease the distinctiveness of products – which are usually processed on the global level – as it is the case of alternative and static photos. The sole availability of zoom is thus expected to decrease the probability of purchase compared to static photos. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Zoom technology decreases the probability of purchase compared to static photos.

In contrast to zoom, alternative photos are assumed to be processed at the global level, which is more beneficial for the overall perception of products. Specifically, products are diferentiated at the global level by aspects such as style and design [28]. Compared to static photos, the presence of alternative photos conveys a higher degree of vividness, allowing consumers to visualize future outcomes [29] as well as to increase the imagined consumption [30]. Specifically, alternative photos can efectively demonstrate how products look in a consumption context (e.g., when showing a human model that is wearing the preferred handbag or pair of shoes).

Overall, this indicates that compared to static photos alternative photos provide greater utility to consumers through a higher degree of vividness. The availability of alternative photos is thus assumed to be associated with an increased probability of purchase compared to static photos. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Alternative photo technology increases the probability of purchase compared to static photos.

The combination of detailed product information via zoom and rather holistic expression of products' style and design via alternative photos prompts the questions whether one stimuli may dominate the other, when both are available at the same time. Prior research has suggested that in the presence of multiple stimuli, more salient stimuli are disproportionately more important than less salient stimuli [31]. For example, the first-stage estimation results of De et al. [20] show that the presence of alternative photos is positively correlated with increased purchases, while zoom usage is simultaneously available and not significant. The usage of multiple images in advertising also suggests that the most outstanding image (either positive or negative) determines the overall degree of consumer response and product evaluation [32]. Furthermore, Pieters and Wedel [33] found that the pre sence of photo elements in ads comes at the expense of attention for other ad elements including brand and text. The availability of multiple alternative photos hence seems to draw more attention. We thus assume that alternative photos will dominate the impact of zoom and maintain information processing at the global level when both options are simultaneously available. Therefore, based on the insights from previous literature, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The combination of alternative photo technology and zoom increases the probability of purchase compared to static photos.

## 2.2.2. Mobile vs. non-mobile devices and cognitive fit

When considering the role of device types, previous literature has shown that mobile devices (i.e., smartphones and tablets) mainly difer from non-mobile devices (i.e., laptop and desktop PCs) in the dimensions of ubiquity and usability [21]. We thereby focus on usability aspects related to display size. Smaller display sizes lead to a higher preference of easily accessible content on smartphones (compared to PCs), possible due to the higher cognitive load and search costs [8]. One implication of this finding is that consumers scroll less on smartphones, which is why higher ranked content may be more valuable to content providers. Furthermore, display size can negatively influence users' navigation and typing abilities [34,35], potentially afecting their device-specific shopping behavior. This might indicate that tablets' com parably larger display sizes are more beneficial for shopping-specific applications. Put diferently, larger display sizes provide higher cognitive fit [15], resulting in a better problem solving performance in the context of shopping tasks.

However, display size also decreases device portability, making it a less mobile device, rather akin to PCs on that dimension [7]. Specifi cally, in the aforementioned study, tablets were shown to act as sub stitutes for PCs in online shopping, while smartphones were found to be complements to both channels. These results, hence, suggest that tablets and PCs share numerous common characteristics – similar average display sizes in particular. Smartphones' limited display sizes, in contrast, seem to trigger a diferent set of behaviors such as the tendency to prefer higher ranked, more visible content and less scrolling. This can be summarized as lack of cognitive fit when conducting shopping tasks on smartphones.

Combining these insights with product presentation technologies and information processing theory, we assume that the underlying impact of presentation technologies are diminished by smaller display sizes, as they complicate visual inspection and assessment of products. Therefore, we expect the hypothesized main efects of visual presentation technologies (H1–H3) to be amplified by the devices' cognitive fit via diferences in display size. Since tablets and PCs are comparably similar in terms of display size, we conjecture them to lead to similar outcomes. Thus, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4 (H4). Larger display sizes amplify (a) the negative efect of zoom, (b) the positive efect of alternative photos, and (c) the positive efect of the combination of zoom and alternative photo technology on the probability of purchase.

## 3. Field experiment

To test the hypotheses and to investigate how diferent visual pro duct presentation technologies on mobile and non-mobile devices afect sales, we designed and executed a randomized field experiment in collaboration with both a large European online afiliate platform for lifestyle and fashion products and one of their largest partners, a leading European fashion retailer that sells apparel, shoes and accessories.<sup>2</sup>

In our experimental design, we manipulated the way products were presented to potential buyers on the afiliate's website. The website itself was fully responsive by allowing to render the site well for every display size, independent of the device type. During a period of eight days, our pool of participants was randomly sampled from all website visitors who landed on our partner's start page either directly (i.e., typein trafic) or via organic search. We did not include visitors from paid trafic sources, which may have been biased by product-specific ads or by deep links to specific products.

Upon landing on the start page, an automated server-side allocation mechanism – so-called round-robin load balancing that distributes trafic equally across computing resources – randomly assigned website visitors (i.e., participants) to one of four treatment groups, which were hosted on four diferent servers. This setup allowed for so-called sticky sessions, visitors were hence unable to self-select or change their treatment group throughout their respective session, which was logged by a combination of IP address and cookie information

Fig. 1 shows the 2 × 2 between-subjects experimental design. We manipulated the visual product presentation technologies that consisted of zoom, alternative photos as well as a combination of both. Static product photos served as control group. Based on the manipulation process, the four resulting experimental groups thus comprised the following four product presentation stimuli: (1) static photo (control), (2) zoom, (3) alternative photos, and (4) alternative photos & zoom. A visual example of group 4 can be seen in Fig. 2. In group 1, the control group, only one static product photo was presented to participants, without any additional options to refine the product presentation. In group 2, participants had the option to use the zoom technology, which allowed them to see more product details such as the fabric, crafts manship or ornaments. In group 3, participants were enabled to view alternative photos of each focal product, allowing them to evaluate and contextualize products from diferent perspectives, worn by human models.<sup>3</sup> In group 4, participants had the option to use the zoom technology as well as to view alternative photos of products. Note that prior to the experiment, potential buyers only saw the static product photo (our control group).

## 4. Results

In the following, we first present the descriptive results on purchase rates between diferent experimental groups, device types and product categories. We then depict the estimation results by making use of dif ferent choice models. In addition, we map the results to the hypotheses. The section closes with several robustness checks to validate our results.

## 4.1. Descriptive result

The descriptive results of all variables are shown in Table 2. Fig. 3 illustrates purchase rates clustered by experimental treatment group. Average purchase rates are significantly diferent between all treatment groups $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 3 1 . 0 2 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 5 )$ ). Mean purchase rates in group 4 (alt photo & zoom: 7.58%) are higher than the corresponding purchase rates in group 3 (alt. photo: 7.37%), group 2 (zoom: 5.7%) and group 1 (control: 6.38%). The diference between groups 4 and 1 is significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 9 . 3 2 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 5 )$ , as is the diference between groups 4 and 2 $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 3 . 8 1 , \ \mathrm { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . Similar, we find significant diferences between groups 3 and 1 $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 6 . 3 8 , \ \mathrm { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ and groups 3 and 2 $( \mathbb { \chi } ^ { 2 } = 1 8 . 8 4 , \ \mathbb { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . The purchase rate in group 1 is also significantly higher than in group $2 \ ( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 . 0 7 , \ \mathrm { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . However, there is no significant diference between groups 3 (alt. photo) and 4 (alt. photo & zoom). The overall insight is that the lowest purchase rate can be found in group 2 (zoom), which is consistent with hypothesis 1. Our explanation for this finding is that zoom shifts the processing level to local processing by highlighting product details such as the fabric, craftsmanship or ornaments. The resulting detailed insights may eventually diminish the distinctiveness of products, resulting in lower purchase rates at the margin.

## 4.3. Mapping the results to the hypotheses

Our hypotheses were tested using a variety of descriptive and model-based approaches. Hypotheses 1–3, regarding the efects of presentation technology-specific diferences, are supported. This support can be pinpointed to the significant diferences in mean purchase rates between the control group (i.e., group 1) and all other groups (see Fig. 3 and Section 4.1). Specifically, hypothesis 1 refers to the significant diference between groups 1 (static photos) and 2 (zoom). Hypothesis 2 is based on the significant diference between groups 1 and 3 (alternative photos), while hypothesis 3 refers to the significant diference between groups 1 and 4 (alternative photos & zoom). Additional model-based support can be found in Table 3 (see Section 4.2). However, hypothesis 4, regarding the efects of device-specific diferences in combination with presentation technologies, is not supported. This relationship was tested by estimating interaction efects between presentation technologies and devices in our choice model. The results in Table 3 reveal that there are no significant interaction efects.

## 4.4. Robustness checks

The randomization procedure in our experimental design already addresses important issues related to self-selection and causality. However, we additionally make use of diferent model specifications, subsample analyses as well as alternative estimation models to test the robustness of our results that might have been afected by the logit model itself and/or the selection of variables. Table 4 shows the results including diferent logit model specifications (models 1–3) as well as the results based on alternative estimation models (models 4–5). Table 5 depicts diferent price-specific subsamples.

Specifically, a concern may be that our results are driven by the usage of specific control variables. This is why we estimate the purchase likelihood based on three diferent model specifications: (model 1) only using the main efects, which include the experimental treatments and device-specific variables; (model 2) using the main efect in combination with the control variables (without interaction efects), including product price, product type and product category; and (model 3) using the main efects in combination with the interaction efects between the experimental treatments and the device-specific variables. The results in Table 4 demonstrate the robustness of our main efects.

To address the possibility that our results might be driven by the usage of the logit model, we replicate the estimated main model using probit (model 4) and linear probability models (model 5). In contrast to the logit model, the estimation process in the probit model is based on the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution [39]. The linear probability model is conceptually identical to a linear regression, but estimated with weighted least squares [40]. Both models make use of robust standard errors. The results in Table 4 (model 4 and model 5) indicate that our results are robust to controlling for diferent estimation models.

We also test alternative price-level specific subsamples based on diferent cut-of ranges – at the 90th respectively 99th percentile – to rule out the potential impact of overly expensive products. Our results are also robust to these price-level specific cut-of ranges (see Table 5).

In sum, our results are valid for a variety of logit model specifications, alternative estimation models, and subsamples, as shown Tables 4 and 5.

## 5. Discussion of results

Our results largely support the main efects proposed in hypotheses 1–3. Compared to the control group that included only one static product photo, the availability of zoom decreases consumers' purchase likelihood. The hypothesized explanation for that finding is that zoom shifts consumers' perceptual focus to local processing of product information. Zooming allows consumers to inspect product details and learn about product-specific aspects, for instance fabric or ornaments, which may obfuscate and diminish the distinctiveness of products though. Alternative photos as well as the combination of alternative photos and zoom, in contrast, increase the purchase likelihood. Alternative photos are assumed to be processed on the global level, which is where products are diferentiated. They allow for inspection of diferent angles as well as for contextualization by depicting products in a consumption context (e.g., products are worn by human models). Thi way, consumers can visually learn through the availability of additional photos, showing products in use, if it fits their preferences.

The overall positive efect of alternative photos compared to static photos – also assumed to be processed globally – can be explained by their underlying tendency to convey vividness which was found to increase consumers' imagined consumption [30]. This seems to be particularly relevant for apparel products, as can be seen in Fig. 5. Further, the presence of alternative photos is assumed to dominate the detrimental efects of zoom, when jointly available, as it was found that the presence of outstanding photos dominates consumers' overall response products [32] and thus global information processing. Put diferently, even if consumers seek to gather more detailed product information, the salience of alternative photos (e.g., diferent photos of a human model wearing the selected handbag) maintains information processing on the global level.

Besides the efects of product presentation technologies, devicespecific efects show that smartphones lead to smaller purchase probabilities compared to tablets and PCs. This is in line with previous results that found smartphones to be complements to tablets and PCs based on browsing patterns, including lower purchase rates [41]. For most search-intensive products, smartphones are mainly considered as research channel in contrast to tablets and PCs that are considered as transaction channels [42,43]. Despite diferent browsing patterns (see Fig. 6), tablets are rather similar to PCs when considering the display size and navigational convenience.<sup>9</sup> This is reflected in similar purchase rates (7.33% (PC) vs. 6.79% (Tablet)) and magnitudes of responses to the experimental treatments (see Fig. 4). Tablets and PCs, thus, seem to be substitutes in terms of transactional activities, which can be ex plained by similarities in cognitive fit.

Yet, none of the interactions between our product presentation technologies and devices were found to be significant. This indicates that, compared to the baseline interaction between our control group and PCs, none of the combinations between alternative photos & zoom and mobile devices (i.e., tablets or smartphones) are significantly dif ferent from zero. It seems that display size, as key diferentiating factor, does not moderate (i.e., neither amplify nor abate) the impact of dif ferent product presentation technologies on purchase rates.

When comparing our results to previous research on presentation technologies (e.g., [10,20]) by briefly ignoring the underlying diferences between studies,<sup>10</sup> the overall communality seem to be that a more vivid product presentation (e.g., alternative vs. static photos or virtual fitting rooms vs. static photos) increases consumer preferences for those promoted products. However, we can also show that there are some substantial constraints to increased vividness: more vividly presented products do not necessarily lead to more sales, as it was found in the case of zoom versus static photos.

Despite these findings, it has to be noted that alternative photo technology may serve two purposes [44]: besides demonstrating the overall style of products (e.g., by showing a human model wearing the focal product), it enables consumers to observe diferent angles of the product (e.g., via rotation). This indicates that alternative photos might also provide some information about product details, dependent on the product category [20]. For example, in the case of electronics or household appliances, diferent visual angles via rotating alternative photos might provide useful information about additional underlying technological details, such as the location of input slots and required cable types. However, in cases where the products' overall appearance, style, and design plays a major role, such as for fashion, home design or furniture products, we argue that global information processing dominates more detailed local information [6].

## 6. Implications and limitations

Our results have important theoretical implications for understanding the interplay between product presentation and information processing in e-commerce. Specifically, we extend prior work on in formation processing [5] and vividness theory [14], which both originate from social psychology, to the context of product presentation technologies. By integrating and jointly applying these theories, this paper helps understanding that only those presentation technologies that are able to convey a global level of distinctive product information in combination with a large degree of vividness will lead to higher purchase probabilities. Although this combination seems logic in ret rospect, it requires to be theorized and empirically tested. In addition, we extend the scope of cognitive-fit theory [15] to the domain of de vice-specific consumer behavior. Cognitive fit is a core information systems theory, but it has not yet been directly used as theoretical explanation for the diference between mobile and non-mobile devices. We thus provide an example to utilize a well-established theory that helps to summarize the diferences between mobile and non-mobile devices which are largely based on display size and navigational convenience.

Our findings also have important managerial implications for online retailers who aim to optimize the shopping experience in their e-com merce channels (e.g., websites and apps). Optimizing sales conversions is especially important for afiliates and online marketplaces that are dependent on sales commissions. The general sentiment is that the availability of product presentation technologies helps to reduce product uncertainties [4]. However, our findings indicate that ofering a more vivid visual product presentation does not necessarily lead to more sales compared to static photos. This is especially valid for the sole availability of zoom, which is suggested to shift consumers' information processing to the local level and thus deters the distinctive ness of products. In contrast, the usage of alternative photos seems to boost online sales compared to static photos, as it allows for a vividly induced diferentiation of products. When considering diferences between devices, it seems that display size is the decisive factor for device similarity. The interface design (touch vs. non-touch), in turn, does not lead to any obvious empirical diferences, as can be seen for tablets and PCs. Online retailers should thus be aware that tablets and PCs can act as substitutes, and not necessarily consider smartphones and tablets as equal mobile devices that lead to similar usage and purchase behaviors. However, when implementing product presentation technologies on a larger scale, there do not seem to be any overly negative (or positive) diferences between devices either.

Since we focused on the intention to treat [12], our study comes with the limitation that we did not observe whether participants actually used the available product presentation technologies while browsing the product pages. In addition, the overall generalizability of our results might be dependent on the underlying product category respectively taxonomy, especially when diferentiating between the impact of global and local information processing. We assume that fashion products, such as apparel, with a rather hedonic tendency might be more susceptible to the holistic presentation of distinctive styles (via alternative photos) compared to rather utilitarian (e.g., household appliances) or commodity products (e.g., groceries) with a lower visual appeal and other key diferentiation criteria. In addition, we might also find opposite efects (e.g., a larger benefit of zoom) in the case of technical products where functional product details, such as visual information about input slots or cable types, are assumed to be more important product diferentiators. In addition, we only consider purchases, without looking at product returns, as we are collaborating with an afiliate platform that is interested in optimizing sales commissions via increasing sales. Finally, the experiment only ran for a period of eight days. It would be interesting to investigate long-term efects including measures for customer loyalty and customer lifetime value.

## 7. Conclusion

We use a randomized field experiment to investigate how the combination of visual product presentation technologies on mobile as well as non-mobile devices can afect sales in online shopping. We find large diferences between treatment groups. Specifically, we observe that the usage of alternative photos and the combination of alternative photos and zoom increase consumers' purchase likelihood. However, the sole availability of zoom decreases their purchase likelihood. We also find that smartphones decrease consumers' purchase likelihood compared to PCs and tablets. On the contrary, tablets do not difer from PCs in terms of purchase likelihoods. Lastly, we find no significant interaction efect between product presentation technologies and device type.

Conceptually, product presentation technologies are assumed to reduce product uncertainties in e-commerce settings that lack physical touch before product purchase. To this end, we theorize the distinctiveness of diferent product presentation technologies – zoom and alternative photos – based on information processing theory, which dif ferentiates between global and location processing of visual information as well as vividness theory. Here, zoom is associated with local information processing, while products presented via alternative photos are assumed to be processed on the global level. This implies that local processing via zoom might lead to a reduced perceived dis tinctiveness and thus lower attractiveness of products, as compared to alternative photos that convey the holistic presentation of products, leading to more purchases at the margin. In addition, while static and alternative photos are both assumed to be processed on the global level, alternative photos entail a higher degree of vividness compared to static photos, increasing the utility of the presentation format and strength of preferences towards such promoted products.

Regarding the distinction between device types, tablets and PCs can be considered as potential substitutes, which is reflected in comparable purchase likelihoods and display sizes that are suficiently similar, leading to a higher cognitive fit and a similar usage experience. Smartphones instead lead to diferent purchase behaviors, suggesting that they are not the most preferred purchase channel for products which are largely dependent on non-digital attributes such as the feel, texture or fit, conveyed via visual product presentation technologies. Further research could revisit these questions by focusing on diferent product categories such as utilitarian products with a lower visual appeal or rather functional products with a higher importance of product details. It might also be interesting to expand this experimental setting to augmented reality, virtual reality or eye-tracking applications.
