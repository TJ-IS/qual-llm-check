# An exploratory study on meta skills in software development teams: antecedent cooperation skills and personality for shared mental models

## Abstract

Shared mental models (SMMs) provide an approach to improving team learning and performance. SMM means that team members share common expectations about the team processes, results, !and individual roles in achieving the team’s objective. Our model of antecedents and consequences of SMMs demonstrates how cognitive capabilities contribute to effective software development teams. Higher scores on two meta-level cognitive skills (A-shaped skills and T-shaped skills) and a personality characteristic (agreeableness), which help teammates coordinate their skills and knowledge, enhance the development of an SMM and thereby enhance team performance. The results open new and important areas for research into both the meta-level cognitive skills and the agreeable characteristic required for team effectiveness. There is also promise for new approaches to team building. European Journal of Information Systems (2008) 17, 47–61. doi:10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000730

Keywords: shared mental model; T-shaped skill; A-shaped skill; agreeableness; team effectiveness; software teams

## Introduction

Team-based activities have become ubiquitous in organizations, and researchers have directed increased attention to team effectiveness issues. Early studies of team effectiveness addressed the issues of blue-collar work, and recently investigations have addressed the issues facing knowledge workers (Janz et al., 1997). Research results may be sensitive to the context of the team (McGrath, 1986), so research on product development team effectiveness should enable better understanding of the effectiveness of knowledge workers in diverse settings. Our research context is software development teams in Korea, a region that has experienced rapid progress in the expansion of its information technology (IT) industry.

The development of a complex product such as software requires a team approach, and this is the approach generally taken for significant software development projects. By constructing a team with diverse experience and complementary domain skills and experiences, the resulting expanded knowledge base should improve requisite variety and thereby improve the capability of the team to model and control more complex systems (Ashby, 1964). However, the difficulties associated with assuring adequate communication and coordination among members of a software development team have been evident in prior research (Curtis et al., 1988; Walz et al., 1993).

We begin our study with the premise that it is not just the amount of experience or knowledge that improves team’s systemic flexibility (i.e., requisite variety to handle complex systems as explained above), but also the sharing of mental models that each individual team member maintains in conducting his/her tasks. Our presumption is based on the proposition that a shared mental model (SMM) has a positive impact on work team effectiveness (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994; Kraiger & Wenzel, 1997). An individual mental model is an information processing mechanism that helps describe, explain, and forecast our environments (Rouse & Morris, 1986; Mathieu et al., 2000). When individuals’ mental models are shared with other team members, the team has what is termed a ‘shared mental model (SMM).’ With an SMM, each team member comes to have common expectations and interpretations regarding the team processes, goals, and the roles of other team members.

An SMM does not necessarily require that team members possess identical knowledge. Indeed, both knowledge homogeneity and heterogeneity positively but differently contribute to new product advantage according to the level of radicalness of new product designs (Bonner & Walker, 2004). Instead, the SMM, by being embedded in team members’ minds, functions as meta-level system that coordinates and supervises the heterogeneous or homogeneous domain knowledge of individual team members in achieving an organizational objective.

The importance of sharing mental models for the sake of team performance is implied in the studies that have recognized cross-functional integration and sharing of a common goal as key factors for improving new product success (e.g., Griffin & Hauser, 1996; Kahn & McDonough, 1997). Some studies have emphasized the external factors that facilitate or motivate goal congruity of crossfunctional teams (e.g., Xie et al., 2003). Our study is complementary to these studies, focusing on the internal (within-team) facilitators or antecedent factors for the creation of an SMM.

The concept, SMM, recently has been applied to distributed software development teams (Espinosa et al., 2002). Mental models were important for website interface designing for consumers (Hart et al., 2003; Wells et al., 2005). However, few empirical studies have investigated the influential cognitive and personal characteristics that may be antecedent factors for an SMM (Mohammed et al., 2000). Through a longitudinal study, Levesque et al. (2001) find an ironic result that time lapse hinders the development of an SMM, and only differentiates roles within teams due to the reduced interactions. For the development of an SMM, they call attention to the negative aspect of role specialization within teams as the communication block, and suggested three solutions to help overcome such negative influence: SMM at the sub-product levels (treating each other’s work as black-box), a project manager who can coordinate and fit each work effort, and formal specifications of sub-product deliverables (not necessarily associated with SMM). Their study does not deny the importance of SMM on software development team’s performance, but just cautions about the failure of SMM development as time passes by. Our study joins this research and investigates what kinds of efforts or skills are necessary to promote the development of SMM when team members tend to work in differentiated roles.

Among various personal characteristics, we care about the personal capabilities (i.e., skills and personalities) that help individuals cooperate with others. Some skills related to cooperating with others include developing a good meta-theory to embed various conflicting opinions. Cooperating personalities include behaviors such as encouraging and respecting others’ opinions, enabling a ‘good chemistry’ to develop among the teammates. Activity theory (Leontjev, 1978; Vygotskij, 1978) is a social psychological theory about the developmental transformation and dynamics in collective human work activity. It proposes to distinguish between coordination and cooperation activities (Omicini & Ossowski, 2004). The coordination sets the normal and routine flow of interaction. Participants follow their scripted roles either implicitly or explicitly assigned to them. They share and act upon a common object, but are not necessarily aware of this fact. Besides, the scripts coordinating participants’ actions are not questioned or discussed, and need not be known and understood in all their complexity. This coordination artifact can be embodied (physical) or disembodied (psychological or cognitive), and this artifact both enables and constrains the interaction of people in the same social context.

Meanwhile, cooperation means the process by which agents define how the social goals are to be achieved, by designing and defining suitable coordination artifacts. So, cooperation requires some intelligence, while coordination does not (Omicini & Ossowski, 2004). Agent intelligence is required to reason about which kind of coordination is needed, which kind of coordination laws should be developed to manage interactions, and how such laws can be enacted through a suitably designed coordination artifact. Agents’ intelligence is useful to cooperatively build effective coordination artifacts to be used in the coordination stage by means of negotiation and high level (semantics-driven) interaction protocols.

Therefore, it is worthwhile to examine whether SMM (as a coordination artifact) has positive influence on software development team performance in a Korean setting and to identify the significant cooperation skills and personalities (as intelligent agents for cooperation) preceding to SMM. In examining these questions, our study represents an exploratory study because we import new constructs as antecedents to SMM in the Korean research context. The Korean context is appropriate for a study of software teams, as Korea was ranked second in IT infrastructure and was a leading nation in broadband subscribers, Internet users, and investment in telecommunications according to the World Competitiveness

Yearbook of IMD (Garelli, 2005). A study of Korean software development teams provides not only the cultural distinctiveness in the role and antecedents regarding SMM but may also suggest insights for other countries that possess high commitment to the IT industry and software development teams.

SMM theory: concepts, objects, and measurement The SMM is defined as the knowledge structure shared by team members in conducting team tasks. By means of an SMM, team members come to have a more precise understanding and expectation of tasks to be performed and are more likely to meet other team members’ expectations (Langan-Fox et al., 2004). Studies on SMMs have evolved from studies of personal mental models, and have focused on the similarity of personal mental models to other team members’ mental models. While others have referred to an SMM as a team mental model (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994), SMM is distinct from team mental model in that the latter refers to what is shared among the members of a team as a collectivity, not shared cognition among dyads of individuals, which the former phrase allows for (Langan-Fox et al., 2004, p. 335). In other words, SMM is a configural construct derived from the consistency of individuals’ models, yet there may be no team model per se (Mathieu et al., 2005, p. 38). The cognitive mechanisms that help individuals understand their environment include categories (Rosch, 1978), cognitive maps (Axelrod, 1976), belief structures (Fiske & Taylor, 1991), schema (Neisser, 1976), and scripts (Abelson, 1976). These concepts help individuals interpret circumstances and, by enabling individual to choose relevant and critical information, help prevent information overload and extreme uncertainty.

When team members share similar and accurate mental models, members perform more effectively (Langan-Fox et al., 2004, p. 335). The emphasis in our study is on the similarity rather than the accuracy (or quality) of team members’ SMM; we posit that the quality of the mental model significantly moderates the influence of SMM (Mathieu et al., 2005). In our research context (i.e., software development), however, we posit that inaccurate and poor mental models eventually disappear and will not be shared among team members. Software development requires nimble team coordination and captures only effective outputs from numerous team efforts. Considering the dynamic nature of coordination artifact as Activity theory argues, we believe that SMM as it exists and is used in teams must be of good (sufficient) quality. Therefore, we believe that SMM in our study embeds the accuracy of mental models.

Mental models may be considered to comprise four categories: technology and equipment, task procedures, team interactions, and teammates (Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001). Technology and equipment are the artifacts that help team members share functions, usage, limitations, and possible failures. Task procedures mean the general instructions, strategy, constraints, and relations.

Team interactions facilitate sharing roles and responsibilities, information source and flow, interaction pattern, communication channel, and mutual dependency of team members. Teammates understand teammates’ knowledge, skills, attitude, preferences, and tendencies.

These four conceptual categories of team mental model can be compactly converged into two categories: taskrelated and team-related (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994; Mathieu et al., 2000, 2005). The first and second types of Cannon-Bowers & Salas (2001) relate more to the taskrelated model, whereas the third and fourth types relate to the team-related model. Task-related mental models help team-members share task-specific and task-related knowledge. The team-related mental model is related to transactive memory (Moreland, 2000), the phenomenon by which individuals learn the distribution of team expertise among team members.

Even under our definition of the SMM there remains the dilemma of whether the SMM is measured by similarity in knowledge ‘structure’ or knowledge ‘content’. Cannon-Bowers & Salas (2001) help identify three approaches to measuring knowledge structure. The first concentrates on the ‘pathfinder’ that calculates the psychological distance between constructs. The second, the ‘concept map’, depicts the structure of individual beliefs in a particular domain. The third checks the similarities of cards chosen as the ‘reflective measure’ of psychological similarities or diversity. These all focus on measuring knowledge structure rather than knowledge content contained within it. From standpoint of structure-similarity, SMM is measured by the convergence among individuals’ models rather than by aggregating those (Mathieu et al., 2005). For example, an index of sharedness of teammates’ mental models (i.e., QAP: Quadratic assignment proportion) can be derived using a social network analysis software such as UCINET (Mathieu et al., 2005). QAP correlations capture the identical elements of two mental model matrices, ranging between -1 and þ 1.

The degree of shared content within a knowledge structure is an alternative measure of SMM (Rouse & Morris, 1986). The level of the SMM can be measured by asking members the degree to which they share certain content about tasks and team members (Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001). Klimoski & Mohammed (1994) suggest two approaches to the issue of how many similarities are needed to indicate ‘sharedness’.

The first measures similarities using actuarial assessment. This approach assumes that team members with a high SMM think in a very similar manner in specific circumstances. However, this perspective still does not resolve the issue of what is the ‘proper’ level of overlap between the mental models of team members in order for it to be evaluated as being shared (ibid).

The second approach is phenomenological because it puts an emphasis on beliefs that members share in the interpretation of occurrences inside the team. The level of the SMM is reflected by the beliefs in the SMM and concordant behaviors of team members. Kraiger & Wenzel (1997) also proposed measuring the shared expectations about behavioral tendencies using the surrogate measure of SMM. Klimoski & Mohammed (1994, p. 422) explain the merit of this approach: ‘There must be some level of awareness by and among group members regarding how they interpret tasks, situations and events. A statistical similarity is not enough. To be more specific, a model would be shared only if one or more team members believed this were so, and acted on this belief.’ Kraiger & Wenzel (1997) also propose measuring the shared expectations about behavioral tendencies as the surrogate measure of an SMM.

One may make a case for one approach over the others, depending on the questions being addressed. Our study takes the latter approach because shared expectations help team members provide necessary information in a timely manner and cooperate to achieve organizational objectives. Cannon-Bowers & Salas (2001) also insist that team members actually share expectations, not the real mental model itself. Therefore, the level of an SMM can be measured by team member perceptions about the degree of ‘sharedness’ of the characteristics of tasks and team members.

## Research model and hypotheses

## SMMs and team effectiveness

SMM was first introduced because it was expected to help explain the differences in team effectiveness (Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001; Kraiger & Wenzel, 1997). Conceptually, the shared interpretation of tasks and team-members should lead to the appropriate expectations of operations and results and, in turn, develop improved organizational effectiveness.

In addition to such theoretical propositions, empirical evidence suggests that SMMs can have significant influences on team or organizational performance. Mathieu et al. (2000) reported that both teammaterelated and task-related SMMs make positive influences on team performance. Espinosa et al. (2002), found that the team component of the SMM had a positive effect on team coordination but that the task element did not. Mathieu et al. (2000) found that SMM facilitates team communication even in stressful situations and enhances team performance and cooperation. Using the term ‘team member schema agreement’ instead of SMM, Rentsch & Klimoski (2001) showed a positive relationship with team effectiveness.

In summary, an SMM promotes the common expectations for team tasks and team-members and facilitates the information processing and coordination in team. The shared cognition helps identify team-members’ needs, forecast task operations, strengthen teamwork, and thus improve aspects of team effectiveness such as team performance and team commitment. Based on these prior works, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 Team-members’ SMM is positively associated with software development team effectiveness.

The antecedent cooperation capabilities for an SMM While a relatively large number of studies have investigated the influence of SMM on team effectiveness, the influential factors for SMM have not been as popular. However, there can be factors inside and outside the team that influence an SMM at both the individual and team levels. Kraiger & Wenzel (1997) suggested that influence factors occur across four different levels: environment, organization, team, and individuals. Because organizations are exposed to open environments, the social, political, economic, and especially cultural factors must influence individuals’ behaviors, cognition, attitude, and thus SMM. Organizational factors include the compensation system, education, and training. Team-level factors relate to the characteristics of tasks and processes. Individual factors include personality and motivations and have been regarded as the proximate causes to SMM.

Even though all these four levels of factors are important for SMM, our study concentrates on the personal factors because we believe that individual differences in teams are the most proximal variables to SMM (Kraiger & Wenzel, 1997). Furthermore, because team composition is a critical issue in team design, the relationship between individuals’ personal characteristics and SMM can be significant.

Personal characteristics can be further categorized into cognitive and non-cognitive (or attitudinal) aspects (Bagozzi & Burnkrant, 1979). For our purposes, we attend to the characteristics that are tightly related to coordinating with other teammates rather than stand-alone personal characteristics such as absorptive capacity to understand new knowledge and computer self-efficacy. In our study, we use the concepts of the T-shaped skill and the A-shaped skill to represent the cognitive aspect of coordination capabilities. For the attitudinal aspect, we refer to Costa & MaCrae’s (1992) personality measures. These cognitive and personality measures are rarely included as influential factors in software development teams, and our study of them as antecedent factors for an SMM is the foundation of our exploratory study.

T-shaped skill and A-shaped skill Numerous studies have confirmed that personal cognitive capabilities positively influence task performance (Tziner & Eden, 1985). Toquam et al. (1997) provided the empirical evidence for this argument by measuring team-members’ cognitive capabilities by a mechanical aptitude test. Barrick et al. (1998) also reported that team-members’ general intelligence is positively related to team performance and survival.

However, the cognitive ability tests measuring general intelligence have been criticized as less appropriate for task execution context than for a school context.

Therefore, new approaches need to be developed to measure the cognitive capabilities of team members. In this study, we concentrate on the cognitive capabilities associated with coordinating with others (not with personal understanding of others or tasks), because members of a software development team bring in and integrate knowledge and skills from various domains such as programming languages, tools and technologies, and functional area expertise. T-shaped skill and A-shaped skill are adopted in our study.

The T-shaped skill measure denotes the breadth of cognitive capabilities that embed one’s expertise as well as others’ knowledge (Iansiti, 1993). The vertical line in the ‘T’ shape (&) signifies the expertise in his/her own major area, and the horizontal line (—) represents the understanding of others’ major areas. Therefore, it can be defined as the capability to combine or integrate others’ skill or knowledge onto one’s own knowledge base. It has been asserted that multi-skilled persons are more likely to be able to digest diverse knowledge and skills (Schilling, 2005). Such a capability can be expected to stimulate creative conflicts and promote active communications among team-members (Leonard-Barton, 1995); it is not just a socializing skill to get along with others (Madhavan & Grover, 1998).

Madhavan & Grover (1998) proposed that T-shaped skills positively influence team performance in knowledge creation, but their study was limited to the concept and provided no empirical evidence. Leonard-Barton (1995) found that organizations with engineers of high T-shaped skills completed comparable projects 2.6 years earlier than other organizations.

We know of no studies that test the relationship between T-shaped skill and SMM. Brown & Duguid (1998) contend that task collaboration could promote synergy among diverse knowledge areas and improve mutual understanding. Experiences of collaboration are shared and remembered as group memory by teammembers rather than just an isolated memory of individuals. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 2 Team-members’ T-shaped skill is positively associated with the level of an SMM.

Leonard-Barton (1995) suggests that another cognitive skill, the A-shaped skill, could be important. She suggests that individuals tend to forge a technical alliance and graft multiple disciplines into understanding for executing tasks. Integrating two disciplines takes the shape of two legs in the character ‘A’: that is, the A-shaped skill denotes the individual capability to integrate different disciplines and graft them into one. Individuals with such a skill are more likely to understand phenomena from a higher level of abstraction and perhaps to form metatheories. Madhavan & Grover (1998) proposed that team-members’ A-shaped skills are positively related with team performance in knowledge creation. Because developing A-shaped skill sets is an expensive task for both the person and the organization, it might not be feasible to ensure that all knowledge team members have them (Madhavan & Grover, 1998, p. 4). They contended that this is a critical caliber for team leaders to integrate diverse knowledge and develop new knowledge, and that teams are better with leaders with such caliber than the teams with leaders without such caliber. Therefore, we believe that teams can vary in terms of A-shaped skill and that the A-shaped skill can be treated as team characteristic. According to the positive correlations among the development of mental models, the individual learning styles, and cognitive capability (Jih & Reeves, 1992; Suedfeld & Coren, 1992), we believe that such cognitive characteristics facilitate the establishment and increase the level of a team SMM. Based on these findings, we make the following hypothesis regarding the relationship between A-shaped skill and SMM.

## Hypothesis 3 Team-members’ A-shaped skill is positively associated with the level of an SMM.

Agreeableness Among non-cognitive aspects of team characteristics, personality characteristics have long been considered as important to performance. However, such a relationship has not been discussed in studies of product development or innovation. Costa & MaCrae’s (1992) ‘Big Five Personality’ has been popularly used for the definition and characteristics of personality, which include extraversion, emotional stability (neuroticism), agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience. Extraversion and neuroticism are related to temperament, so that extraversion denotes being cheerful, whereas neuroticism is associated with negative affects. Conscientiousness promotes achievement of individual tasks, but is not necessarily related to relationship with others. Open individuals are characterized ‘by both a broader and deeper scope of awareness and by a need to enlarge and examine experience [such that Openness to Experience is] positively correlated with both positive and negative affect’ (McCrae & Costa, 1991, p. 228). In this way, openness to experience predisposes an individual to feel both the good and the bad more deeply. Furnham et al. (2005) conducted three crosscultural studies to identify the relationship between personality traits and work values, and found that only agreeableness, extraversion, and openness seem to be robust predictors of Work Relationships, Influence and Advancement, and Financial and Working Conditions, respectively (Furnham et al., 2005, p. 28). Consequently, among these ‘Big Five’ factors, our study focuses on agreeableness, which we judge would be most related to cooperation with teammates.

Agreeableness is maintaining the trustworthy and cooperative relationship with others and used to be called as friendliness or social conformity. Persons with high agreeableness scores are courteous, cooperative, generous, conciliatory, considerate, patient, and warm-hearted, whereas the low-profiled persons are hostile, indifferent to others, egocentric, and jealous (Costa & MaCrae, 1992). Team members with high agreeableness scores were reported to present their own ideas proactively, accept the team-decisions favorably, and cooperate to achieve team objectives (Janis, 1982). Agreeableness promotes group cohesion and eventually contributes to team performance (Greence, 1989). Stodgil (1972) also reports that teammates with high agreeable scores tend to have high sympathy and volunteer to help others, so that they facilitate the team cohesiveness by promoting friendliness among teammates. Other studies on agreeableness indicate that teams with high agreeable persons are more likely to support the formation and level of an SMM because such traits help resolve the conflicts among team members (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994; Neuman & Wright, 1999; Schilling, 2005). Aronoff & Wilson (1985) also report that agreeable team members tend to cooperate better to achieve team goals. All these studies suggest that team members with high agreeableness scores are not exclusive about others’ opinions and cooperative to develop SMM about team procedures and role assignments. Therefore, we suggest the following hypothesis regarding the relationship between agreeableness and SMM:

Hypothesis 4 Team-members’ agreeableness is positively associated with the level of an SMM.

## Research methodology

## Samples and data collection

As our interest is in the effectiveness of knowledge worker teams, we chose the samples from IT industry, specifically software development and IT consulting. IT staff fit the general definition of knowledge workers since they apply their theoretical and analytical skills to develop new products and services. Software and system development projects are conducted in teams, and the team-members are required to cooperate in team activities and contribute to interpretations based on their expertise (Espinosa et al., 2002).

To verify that SW development teams did fit our research context, we interviewed seven SW developers. Through these interviews we examined their experiences of projects and team members, which confirmed they satisfied our requirements for data collection through a survey. SW and system development projects are usually conducted in teams and members are required to cooperate in activities and contribute to teamwork based on their expertise.

Data were collected by surveys. An initial list of 130 Korean SW development companies was constructed from reports in the popular media (e.g., PC magazine) and from the Internet. Most of the IT companies were new ventures and small in size, with two-thirds having fewer than 100 employees. The firms were mainly located in and near Seoul. We contacted all these firms, getting positive responses from 42. Each firm was asked for access to SW development teams to survey. Some 320 surveys were distributed to the randomly selected SW developers, with 304 replies (95% return rate). Respondents replied regarding their own teams. Some 277 surveys from 83 teams in 42 companies were eventually used in our analysis (after excluding those with missing answers or other data problems). The average number of members in each team was eight. The size range varied: 8.3% had fewer than five members; 38.6% had 5–10; 34.3% had 11–15; 11.9% had 16–20; and 6.9% had 21–30 members. The teams were predominantly male (70.8%), 25–30 years old (43.7%), with bachelor degrees (66.8%).

## Variables

This section describes the operationalization of constructs in the research model. We present the development of measurement items and also discuss whether the items are formative or reflective indicators for the construct. Two criteria in judging whether indicators are reflective or formative are applied. First, the reflective indicators are affected by the underlying construct. Second, reflective indicators are highly correlated with each other so that a change in an indicator implies a similar directional change for the other indicators (Gefen et al., 2000). All the measurement items are introduced in the appendix.

Another issue for the variables in our model is whether A-shaped skill, T-shaped skill, and agreeableness are the personal or team-level constructs. We believe that those constructs can be treated as both personal and team-level characteristics according to the research objective. We understand that all these constructs were originally developed at the personal level. Therefore, teams can vary in terms of composition of such personal traits. Our research objective is to identify the team-level characteristics (that tend to be aggregation of personal traits) in regards to SMM.

We adopted as many measurement items as possible from previous studies. We also conducted open interviews to verify whether our survey items fit the Korean software industry context and if improvements in these items were needed. Through three pilot tests and open interviews, the initial items were reduced and measured on a seven-point Likert scale.

Shared mental model (SMM) As noted above, we measured the perception of team-members to determine the level of SMM of a team. Our measures of SMM were adapted from Cannon-Bowers & Salas (2001), Kraiger & Wenzel (1997), and Mathieu et al. (2000). In adapting these previous measures, we made sure that our measures relate to both task- and member-related SMM. We originally developed 25 items and reduced to eight items through three round preliminary studies (interviews and pilot studies). All items were worded with the team, not the individual, as the referent because the critical measure is the perceived degree of sharedness (Rousseau, 1985). In our model, SMM is a second order factor with two task- and member-related SMM as formative first order factors.

T-shaped skill We used the construct of Madhavan & Grover (1998) and resulted in 12 items. We further reduced the items according to our research context and ended up with five items on a seven-point Likert scale. These items are not the direct measures of T-shaped skill but instead represent the relevant behavioral tendencies. This operationalization is appropriate because these five items are modeled as the formative indicators of T-shaped skill in our statistical analysis.

A-shaped skill Survey items from prior studies were not available, requiring us to develop items for the operationalization of the A-shaped skill. We had interviews with practitioners regarding the theoretical concepts developed by Leonard-Barton (1995) and Madhavan & Grover (1998). These interviews resulted in 15 initial items. After the pilot test, these were reduced to five questions on seven-point Likert scale. Items stress the efforts to integrate diverse opinions and knowledge. Like the T-shaped skill, these five items may not directly represent the skill, but are used in our statistical analysis as the formative indicators of the A-shaped skill.

Agreeableness Using Costa & McCrae’s (1992) ‘Big Five Personality’ model, we adopted four items from the popular NEO-PI-R test. These items were chosen as the reflective indicators of agreeableness.

Team effectiveness As Hackman (1990) argues, team effectiveness is multidimensional, thereby producing diverse operationalizations. We focused on product and project level measures rather than on overall measures of financial performance (Griffin & Page, 1996). The majority of measures of team effectiveness include performance outcomes (i.e., quality, speed, accuracy, etc.) and socioemotional outcomes (i.e., satisfaction, commitment, cohesiveness, etc.) (Shea & Guzzo, 1987). Customer satisfaction could be another dimension of team effectiveness. However, we decided to include only team performance and team commitment because customer satisfaction is a belief or attitude held by a third party, and the team-members’ perception of this may not be reliable. In our analysis, team effectiveness is regarded as the second order factor with team performance and team commitment as formative first order factors.

We adopted five items to assess team performance from Henderson & Lee (1992), who measured the quality of the team’s output and the efficiency in budget and scheduling. Bishop & Scott (2000) developed the items of team commitment with the definition of ‘the strength of individual’s participation and identification to referent groups.’ We adopted four items from their six items.

Control variables We included four control variables: team size, team history, tenure similarity, and gender. Size was measured by the number of team members and history by period since launched. The coefficient of variation (CV) was the measure we adopted for tenure similarity. The CV was calculated by dividing the standard deviation of the sample by the average value. Therefore, we put the minus sign before this value to convert its meaning to the degree of similarity. A dummy variable was used for gender information and was measured by binary code (0 for male, 1 for female). Gender information was measured by the percentage of females in teams. This scale was more useful than the CV because gender is a nominal scale not an interval or ratio scale. Therefore, the coefficient of team gender to the dependent variable means the different effect of females compared with males in the team-level dependent variable.

## Data analysis

Among various applications using SEM (Structural Equation Modeling), our study used PLS (Partial Least Squares) analysis because it is appropriate for exploratory studies and studies during the early stages of theory building (Howell & Hall-Merenda, 1999). PLS does not make assumptions about data distributions to estimate model parameters, observation independence, or variable metrics. Our sample number (83) is not large enough for covariance-based analysis (such as Amos and LISREL). Our study explores the possible importance of new cognitive capabilities in IS team effectiveness, and we need to identify whether our research model generates enough explanatory power on the variance of dependent variable, and the ${ \check { R } } ^ { 2 ^ { \bullet } }$ of PLS enables us to estimate this power.

## Reliability and validity of measurement items

T-shaped skill, A-shaped skill, and agreeableness are all the personal-level characteristics, not necessarily related to the team-level nature. Because our analysis is conducted at the team level, we aggregated the individual scores and used this measure as the team scores (James, 1982).

Of the various procedures to justify aggregation, we took the general approach and used one-way ANOVA and the value of $\eta ( \mathrm { { e t a } } ) ^ { 2 } .$ . Aggregation is justified if the following conditions hold: the between-group variance is greater than the within-group variance (Katz & Allen, 1985) and the $\eta ^ { 2 }$ value exceeds 0.20 (Simon & Peterson, 2000). In our data set, the between-group variance was significant for SMM, T-shaped skill, A-shaped skill, and agreeableness (P ¼ 0.05), and all the $\eta ^ { 2 }$ values exceeded 0.20. Therefore, we conclude that individual scores can be aggregated and used as the team scores. These are shown in Table 1.

Because all the items were measured on a single instrument, the possibility of mono-method bias needs to be addressed. To examine this possibility, we ran an exploratory factor analysis of items (aggregated at the team level) and checked if any single construct accounted for significant variance across all items. Table 2 shows that all the seven constructs (with SMM broken down to task- and team-related) show eigen values exceeding one, meaning that no single construct is dominant in the factor structure.

Convergent validity refers to the integrity between the latent variables and their indicators. Exploratory factor analysis in Table 2 clearly shows that seven factors were extracted with significant factor loadings for each relevant survey item. However, in our research model, some constructs (A-shaped skill and T-shaped skill) are formative indicators, whereas others (agreeableness, taskrelated SMM, member-related SMM, team performance, and team commitment) are reflective indicators. For the formative indicators, measurement modeling is assessed by regression analysis using the indicators and latent variables as independent and dependent variables, respectively. In this analysis, the dependent variable is the aggregate measure of indicators, and convergent validity is secured if the coefficients are all significant. Measurement modeling for the reflective indicators is assessed by a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). In this case, convergent validity is secured if the coefficients between indicators and latent variables exceed 0.7 (Gefen et al., 2000). The ‘common variance’ column of Table 2 presents the coefficients of these two different approaches (formative and reflective indicator models).

Discriminant validity evaluates whether the indicators load more on their intended factors than on other factors. Discriminant validity is verified if the average of variances extracted (AVEs) from each factor is greater than the construct correlations with other constructs. The AVEs (diagonals) in Table 3 exceed 0.5, which satisfies the minimal requirement that at least half of the variance in indicators is accounted for by their respective construct (Gefen et al., 2000). All the AVEs are greater than the off-diagonals in Table $^ { 3 , }$ verifying the discriminant validity.

## Hypothesis test

The criterion in model choice is on parsimony as long as the appropriate explanatory power is sustained (Pedhazur & Schmelkin, 1991). The basic principle in designing a parsimonious model is to eliminate the statistically nonsignificant relationships between variables.

Multiple competing models could exist in our study. First, we need to verify whether SMM is better included in the model at all. If yes, we further need to test the mediating role of SMM between independent variables (A-shaped skill, T-shaped skill, and agreeableness) and team effectiveness. Investigating this second issue helps determine whether SMM is the full or partial mediator between the independent and dependent variables. In PLS, the superiority between competing models can be identified by the differences in $R ^ { \overset { \cdot } { 2 } }$ value (Gefen et al., 2000).

Figure 1 compares those three competing models. The direct model presumes only the direct effects from independent variables to team effectiveness without SMM as mediator. The partial- and full-mediator models differ on admitting the existence of other mediators except for SMM. This difference is depicted by the direct paths from three independent variables to team effectiveness in addition to indirect paths through SMM. Figure 1 addresses that the partial-mediator model is superior to other models in terms of the pure size of $R ^ { 2 } .$ However, this value is not substantially higher than that of the full-mediator model. $R ^ { 2 } s$ are 27.9% for partialmediator model, and 25.7% for full-mediator model. The $f ^ { 2 }$ is 0.03 (0.279-0.257/[1-0.257]), which is very small according to the criteria introduced by Chin et al. (2003).

In the full-mediator model, we included four control variables and retested the influence of target variables on SMM and team effectiveness (see Figure 2). Control variables were assumed to influence both SMM and team effectiveness. Significance of path coefficients was tested by bootstrap (100 resamples). All the target paths were significant. SMM’s influence on team effectiveness was more noticeable for team commitment than on team performance (0.500 vs 0.218 for path coefficients). Therefore, all the hypotheses are supported. In our data set, we can conclude that SMM is important for the team effectiveness, and that both the cognitive capabilities of team members and personality are important for a team’s SMM.

## Discussion

The increase in knowledge-based economic activity, particularly innovation and new product development activity, requires that we have a greater understanding of the factors that enable knowledge creation and sharing in teams. The concept of an SMM, based on the individual mental model in cognitive psychology, has surfaced as a promising lens with which to improve our understanding of knowledge work. The findings in this exploratory study support the following hypotheses: SMM is positively related to team effectiveness, and the measures of T-shaped and A-shaped abilities available in teams are positively related to SMM. Moreover, the importance of SMM is noteworthy for team effectiveness because it fully mediates the influence of team-members’ cognitive capabilities (T-shaped- and A-shaped-skill) and personality (agreeableness) on team effectiveness.

This study has contributed to the understanding of SMM as a lens and to its utility in three significant ways. First, the study has shown that SMM is associated with increased effectiveness in Korean software development teams. This finding is consistent with other recent studies that show that software development is coordinated through mechanisms such as SMM. The robust relationship of SMM to team effectiveness in an additional cultural and national setting suggests that SMM is worthy of additional studies to understand further the mechanisms and the limitations of how SMM enables improved team effectiveness.

Second, the empirical study demonstrates the antecedent relationship of three personal cooperation capabilities with SMM. Both the cognitive capabilities (T-shaped skill and A-shaped skill) and personality characteristic (agreeableness) contribute positively to a SMM. As a mediator of meta-level cognitive skills, the SMM provides a means by which these cognitive skills and personality enhance particular aspects of team effectiveness. An SMM has a greater influence on team commitment than on other aspects of team effectiveness (team performance) when all these variables are considered together as the dependent variables of SMM. This finding implies that the SMM’s influence occurs first on team commitment but gradually and sequentially influences other aspects of team effectiveness. This finding suggests that software development team performance is subject to diverse issues beyond team psychology. Software development team leaders may well be concerned with the possible mediators and moderates between team commitment and team performance.

Notable is that all the personal cognitive and personality factors do not influence team effectiveness directly. The influence of personal characteristics on team effectiveness is totally controlled by SMM. Therefore, in order to enhance team effectiveness, selecting team members on the basis of personal characteristics is helpful, but such efforts should be complemented by organizational efforts that foster the development of SMM. An interesting result is that gender similarity made a significant influence on SMM but not on team effectiveness. We judge that this gender issue is a facilitator for better team chemistry but too sensitive an issue to directly lead to team effectiveness. We can verify that gender similarity only helps understand team members and procedures.

The study has some obvious limitations. The first is that the empirical data are self-reported. In other words, the study investigated the perception of SMM and did not attempt to calculate or determine an objective measurement of SMM. Consequently, knowledge and information sharing could have been exaggerated or underestimated by the responses.

Another limitation relates to T-shaped and A-shaped skills. The T-shaped skill has a history of both theoretical and empirical studies. However, the A-shaped skill has been conceptually explained by Iansiti (1993) to distinguish it from the T-shaped skill, but it does not have a history of empirical tests. Our operationalization of these constructs might be refined through additional studies with a broader sample.

Third, we could have included more control variables to purify the effects of our target constructs on SMM (i.e., A-shaped skill, T-shaped skill, and agreeableness,). Additional constructs can be training (Mathieu et al., 2005), use of IT (Swaab et al., 2002), leadership style (Madhavan & Grover, 1998), interaction among team members (Langan-Fox et al., 2004), and quality of mental model (Mathieu et al., 2005). Cohen & Bailey (1997) point out that the studies about project team tend to miss examining in-depth team descriptions such as type of organization, team organization structure, and leadership structure. This focus on the target constructs (e.g., expertise coordination) and limited attention to control variables is a constraint shared with other studies on software team effectiveness (e.g., Faraj & Sproull, 2000; Levesque et al., 2001). Such a focus, it may be argued, is an appropriate limitation for exploratory studies. Future studies may well include more variables to verify our proposed conclusions.

External validity and generalizability may be questioned. Our respondents were knowledge workers in the Korean IT industry, and such workers may not be the ideal samples to test our hypotheses. The restriction to a single national culture can be considered a limitation on the generalizability of the findings, but having samples from a single culture enables the study to avoid any confounding that might be introduced with samples from a more heterogeneous environment.

## Conclusion

Our findings have several practical implications. Personnel managers who are concerned with personal characteristics should recognize that such characteristics lead to the improvement in team effectiveness only by way of an SMM. While excellent personal knowledge and skills are important, organizations should foster the development of a SMM as a means of achieving team objectives with high team effectiveness.

The findings also provide suggestions for recruiting and training. Hiring people who have high T-shaped, A-shaped skills, and agreeableness can help facilitate and establish a team’s SMM, leading to improved team performance.

Our emphasis on SMM does not necessarily disregard the importance of diversity of team knowledge and skills. Instead, the results imply that the advantage of knowledge diversity can be realized more explicitly if team members are aware of each other’s differences and uniqueness and share the expectation of each other’s response patterns. In other words, it is not just about ‘the degree of diversity’ but ‘the degree of awareness of diversity’ that matters. This suggests that preparing individuals to recognize and appreciate their team members’ abilities could have significant benefits. This also suggests the potential value of studying the requirements generation phase of a software project in which the technical experts (analysts and designers) work with the functional experts (clients) to develop specifications
