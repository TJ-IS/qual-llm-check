# WHEN DIGITAL TECHNOLOGIES ENABLE AND THREATEN OCCUPATIONAL IDENTITY: THE DELICATE BALANCING ACT OF DATA SCIENTISTS<sup>1</sup>

Emmanuelle Vaast McGill University, Montreal, CANADA {emmanuelle.vaast@mcgill.ca}

Alain Pinsonneault McGill University, Montreal, CANADA {alain.pinsonneault@mcgill.ca}

Occupations are increasingly embedded with and affected by digital technologies. These technologies both enable and threaten occupational identity and create two important tensions: they make the persistence of an occupation possible while also potentially rendering it obsolete, and they magnify both the similarity and distinctiveness of occupations with regard to other occupations. Based on the critical case study of an online community dedicated to data science, we investigate longitudinally how data scientists address the two tensions of occupational identity associated with digital technologies and reach transient syntheses in terms of “optimal distinctiveness” and “persistent extinction.” We propose that identity work associated with digital technologies follows a composite life-cycle and dialectical process. We explain that people constantly need to adjust and redefine their occupational identity, i.e., how they define who they are and what they do. We contribute to scholarship on digital technologies and identity work by illuminating how people deal in an ongoing manner with digital technologies that simultaneously enable and threaten their occupational identity.

Keywords: Occupational identity, data science, digital technologies, persistence/obsolescence, similarity/distinctiveness, process theorizing, composite process, identity work, qualitative research, data scientists

## Introduction

Ever since the Harvard Business Review named data scientist “the sexiest job of the $2 1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ century” (Davenport and Patil 2012) in 2012, this occupation has captivated people, companies, and the media. The appeal of the occupation is based in large part on the skills required and its ability to extract actionable knowledge from large data sets with the use of sophisticated digital technologies such as programming languages, visualization techniques, and artificial intelligence (AI). Digital technologies correspond to digitized techniques, instruments, and systems that enable people to store, process, and circulate data and that rely upon digital innovation (Yoo et al. 2012). However, beyond this fascination with the data scientist occupation, ambiguity continues to reign regarding what data scientists do, who they are, and whether their occupation will endure (Goodman 1019; Thompson 2019). The uncertain identity of data scientists is intricately related to the same digital technologies that have helped create their occupation and that they continuously use.

The prominence of the data scientist occupation has made this combination of attractiveness and precariousness particularly notable. Yet this situation is far from unique. The trade press provides many examples of other occupations impacted by digital technologies. Accountants fear that automation will put an end to their work (Kepes

2017). Consultants struggle with reinventing their craft as new technologies continuously change their field (Rousset 2019). Teachers have to deal with technological innovations that drastically affect what they do and threaten their traditional connections with students (Meilleur 2018).

Digital technologies substantially affect not only what people do and how they work but also their sense of self associated with their work (Boudreau et al. 2014). The constant innovation and widespread adoption of digital technologies have led people to consider anew their occupational identity, i.e., the ways in which they define who they are and what they do at work (Ashcraft 2013; Nelson and Irwin 2014). In the case of data scientists, digital technologies both facilitated the emergence of their occupation and threatened its very existence. Data scientists, as well as those working in other related occupations, constantly face disruptions in their occupational identity, its distinctiveness, and its ability to endure.

Previous scholarship has investigated how new digital technologies affect occupational identity (Stein et al. 2013; Whitley et al. 2014). This scholarship has noted, in particular, that digital technologies can represent threats (e.g., Barrett and Walsham 1999; Lamb and Davidson 2005) and occasions for the renewal of occupational identity (Boudreau et al. 2014; Nelson and Irwin 2014). The existing scholarship has, however, not yet delved into how digital technologies affect occupational identity and how people deal with the tensions that emanate from these technologies.

In particular, two fundamental characteristics of digital technologies, convergence and generativity (Yoo et al., 2012; Zittrain, 2006), trigger tensions for occupational identity that merit our attention. Convergence melds initially different fields and activities. Convergence creates a tension in occupational identity because it renders occupations quite similar, which causes confusion about the required skills and roles performed by different people (similarity/distinctiveness tension). Generativity (i.e., the fact that digital technologies continuously evolve) also creates a tension in occupational identity. This tension emanates from the fact that people lack clarity on who they are and whether their occupation will persist (persistence/obsolescence tension).

Examining how people deal with these tensions is essential to understanding and defining identity at work in the digital age. Occupational identity is particularly important for people to define their sense of self and work (Ashcraft 2013). Further, these tensions are likely to become widespread as digital technologies become increasingly used in the knowledge economy (Brynjolfsson and McAfee 2014; Forman et al. 2014). Digital technologies have become part of how people define who they are and what they do at work (Stein et al. 2013). IS scholarship has examined people working with technologies and how their identity is affected by the technologies they use (Whitley et al. 2014). Yet this scholarship has neither conceptualized the specific tensions that result from working with digital technologies nor has it investigated how people address these tensions.

This study examines the following research question: How do members of an occupation deeply affected by digital technologies manage the identity tensions of similarity/ distinctiveness and persistence/obsolescence?

To address this research question, we rely upon theoretical foundations related to occupational identity and its referents, identity work, and digital technologies. We adopt a process perspective that relies on a qualitative, interpretive study of an online community dedicated to data science. This research design enables us to examine how data scientists dealt with the interrelated tensions of similarity/distinctiveness and persistence/obsolescence triggered by their use of digital technologies in their work.

This study contributes to scholarship on digital technologies and identity work by unpacking how people engage in identity work in an ongoing manner as their occupation is jointly and repeatedly enabled, shaped, and threatened by digital technologies. It advances scholarship by explaining that convergence and generativity are connected to two key tensions of occupational identity. This study also deepens the understanding of how people address the tensions associated with their occupational identity by reaching transient syntheses that provisionally establish the optimal distinctiveness (Gioia et al. 2010; Navis and Glynn 2010) of an occupation with regard to other occupations and the sense of what we call persistent extinction related to digital technologies. That is, because of digital technologies, some people may define their occupation on the basis of an enduring sense that their occupation will eventually become extinct, even as it is currently persisting.

Moreover, this study adds to the scholarship on occupational identity and its referents when digital technologies are deeply used. It does so by connecting theoretically occupational identity with IT identity, i.e., the ways in which people view IT use as “integral to their sense of self” (Carter and Grover 2015, p. 931). This is particularly relevant for occupations deeply affected by digital technologies. For these occupations, this study explains how a stronger IT identity is associated with threats and tensions of occupational identity. It also contributes to scholarship on occupational identity and its social referents by deepening the process perspective on optimal distinctiveness. Existing scholarship has highlighted a two-stage process whereby optimal distinctiveness is reached by establishing similarity with another occupation first, and then the distinctiveness of an entity (see, e.g., Navis and Glynn 2010; Zuckerman 2016). This study develops a dialectical and life-cycle process theorizing that goes beyond this two-stage process. It explains how achieving optimal distinctiveness for occupations drastically affected by digital technologies requires people to engage in a repeated manner with identity work in ways that define and redefine, cumulatively and jointly, how similar and different their occupation is from other occupations.

## Process Theorizing of Occupational Identity Work with Digital Technologies

## Occupational Identity, Its Referents, and Identity Work

Occupational identity is a social identity that is related to but distinct from other work-related identities such as organizational identity (i.e., the sense of self associated with the organization one works at, see Pratt et al. 2006) and the specific roles that one fulfills (Abbott 1993). Occupational identity is key for people to make sense of their work and give meaning to what they do, to their careers, and to their interactions with others (Brown 2015; Ibarra and Barbulescu 2010). Occupational identity is defined in relation to identity referents (Goodrick and Reay 2010), which can include other occupations and other social actors (e.g., clients, managers; Eckhardt et al. 2009). These identity referents help define what is similar and distinct about an occupation and its members (Barrett et al. 2012).

More recently, the growing use of digital technologies has led people to define themselves and their work not only with regard to other social referents but also in terms of technologies (Nelson and Irwin 2014; Utesheva et al. 2016). The concept of IT identity, i.e., “the extent to which an individual views use of an IT as integral to his or her sense of self” (Carter and Grover 2015, p. 931), becomes particularly relevant for occupations deeply affected by digital technologies. With IT identity, one of the identity referents for an occupation comes from the technologies that a person uses rather than another social actor. IT identity can thus help people define and communicate what their occupation is and what its members do.

As noted by Carter and Grover (2015), existing scholarship has not yet examined the relationships between occupational identity and IT identity. For occupations deeply affected by digital technologies, however, it stands to reason that the IT identity of members of such occupations is strong and deeply intertwined with their occupational identities. In such situations, people are likely to define themselves and/or their occupation in close connection to the digital technology(ies) they use (Stein et al. 2013). How a strong IT identity may affect an occupational identity, however, remains to be understood.

Existing scholarship also suggests that how members of an occupation define what they do and who they are can be stable for some period of time but typically also changes over time. Identities are not fixed. They fluctuate and evolve over time as contexts change (Abbott 1993; Gioia et al. 2010; Liedtka et al. 1997). People thus frequently engage in identity work. Building upon Snow and Anderson’s (1987) seminal work, Sveningsson and Alvesson (2003, p. 1165) defined identity work as happening when people are “engaged in forming, repairing, maintaining, strengthening or revising the constructions that are productive of a sense of coherence and distinctiveness.” With identity work, people address and solve issues associated with who they consider themselves to be. Identity work is especially important as people define and redefine their occupational identity (Brown 2015; Van Maanen 2010). People may need to engage in identity work when they react to a threat to their occupational identity, such as when a new technology is introduced (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Lamb and Davidson 2005) or when they go through transitions in their lives (Ibarra 1999). Identity work enables them to create and recreate their identity while also building upon prior identity. Identity work is a process that involves people making sense of and “trying on” various identities until the new identity is stabilized (Boudreau et al. 2014; Chreim et al. 2007; Ibarra 1999).

The existing scholarship on occupational identity has thus far suggested that, with the introduction of a new technology, identity work follows a linear life-cycle process (Poole et al. 2016; Van de Ven and Poole 1995). This lifecycle process includes cumulative stages and leads to the “stabilization-for-now” of a new occupational identity (Ibarra 1999). According to this conceptualization of identity work, a new technology constitutes a trigger that can lead people to question their existing identity and/or bring about the need for a new identity (Boudreau et al. 2014). This literature thus suggests that occupational identity progresses over time and exhibits tendencies toward the stabilizationfor-now (Ibarra 1999; Nelson and Irwin 2014).

The current paper has a dual focus: We examine occupations whose practices are deeply affected by digital technologies, and we focus on the particular digital technologies that their practitioners use to do their work. We chose this focus because it has the potential to get to the core of how specific digital technologies affect how members of an occupation define what they do and of who they are. For a particular occupation, we thus only consider the digital technologies that have the most potential to affect occupational identity and be associated with identity work. We consider these digital technologies to be associated with tensions of occupational identity that people need to address when engaging in identity work.

## Two Tensions of Occupational Identity Associated with Digital Technologies

In order to conceptualize the tensions of identity for occupations deeply affected by digital technologies, we draw on the defining characteristics of these technologies. Scholarship has long highlighted key characteristics of digital technologies, such as their modularity (Brusoni and Prencipe 2001), malleability (Nylén and Holmström 2015), and relationality (von Briel et al. 2018). Most scholarship on digital technologies so far has studied the implications of these characteristics for innovation and value creation (e.g., Boland et al. 2007; Boudreau 2012). In this study, we examine the implications of two key characteristics of digital technologies for occupational identity and identity work: convergence and generativity.

Convergence refers to digital technologies bringing together previously separate user experiences, products, fields of work, and businesses. Convergence can thus make hitherto distinct industries closer (e.g., software development and telecommunications companies may converge in their activities) (Yoo et al. 2012). Convergence implies that, with the growing reliance upon digital technologies, it might be difficult for people to establish or maintain an occupational identity that is clearly distinct from that of other occupations. Convergence is important because it can create a tension of similarity/distinctiveness, which participants in any social entity such as an occupation must address (Brewer 1991; Gioia et al. 2010). Because of convergence, people may struggle to establish or maintain the distinctiveness of their occupation compared to other ones. They need to engage in identity work to establish that their occupation is neither too similar to others nor too distinct and thus poorly understood (Alvarez et al. 2005).

In this study, we argue that the convergence of digital technologies leads to a tension of occupational identity between similarity and distinctiveness. In other words, people’s identity work associated with digital technologies involves working towards building an appropriate balance in the tension between similarity and distinctiveness in occupational identity. For example, Vaast et al. (2013)

investigated tech bloggers grappling with this tension. They were constantly defining their identity as similar to (e.g., to help them be seen as legitimate by others) but also distinct from (e. g., to be recognized as new and different actors) longer-established occupations such as journalists.

Generativity is associated with the reprogrammability of digital technologies, which makes it possible for new capabilities to emerge after the initial design and for new technologies to appear in an ongoing manner (Zittrain 2006). The generativity of digital technologies also affects occupational identity and identity work. Generativity implies that the practices of people affected by digital technologies change on an ongoing basis. The practices of members of an occupation may thus become increasingly enhanced by generativity but also disrupted. Because of ongoing changes in practices associated with the generativity of digital technologies, who belongs in an occupation and what they do is repeatedly questioned and renegotiated. The ongoing and unpredictable change and new capabilities associated with the generativity of digital technologies can thus continuously disrupt occupational identity (Utesheva et al. 2016) as people constantly need to redefine who they are and what they do.

Existing scholarship has documented that members of occupations can experience new digital technologies as threats or challenges (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Boudreau et al. 2014; Lamb and Davidson 2005). We argue that the generativity of digital technologies can trigger repeated challenges to occupational identity because they are key to defining an occupation, yet they can also render it obsolete (Autor et al. 2003). For example, librarians saw their identity deeply disrupted by the arrival of internet search technologies. They redefined who they were and what they did to avoid being replaced by these technologies (Nelson and Irwin 2014). They redefined their occupational identity to encompass being experts in the use of search technologies. Similarly, tech bloggers defined themselves in tight connection with the technologies that make their practices possible, but they also struggled with the ongoing onslaught of available new digital technologies (e.g., RSS, microblogging, video blogging) and feared the fragmentation and dispersion of their identity. They had to redefine who they were in an ongoing manner because digital technologies transformed what they could do and could potentially render them obsolete (Vaast et al. 2013). More generally, we expect that the generativity of digital technologies can disrupt occupational identity by creating a tension between the persistence (continued existence) and obsolescence (extinction) of an occupation. Members of occupations deeply affected by digital technologies thus need to address this persistence/obsolescence identity tension.

## A Composite Life-Cycle and Dialectical Process Theorizing

We suggest that people engage in identity work to address one or both of the similarity/distinctiveness and the persistence/obsolescence tensions created by digital technologies. As noted above, prior conceptualizations have suggested that identity work follows a life-cycle process, i.e., that it goes through different, cumulative stages progressing toward a stabilized-for-now new social identity (Boudreau et al. 2014; Ibarra 1999). This life-cycle theorizing however does not account for the two tensions of occupational identity associated with digital technologies.

We account for these tensions by engaging in composite process theorizing (Van de Ven and Poole 1995). We posit that people deal with two ongoing tensions associated with digital technologies and that identity work involves a composite lifecycle and dialectical process (Pentland 1999; Poole et al. 2016). Dialectics correspond to a contradiction that carries with it a distinct logic of change (Farjoun 2016, p. 95). Originating in philosophy, dialectics has been used in management and IS research (see, in particular, Van de Ven 2007; Van de Ven and Poole 1995) to explain change that happens through tensions between opposing entities (Farjoun 2010; Karjalainen et al., 2019; Putnam et al. 2016). In our context, dialectical theorizing comes from the fact that digital technologies create two ongoing tensions of occupational identity.

We also propose an alternative conceptualization of identity work associated with digital technologies. We suggest that it is a process that involves cumulative stages, as in the ideal type of the life-cycle process. People transition between stages by engaging differently with the two interrelated tensions of identity. Consistent with the dialectical process model, we also suggest that the motor of the change in the process comes from ongoing, interdependent tensions associated with digital technologies: one associated with how similar and/or distinct an occupation is from other occupations, and the other associated with how the occupation’s persistence is enabled and threatened by digital technologies. People engage in identity work to address these two tensions and can sometimes reach particular syntheses. The concept of synthesis is closely associated with dialectical theorizing. It highlights that two elements of a tension “cannot be dissolved onto one another, but only overcome by the creation of a synthesis that is not reducible to either of its constituent elements” (Farjoun, 2016, section 6.2). We treat the synthesis for each tension of occupational identity as representing a solution to the tension. It combines aspects of both elements of the tension (e.g., similarity and distinctiveness) without being reducible to either and is adequate-for-now to define the occupational identity.

Existing scholarship has labeled the synthesis of the similarity/distinctiveness tension “optimal distinctiveness” (Brewer 1991; Navis and Glynn 2010; Zhao et al. 2017). Optimal distinctiveness corresponds to the state whereby a social entity defines itself as both sufficiently similar (to be understood and accepted by others) and distinct (to be recognized and differentiated) (Brewer 1991; Zuckerman 2016). The concept of optimal distinctiveness helps to understand social identity dynamics (Brewer 1991), identity formation (Vaast et al. 2013), industry emergence, and transformation with new technologies (Navis and Glynn 2011, Zhao et al. 2017). Recent scholarship has proposed that social entities can reach optimal distinctiveness through a two-stage process model (Navis and Glynn 2011; Zuckerman 2016) in which, in a first stage, the similarity pole of the tension is emphasized, then, in a second stage, the distinctiveness pole of the tension is emphasized. Extant research on identity has not examined the persistence/obsolescence tension. We build upon our theoretical foundations and the qualitative investigations below to propose the label of persistent extinction for this synthesis and to elaborate upon it. Persistent extinction is the synthesis of the persistence/obsolescence tension in which members of an occupation define their identity as currently persisting by morphing and changing, while also acknowledging its prospect of becoming obsolete. With the synthesis of persistent extinction, people find a particular balance in their occupational identity as they acknowledge the prospect of eventually becoming obsolete while currently persisting. This synthesis enables them to define who they are and what they do for now, even though the digital technologies that shape their identity are also likely, in time, to render their occupation archaic.

Our theorizing further proposes that the syntheses of both tensions are transient rather than enduring for two reasons. First, tensions are not resolved once and for all, given that digital technologies evolve and affect occupations differently over time. Hence, we argue that this creates conditions for identity work to take place in an ongoing manner and to lead people to address these tensions repeatedly. Second, the two tensions of occupational identity are intertwined with each other. Both tensions arise from the characteristics of digital technologies. Addressing one tension may trigger changes in the other tension. How this happens, though, remains unknown and therefore needs to be theorized and investigated empirically.

This theoretical development highlights how occupational identity is defined and redefined with digital technologies as people engage in a process of identity work that follows a composite life-cycle and dialectical process. ## Methods

## Research Design and Setting

We adopted an interpretive case study design (Sarker et al. 2018; Walsham 1995) in line with prior research associated with occupational identity and new technologies (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Boudreau et al. 2014; Nelson and Irwin 2014). This qualitative approach enabled an in-depth and longitudinal investigation of the phenomenon of interest (Langley 1999; Walsham 1995). We examined how data scientists engaged in identity work when their occupation was enabled and threatened by digital technologies. While some of the tasks, practices, and skill sets of data scientists were introduced in the 1960s (see Appendix A for more information on the background origins and history of data science and data scientists) the terms data science, data scientists, and associated job positions have become widely recognized in the 2010s and are closely associated with the development of sophisticated digital technologies (Davenport and Patil 2012; Waller and Fawcett 2013). Many companies have hired data scientists (Blau 2015; King and Magoulas 2014), universities have developed programs in data science, and the media, as well as academic publications, have noted the appeal and growth potential of the occupation (Provost and Fawcett 2013; van der Aalst 2014). The occupation has been widely talked about and associated with trends such as big data and business analytics (Agarwal and Dhar 2014).

We investigated identity work for data scientists through the focal setting of Data Science Central (DSC). DSC has been in operation since late 2011 and is one of the largest online communities specifically dedicated to issues of relevance to data scientists. Its mission is to “provide a community experience that includes a robust editorial platform, social interaction, forum-based technical support, the latest in technology, tools and trends and industry job opportunities” (www.datasciencecentral.com). As of March 2018, DSC had more than 85,000 registered members, active forum discussions, and blog posts on which all registered users could comment. DSC constitutes an appropriate setting for this study for several reasons. DSC was created for the explicit purposes of connecting people interested in data science and of being a repository of information about the occupation. Also, DSC is highly popular with and broadly used by data scientists, others in adjacent occupations, and students. Discussions of identity work are particularly prevalent. Furthermore, DSC offers full public access to online discussions posted since its launch, which allows for longitudinal investigations. This is well suited to our interest in identity work as a process unfolding over time. Furthermore, DSC provided the authors with the ability to access but not influence the perspectives of people closely associated with the occupation and their identity work, in line with Vaast and Levina (2015).

## Data Collection and Analysis

We relied upon three key sources of data. First, the main data included the systematic collection of all blog posts and their comments, as well as all discussion threads from April 2012 (when members started to develop content, exchange with one another, and engage in identity work) to March 2018, cumulating in total the first six years of activity in DSC. We collected these data manually, complied with the terms of use of the online community, and anonymized entries that could be delicate, as expected based on standards of ethical research in online environments (Schultze and Mason 2012; Vaast and Walsham 2013). Collecting these data helped us access all members’ activity in DSC during its first years and gain a holistic understanding of the exchanges taking place among participants.

Second, we conducted 42 semidirected interviews: 37 with DSC members and five with nonmembers. These five interviews, which were conducted at the beginning of the research project, were with experts who had witnessed developments of data science over the years and aimed at providing important background regarding the digital technologies, practices, challenges, and opportunities faced by data scientists. The 37 interviews with DSC members aimed to examine how members perceived their participation in DSC, the dynamics of exchanges taking place, and what DSC represents for the occupation. All interviews helped grasp the context of the data scientist occupation and establish a better understanding of what was happening regarding identity work at DSC. We conducted these interviews in person, via telephone or Skype. Interviews lasted an average of 45 minutes. We recorded and transcribed them. As the study progressed, as is customary in qualitative research (Denzin and Lincoln 2000; Myers 2008), we amended the interview guide to reflect the development of the ongoing analyses. See Appendix B for more information about the interviewees and interview guide.

Third, additional archival documentation also provided us with insights into the occupation of data scientists. Table 1 summarizes the main collected data for this project. We analyzed the data as we collected them, which is typical of interpretive case study scholarship (Sarker et al. 2018). The first author served as the field researcher and worked closely with the second author and a research assistant. We used Atlas.ti to manage the data and the analysis process. We worked as one another’s sounding board and devil’s advocate for the analyses in progress as recommended in Barbour (2001) and Feldman (1995). As data analyses revealed new aspects in the data, we revisited the rest of the trace data. Subsequent analyses of further data helped us assess these insights and generate new ideas.

The iteration between data analysis and literature review sharpened our analytical approach. Our initial objective with the study was to investigate how data scientists develop a shared understanding of their practices of using digital technologies. The first interviews and early analyses of the discussion threads in DSC, however, soon revealed that issues of who data scientists were in relation to digital technologies, rather than simply how they did what they did, were of the utmost importance for them. These early insights led us to revisit our research question and refine our literature review to address issues associated with identity work. We refined the interview guide to more directly tackle questions of occupational identity and identity work associated with digital technologies. We also distinguished the two key tensions between similarity and distinctiveness and between persistence and obsolescence associated with the convergence and generativity of digital technologies. We went through the entire data set to investigate systematically how each of these tensions manifested, how DSC participants expressed them, and, when possible, how they responded to them. We also looked for instances where both tensions of identity were expressed simultaneously and searched for contrasting ways in which DSC participants addressed these issues.

The analyses of DSC discussions started to focus on episodes in which identity work was particularly prevalent. We engaged in a theoretical sampling of identity work episodes (Miles and Huberman 1994). The criteria for inclusion were that selected discussion threads (blog posts and associated comments as well as forum discussions) had to deal in large part with the identity of the data scientist occupation. The DSC threads we examined dealt with a variety of topics (e.g., techniques to learn a new statistical procedure, links to training programs, application of big data analytics to various data sets). We included discussion threads in which participants predominantly grappled with questions associated with who data scientists are and what they do, which is consistent with the definitions of occupational identity (Ashcraft 2013) and identity work (Brown 2015; Sveningsson and Alvesson 2003).

The theoretical sampling generated 30 episodes of identity work. These episodes constituted the unit of observation of this study. We examined each episode on its own (e.g., for themes, duration, participants, number of posts, escalation, resolution) as well as in relation to other episodes and other discussions in DSC. We developed an understanding of identity work in DSC and of how, specifically, participants engaged in it as contrasted with other activities. Appendix C provides detailed information about our analytical procedures.

## Questioning the Distinctiveness of Data Scientists (April 2012 – May 2014)

## Elements of Context and Need for Identity Work: Initial Growth of the Occupation

The first stage, which corresponds to the beginning of operations at DSC, was one of rapid development and rising recognition for data scientists. The growth of interest in and available positions associated with the occupation at the time were notable, as shown by the 2012 Harvard Business Review article cited above (Davenport and Patil 2012). This led to increasing activity and participation in DSC and to the creation of data scientist positions in numerous organizations (see Appendix A). Moreover, data scientists were increasingly being associated with new digital technologies such as programming languages (R, Python) and developments of big data and data analytics. During this stage, we noted many questions about who exactly data scientists were and what they did: some DSC participants were engaging in identity work. A DSC participant articulated the need to clarify the concepts and positions associated with data science and thus the importance of tackling identity work as follows: “We share responsibility to take [the] effort to the next level by reducing, if not eliminating, ambiguity from multivocal terms and titles we are using in data science” (John, 06/2013).

## Addressing the Similarity/Distinctiveness Tension of Occupational Identity

Similarity/distinctiveness was the main tension of occupational identity addressed by DSC participants during this stage. Given how recently the data scientist occupation had appeared, DSC participants openly questioned what made data scientists new and different from other, longerestablished occupations and disciplines. An illustration of the similarity/distinctiveness tension was given by one DSC participant, who highlighted the need to clarify what made data scientists distinctive in a complicated job market: “With data science so new, many employers want to bucket horizontal data scientists into existing categories. ‘You have coding skills, but you aren’t a coder? You know statistics but aren’t a statistician? You know SQL, but you aren’t a data architect? Then what are you?’” (Stephen, 03/2013). This quote shows that some DSC participants and some of their key stakeholders (e.g., in the above quote, employers) were struggling to find an adequate definition for the data scientist identity that would be sufficiently distinctive from that of other occupations (e.g., from quote: coder, statistician, data architect).

Our DSC participants tackled the similarity/distinctiveness tension in two main ways that enabled the expression of optimal distinctiveness for the new occupation: they highlighted the interdisciplinary nature of their work as distinct for the data scientist occupation, and they appealed to the sophisticated digital technologies that they used, i.e., they treated digital technologies as identity referents for the occupation. With regard to the first way of tackling the similarity/distinctiveness tension during this stage, we observed that some DSC participants reached the synthesis of optimal distinctiveness by acknowledging that the data scientist identity could seem similar to other occupations but was distinctive in its mission and practices. Some DSC participants therefore engaged in identity work to identify the optimal distinctiveness of the data scientist occupation with regard to its closest social referents. The following quote that connects and separates data scientists from statisticians illustrates this point: “Statisticians don’t commit to their models. Data scientists do. It is (and should be) by its very nature a much more operational role that demands more responsibility and a deeper sense of accountability.” (Pradyuamma, 09/2013)

We also noted that other DSC participants addressed the similarity/distinctiveness tension by emphasizing that the distinctiveness of the data scientist occupation came from data scientists being at the intersection of other occupations with which they shared similarities: “The role of the data scientist is to be the value creator—the bridge between statistician/computer engineer/etc. and key decision makers. … That is how I reconcile the blending of disparate skill sets (statistics, programming, business acumen) into the ideal role of data scientist.” (Mike, 05/2014)

Some DSC participants therefore addressed the similarity/distinctiveness tension by claiming that the very interdisciplinarity of data scientists, which could make them similar to other occupations, constituted the occupation’s distinctiveness. During this first stage, we therefore determined that some DSC participants reached a synthesis of optimal distinctiveness by defining the data scientist identity in a way that combined elements of similarity and distinctiveness vis-à-vis other occupations.

Furthermore, some DSC participants expressed the optimal distinctiveness of the identity of data scientists in terms of it being “more than” the interconnection of existing disciplines, as illustrated below in the following proposed definition of data science: “Data science is more than statistics: it also encompasses computer science and business concepts, and it’s far more than a set of techniques and principles” (Vincent, 04/2013). This quote is telling because it associates the optimal distinctiveness of data scientists with some of the technologies they used.

This leads us to the second way that participants tackled the similarity/distinctiveness tension during this stage. DSC participants at times addressed this tension by noting that what made data scientists distinctive was their reliance upon and development of sophisticated digital technologies. In other words, some DSC participants established the optimal distinctiveness of the data scientist identity by connecting the occupation to digital technologies. This point is illustrated in the following claim that establishes the distinction between data scientists and other occupations by alluding to digital technologies as identity referents for data scientists: “Sure we [statisticians and data scientists] are both using DAD [discover/access/distill], but in different universes, on very different data sets, and with different tools” (Vincent, 10/2013). This post signals that some DSC participants claimed optimal distinctiveness for the data scientist identity by emphasizing the distinctive connections between data scientists and the digital technologies they relied upon. It reveals that some DSC participants reached a synthesis of the similarity/distinctiveness tension by appealing to digital technologies as important referents for the data scientist identity. This, in turn, provoked questions regarding whether the data scientist occupation could persist over time, as it might be at risk of becoming obsolete. By appealing to digital technologies as referents for their occupational identity, data scientists thus invoked the tension of persistence/obsolescence.

## Transition toward the Next Stage

In the transition from the first stage to the second stage, we noted that DSC participants, while still having to deal primarily with the identity tension of similarity/distinctiveness, also started to tackle the other identity tension of persistence/obsolescence. Some DSC participants began to ask whether the occupation could eventually be replaced or automated by the digital technologies it relies upon. This tension of identity was at the core of the questions asked in the following post: “What can be automated? Can exploratory data analysis be automated? Isn’t one of the main goals of data science being machine-to-machine communications and automating decision processes? Which jobs are safe?” (Data Science Girl, 01/2014).

Other DSC participants responded to questions about the risk of obsolescence by considering the potential implications for data scientists and their jobs in the face of new technological developments. They emphasized that the occupation could persist because it was more than just the digital technologies its participants used: “If data science skills are in short supply in total, it may be a bit early to wonder why these skills have not yet been abstracted into automation. (Hint: It may be because this is a broad science not easily captured in simple ‘push-this-button’ software)” (Mitchell, 01/2014).

## Analytical Summary

During the first stage, we noted that DSC participants engaged mostly in identity work to deal with the similarity/distinctiveness tension. They needed to distinguish the identity of data scientists from that of other occupations— in particular, statisticians. To reach the synthesis of this tension, DSC participants at times appealed to the interdisciplinarity of data science as part of what made it optimally distinctive. They also emphasized the relationships between the data scientist identity and the digital technologies it relied upon as identity referents. This contributed to establishing optimal distinctiveness by associating the occupational identity of data scientists with their IT identity.

Toward the end of the first stage, we realized that some DSC participants were starting to ask whether the occupation could last, given its connection to ever-changing digital technologies. This revealed that the optimal distinctiveness synthesis reached by appealing to the connection between data scientists and digital technologies was transient. Further, addressing the similarity/distinctiveness tension by relating data scientists to the digital technologies they used and developed contributed to making the second tension of identity more palpable and motivated the transition from the first stage to the second.

## Dealing with the Prospect of Becoming Obsolete, and Claiming Distinctiveness (June 2014 – February 2016)

## Elements of Context and the Need for Identity Work: Continued Growth of the Occupation

During this stage, DSC continued to grow and the occupation was becoming increasingly popular in terms of media attention and of available positions (see Appendix A). The occupation was becoming more broadly known, although still not necessarily well understood, with high profile hires such as DJ Patil as White House data scientist in 2015 and Forbes magazine naming data scientist as the best job to pursue in 2016. The digital technologies discussed in DSC during this stage were the same ones addressed during the previous stage (e.g., R, Python) and included developments related to big data and data analytics. New technological developments such as AI became important for data scientists during this stage. There were thus ongoing new technological changes that affected the occupation and, more generally, the business environment in which the occupation was flourishing (see Appendix A).

DSC participants continued to express the need to address issues associated with who data scientists were and what they did, i.e., they engaged in identity work. One DSC participant explained, for instance, that the occupation was not yet well understood and that this could lead to issues: “The main problem what I see with data science is that there is no fixed paradigm for it. ... This is leading to a chaos where people are addressing methodologies more than [the] problem itself” (Jitin, 02/2015). Another DSC participant noted that the very popularity of the term “data scientist” made it difficult to establish clearly who data scientists were and was detrimental to the occupation: “There’s no consensus on what a ‘data scientist’ really is. Because the role is so hyped, everyone and their cousin is loosely donning the title ... greatly diluting its value” (Eric, 07/2015).

## Addressing the Persistence/Obsolescence Tension of Occupational Identity

During this stage, we found that the persistence/obsolescence tension was the more prevalent one because DSC participants expressed anxiety related to data scientists becoming increasingly seen as equivalent to and dependent upon the sophisticated digital technologies that made their work possible. DSC discussions turned to whether data scientists would eventually reach obsolescence and be replaced by these digital technologies. We noted that DSC participants addressed the persistence/obsolescence tension by distancing themselves from digital technologies, by rejecting the data scientist occupation and its label, and by reaching a synthesis of persistent extinction.

First, some DSC participants rejected the prospect of obsolescence by considering data scientists to be professionals that mastered sophisticated technologies rather than merely depended on them. In this case, they often distanced their occupation from digital technologies and claimed that data scientists would persist through their positioning above the digital technologies they used. This is illustrated in the following quote that rejected the prospect of obsolescence of data scientists raised in a trade press article: “‘You need an algorithm, not a Data Scientist.’ Um ... not quite. ... What you actually need is a data scientist and then an algorithm.” (Guerrilla, 07/2015). This DSC participant argued that the data scientist occupation would persist and established a hierarchy between the occupation and digital technologies as identity referents. Other DSC participants rejected the prospect of obsolescence by emphasizing the preeminence of data scientists over digital technologies: “Tech cannot replace highly educated and trained data scientists who have deep analytical skills, scientific training to detect signal from noise” (Michael, 10/2014).

Others also affirmed the identity of data scientists above and beyond digital technologies by establishing that data scientists did more than merely use specific digital technologies: “Data Science = Python v R is the very embodiment of mistaking the part for the whole—data science is not statistical data programming” (Ihe, 02/2015). By rejecting the strong connection between the data scientist identity and digital technologies, this quote also separated the data scientist identity from the IT identity that some could experience with regard to the Python and R programming languages.

Second, some DSC participants addressed the persistence/obsolescence tension by rejecting the data scientist identity altogether. These participants maintained that the data scientist identity was indeed becoming obsolete because it had never been sufficiently established. A DSC participant, for instance, rejected the data scientist identity, expressing regret at the lack of clear definition underlying the label of data science: “In time, I believe the ‘data scientist’ title will dissolve for a few reasons: Like ‘big data,’ the term is too broad and undefined. It means many different things to different people” (Eric, 08/2014). Similarly, another DSC participant expressed doubt that there was any reality behind the hype attached to the data scientist occupation: “The term data scientist is a fad, and people jump on the bandwagon because it makes them sound or look awesome that the word ‘scientist’ is attached to their title” (Sione, 07/2015).

Third, still other DSC participants addressed the persistence/obsolescence tension by reaching a synthesis of persistent extinction whereby they articulated the prospect of the data scientist occupation eventually becoming obsolete, while emphasizing that it was currently still continuing to persist and morph. One DSC participant expressed this synthesis by tackling the issue of whether AI would one day be used in lieu of data scientists: “Will AI replace data scientists? I think so, though data scientists will be initially replaced by ‘low intelligence’ yet extremely stable and robust systems” (Vincent, 02/2016). The same DSC participant also recognized that new digital technologies would automate and, in time, replace data scientists’ work. Yet he noted that this reliance upon digital technology and the synthesis of persistent extinction participated in making the occupation of data scientist distinct from that of statisticians: “The future of data science is to have algorithm parameters fine-tuned automatically. … This is [a] billion miles away from statistical science and is true data science” (Vincent, 06/2015). Notably, in the previous quote, the DSC participant emphasized the tight connection among the development and use of sophisticated new digital technologies, the persistent extinction synthesis, and a strong IT identity, thereby establishing the distinctiveness of the data scientist occupation versus that of statisticians. During this stage, we thus noticed that the persistent extinction synthesis was used as a way to address the similarity/distinctiveness tension.

## Transition toward the Next Stage

During the transition toward the next stage, we noticed that DSC participants began to consider how digital technologies such as AI would not only make data scientists obsolete but that this fate would also be shared with many other occupations. Therefore, some DSC participants started to address the persistence/obsolescence tension by highlighting the similarity of data scientists with other occupations in terms of the latest round of digital technologies: “By the time AI can figure out on its own what data it needs to solve a problem and how to get it, aren’t we talking about the end of \*all\* jobs? Once AI can do that, humans can no longer lie about observable events and get away with it” (Jeb, 02/2016). We observed that the transition between the second and the third stage was marked by changes in the ways in which DSC participants dealt with the persistence/obsolescence tension. They began to associate the synthesis of persistent extinction with claims not of the distinctiveness of the data scientist identity vis-à-vis other occupations but of its similarity with them.

## Analytical Summary

During the second stage, DSC participants engaged in identity work in order to grapple with the persistence/obsolescence tension. They associated the digital technologies that made their work possible with IT identity and characterized them as making their occupational identity optimally distinctive from that of other occupations. Yet they also observed that those digital technologies could also, in due time, make their occupation obsolete. As they faced the persistence/obsolescence tension, some DSC participants distanced their occupation from the digital technologies it relied upon. Other DSC participants rejected the data scientist identity. Still others produced a synthesis of the persistence/obsolescence tension. They presented the data scientist occupation as experiencing persistent extinction. This synthesis acknowledged that data scientists may eventually disappear and be replaced by digital technologies, while also emphasizing that, for the time being, the occupation still persisted. The persistent extinction synthesis was often used by DSC participants as a means of defining the optimal distinctiveness of data scientists and punctuating the distinctiveness of the data scientist identity in comparison to other occupations. Toward the end of the second stage, we observed that DSC participants started to connect the persistent extinction synthesis with the similarity between the data scientist occupation and other occupations rather than their differences.

## Dealing with the Prospect of Becoming Obsolete, and Claiming Similarity (March 2016 – March 2018)

## Elements of Context and the Need for Identity Work: Continued Growth of the Occupation

This stage saw continued growth of the occupation. For three years (2016-2018), the human resources platform “Glassdoor” named data scientist the best job in the United States based on its earning potential, job satisfaction, and number of job openings. An IBM report projected that the number of people employed as data scientists would continue growing at a rapid pace (see Appendix A). There continued to be intense societal interest in data scientists (see Appendix A). Among the digital technologies associated with data scientists that were discussed by DSC participants during this stage were programming languages such as R or Python. We noticed that references to big data and data analytics receded in favor of discussions associated with AI such as machine learning and deep learning. At this point, there had been a decoupling of the data scientist identity from some digital technologies and new couplings with other, newer digital technologies.

The development of the occupation and of digital technologies continued to be intertwined with the ongoing identity work of DSC participants. DSC participants commented that the growth of the occupation meant that there were now multiple positions associated with the label, along with increasingly diverse expectations, skill sets, and still fuzzy notions of what data scientists could actually do. A few rare DSC participants took these developments in stride and mentioned that working toward clearly defining the identity of the occupation had become pointless: “Why are folks so hung up about what a data scientist should be or shouldn't be? As far as I’m concerned, get the individual that will get the job done, and call him whatever you want to call him or her” (Oliver, 03/2016). DSC participants, however, continued to engage in identity work to define and refine the identity of the data scientist occupation. Another participant, for instance, acknowledged that: “The problem in our profession, and I’m not going to solve it here, is there is not an accepted nomenclature that differentiates the various skill levels of data scientists or who gets to wear that title at all” (William, 04/2016).

## Addressing the Persistence/Obsolescence Tension of Occupational Identity

During this stage, DSC participants struggled with the persistence/obsolescence tension. The new association of AI and machine learning with data science, and thus the IT identity of data scientists, threatened to take over the occupation and its identity. We observed that this was a continuation and outcome of the previous stages. During the first stage, a few DSC participants questioned whether AI could replace data scientists, while the majority thought that this was a long-term prospect only. During the second stage, questions about the eventual replacement of data scientists by AI became prevalent. Some DSC participants acknowledged this possibility in their articulation of the synthesis of persistent extinction. During the third stage, despite the rapid growth in the number of data science positions, no indication of slowdown, and favorable industry reports, some DSC participants expressed strong doubts about whether data scientists could survive AI: “The job of data scientist—the quintessential big data job, and the job that was just voted the best job in America for 2016—is at risk. Data scientists have been called ‘unicorns’ because finding the right person with the right set of skills … could be practically impossible. But machine learning and big data itself may be making those unicorns as obsolete as they are mythical” (Bernard, 05/2016). Another participant noted: “As data scientists, we all understand how AI and machine learning is going to disrupt industries. If AI can power autonomous vehicles, I’m quite sure it can automate data prep and algorithm selection. Yes, even data scientists have to be concerned about the future of work!” (Steven, 01/2018).

Moreover, some DSC participants engaged in identity work in ways that indicated a growing concern that the practices of current data scientists might be precipitating their obsolescence. One participant noted how the technological developments that threatened the persistence of the occupation came from data scientists themselves: “It [automation] may raise an ethical question within our data science community— ‘why saw off the branch we are sitting on?’ or ‘The justification of the fully automated analytics’” (Danylo, 07/2017). To address the persistence/obsolescence tension, DSC participants engaged in identity work in ways that distanced the occupation from digital technologies, differentiated who the “real” data scientists were, and reached a synthesis of persistent extinction while highlighting that the fate of data scientists would be similar to that of many other occupations.

First, we observed that some DSC participants addressed the persistence/obsolescence tension by claiming that the data scientist identity came from something beyond digital technologies. They distanced the data science occupation from the digital technologies its practitioners relied upon, emphasizing that data scientists would continuously update their skills to fit new digital technologies and that this ability made data scientists more valuable than digital technologies: “Data science has been and continues to be a field in which knowledge of tools as well as business in paramount. … Innovation is simplifying and automating the toolbox of existing tools even as new tools are arising. In the past, if we were expert carpenters with great skill with our tools, in the future we will be more like architects bringing a broad range of tools and design skills to bear to build value” (William, 04/2016). This DSC participant explained how the data scientist identity would continue to change as digital technologies became more sophisticated. He also noted that the occupational identity of data scientists went beyond their mere IT identity.

Second, some DSC participants addressed the persistence/obsolescence tension by differentiating between “real” data scientists (who, according to them, would persist) and “fake” ones (who would become obsolete). This differentiation among different types of data scientists (the “real” ones vs. the other ones) came with an expressed sense of superiority for the contributions that “real” data scientists made: “We call people who don't understand the data first before applying ML [machine learning] algorithms ‘Data Monkeys.’ They play with data or they mock what real data scientists do and call themselves data scientists” (Taihua, 07/2016). This DSC participant established a forceful contrast between “data monkeys” and “real data scientists.” This differentiation among diverse data scientists formed the basis for this DSC participant’s appeal to the distinctiveness of “real” data scientists as a means of establishing how they could avoid obsolescence.

Third, some DSC participants acknowledged that the data scientist occupation would likely fade over time because of new digital technologies, but that this process would be shared by many other occupations. Such DSC participants therefore appealed to the similarity between the data scientist occupation and other occupations (i.e., a pole of the other tension of identity) in order to reach a synthesis in terms of persistent extinction. One DSC participant, for example, relied upon an analogy while engaging in identity work: “Just like software developers continue to exist in today’s market ecosystem, so will there be a need for data scientists, we just don’t need as many” (Brian, 07/2017). This DSC participant articulated the synthesis of persistent extinction in two ways: (1) by acknowledging that the data scientist occupation would change and that its size would dwindle over time because of innovations in digital technologies, and (2) by noting that, like other occupations that also faced the prospect of obsolescence, data scientists would survive for now.

## Analytical Summary of this Stage and the Entire Observation Period

During the third stage, DSC participants engaged in identity work as they continued to question the data scientist identity. They dealt with the two tensions of identity. DSC participants continued to question the data scientist identity as they pondered how new digital technologies could eventually make them obsolete. Although these digital technologies could strengthen their IT identity, they also threatened their occupational identity. One way in which DSC participants dealt with the persistence/obsolescence tension and reached a synthesis in terms of persistent extinction was to associate what data scientists were going through with what members of other occupations also experienced. They reached the persistent extinction synthesis by appealing to the similarity between data science and other occupations.

All in all, during the entire observation period, DSC participants sometimes addressed the two tensions of identity separately and sometimes jointly. Nevertheless, they eventually reached the syntheses of optimal distinctiveness and the persistent extinction of the data scientist identity. Reaching each identity tension synthesis often required DSC participants to appeal to one pole of the other identity tension. These syntheses however remained transient: the two tensions of identity were expressed in new ways and required ongoing yet changing identity work as new digital technologies were being developed and used.

## Discussion

## Occupational Identity Work and Digital Technologies: A Dialectical and Life-Cycle Process

Digital technologies trigger deep transformations in what people do and in how they define their work, thus prompting identity work (Stein et al. 2013; Whitley et al. 2014). We conceptualized identity work associated with occupational identity and digital technologies as a composite life-cycle and dialectical process. We explained how the generativity and convergence of digital technologies elicit two key tensions for occupational identity: (1) the tension of an occupation both persisting because of and continuously being threatened by digital technologies, (2) the tension of how similar to and distinct from other occupations an occupation is.

We brought together scholarship on process theorizing, occupational identity, and digital technologies to theorize how people deal with the tensions of occupational identity associated with digital technologies. An interpretive longitudinal qualitative case study illustrated and deepened our theorizing of identity work for occupational identity associated with digital technologies as a composite life-cycle and dialectical process. Building upon the insights from the case study and our initial theoretical development, we developed an integrative process model through which an occupational identity was defined and refined over time (illustrated in Figure 1).

Consistent with life-cycle process theorizing, in our model, identity work is composed of relatively discrete stages that accumulate over time (as illustrated in the gradually thicker outlines of the occupational identity rectangles in Figure 1). The two tensions of occupational identity associated with digital technologies are temporarily addressed differently in each stage. People deal with these tensions in syntheses of optimal distinctiveness and persistent extinction, which is consistent with dialectical process theorizing. These syntheses enable temporary expressions of occupational identity, which is depicted in the dotted rectangles of Figure 1. In each stage, the syntheses reached for the two tensions of identity reflect the syntheses of the previous stages while also showing progression over time. The tensions are also connected to each other and mutually constitutive. They help form the definition of the occupational identity at a particular time. How people address the two tensions both separately and jointly participates in changing the occupational identity and driving transitions from one stage to the next.

The two tensions of identity associated with the dialectical dimension of the process lead to transient syntheses reached through ongoing identity work. Key characteristics of digital technologies challenge people’s ability to stabilize their occupational identity as they engage in identity work. People deal with digital technologies that both threaten and enable their occupational identity in a constant yet changing manner. In their identity work, people thus repeatedly need to deal with two interconnected tensions of identity. Since the stages are cumulative, how people deal with the tension of one stage is shaped by how they dealt with these tensions in previous stages. In turn, the availability of new digital technologies and the ways in which they affect adjacent occupations also shape identity work in an ongoing and changing manner.

Our study explained that because of the combination of convergence and generativity of digital technologies, occupation members deal with two key tensions of occupational identity and engage in a composite process of identity work. This life-cycle dimension of the process is seen as unitary: i.e., it concerns occupational identity. It is also cumulative as each stage builds on the previous one. Further, it is conjunctive in that each stage is related to the previous and following ones. This is evident in the case of the data scientists we examined, as the three stages were connected to and built upon one another. During the first stage, the similarity/distinctiveness tension was dominant, whereas during the second and third stages, the dominant tension was the persistence/obsolescence one. During each stage, DSC participants tackled these tensions in a manner that differed from and built upon identity work from the previous stage.

The process of identity work for occupations dealing with digital technologies is also dialectical. Occupation members need to address the two tensions of similarity/distinctiveness and persistence/obsolescence. They can at times reach syntheses of optimal distinctiveness and persistent extinction, but these syntheses are transient rather than sustained over time. Our study shows the different and changing ways through which DSC participants addressed the two tensions. The syntheses of persistent extinction and optimal distinctiveness associated with the data scientist identity did not last. The transitions between stages of identity work come from changes in digital technologies and/or from the transient synthesis of one tension that triggers the other tension anew. For example, the transition between Stages 2 and 3 came in part from data scientists connecting the persistent extinction synthesis not to the distinctiveness of their occupation, as they did previously, but to its similarity with other occupations.

The findings of our study also enable us to theorize the relationship between optimal distinctiveness and persistent extinction. In particular, we found that the synthesis for each tension associated with a defining characteristic of digital technologies can be reached by an appeal to the other defining characteristic of digital technologies. For example, DSC participants reached optimal distinctiveness in part by referring to the generativity of digital technologies and they reached persistent extinction partly by referring to the convergence of digital technologies. We also found that appealing to the other key characteristic of digital technologies to address a given tension of occupational identity precipitates the other tension. People who engage in identity work associated with digital technology thus risk bolstering the two tensions of identity in turn. This contributes to the transient nature of the syntheses. For occupations deeply affected by digital technologies, the two tensions of identity need to be addressed together and in relation to each other. However, it is difficult for people to address them jointly, because addressing one typically triggers the other.

## Generalization of the Identity Work Process Model

The process model of identity work we developed for data scientists can help guide research on other occupations deeply affected by digital technologies. Convergence, which is associated with the identity tension of similarity/distinctiveness, is perceptible in occupations other than data science. For instance, many healthcare occupations and professions have experienced disruptions of their identity associated with the convergence of digital technologies such as digital imaging and electronic medical records. These disruptions have led people to redefine the boundaries among occupations (e.g., Barrett et al. 2012). The generativity of digital technologies is associated with the identity tension of persistence/obsolescence. Data scientists in our study feared that new technological developments associated with AI were both key to the identity of their occupation and introduced the potential for its obsolescence. Other occupations have also experienced this tension. Journalists, for instance, since the development of Web 2.0 technologies, have contemplated the disappearance of their occupation (Jones 2010). The rise of AI has also caused journalists to experience anew the fear of becoming obsolete (Latar and Nordfors, 2009).

The identity work process endures as long as convergence and generativity continue to introduce changes to the occupation. In our study, data scientists had to engage in identity work in an ongoing manner because changes in the digital technologies they used continuously (re)triggered the two identity tensions. Tech bloggers also engage in identity work in an ongoing manner because they deal with the two tensions of similarity/distinctiveness and persistence/obsolescence over time (Vaast et al. 2013). While we have not observed the end of identity work for data scientists because the occupation is still evolving, it is possible that the process of identity work will be terminated under two conditions. First, the process may stop if the occupation completely disappears (i.e., if it becomes fully obsolete, because of generativity, and/or it becomes subsumed under other occupations because of convergence). Second, the closure of the process could occur if the two characteristics of convergence and generativity largely stop producing disruptions for the occupation and its practices. For instance, if the digital technologies used by an occupation stabilize, the syntheses of the two tensions may become more durable and less transient. This could make the occupational identity more stable. For example, following the stabilization of internet search technologies, a new identity for librarians appeared and stabilized-for-now and identity work diminished for librarians (Nelson and Irwin 2014; Boudreau et al. 2014).

## Implications

## Implications for Scholarship

Scholarship has noted the importance of examining how the introduction of new digital technologies can affect occupational identity (Heracleous and Barrett 2001; Lamb and Davidson 2005) and group identity (Boudreau et al. 2014). Our study has implications for two streams of research on this topic.

## Digital Technologies and Occupational Identity

Thus far, scholarship has examined identity work associated with the introduction of a single new digital technology and has assumed that the occupational identity is steady before and after the transitional identity work period. Our study adds to this scholarship by examining how key characteristics of digital technologies affect occupational identity in a manner unanticipated by existing scholarship. We conceptualized identity work related to digital technologies as involving a composite life-cycle and dialectical process in which people deal in an ongoing yet changing manner with two distinct but interrelated tensions of identity: similarity vs. distinctiveness and persistence vs. obsolescence. The latter tension highlights that the generativity of technology may help define an occupation’s identity but may also render it obsolete. Our study delved into why and how people address the prospect of their occupation risking becoming obsolete from the very digital technologies that anchor its identity. We contribute to this stream of research by elaborating upon how people deal with identity tensions in an ongoing yet cumulatively changing manner. They reach transient syntheses that address these tensions directly and they repeatedly oscillate from one tension to another, reframing or rejecting the occupational identity.

Our study also questions the assumption of the stabilizationfor-now of occupational identity when people respond and adjust to new technologies (Boudreau et al. 2014; Nelson and Irwin 2014; Stein et al. 2013). On the contrary, our study suggests that the two tensions of identity might lead to unexpected consequences stemming from digital technologies for the identity of various occupations. As digital technologies continue to affect multiple occupations, people engage in identity work in ways that affect how they define their own and others’ occupations. Our study reveals that the growing reliance on digital technologies in the work of multiple occupations, along with the ongoing changes in the digital technologies being used, can lead to repeated and changing challenges to the identities and relations among occupations.

Scholarship so far has addressed the identity work coming from the renegotiation of occupational mandates of people engaged in collaborative tasks (Barrett et al. 2012; Oborn et al. 2011). Our study contributes to this scholarship by illuminating how the introduction and ongoing changes in multiple digital technologies lead people to reconsider the contrast between their occupation and other occupations with whom they may not collaborate directly but whose identity and mandates partly overlap (e.g., in this study, data scientists and statisticians or software developers). The identities of such adjacent occupations can be disrupted by the same digital technologies, prompting people to engage anew in redefining the optimal distinctiveness of their occupational identity. Members of adjacent occupations redefine themselves in relation to one another, and do it over and over again, negotiating who they are and what they do as they face the identity challenges brought about by digital technologies.

## Occupational Identity and its Referents

Our study also has implications for the relationships between occupational identity and its identity referents, including IT identity and other occupations’ identity. Carter and Grover (2015), for example, noted the potential for IS scholarship to examine the relationship between IT identity and other identities. We offer original insights in this regard by explaining how the generativity of digital technologies leads to an occupational identity that is closely associated with IT identity but also with the occupational identity that is continuously threatened by obsolescence through digital technologies. Hence, the relationship between occupational identity and IT identity is repeatedly redefined for occupations drastically affected by digital technologies.

Further, our study indicates that IT identity can become a part of occupational identity when digital technologies are deeply embedded in the practices of an occupation. IT identity, however, can take over occupational identity. Our study provides insights into this phenomenon by examining how IT identity and occupational identity are defined in relation to each other in an ongoing manner through identity work. We examined the relationship between IT identity and occupational identity and documented how IT identity can overshadow occupational identity. Our study also contributes to the scholarship on the social referents of occupational identity. We offer insights into whether and how an occupational identity can become and remain optimally distinctive from other occupations. Scholarship has presented optimal distinctiveness as an illuminating concept for multiple situations across various social sciences (Zhao et al. 2017; Zuckerman 2016). This concept captures the need for balance between similarity and distinctiveness for an entity, be it individual (see Brewer 1991) or social, such as an occupation (see Vaast et al. 2013) or a category (see Navis and Glynn 2011). Any entity needs to be different but not too different, in order to be both socially acceptable yet differentiated (Brewer 1991). Navis and Glynn (2011) and Zuckerman (2016) further proposed a two-stage process of optimal distinctiveness (first similarity, then distinctiveness) as a way for an entity to address the tension between similarity and distinctiveness.

Our study revealed how a two-stage model cannot apply to occupations deeply affected by digital technologies because of the intricate relationships between the convergence and generativity of digital technologies. For instance, generativity leads to the ongoing redefinition of practices and identity, and thus also repeatedly calls for new articulations of the similarity/distinctiveness tension. A simple two-stage process is insufficient for an occupational identity to be defined in relation to its social referents. We developed a composite process that integrates stages and ongoing tensions, which thus more fully captures the complex reality of identity work.

## Practical Implications

Our study also has implications for practice because it highlights how digital technologies affect occupations in an ongoing manner, rather than in a series of limited discrete phases. Our study suggests that people whose work is deeply affected by digital technologies need to constantly define and redefine their occupational identity, while also acknowledging threats to it. We show how members of occupations deeply affected by digital technologies can recognize that the threats to the persistence of an occupation at any given time also represent the elements of what the occupation will become in the future. Our study can help practitioners recognize particular sources of stress and anxiety associated with their work and the digital technologies they rely upon and can help them understand how these sources of stress can turn into new value creation and competencies.

Moreover, our study offers practical implications for the managers and administrators of online communities in which members discuss their work and digital technologies. While much scholarship has provided insights regarding occupational knowledge dynamics in such online communities, this study reveals the importance of discussions associated with occupational identity and its challenges. Managers of online communities could facilitate the creation of shared resources associated with occupational identity in addition to more typical libraries of “best practices” and disciplinary knowledge that are often archived online. Given how unsettling the constant challenges to and changes associated with an occupational identity can be, it would be worthwhile for managers of online communities to encourage participants to develop shared resources to enhance identity work. Developing such resources could involve welcoming discussions about who practitioners are and how they see themselves in relation to others and/or to digital technologies. The generativity of digital technologies would dictate that such discussions be reiterated and change over time. Such discussions could help participants come to terms with tensions associated with their occupation and digital technologies.

## Limitations and Future Research

Like all research projects, this study has some limitations that provide boundary conditions for this work and open avenues for future scholarship. Our theorizing focused on identity work for an occupation that is deeply affected by digital technologies. It is unclear how the composite process theorizing developed in this study might apply in the case of occupations less deeply affected by digital technologies. Further theoretical development would thus be useful to qualify the process for occupations more peripherally affected by digital technologies.

Moreover, this research relied on the study of a single occupation through the investigation of a dedicated online community. The occupation of data scientists and the ways in which DSC participants engaged in identity work constitute a critical case (Flyvbjerg 2006), anticipated to reveal identity work for an occupation deeply affected by digital technologies. It would be worth examining a different occupation with a different research design. This might yield interesting complementary insights. For instance, future scholarship could follow transformations in the interactions of members of a focal occupation with other occupations as they use changing digital technologies. Another alternative research design might ask members of an occupation to report in their own words on their occupational identity at different periods of time. Such a research design could be useful to capture changes in the ways in which people define themselves in relation to their work as they use new digital technologies.

## Conclusion

In this study, we examined how people engage in identity work in ways that shape and reshape their occupational identity in relation to digital technologies. We conceptualized identity work associated with digital technologies as a life-cycle and dialectical process in which people address and oscillate repeatedly between the two key tensions of identity: persistence/obsolescence and similarity/distinctiveness. We provide a fresh perspective that helps improve the understanding of the delicate balancing act of building and transforming occupational identity for occupations deeply embedded with digital technologies. We propose a composite process theorizing with two key motors that captures the complexity of identity work with digital technologies. We hope that our study offers theoretical foundations for more process research on occupational identity and identity work in an increasingly digital world.
