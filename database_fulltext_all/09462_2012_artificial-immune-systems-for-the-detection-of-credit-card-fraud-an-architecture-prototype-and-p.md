# Artificial immune systems for the detection of credit card fraud: an architecture, prototype and preliminary results

Nicholas Wong,\* Pradeep Ray,\* Greg Stephens\* & Lundy Lewis<sup>†</sup>

\*C/- School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney, New South Wales Australia, email: p.ray@unsw.edu.au, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Computer Information Technology, Southern New Hampshire University, Manchester, New Hampshire, USA, email: l.lewis@snhu.edu

Abstract. Some biological phenomena offer clues to solving real-life, complex problems. Researchers have been studying techniques such as neural networks and genetic algorithms for computational intelligence and their applications to such complex problems. The problem of security management is one of the major concerns in the development of eBusiness services and networks. Recent incidents have shown that the perpetrators of cybercrimes are using increasingly sophisticated methods. Hence, it is necessary to investigate non-traditional mechanisms, such as biological techniques, to manage the security of evolving eBusiness networks and services. Towards this end, this paper investigates the use of an Artificial Immune System (AIS). The AIS emulates the mechanism of human immune systems that save human bodies from complex natural biological attacks. The paper discusses the use of AIS on one aspect of security management, viz. the detection of credit card fraud. The solution is illustrated with a case study on the management of frauds in credit card transactions, although this technique may be used in a range of security management applications in eBusiness.

Keywords: artificial immune systems, security management, credit card fraud detection, eBusiness

## INTRODUCTION

A wide range of security problems beset e-commerce. For example, a major problem faced by e-commerce retailers is the problem of online credit card payment fraud. The following statistics highlight the prominence and cost of this problem:

Cybersource Corporation (2006a) estimates the annual cost in USA/Canada of online fraud to have been over US\$2.5 billion in 2005.

Financial Fraud Action UK (2011c,d) card-not-present fraud in the UK increased 21% in 2005 to a cost of £183.2 million.

VISA processes about US\$3 trillion per year, and out of every \$100 processed, 7 cents is represented by anomalous transactions what? (VISA, 2005a).

By accepting payment for purchases made online with a credit card, a merchant is exposed to the risk inherent in card-not-present transactions for which the retailer has to bear the cost caused by anomalous transactions. Such costs may include the cost of goods sold, shipping costs as well as chargeback administration costs (Financial Fraud Action UK, 2011a,b).

The online credit card fraud detection problem domain is essentially a classification problem whereby transactions need to be classified as either legitimate or anomalous. Algorithmic and fixed-rule systems are struggling to cope with complex and rapidly evolving domains such as this. More adaptive solutions have accordingly been sought. The biological immune system is a natural classifier; it discriminates between self-proteins and the foreign antigens tha compose non-self. Thus for this reason, as well as other features described below, an artificia immune system (AIS), which is primarily modelled on the biological immune system, is an attractive approach to online credit card fraud detection. The key contribution of the article is the use of the pattern of AISs in eCommerce transaction analysis.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides a concise discussion of the biologica immune system and derives a set of design principles for AISs. Section 3 provides current approaches to credit card fraud detection and their weaknesses, presents an AIS model and discusses the application of the model to the problem of credit card fraud detection. Section 4 provides results and analyses of the system Artificial Immune System for Credit Card Fraud Detection (AISCCFD). The paper closes with a conclusion and directions for further research.

## IMMUNE SYSTEMS

The field of AISs is still in its infancy, with the majority of research in the field only occurring within the last 15 years. AISs continue to be applied to a variety of problem domains from computer security applications (Kim & Bentley, 1999; 2001a; Harmer et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2003; 2005), to assisting with medical diagnosis (S¸ ahan et al., 2005), to data mining (Freitas & Timmis, 2007). The latter reference offers a methodology by which to design an AIS whilst taking into account the character of the data to be mined. Meanwhile, Kim et al. (2003) are concerned with the detection of anomalies in retail transactions and thus is related to the research reported in this paper. The limitations of the approach are discussed in the following section. Our research aims to develop an AIS specifically for the problem domain of online credit card fraud detection. This is an area for which there has been a limited amount of previous research.

In order to properly understand AISs, it is important to understand the basic composition, processes and operation of the human biological immune system at a high level. We briefly explain the features of the biological immune system in the following section and then follow with a discussion of adapting those features in AISs towards applications to real-world problems.

## The biological immune system

The biological immune system is composed of a diverse range of cells, but of particular interest to most AISs are the cells known as lymphocytes. Lymphocytes are created in the bone marrow from a random selection of gene segments from various gene libraries (Hunt & Cooke, 1996; De Castro & Von Zuben, 1999a,b; Tuo et al., 2004). They can also be created through cloning of existing lymphocytes.

Lymphocytes, or white blood cells, are the immune system’s antigen detectors, where antigens are harmful foreign organisms. There are two classes of lymphocytes – T cells and B cells – that have specific roles within the immune system. However, for the purposes of this discussion, we treat a lymphocyte as a single type of detector thus merging the functions of T cells and B cells. Lymphocytes contain receptors on their surface that allow them to detect antigens by binding to them if the affinity between the receptor and the antigen is sufficient. Figure 1 shows antigens at the bottom left that bind with the lymphocyte with high affinity.

Self-proteins are useful organisms. Unlike antigens, self-proteins are produced by the human body and perform essential functions such as metabolism. In order to prevent the detectors binding with self-proteins, immature lymphocytes undergo a process of censoring wherein they are exposed to them. During this process, if a lymphocyte binds to a self-protein, the lymphocyte undergoes programmed cell death. If the lymphocyte survives being exposed to organisms for a sufficient time period, the lymphocyte becomes a mature operationa lymphocyte. This process of censoring and maturation is known as negative selection, which is depicted in Figure 2 (Forrest et al., 1994; 1997; Hofmeyr, 1999; De Castro & Von Zuben, 1999a,b; Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a,b; Wightman, 2003).

A safety mechanism in the immune system response process is a co-stimulation requirement. When a lymphocyte has a sufficient affinity to bind with a suspected antigen, it stil requires an extra signal before an immune response is stimulated. This signal is known as co-stimulation and typically is provided by other components of the immune system. This mechanism prevents an overzealous immune response to an insignificant antigen and allows the immune system to focus and prioritise its responses on more prominent antigens (Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a; Hofmeyr, 1999; Harmer et al., 2002; Wightman, 2003).

Upon successful binding, known as activation in the presence of a co-stimulatory signal, a lymphocyte will initiate an immune system response in order to neutralise the antigen. The lymphocytes that successfully match an antigen will undergo a process known as affinity maturation (see Figure 3). The lymphocytes compete, and the end result is that the lymphocyte that binds to the antigen best (i.e. has the strongest affinity) is selected to undergo a process called somatic hypermutation (Hofmeyr, 1999; Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a,b). Somatic hypermutation is a process whereby the lymphocyte is cloned numerous times and is exposed to a high rate of mutation. In this way, lymphocytes are finally differentiated into memory cells or plasma cells, where memory cells are the ultimate fighters against foreign antigens. This is one of the important learning processes of the immune system. Somatic hypermutation adds cross-reactivity (i.e. the detection of similar antigens) into the immune system and allows a faster secondary response to similar antigens (Hunt & Cooke, 1996; De Castro & Von Zuben, 1999a,b; Dasgupta et al., 2003).

Finally, a vaccination is the purposeful introduction of weakened foreign antigens into the body in order to stimulate the processes described above, thus strengthening the body’s immunity to infectious diseases.

In order to model the immune system, a representation of a detector and an antigen must be established. This is accomplished typically by using a binary string to represent antigens and the receptor patterns. Using a larger alphabet for representation has shown to increase matching complexity (D’haeseleer, 1996; D’haeseleer et al., 1996). The string is of arbitrary length depending on the problem domain and is usually composed of a number of subcomponents. For example, in the problem domain of network intrusion detection, subcomponents of the string include source and destination IP address as well as port number (Hofmeyr, 1999). There have been calls for further research into the generic representation of antigens detectors within AISs in general (Dasgupta et al., 2003) as well as within the specific problem domain of credit card fraud detection (Wightman, 2003). Other applications of AISs include the detection of dangerous situations in near-earth space, the detection of dangerous ecological or medical situations and intelligent network security (Tarakanov et al., 2003).

After establishing a suitable representation, a matching algorithm is constructed to determine the strength of a bind (i.e. the affinity) between a detector and an antigen. Various matching algorithms, such as Hamming distance, edit distance and others, have been examined (Forrest et al., 1997; Harmer et al., 2002). A popular algorithm is the r-contiguous bit matching algorithm as it is immunologically plausible (Percus et al., 1993; Hofmeyr, 1999; Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a). The r-contiguous bit matching algorithm determines the degree of affinity between detector and antigen by finding the longest series of contiguous matching bits between the detector’s receptor and the antigen. For example, a match with eight contiguous matching bits has a higher affinity than one with only three contiguous matching bits. However, all fixed matching algorithms have holes. Holes are blind areas with which it is not possible to detect non-self organisms (D’haeseleer, 1996; D’haeseleer et al., 1996).

Utilising the representation and matching algorithm, the process of negative selection is one of the core algorithms that is modelled within AISs. This is implemented by exposing the detectors to a self-set and destroying any that incorrectly bind with any member of the self-set (e.g. using the r-contiguous bit matching algorithm). This is the primary process that indirectly teaches detectors to detect foreign antigens and is accomplished. In general, important research areas in AISs include improvements in efficiency (Kim & Bentley, 2001b; Harme et al., 2002; Dasgupta et al., 2003), detector generation (D’haeseleer, 1996; D’haeseleer et al., 1996) and the adaptation of more human biological immune system mechanisms into AISs (Forrest et al., 1994; 1997; Hunt & Cooke, 1996; Dasgupta et al., 2003).

Within the specific problem domain of credit card fraud detection, there have been research efforts proposing and/or prototyping an AIS solution (Kim et al., 2003; Wightman, 2003; Tuo et al., 2004). The results highlight a number of deficiencies with the prototype system (Wightman, 2003): (1) by failing to integrate a self-set library, the prototype occasionally failed to have sufficient mature detectors for fraud detection; (2) the representation used (credit card number, expiry date, source IP address) was limited; (3) tests were performed with simulated data; and (4) the system achieved a detection rate of less than 80%, although it successfully managed to keep false positives below 1%.

Our work addresses the deficiencies above. First, a more encompassing representation of a transaction incorporating more elements of credit card transaction data is examined along with an associated matching algorithm. A binary string representation is used, but individual components were not concatenated into a single string as in Wightman (2003). The overall matching occurs based on each individual component satisfying its matching threshold. By using a variable width r-contiguous bit matching algorithm on each component of the representation, the existence of holes can be eliminated. This will also have the benefit of allowing certain components to have a more significant impact on determining whether a transaction is legitimate or anomalous, and the AIS will be able to learn this through its normal adaptation process.

Second, we introduce the idea of vaccination into the AIS. This involves a positive selection component to reinforce system learning and answers the call for further adaptation of human immune system characteristics. A certain number of memory detectors for each anomalous example in the non-self-set will be maintained, and an algorithm is developed to maintain the appropriate ratios of detector types. This mechanism should allow the system to be immunised against particular classes of anomalous transactions through distributed vaccination sets.

Finally, our approach adapts the affinity maturation and somatic hypermutation mechanisms, whereby memory cells evolve genetically via cross-reactivity into a defence mechanism for online credit card fraud detection. This mechanism minimised the loss of information because poor specificity in which memory cells that incorrectly identified a legitimate transaction were destroyed. Instead of immediate destruction, this mechanism allows for the memory cell to evolve more specificity and undergo re-censoring as necessary thus preventing the loss of information.

## CREDIT CARD FRAUD DETECTION

## Current approaches and their weaknesses

There exists an array of approaches to tackle the credit card fraud problem. The following section details the most prominent methods currently in use as well as their limitations and deficiencies (VISA, 2005a,b; MasterCard, 2006).

Credit card numbers conform to the Luhn algorithm, also known as the Modulus 10/Mod 10 algorithm, which effectively acts as a checksum assisting in the detection of simple one digi or transposition errors in data entry. As the algorithm itself is well known, it offers no rea defence against credit card fraud but rather only assists with first step client-side data entry validation.

Manual human verification of transactions by methods, such as physical examination of customer and order details or the calling back of a customer to verify telephone numbers, are sometimes used by smaller businesses. Manual verification is a slow, time-consuming and thus a costly process, and it is only suitable for extremely small transaction volumes.

Card Verification Code 2 (CVC2) or Card Verification Value 2 is used by most card schemes. With CVC2, an additional three-digit code is printed on a tamper signature strip, and this code is required by merchants accepting card-not-present transactions in order for the transaction to be approved. CVC2 helps prevent card-not-present credit card fraud; however, it does no protect against fraud where the credit card has been physically compromised, such as when the card is lost or stolen, or in situations where the details have been copied and the copy includes the CVC2 code.

Address Verification Service is an electronic service used to help prevent card-not-present fraud by verifying a customer’s shipping address details, where the credit card’s billing address are stored in the credit card’s issuing bank. A merchant can enforce a policy that they will not ship to non-verified addresses thus reducing fraud but also resulting in lost sales because not all legitimate customers may wish delivery to their billing address. Also this method does not protect against false application fraud whereby the details on file at the issuing bank are not correct or legitimate.

MasterCard Secure Code and Verified by VISA are programmes that add an additional factor of verification for internet purchases. These programmes operate with a 3-D Secure XML-based protocol that verifies the merchant via digital certificates and the customer either through a password or other mechanism such as a smart card containing digital certificates. These programmes are effective in preventing internet-based card-not-present fraud only and, as they work on a client opt-in basis, they have had limited uptake because of increased infrastructure requirements.

Rules-based systems, such as Alaric Fractals (Alaric, 2005) and Authorize.Net Fraud Detection Suite (Authorize.net, 2006), utilise a series of user- and system-defined rules to categorise transactions as legitimate or anomalous. Rule-based systems are effective and efficient in processing transactions and recognising well-known and understood patterns of fraud; however, they are not naturally adaptive, and they require significant maintenance effort to classify new patterns of anomalous transactions (MacVittie, 2002; Wightman, 2003). Also, in order to achieve satisfactory initial protection, substantial and complex customisation is often required, and this generally requires an expert understanding the nature of frauds that may be perpetrated against an organisation (MacVittie, 2002; Wightman, 2003).

Neural network-based fraud detection systems, such as MasterCard’s RiskFinder (by Fair Isaac) which detects fraud in near real time detections systems are trained with past transac tion data to create profiles of legitimate and anomalous behaviour of customers and merchants. These profiles are then used to test current transactions that are given a rating determining the likelihood of the transaction being anomalous. The complex nature of neural networks causes a number of limitations. As neural networks are based on the complex arrays of connections between synapses in the human brain, the specific mechanisms behind a neural network’s classification of a transaction is not directly examinable, and thus the user is unable to understand the reasoning behind a transaction’s classification (Wightman, 2003). Neural networks can also be overtrained and can subsequently only produce accurate results for members of the training set (Hunt & Cooke, 1996). In practice, neural networks can suffer from high false positive rates resulting in non-reliance upon the system as too many resources are wasted verifying legitimate transactions instead of identifying anomalous transactions.

Hybrid systems such as CyberSource Advanced Fraud Screen (CyberSource Corporation, 1295 Charleston Road, Mountain View, CA, USA), which is part of CyberSource Decision Manager (CyberSource, 2006c) and FairIssac’s Falcon Fraud Manager (FairIsaac, 2006), combine a number of the above approaches such as rule-based systems and neural networks. These hybrid systems offer the best method for credit card fraud detection, but there is still room for improvement. CyberSource claims that its Advanced Fraud Screen reduces fraud to 0.5% of transactions (CyberSource Corporation, 2006b,c), whilst a FairIsaac case study demonstrates an 80% detection rate of anomalous transactions. Because such large numbers of transactions are processed by the industry, 0.5% of all credit card transactions processed worldwide annually would exceed 250 million, a figure that the banking industry would like to see decreased.

Our experiments aimed to improve the detection performance of an AIS for credit card fraud detection. Thus, it is important to highlight how this performance requirement is measured in order to properly understand the context of the design decisions. Before these measures are explained, it is important to understand their underlying component statistics.

A true positive is an anomalous transaction that has been correctly identified as anomalous, whilst a false negative is an anomalous transaction that has been incorrectly identified as legitimate. Conversely, a true negative is a legitimate transaction that has been correctly identified as legitimate, whilst a false positive is a legitimate transaction that has been incorrectly identified as anomalous.

The common statistics used to measure performance in research experiments and also in commercial products are the detection rate and the false positive rate. The detection rate is defined as the number of true positives divided by the number of anomalous transactions tested (true positives + false negatives). The false positive rate is defined as the number of false positives divided by the number of legitimate transactions tested (false positives + true negatives).

The matching algorithm for each field was designed based upon the real transaction metadata description of each data field (see Table 4). The data representation for the unaugmented version, which was used as a basis for comparison during the testing and evaluation, contained a single r-contiguous field match rule (FMR) that was composed from the concatenation of all of the data fields as described in Table 4.

In contrast to this single-field representation, an augmented representation used individual field match rules for each data field of the transaction (see Table 5). With the individual field representation, each field match rule contributed to the detectors overall match value when testing a transaction. The individual match values were then summed together and compared against the detector’s threshold value to determine whether a match between a detector and transaction occurred. The FMR in the augmented version required an exact match rather than an r-contiguous match.

The next augmentation examined is the memory cell somatic hypermutation evolution process. The mutation algorithm was designed based on simple genetic algorithm-based mutations, which were themselves derived from biological mutation. A re-censoring of mutated detectors was incorporated within the algorithm to ensure that the mutations did not cause the detector to violate their self-tolerant characteristic. The mutation process was added to the detector life cycle and a state machine diagram was produced to document a detector’s life cycle.

The last augmentation examined is the vaccination algorithm, depicted as an activity diagram in Figure 5. The detector’s FMRs were generated from the data fields extracted from the fraudulent transaction. This approach resulted in a detector that is very specific in its detection of the fraudulent transaction from which it was derived. In order to increase the generality of the detector to improve its overall usefulness, the detector underwent a mutation phase. Following this, the detector was retested to ensure it still matched the fraudulent transaction. Because the detector was never originally censored, it underwent a negative selection process to ensure that would be self-tolerant.

The AISCCFD prototype has simplistic measures in place to manage the population of memory and mutated detectors. The maximum number of memory and mutated detectors as well as the number of mutated detectors generated per new memory detector was controlled by customisable initialisation parameters. If the maximum number of mutated detectors is reached, a mutated detector is selected and destroyed. This selection process finds the memory detector with the lowest decay value and has the longest time since last activation, thus effectively removing the least useful mutated detector.

There were a number of design issues that this resolves including designing the process by which memory detectors were created from the anomalous set of transactions. Possible methods included generating them in a similar fashion as to normal mature detectors via an exhaustive random search or by creating the memory detector algorithmically from the anomalous transaction. Other design issues that were resolved include the process by which the memory cell population is managed, including maintaining a certain level of memory detectors per anomalous transaction in the stored non-self-set in order to maintain a minimal level of protection. Also, the ratio of memory detectors to regular mature detectors was balanced, based on a specified initialisation parameter. Another design issue was handling the maintenance of the self and non-self sets; this research implemented a manual process, as incorrectly adding an anomalous transaction into the self-set would create a significant hole in detection. Transactions were added to the self-set only upon manual co-stimulation of a transaction as legitimate and were only added to the non-self set via regular manual co-stimulation to gradually introduce immunity into these transactions in much the same way as medical immunisations work.

Following the design of the new augmentations, the remainder of the basic elements of the system was based largely upon the previous work of Hofmeyr (Hofmeyr, 1999; Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a,b) and Wightman (2003). The basic components were designed last to prevent unnecessary redesign, because it was expected that the design of the augmentations would most likely have a significant impact on the structure and operation of the basic components. However, throughout the process of the augmentation design, the impact of changes on the basic system was identified and evaluated.

Because sets of legitimate and anomalous transactions as well as detectors are stored within a database, a database schema was designed. The initial design of the database schema was based upon the obtained real credit card transaction data; however, the initial design evolved and required modifications as a result of the newly designed augmentations.

The aim of the developed system was to act as a proof of concept prototype, and it was not intended for deployment in a production environment. The implementation phase utilised the UML design documents that were created in the previous phase of the research – as a blueprint for the prototype. The prototype’s implementation was coded in Java in order to leverage the existing work by Hofmeyr and colleagues (Hofmeyr, 1999; Hofmeyr & Forrest, 1999a,b) in the Lightweight Intrusion Detection System (LISYS) AIS implementation as well as

Wightman’s (2003) AIS for credit card fraud detection, both of which were object-oriented and implemented in Java. A Java implementation also offered the benefit that the prototype would be platform-independent.

The development of the prototype utilised various open source tools including the Eclipse Integrated Development Environment (Eclipse Foundation, Inc., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada), which provided numerous tools and assistance for Java development. The Hibernate library (Eclipse Foundation, Inc., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada) was used to manage database connectivity. This allowed for easy integration of the prototype with several database-backend systems including MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle and SQLServer. The open source MySQL Community Server was used as the database-backend system for this initial prototype. Concurrent Versions System was used to maintain version control of the source code.

The prototype was designed and implemented in modular fashion so that the system could be executed with or without any combination of the augmentations and hence assisted in the evaluation of the prototype.

## EVALUATION OF THE AISCCFD SYSTEM

The aim of the AISCCFD system is to have a high anomalous transaction detection rate and a low false positive rate. These two components formed the main performance statistic and were used as the basis for evaluation in determining whether a significant improvement in system performance had been obtained through the implementation of the augmentations.

The use of real data to test and evaluate the system allows for a stronger validity claim to be made in regards to the obtained results. In order to evaluate its performance, the developed system was tested experimentally by running a number of computer lab simulations using the real credit card transaction data. The system was run with and without all combinations of the augmentations, and the subsequent results analysed to determine whether the augmentations improved the system’s performance. In order to facilitate the smooth running of these tests, a test platform was developed that provided automated executions of tests without the need for manual input. In effect, the test platform simulated the interaction that a user would make with the system during the input of transactions and co-stimulation of the system. In addition, the test platform recorded performance statistics automatically and saved them to the database for subsequent analysis. The use of the testing platform allowed for more tests to be run during the limited available time as well as allowing test reruns to be carried out if necessary.

The prototype has a large number of configurable parameters that control its operation. As a result of the limited available time, only minimal optimisation was carried out in selecting the configuration parameters. Further optimisation of the parameters was left as a task for future research.

After all tests were run, the results were analysed to determine the effectiveness of each augmentation in improving the overall system performance. Because there has been no previous research that has tested an AIS for credit card fraud detection with real life credit card transaction data, no benchmark for such a system exists. However, the best results obtained are compared against the reported performance statistics of other approaches including FairIsaac’s Falcon and Cybersource’s Advanced Fraud Screen. The results give an approxi mate indication of how the AISCCFD approach performs in comparison to alternative approaches.

Table 6 provides the summary statistics for the anomalous transactions of each test run, where anomalies are grouped according to potential fraud type: lost card fraud, stolen card fraud, skimmed fraud (i.e. the theft of credit card information used in an otherwise legitimate transaction, typically as an ‘inside job’ by a dishonest employee of a legitimate merchan [Wikipedia, 2010]) and mail/phone fraud. For each type of anomaly, the detection rate is calculated, defined as the number of anomalous transactions of that type correctly detected as anomalous, divided by the total number of that type of anomalous transaction and expressed as a percentage.

Detection rate – defined as the number of anomalous transactions successfully identified as anomalous, divided by the total number of anomalous transactions tested, expressed as a percentage.

False positive rate – defined as the number of legitimate transactions incorrectly identified as anomalous, divided by the total number of legitimate transactions tested, expressed as a percentage.

• False positive ratio – defined as the ratio of the number of legitimate transactions incorrectly identified as anomalous to the number of anomalous transactions correctly identified as anomalous.

Initial analysis of the test results show that an AIS approach to credit card fraud detection may be a viable solution with the fully augmented AISCCFD system, achieving a detection rate of 71.3% on real transaction data. A comparison of the fully augmented system (test run 1) vs. the unaugmented basic system (test run 8) shows that in combination the augmentations designed and implemented during this research have significantly improved detection performance. However, this increase in detection performance has come at the cost of an increased false positive rate.

Comparison of the results of the test runs, which had used the improved representation and matching algorithm augmentation over the corresponding basic unaugmented representation, showed that this augmentation significantly increases the detection rate of the AISCCFD system.

The tests results strongly support the effectiveness of the vaccination algorithm augmenta tion in improving detection performance. All test runs that utilised the vaccination algorithm augmentation had a higher detection rate than the corresponding test run that did not utilise the vaccination algorithm augmentation.

The evidence for the memory detection somatic hypermutation evolution process augmentation was not as significant. Test runs that utilised this augmentation did not always have an improved detection rate over the corresponding test that did not utilise the augmentation. Also, the use of the evolution augmentation always resulted in an increased false positive rate.

## CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

This paper highlighted the need for innovative solutions to the problem of security management for eBusiness. The paper examined the specific problem of credit card fraud management. An AIS approach to online credit card fraud detection justifies further research on the approach. However, more extensive studies are required with various options of AIS to identify the most appropriate models for tackling various types of security management problems.

If the three mechanisms (new transaction representation and variable width r-contiguous bi matching algorithm, vaccination process and memory cell evolution process) significantl improve the performance of the AIS’s implementation, it is possible that these mechanisms could be applied to AIS applications in other related problem domains, such as health insurance fraud management and network intrusion, and may result in significant detection performance improvements.

There are many avenues for future research that would enhance the understanding of AISs and their applications to general problem domains. Some key areas that require future research are as follows.

A biological immune system is inherently distributed. Future research into the distribution o the components of an AIS will assist in reducing system execution times to manageable levels and may increase performance rates. The AIS for credit card fraud detection discussed in this paper could be distributed on several levels. First, the database components could be distributed to a separate cluster of servers, and the transaction processing and detector generation subsystems could also be distributed. It is possible that the detector generation space could be separated such that different members of the detection generation cluster would be responsible for generating and maintaining mutually exclusive detectors sets. For example, a first server could be responsible for handling all credit cards starting with the digit one, whilst a second server could be responsible for handling all credit cards starting with the digit two, and so on. This could also further be separated on a merchant level, for example server one could handle all credit cards starting with the digit one only for all merchants with IDs between 1 and 1000.

Whilst the internals of a single detector can be directly examined in an AIS, there may exist more intricate patterns within a set of detectors. Future research could examine the extraction of rules or patterns, as a data mining method, from these detector sets that have been evolved using past transactional data. These extracted rules could then be utilised in a more transaction processing efficient rule-based system for real-time credit card fraud detection.

This research only explored very simple and naïve individual field match rules as a proof of concept. Future research could examine more sophisticated field match rules. For example, the spatial displacement between merchant locations could be used, the merchant category codes grouping merchants in particular categories could be used and the components of a credit card number (card scheme, issuer number and account number) could be separated ou further as individual fields and matched on a more specific level.

The current negative selection and detector generation algorithm has an exponential time complexity based on the size of the self-set. Future research could examine improving the efficiency of this algorithm as well as the new algorithms designed and implemented within this research in order to improve overall efficiency. Further research into the detector generation procedure to enhance detector diversity would assist with improving detection performance. Improvements could also be researched with regard to the management of self and non-self sets. For example, five dissimilar legitimate transactions are more effective at censoring detectors than five similar legitimate transactions. The process by which transactions are added to the self-set could be improved thus reducing the overall size of the self-set and improving the speed of execution of the system. As such the co-stimulation process could be improved thus only adding the necessary transactions to the self/non-self sets as necessary in order to improve the system’s censoring.

The mutation procedure is simplistic and may have limited effectiveness. Research into more sophisticated mutation and crossover mechanisms in genetic algorithms could result in an improvement in performance. The vaccination procedure was also naïve in the method it generated vaccinated detectors. Whilst it proved useful in improving system performance, future research could investigate other methods of generating vaccinated memory detectors. Also, research is required into a procedure to generate ‘vaccination sets’, which could be used to distribute immunity among separate AISs in a similar fashion to biological immunisations. These could then be distributed in a similar fashion as antivirus signature updates.

Lastly, research is required into adapting the response mechanisms present in the immune system into an autonomous AIS. This would enable the AIS for credit card fraud detection to be integrated into transaction processing infrastructure in order to prevent fraud during the approval cycle of credit card transaction processing.
