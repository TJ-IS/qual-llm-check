# Software Patents and Firm Value: A Real Options Perspective on the Role of Innovation Orientation and Environmental Uncertainty

Abstract. Although software patents have been growing steadily since 1996, when the restrictions on the patentability of software were eliminated, their value and impacts on the firm’s profits remain unclear and ambiguous. Drawing on the real options theory and the literature on exploration and exploitation, we develop a novel theoretical framework to assess the value of software patents. Moreover, we examine the impact of contextual factors related to the nature of innovation underlying firms’ patent portfolios (exploitative versus explorative) and the environmental uncertainty (competitiveness and dynamism) on the value of software patents. Specifically, we examine the interaction effect of a firm’s software patent stock and its innovation orientation on firm value in markets exhibiting different levels of environmental uncertainty. Based on a large-panel data set consisting of 602 U.S. firms, our results indicate that a software patent portfolio having higher levels of explorative orientation is associated with a higher firm value (as measured by Tobin’s q) in environments exhibiting low dynamism and high competitiveness. By contrast, a software patent portfolio with higher levels of exploitative orientation is associated with a higher firm value in environments with high dynamism and low competitiveness. We discuss the implications for research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

Software innovations have been important strategic assets that can provide firms with a competitive advantage (Shneiderman 2007). To protect valuable software innovations and prevent other firms from using them, firms rely on patents, which are one of the strongest forms of firms’ intellectual property (Teece 1998). Until the mid-1990s, software innovations were not patentable. However, a few landmark decisions of the U.S. Federal Circuit Court in the 1990s and the release of new guidelines from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) in 1996 eliminated earlier restrictions on the patentability of software (Sterne and Bugaisky 2004). Consequently, software patents involving logic or algorithm for processing data grew steadily from the mid-1990s to 2005. In 2005 alone, more than 15,000 software patents were granted, and its proportion exceeded 12% of total patents (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 2008). Recent high-profile patent lawsuits (e.g., Apple versus Samsung), as well as strategic deals to acquire patents (e.g., Google acquiring Motorola Mobility for \$12.5 billion and Microsoft spending \$1.1 billion to license patents from AOL), highlight the importance of software patents in the current business world (Duhigg and Lohr 2012, Ovide and Letzing 2012).

A large body of research has examined the value of patents, in general, to a focal firm (Connolly and Hirschey 1988, Hall et al. 2005) and found that a firm’s patent portfolio is positively associated with the firm’s performance and market value (Ernst 2001, Hall et al. 2005). However, there has been an ongoing debate about the value of software patents, especially among practitioners who argue that software patents do not have a significant impact on a firm’s profits because such technological inventions are often fuzzy and ambiguous. Therefore, many practitioners, including venture capitalists, have been skeptical about the value of investing in software patents (Graham and Mowery 2006, Fitzgerald 2007).

The increasing importance of software patents and the debate on their value has led to numerous call for research on the impact of software patents on firm performance and competitive advantage (e.g.,

Mykytyn et al. 2002, Kazuyuki 2009). However, to date, research on the topic remains limited (e.g., Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Arora et al. 2013). Although existing studies such as Hall and MacGarvie (2010) provide important insights regarding the overall impact of software patents on firm value, they do not provide theoretical explanations on the value of software patents, and more important, they do not examine contextual factors that can affect the value of software patents. As a result, we do not know under which conditions software patents are more (or less) valuable and why; therefore, little guidance can be offered to practitioners on how they can increase the value of their software patents.

In this study, we employ a real options lens to develop a research model that extends prior studies by theorizing and empirically examining the factors that may explain the variation in the market valuation of software patents. We argue that the value of software patents will depend on the innovation orientation underlying the software patents and that this relationship will vary across market environments that exhibit different levels of environmental uncertainty. The real options literature suggests that the value of a real option mainly depends on the expected value of potential returns and the variance of potential returns (Fichman 2004). Conceptualizing software patent as a real option suggests that the value of a patent depends on the expected value of returns to a patent and the variance of its returns. The argument of the seminal work of March (1991) parallels that of the real options. March (1991) suggests that value of innovation efforts depends on the nature of the innovation itself (i.e., whether it results from exploration or exploitation efforts) and the nature of the environment as these factors affect both the expected value of returns and the variance associated with returns.

Drawing on March’s arguments and those of the real options, we examine the innovation orientation (i.e., exploration versus exploitation) of a firm’s patent portfolio as an internal factor that can affect the value of software patents in different market environments that vary in terms of environmental uncertainty (an external factor). Specifically, we argue that the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio (i.e., the percentage of patents in a focal firm’s patent portfolio that are based on explorative versus exploitative innovation) affects the expected value and variance of the potential returns to the firm’s software patents, thereby affecting the real option value of the software patents. However, March’s (1991) theory suggests that the value of software patents with a different innovation orientation is likely to depend on the uncertainty of the environment in which the firm operates. Hence, we argue that the expected value as well as the variance of the returns to a firm’s software patents would depend on the firm’s innovation orientation and that the nature and strength of this relationship would vary depending on the level of uncertainty in the firm’s market environment—conceptualized in terms of different levels of dynamism and competitiveness (Dess and Beard 1984, Randolph and Dess 1984, Keats and Hitt 1988).

To summarize, we argue that the moderating effect of innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock varies across market environments having different levels of uncertainty. Therefore, we examine how the interplay between the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio and software patents stock affects the firm’s market value in different environmental uncertainty conditions (i.e., in terms of different levels of dynamism and competitiveness).

Using a large-panel data set consisting of 602 U.S. firms holding software patents, we find that the impact of software patents on firm value is positive and significant. Furthermore, our results indicate that a firm’s market value is influenced by the interaction between the firm’s innovation orientation and its software patent stock, but this interaction effect differs across market environments exhibiting different levels of environmental uncertainties (i.e., low versus high competitiveness and low versus high dynamism). Specifically, we find that firms with a software patent portfolio having higher levels of explorative innovation orientation achieve higher firm value (as measured by Tobin’s q) in environments exhibiting high competitiveness and low dynamism. By contrast, firms with a software patent portfolio exhibiting lower levels of explorative (i.e., higher levels of exploitative) innovation orientation achieve higher firm value in environments with low competitiveness and high dynamism.

Our paper makes several important contributions to research and practice. First, whereas recent studies have examined the market performance of software patents in general (e.g., Hall and MacGarvie 2010), we develop a novel theoretical framework based on the real options theory and the literature on exploration and exploitation, which suggests that the value of software patents varies depending on the firm’s innovation orientation and that the interaction between software patents and the firm’s innovation orientation differs across market conditions with different levels of environmental uncertainties. Thus, our paper contributes not only to the debate about the value of software patents but also to the broader patent value literature by providing a framework that can offer rich insights into the conditions under which patents, in general, can contribute more (or less) to a firm’s market value. Second, our paper contributes to prior studies assessing the real option value of patents (Chi and Levitas 2007, Oriani and Sobrero 2008) that have mostly focused on the role of patents in increasing the value of real options, which consequently affects firm value. Our paper extends these prior studies by examining how innovation orientation impacts the expected value as well as the variance of the option value of patents in market environments exhibiting different levels of environmental uncertainty. By doing so, we shed new light on the conditions under which the real option value of patents can be higher or lower. Third, our additional analyses extend the literature that has examined the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty (i.e., competitiveness and dynamism) separately (Jansen et al. 2006, Xue et al. 2012, Tian and Xu 2015) by categorizing environmental uncertainty in terms of different levels of competitiveness as well as dynamism and examining the role of different levels of the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty in the interaction between innovation orientation (exploration versus exploitation) and software patent stock. Our paper also contributes to practice. Our findings can help firms investing in software patents to assess the value of their software patents. Moreover, our results suggest that such firms should tailor their software innovation approach (exploitation or exploration) according to the levels of the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty (competitiveness and dynamism) they face in order to increase the returns from their software patents.

## 2. Related Literature

## 2.1. Value of Software Patents

Given the large investments in building a patent portfolio and the uncertainty regarding the ability to benefit from patented innovations, prior research has examined the value of patents to a focal firm (Connolly and Hirschey 1988, Hall et al. 2005). To the extent that a firm’s patent portfolio influences the firm’s expected future profits, the value of the patent stock should be reflected in the firm’s observed market value. On the basis of this notion, researchers have examined the impact of patents on firm performance using measures such as Tobin’s q. For the most part, these studies have found that a firm’s patent portfolio is positively associated with the firm’s performance and market value (Ernst 2001, Hall et al. 2005).

Software patents, which are among the most recent categories of patents and have been growing steadily since 1996, when the restrictions on the patentability of software were eliminated, are of particular interest because of their unique features. Unlike patents for mechanical devices, chemicals, drugs, etc., which are tied to tangible objects, software patents cover broad classes of interactions abstractly (Bessen 2013, Venkatesan 2015). For instance, a given software patent can cover multiple computers programs designed to solve dramatically different problems (Bessen 2013).

The abstract nature of software patents and the fuzziness of their boundaries have recently led to a larger number of litigations (Vo 2012, Bessen 2013, Venkatesan 2015).

Because of these unique features and the significant growth of software patents in recent years, there has been a call for research to examine their impact on firm performance (e.g., Mykytyn et al. 2002, Kazuyuk 2009). However, relatively few empirical studies have been conducted to examine the value of software patents. Hall and MacGarvie (2010) found that firms with software patents have higher market values on average, compared with firms without software patents. Furthermore, the study found that the impact of software patents on firm value was similar to that of other patents prior to 1995 but became stronger than that of other patents after changes in the patentability of software innovations around 1995.

We extend prior studies on patents in general and software patents in particular by employing a real options perspective to not only assess the value of software patent but also theorize and empirically validate the impact of contextual factors related to the nature of underlying innovation and the environmental uncertainty on the value of software patents. Furthermore, by developing a theoretical model to explicate the role of innovation orientation (an internal factor) in influencing the value of software patents in different market environments characterized by different levels of environmental uncertainty (an external factor), we also extend the broader patent literature that has focused on the main effect of patent portfolios on firm value (e.g., Ernst 2001, Hall et al. 2005).

## 2.2. Patents as a Real Option

The real options approach is an effective valuation mechanism for investments that tend to exhibit high uncertainty and irreversibility (Trigeorgis 1993a). The real options theory, which originated from the financial options theory that is widely used for the valuation of financial securities, extends financial option pricing concepts and tools to real (nonfinancial/physical) assets (Trigeorgis 1993b, Kogut and Kulatilaka 1994, Benaroch and Kauffman 1999, Fichman 2004). A rea option creates the right, but not the obligation, to take action (e.g., deferring, expanding, contracting, or abandoning the underlying asset) at a defined cost, called the exercise price. Similar to a financial option, a real option is an option to do something but is not a requirement to act. The main difference is that, instead of a financial instrument (i.e., a stock, bond, or some other underlying security), real options are related to a capital investment/budgeting project. Similar to the financial options, real options capture the flexibility of management to address uncertainties relating to projects such that if a project is successful, the firm reaps significant returns; however, if the outcome is unsatisfactory, then the upper limit of the loss is the investment cost of initiating the project.

Consistent with the real options theory, the outcome from commercializing a patented innovation is uncertain as a result of uncertainty in market demand and the future adoption of a new product based on the underlying innovation (Gonzalez 2006). Furthermore, in line with the requirements of the real options theory, patents involve extensive sunk cost investments and significant outlays, which are (at least partially) irreversible, for developing and commercializing new products based on patented technology (Bloom and Reenen 2002). Therefore, given that a patent allows the patent owner to choose between exclusively commercializing the patented invention at any time during the patent term and forgoing commercialization altogether (Cotropia 2009), it can be considered a real option.

The real options lens suggests that patent stock should lead to valuable real options by providing the firm with an exclusive right to develop new innovations (Bloom and Reenen 2002). Enabled by a patent’s legal protection, a firm can wait and watch before making extensive, mostly irreversible, sunk-cost investments in further research and development, training, and expensive marketing and advertising to promote their new products based on patented technology. This flexibility to “wait and see” generates valuable real options when the market conditions are uncertain and the value of the underlying asset is uncertain at the time of the option’s purchase (Bloom and Reenen 2002, Cotropia 2009). This is because the option effectively truncates the left-hand tail of a performance distribution (i.e., unfavorable outcomes), thereby giving a firm access to a greater range of potential outcomes on the upside while containing exposure on the downside (Cotropia 2009). In this study, we employ a real options lens to theorize and argue how innovation orientation would interact with software patent stock in influencing firm value and how the nature and strength of this interaction effect would differ across market environments exhibiting different levels of environmental uncertainty.

## 2.3. Assessing the Real Option Value of Patents

Given the real options characteristics of patents, some prior studies have used the real options theory as the theoretical lens for studying the value of patents (e.g., Chi and Levitas 2007, Oriani and Sobrero 2008). Most of these studies have argued that because of the uncertainty associated with the returns from the patented innovation, patents positively impact the value of the real option, and these studies empirically found that patents positively affect the market value of the firm. There have been a few studies that have examined how the patents may vary in terms of the magnitude and variance of the returns and how the uncertainty in the environment would affect the real option value of a patent portfolio (Chi and Levitas 2007, Oriani and Sobrero 2008).

We extend these studies in a couple of important ways. First, whereas these studies have focused solely on the role of environmental uncertainty, our study draws from the real options theory and the literature on exploration and exploitation to examine how the role of innovation orientation (an internal factor) changes across market environments that differ in terms of environmental uncertainty (an external factor), thereby providing richer insights into the factors affecting the real option value of patents. Second, whereas previous studies have mainly investigated the moderating effect of environmental uncertainty on the relationship between patents and firm value, our focus here is on examining how the interaction between the innovation orientation and software patent stock varies across market environments exhibiting different levels of environmental uncertainty.

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses

We employ the real options theory to examine the value of software patents. The theory has been suggested as an appropriate mechanism to evaluate the value of emerging, uncertain technological investments such as those manifested in software patents (Kogut and Kulatilaka 1994, Fichman 2004).

We conceptualize software patents as real options because it allows us to identify and focus on those factors that may affect the underlying value and uncertainty of a firm’s patent portfolio, which, in turn, influence the firm’s market performance.<sup>1</sup> Furthermore, we draw on the literature on exploration and exploitation and employ a real options lens to identify key factors that may influence the market value of software patents. Specifically, we theorize how the uncertainty inherent in the innovation itself (i.e., innovation orientation) influences the extent to which software patents contribute to firm value and how this relationship would vary depending on different levels of environmental uncertainty. ## 3.1. Software Patent Stock and Firm Value

The software patent stock generated by a firm is the result of its intensive investments in research and development (R&D) activities over time. Although proponents of software patents argue that software patents are valuable, some practitioners remain skeptical about the value of investing in software patents and downplay the role of patents in the software industry (Graham and Mowery 2006).

To the extent that software innovations are valuable, and patents can help protect this value, an increase in software patent stock should lead to higher firm value. In addition, the actual commercial value of software patents is highly uncertain; therefore, as explained in detail in the previous section, the real options lens suggests that software patent stock should lead to valuable real options. The value of these real options accessible to a firm, in turn, will be reflected in the firm’s market value. Therefore, we posit the following.

## 3.2. Interplay Between Software Patent Stock and Innovation Orientation Across Different Levels of Environmental Uncertainty

3.2.1. Innovation Orientation. We first examine the role of innovation orientation that can influence the real option value of the innovation by influencing the value of software patents in terms of the magnitude and uncertainty of the returns. A patent is based on an innovation that can be characterized in terms of the nature of learning involved in developing the innovation.<sup>2</sup> March (1991) suggests that innovation can be an outcome of learning that primarily exploits existing knowledge or explores new knowledge. Furthermore, his seminal work highlighting the trade-off between exploitationbased and exploration-based innovation strategy suggests that the outcomes of explorative and exploitative innovations may differ in terms of the magnitude and uncertainty of the returns (March 1991, Levinthal and March 1993). Therefore, our research model includes the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio, which we define as the extent to which the innovations in the firm’s patent portfolio are anchored in new knowledge (i.e., explorative innovation) or existing knowledge used in the firm’s previous innovations (i.e., exploitative innovation).

It is important to examine the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio because researchers argue that an exploitative innovation improves the existing components and builds on existing technological trajectory, whereas an explorative innovation constitutes a shift toward a different technological trajectory (March 1991, Benner and Tushman 2002, Lavie et al. 2010). Therefore, explorative innovation is likely to be radical and when successful, it can be paradigm breaking, positively rewarding, and longlasting. The expected value of the potential returns will be higher for explorative innovations, as these innovations are likely to lead to longer-term performance gains as a result of the creation of new knowledge, compared with the exploitative innovations that provide short-term performance gains because of their focus on efficiency (March 1991, Auh and Menguc 2005). At the same time, because of the high uncertainty involved in the implementation of explorative innovations, the variance of potential returns is also higher for explorative innovation, as these gains are more uncertain and volatile compared with exploitative innovation (Levinthal and March 1993).<sup>3</sup>

The arguments above suggest that, in general, the real option value of explorative innovation may be greater than that of exploitative innovation. However, March (1991) highlights the role of environment in which a firm operates and suggests that environmental uncertainty is one of the most important factors affecting relative gains from exploration versus exploitation (March 1991). Given the critical role of uncertainty in determining the relative returns from exploration and exploitation-based innovations (Levinthal and March 1993), the relative advantage of explorative innovation strategy vis-a-vis exploitative innovation \` strategy is expected to vary across different levels of environmental uncertainty.

3.2.2. Environmental Uncertainty. Environmental uncertainty has been characterized in terms of dynamism and competitiveness (Dess and Beard 1984, Randolph and Dess 1984, Keats and Hitt 1988). Dynamism refers to the environmental instability that makes it difficult to predict changes and affects the volatility that a business unit faces (Child 1972, Dess and Beard 1984). Typically, the volatility of industry sales and income is used to proxy dynamism (Castrogiovanni 2002, Xue et al. 2012). Competitiveness refers to the extent to which a firm’s external environment is characterized by intense competition (Matusik and Hill 1998). Given that a more concentrated industry is likely to have a less competitive environment, industry concentration has been used as an inverse proxy for competitiveness (Keats and Hitt 1988).

3.2.3. Environmental Uncertainty and Innovation Orientation. We elaborate on the relative advantage of explorative versus exploitative orientation across different levels of each dimension of environmental uncertainty to develop hypotheses on how the moderating effect of innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock varies depending on the levels of competitiveness and dynamism.

Competitiveness and Innovation Orientation. <sub>In gen-</sub> eral, high competitiveness leads to the erosion of profits, and a firm can protect and grow its market share and profits by differentiating itself from other firms by making heavy investments in explorative innovation (Rowley et al. 2000). In such a competitive environment, a firm that engages in radical organizational adaptations through intensified exploration strategies is more likely to achieve competitive advantage (Lewin et al. 1999) and higher expected returns. To the extent that patents are effective in preventing copyright infringement of valuable innovation, which can enable a firm to differentiate itself from its competitors in a highly competitive industry, the market value of explorative innovation will be higher than that of exploitative innovation. In other words, the expected payoff from explorative innovation that may lead to radical, paradigm-breaking new products or services is likely to be higher than exploitative innovation in a more competitive environment.

Furthermore, in a more competitive environment, it is more difficult to predict competitors’ actions, which increases the uncertainty (i.e., variance) associated with returns from software patents (Folta 1998, Folta et al. 2006). Given that explorative innovation is inherently riskier than exploitative innovation, an increase in the variance of returns as a result of competitiveness will be greater in the former than in the latter. Taken together, the real options created by explorative innovation can take on greater value than exploitative innovation in more competitive industry environments (Adner and Levinthal 2004, Verdu et al. 2012) as a result of the greater variance and higher expected payoffs.

Alternatively, in market environments exhibiting low competitiveness, the advantage of software patents with exploration orientation vis-a-vis exploitative ori-\` entation is likely to diminish. Specifically, in a less competitive environment, the benefits of explorative innovation are likely to be marginal, and continuous improvement of existing products, services, and organizational processes becomes sufficient for withstanding competition (Levinthal and March 1993). Furthermore, in a less competitive environment, the difference in the variance of returns between explorative and exploit ative innovation will be reduced because competitors actions are easier to predict.

To summarize, the extant studies on innovation orientation consistently points to the superior value of exploration in competitive environment (e.g., Levinthal and March 1993, Lewin et al. 1999, Rowley et al. 2000) Furthermore, the real options perspective suggests that in market environments exhibiting high competitiveness, software patent stock with greater explorative orientation will lead to greater firm value compared with that with greater exploitative orientation. However, in market environments exhibiting low competitiveness, the relative advantage of explorative orientation vis-a-vis exploitative orientation will di-\` minish. Hence, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2A. The moderating effect of explorative innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock will be stronger in the high competitiveness environment than in the low competitiveness environment.

Dynamism and Innovation Orientation. <sub>High environ-</sub> mental dynamism, which may result from changes in technologies, variations in customer preferences, and fluctuations in the product demand or supply of materials (Jansen et al. 2006), makes current products and services obsolete. Therefore, researchers suggest that a firm should create new products and services for meeting the fast-changing needs of customers by engaging in explorative innovation (Zahra 1996, Jansen et al. 2006). For example, Rowley et al. (2000) suggest that firms operating in highly unstable environments should focus on explorative innovation as a result of high uncertainty regarding future directions and a high likelihood of environmental disturbances. The primary argument in these studies is that the existence of a high level of environmental instability increases the rate of radical innovation required to survive, and therefore, firms need to invest more in exploration (Larrañeta et al. 2014). Hence, we expect that in market environments exhibiting high dynamism, software patent stock with greater explorative orientation will lead to higher firm value.

By contrast, in market environments exhibiting low dynamism, firms may be better off focusing on exploiting internal resources as well as reliable external resources because environmental disturbances are stable and adaptation requirements are minimal (Lin et al. 2007). By focusing on exploitation, firms have better chances of survival in stable environments (Hannan and Freeman 1984).

To summarize, the real options perspective suggests that, similar to competitiveness, environmental dynamism can amplify the moderating effect of the explorative innovation orientation on the market value of software patents because both the expected value and the variance of the returns from explorative innovation will be greater in more dynamic environments. As a result, in market environments exhibiting high dynamism, software patent stock with greater explorative orientation will lead to greater firm value, compared with that with greater exploitative orientation. However, in market environments exhibiting low dynamism, the relative advantage of explorative orientation vis-a-vis exploitative orientation will di- \` minish. Hence, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2B. The moderating effect of explorative innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock will be stronger in the high dynamism environment than in the low dynamism environment.

## 4. Data and Empirical Model

The arguments presented above suggest that we need to test an empirical model that examines the interaction effect between software patents stock and innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio across different levels of the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty. We first describe our data and variables, and then we explain the estimation procedure.

## 4.1. Data and Variables

Our empirical analysis uses secondary data from two sources. The first is the patent data obtained from the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) constructed by Hall et al. (2001). This data set covers detailed information on all patents granted by the USPTO between 1976 and 2006. It includes, among other items, annual information on patent assignee names (mainly, the firm), the number of patents, the number of citations received by each patent, the technological class of the patent, and the year that the patent application was filed and granted.

Second, we obtained firm-level financial data from the Compustat database, which provides data of all public firms in the United States. Following the method suggested in Hall et al. (2001), we matched the NBER data with the financial data from Compustat (see also Hall et al. 2005, Hall and MacGarvie 2010, and Liu and Wong 2011). Because the USPTO published new guidelines for software patentability on March 29, 1996, and our analysis requires patent citation information, we chose our sample period to be 1998–2006. We recorded patents by their filing year rather than their granted year, as there may be arbitrarily long lags between the application year and granted year (see Benner and Tushman 2002).

4.1.1. Software Patents. We used patent technology classes to categorize software patents. Specifically, we identified technology classes related to software patents and categorized all of the patents within these technology classes as software patents. First, we identified all of the U.S. patent classes in which 20 software firms<sup>5</sup> patented and categorized patents falling within these classes as software patents, following Hall and MacGarvie (2010). We ruled out those patent classes where the share of the top 20 software firms was under 20%. Furthermore, we also included any additional technology classes that had been classified as software patents in prior research (Graham and Mowery 2003, Hall 2009, Bessen 2011). Finally, we reexamined patent classes for which the consistency among patent classes suggested from prior studies, as well as our own classification, is low. Three coders, who are experts in information technology, independently validated and reclassified these patent classes after reading the definitions of these classes in detail. The interrater reliability for the coding of software patents was 0.94, suggesting a high level of agreement. The resulting categorization of software patents consists of 22 technology classes related to software innovation (see Table A in the online appendix).

In line with prior research (Hall et al. 2005, Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Liu and Wong 2011), we constructed the patent stock measure in year t for firm i using a single depreciation rate (ρ) for the patent value, which is assumed to be 20% in prior research. We also calculated the R&D stock in year t for firm i using the same depreciation rate.<sup>6</sup> To control for the size of R&D investments, we scaled the patent stock by the R&D stock following prior studies (Griliches 1981, Hall et al. 2005, Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Liu and Wong 2011). Another reason we use the ratio of patent stock to R&D stock is that once R&D is observed, the market presumably knows how to price the expected value of the innovative stream that will result from it, including the expected number of patents that will come further down the line (Griliches 1981). Thus, the additional informational value of patents, once R&D has already been factored in, must be captured by the number of patents per dollar of R&D. In sum, the ratio of software patent stock to R&D stock indicates the software patent yield of R&D. If the yield of R&D in terms of software patents is higher than average, it may indicate that the R&D project succeeded beyond expectations—conversely so if the patent yield is low.<sup>7</sup>

Given that more than 90% of software patents are concentrated in the information technology (IT) industry and other industries such as finance industry have small proportion of software patents, we focus on the IT industry to minimize firm-level heterogeneity. On the basis of the 2002 four-digit North

American Industry Classification System (NAICS) code created by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, the IT industry consists of 13 subindustries. Our initial matched panel data set contains 18,534 firm–year observations covering 3,259 unique firms, of which 1,384 firms have positive (nonzero) patent stock (see Table A2 in the online appendix for descriptive statistics). As shown in Table 1, firms in the sample are in a wide range of sectors including computer and peripheral equipment manufacturing, software publishing, and computer systems design and related services. Using the patent stock measure in our observation 5windows (1998–2006), our final panel data set contains 2,321 firm-year observations and 602 unique firms after excluding firms with no patent stock.

4.1.2. Innovation Orientation. We measure the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio as the extent to which a firm’s patenting efforts are anchored in new knowledge (i.e., exploration). Following Benner and Tushman (2002), we coded the prior patents cited by a patent as existing firm knowledge if they were either repeat citations (citing patents that the firm had previously cited) or selfcitations (citing the firm’s own previous patents). Subtracting the number of repeat or self-citations from the total number of citations to prior patents yields the number of new citations (a proxy for new knowledge). We then calculated the degree of exploration (i.e., innovation orientation) for each patent by dividing the number of new<sup>8</sup> citations by the total number of citations to prior patents as a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1 (0 indicates pure exploitation, whereas 1 indicates pure exploration).

To measure the innovation orientation of a firm’s patent portfolio in year t, we calculated the average of the degree of exploration of all the patents that the firm filed in year t.<sup>9</sup> Although a firm’s patents in year t may vary in terms of the degree of exploration, given that such variations are relatively small in our data set, we expect the average of patents’ explorative level in a given year to capture the general innovation orientation of a firm’s patenting within that year. This continuous measure of innovation orientation (with exploitation and exploration as the two ends of a continuum) echoes prior definitions used in the literature (March 1991, Sørensen and Stuart 2000, Gupta et al. 2006).

4.1.3. Environmental Uncertainty. We characterized environmental uncertainty in terms of competitiveness and dynamism, based on the extant literature (Dess and Beard 1984; Keats and Hitt 1988; Xue et al. 2011, 2012; Tian and Xu 2015). To capture competitiveness, we employ two measures of industry concentration: the four-firm concentration ratio (CR4) and the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI). We calculated CR4 and HHI for each NAICS four-digit industry and each year and used the weighted average of the two to measure the concentration. Note that we use the label “concentration” for the variable to avoid confusion. The lower an industry’s concentration, the less competitive the industry is (Han and Mithas 2013, Mithas et al. 2013).

Dynamism was measured as the volatility in industry sales and the volatility in industry operating income (Keats and Hitt 1988). We measured the volatility of industry sales using a two-step procedure. First, the natural logarithm of the total sales of four-digit NAICS industries was regressed against an index variable of years, over a period of the previous five years with rolling windows. We expect the standard error of the regression coefficient to capture the unpredictability or volatility of the sales growth rate. Then, we took the antilog of the standard error of the regression coefficient as the measure of sales volatility The same approach was used to derive the volatility of industry operating income. Because the two measures of each construct were highly correlated and are interchangeable in reflecting environmental uncertainty, we converted the multi-item measures of each dimension involving uncertainty into single-item measures (see Table A3 in the online appendix).

4.1.4. Firm Value and Control Variables. Tobin’s q, which captures the financial performance of a firm, is our main dependent variable. Given that it offers the advantage of capturing both short-term performance and long-term prospects based on the market value, it has been widely used in prior information systems (IS) literature (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Kohli et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013). Moreover, Tobin’s q covers the market-based measure of a firm’s tangible and intangible value because it is forward looking, risk adjusted, and less susceptible to changes in accounting practices. Thus, to effectively capture the market value of software patents, which is a potential intangible asset of firms and is not recorded in financial statements, we use Tobin’s q to measure the market performance.

We included several variables to control for firm, year, and industry effects. To control for the growth opportunities available to a firm, we included R&D intensity, which was computed as the total amount of R&D expenditures in a given year, divided by total sales for each firm, and the industry Tobin’s q (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Xue et al. 2012, Bardhan et al 2013). We also used the total number of employees as a control for firm size and included advertising intensity and market share (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Xue et al. 2012). Industries correspond to the 2002 NAICS four-digit (NAICS4) code level. Year dummies for the sample years from 1998 to 2006 were included in our model to control for year-specific effects. The coefficient on the interaction term, Software $\ P a t e n t s _ { i , t } \times$ Innovation $O r i e n t a t i o n _ { i , t } ,$ in each subsample captures the relative advantage of explorative orientation in terms of influencing the value of software patent stock under different levels of environmental uncertainty. A positive coefficient implies that software patent stock with explorative orientation is more valuable compared with that with exploitative orientation, whereas a negative coefficient implies the opposite.

To account for unobserved heterogeneity across industries in our data, we consider fixed- and randomeffects models. We chose a fixed-effects model based on a Hausman test that rejected the null hypothesis that errors are not correlated with the explanatory variables (Prob $> \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ ). Because serial correlation of errors over time may cause the standard errors of the estimated regression coefficient to be smaller compared with their actual value and inflates the model fit, we use the Wooldridge test to check for serial correlation of the errors. The results indicate the presence of first-order autocorrelation (AR(1)) in our panel data set $( F = 8 6 . 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ .<sup>13</sup> Hence, we employ a fixed-effects estimation model with correction for AR(1) errors.

There is a concern for potential endogeneity regarding our patent stock variable. For example, a firm’s decision to patent its software innovations may be influenced by its current and future performance (expectations) that is reflected in the firm value. Similarly, our innovation orientation variable is also potentially endogenous because a firm may decide on its innovation strategy with an expectation about the impact on firm performance. In this case, the firm’s patent stock and innovation orientation variables would be endogenous, and the estimation results would be biased.<sup>14</sup>

To address this potential endogeneity, we use the system generalized method of moments (GMM)

estimation (Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998). Previous studies suggest that the system GMM estimation can not only address endogeneity but also eliminate bias from unobserved heterogeneity (Aral et al. 2012, Tambe and Hitt 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013).<sup>15</sup> Online Appendix B provides a detailed description of the system GMM estimation. Although the best way to address the endogeneity would be to identify appropriate instruments, finding such instruments that generate exogenous variation in our focal variables (i.e., software patent stock and innovation orientation) is challenging. Taking advantage of our long panel and a large number of firms in our data set, we utilize internal instruments within the data generated by dynamic panel data models. As the system GMM estimation uses lagged levels and differences of independent variables as internal instruments (Blundell and Bond 1998), we use the system GMM estimation with differences in variables, employing the lag terms of our potentially endogenous variables—software patent stock and innovation orientation—and all the differences of other exogenous variables, except for the industry Tobin’s q and year dummies, as our instruments.

Because too many instruments can lead to a finite sample bias and overidentification issue, we follow Roodman (2009) and use the second lag and onward of endogenous variables for the system GMM with differences estimation. In each model, the maximum number of instruments used is 176, which does not exceed the number of firms (about 190) in the panel. Then, we checked the validity of the moment conditions required by system GMM estimation using the Hansen test, and we could not reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are orthogonal to the error term (Blundell and Bond 1998, Roodman 2009).

In sum, we believe that our use of fixed-effects and the system GMM models allows us to effectively address unobserved heterogeneity across firms and the endogeneity of our focal variables. Our use of a system GMM estimation procedure for panel data is consistent with recent studies in the innovation and IT productivity literature (Bardhan et al. 2013, Kim et al. 2016, Pang et al. 2016).

## 5.1. Software Patents and Firm Value

We first estimate Equation (1) including our main independent variables and control variables. As shown in model (1) of Tables 4 and 5, software patents are positively associated with firm value $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 4 , p < 0 . 0 5$ in Table $\dot { 4 } ; \beta = 0 . 2 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 5$ in Table 5). Specifically, our results of estimating the FE model indicate that, on average, a 1% increase in software patent stock is associated with a 0.16% increase in firm value (see Table 4). This elasticity of software patent stock is greater, by about 0.6%, than the elasticity reported by Hall and MacGarvie (2010) based on software patent data from 1995 to 2002. Given that our sample period (1998–2006) is comparatively recent, it is likely that firms have become better at leveraging the change in the regulations related to software patentability which occurred in 1996. Therefore, it is natural to have higher elasticity of software patents as we include more recent data in the analysis. The positive association between a firm’s software patent stock and the firm’s market value provides support for our Hypothesis 1.

## 5.3. Possible Theoretical Explanations

In Hypothesis 2B, we conjectured that the relative advantage of explorative orientation vis-a-vis exploitative\` orientation will be greater in high dynamism environments, based on the mainstream arguments made by prior studies, including Jansen et al. (2006), Rowley et al. (2000), and Larrañeta et al. (2014). However, our result suggests the opposite: the software (SW) patent stock with explorative orientation is more valuable in low dynamism environments. This unexpected, seemingly counterintuitive finding can be explained based on nascent literature that focuses on the market dynamism and suggests that in situations of high environmental dynamism, the value of exploration might diminish, whereas the value of exploitation might increase (e.g., Posen and Levinthal 2012, Stieglitz et al. 2016). Note that Posen and Levinthal (2012) argue that exploration is more beneficial than exploitation only up to a certain level of environmental dynamism, after which exploitation outperforms exploration, suggesting a curvilinear relationship. More recently, Stieglitz et al. (2016), who extend Posen and Levinthal (2012), find that rapid directional changes in the environment (e.g., fast reversals of trends) substantially undermine the value of exploration. Overall, their results indicate that in a high dynamism environment, the bestperforming firms rely more on exploitation than on exploration. Given that IT industry exhibits a higher level of dynamism compared with other industries,<sup>17</sup> our finding that exploitative innovation is more valuable than explorative innovation in high dynamism environments is consistent with the arguments and results o these studies.

In a highly dynamic market environment such as the software-intensive markets that we investigate, rapidly changing consumer preferences and shorter product life cycles may quickly erode the potential rewards for explorative innovation while renewing the importance of exploiting existing knowledge. Because environmental disturbances not only devalue prior knowledge but also degrade new knowledge garnered via exploration, a highly dynamic environment undermines the relative advantage of exploration, making exploitation outperform exploration (Kim and Rhee 2009, Posen and Levinthal 2012).

From the real options perspective, this implies that the expected payoffs from explorative innovation visa-vis exploitative innovation will be smaller in a more\` dynamic environment. Because the variance of payoffs from exploration vis-a-vis exploitation will still\` be greater in a more dynamic environment, the aforementioned alternative logic suggests that the two components of real option value (i.e., expected payoffs and variance of payoffs) in this situation are in the opposite directions. ## 5.4. Considering Both Dimensions of Uncertainty Simultaneously

Although prior literature on the role of environmental uncertainty has examined the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty separately (Jansen et al. 2006, Xue et al. 2012, Tian and Xu 2015), it is important to realize that the environmental uncertainty in the market in which a firm operates is made up of the two dimensions. To shed further light on the role of envi ronmental uncertainty, we classify market environments into four discrete categories based on the levels of dynamism and competitiveness (i.e., ME1–ME4) and examine how the moderating effect of innovation orientation on the relationship between software patents stock and firm value varies across market environments that differ in terms of the levels of the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty.<sup>18</sup> Specifically, the category labeled as ME1 represents the market that exhibits low levels of competitiveness as well as dynamism. The category labeled as ME3 represents the market that exhibits high levels of competitiveness as well as dynamism. The other two categories, ME2 and ME4, exhibit high uncertainty on one dimension (i.e., dynamism and competitiveness, respectively) but low uncertainty on the other dimension (i.e., competitiveness and dynamism, respectively).

The results based on sample-split analysis (see models (1)–(4) of Table 6) suggest that the moderating effect of explorative innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock is the weakest (model (2) of Table 6: $\beta = - 0 . 3 9 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) in a market environment exhibiting low competitiveness and high dynamism (i.e., ME2) and strongest (model (4) of Table 6: $\beta = 0 . 2 0 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ in a market environment exhibiting high competitiveness and low dynamism (i.e., ME4). This is consistent with the aforementioned alternative logic that favors exploitation in high dynamism environment. Furthermore, our results show that the relative advantage of explorative innovation in market environments with low dynamism and low competitiveness (i.e., ME1) and high dynamism and high competitiveness (i.e., ME3) is moderate vis-a-vis\` the other two market environments (i.e., ME2 and ME4). In other words, ME2 and ME4 exhibit the extreme effects of exploration (exploitation) innovation orientation (models (2) and (4) of Table 6: β = <sup>−</sup>0.392 and $\beta = 0 . 2 0 6 ,$ , respectively), whereas the interaction effect between software patent stock and innovation orientation in ME1 and ME3 (see models (1) and (3) of Table 6) lies within these two extremes $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 5 , p >$ 0.10 and $\beta = 0 . 0 2 6 , p > 0 . 1 0 ,$ , respectively).

5.5.3. Alternative Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation for Addressing Endogeneity of Innovation Orientation. As mentioned above, our innovation orientation variable is also potentially endogenous. Although we already treated innovation orientation as an endogenous variable in the system GMM estimation, we employed the control function (CF) approach with a continuous treatment developed by Garen (1984) as an additional way to address this endogeneity. Garen (1984) adapts Heckman’s twostep procedure to the case where the treatment variable is continuous and, in particular, has a conditionally homoscedastic normal distribution with linear conditional expectation. Following this approach, innovation orientation was treated as a continuous choice variable such as the first stage of two-stage least squares (2SLS) and one-year lagged value of innovation orientation along with contemporaneous values of all other independent variables was used as instruments. We then use the errors from this model as an additional regressor in the main model. As shown in Table 10, the results based on the CF approach are qualitatively similar to the main results. ## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Drawing on the real options theory and the literature on exploration and exploitation, we argue that the value of software patents will depend on the innovation orientation underlying the software patents and that this relationship between software patent stock and innovation orientation will vary depending on the levels of environmental uncertainty. Specifically, our research provides a theoretical framework and empirical evidence regarding how the moderating effect of innovation orientation on the market value of software patent stock varies across market environments characterized by different levels of competitiveness and dynamism.

We find that a software patents portfolio having higher levels of explorative orientation is associated with a higher firm value in market environments exhibiting low dynamism and high competitiveness. By contrast, a software patent portfolio with higher levels of exploitative orientation is associated with a higher firm value in market environments exhibiting high dynamism and low competitiveness. We next discuss the contributions and implications of our study.

Second, and more important, our study contributes to the literature assessing the real option value of patents. Unlike prior research that has mainly focused on the relationship between a patent and its real option value (e.g., Chi and Levitas 2007, Oriani and Sobrero 2008), our study examines the factors that influence the expected option value of patents as well as the variance of their option value. Given that the patents are essentially an outcome of a firm’s innovation activities, we draw on the seminal work of March (1991), who considers the trade-off between exploration and exploitation to identify factors that will influence the value of real options by affecting the potential returns and risk associated with the underlying innovation. Specifically, the theorization of March (1991) suggests that innovation strategy would affect underlying value as well as uncertainty associated with the innovation; therefore, according to the real options theory, innovation orientation is expected to influence the value of a patent. However, March (1991) also highlights the role of uncertainty in the environment as an external factor that can affect relative gains from exploration versus exploitation. Therefore, we include both innovation orientation and environment uncertainty in our framework to explain the variation in the real option value of patents, and we empirically test the framework in the context of software patents. By incorporating these factors and theorizing how these factors can interact in influencing the real option value of patents, our study enriches the literature on the real option value of patents.

Third, our study complements and extends the literature which has examined environmental uncertainty (e.g., Jansen et al. 2006, Xue et al. 2011, Tian and Xu 2015) by theorizing and empirically validating how the moderating effect of innovation orientation varies across market environments exhibiting different levels of the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty. Specifically, classifying market environments based on the levels (high and low) of competitiveness and dynamism, we find that the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty favor different innovation strategies. In particular, regarding the role of dynamism, our findings support the emerg ing school of thought, which argues that exploitation strategy has a relative advantage in a more dynamic environment (e.g., Posen and Levinthal 2012, Stieglitz et al. 2016). Our finding that software patents with explorative (exploitative) innovation orientation are more valuable to firms in more (less) competitive and less (more) dynamic environments not only underscores the importance of considering the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty together, but also provides more nuanced and richer insights into how environmental uncertainty influences the market value of software patents.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

The findings of this study provide several implications for practice. First, our findings can help managers assess the value of software patents in terms of their contribution to firm value. Although some practitioners are still skeptical about the value of software patents, we provide empirical evidence that a firm’s software patents, as a form of property rights on intangible assets, do contribute to firm performance. In particular, firms in knowledge-intensive industries often face challenges concerning the valuation of their intangible assets, including software patents. Our findings can help mitigate these challenges by helping them quantify the market value of software patents. Furthermore, our results provide an explanation as to why many firms engaged in software-based innovation have adopted a system of effective redress for the software patents in their compensation and performance management policy and have filed lawsuits against patent infringers (e.g., Nielsen 2013).

Second, our study findings provide managers with actionable guidance on how they should adjust their innovation orientation with respect to their software innovations according to the levels of competitiveness and dynamism of their market environment in order to increase the financial returns to software patents. Specifically, our results suggest that managers should focus their efforts on explorative innovations in more competitive and less dynamic market environments, and on exploitative innovations in less competitive and more dynamic market environments, in order to achieve a higher market valuation of the resulting software patents. Furthermore, our analysis of differences across sectors in the relative advantage of certain innovation orientation can inform managers about the preferable innovation strategy for each major sector. For example, our results suggest that software publishers should focus their efforts on explorative innovation to increase the financial returns to software patents, whereas exploitative innovation strategy is more beneficial for manufacturers of computers and related equipment.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our study has some limitations and offers directions for future research. First, although our theoretical framework based on the real options theory can be applied to patents in general, we tested our theoretical predictions in the context of software patents. Further research is needed to validate the empirical results in non-software patent contexts. Furthermore, given that our results are based on software patents data from firms in the IT industry, the findings may not be generalizable to the software patents owned by firms in other industries. Although our analyses cover almost all the software patents (because more than 90% of software patents have been filed by firms in IT industry), future research should examine whether and how the value of software patents varies in non-IT industries

Second, researchers have raised concerns about the commercial value of patents, as many patents in a firm’s portfolio are actually never used (e.g., Palomeras 2003). Although we tried to address this issue by using an alternative measure accounting for the quality of patents (i.e., citation-weighted patent stock), it is difficult to prove the commercial value of each patent over time. To alleviate these concerns, future research can examine the transactions of patents as commodities via mergers and acquisitions, or strategic alliances, as potential channels through which firms can derive value from patents. On a related topic, future research should also examine the role of firms’ internal capabilities (in terms of R&D, marketing, and operations) that are critical to the commercialization of firm’s software patents (e.g., Chung et al. 2017). Furthermore, it would be interesting to examine whether and how software inventors’ turnover affects market performance and innovation outcomes (Palomeras and Melero 2010). Together, these can ease the concerns of the skeptics who question the commercial value of patents and answer how firms can derive value from patents.

Third, we measured innovation orientation based on firms’ patenting activities. Although a diverse range of operationalization has emerged for exploration and exploitation concepts using objective proxies, such as the depth and breadth of technology search activity (Katila and Ahuja 2002) or the annual relative amount of explorative orientation of the company through content analysis (Uotila et al. 2009), we sought to quantify the relative innovation orientation from patenting activities using a novel methodology. This measure of innovation orientation may be biased if a significant proportion of firms in our sample were systematically not patenting certain types of innovation. Although we have no reason to suspect that this is the case with our sample, future research should employ other measures of innovation orientation to validate our results. Nevertheless, the use of patents’ citation histories to operationalize March’s (1991) exploration and exploitation concepts that we proposed in this study appears to have great promise for empirical research, because it offers a relatively easy way to capture these concepts objectively.

Fourth, we acknowledge that dichotomization of continuous variables has some limitations such as loss of power, although previous studies justified and employed this approach under proper conditions (e.g., Dewan et al. 2007, DeCoster et al. 2009, Iacobucci et al. 2015). In this study, whereas we believe that the current sample-split analysis is justified and is the best option to analyze the interactions between innovation orientation and environmental uncertainty variables across different levels of uncertainty, future research should replicate this study by using continuous measures while addressing the issue of multicollinearity. Finally, the causality issue remains similar to other empirical studies using secondary data. Although we have done our best to address reverse causality and endogeneity by using appropriate estimation procedures, further studies are needed to provide stronger support for causality.

To conclude, our findings suggest that software patents do matter for firm value and that the value of software patents critically depends on contextual factors. Our paper highlights the importance of identifying key factors—both internal (i.e., innovation orientation) and external (i.e., environmental uncertainty)—that may affect the value of software patents and formulating an appropriate innovation strategy for software patents that can lead to the greatest firm value in different market environments. Although our study answers some questions about the business value of software innovation, it also raises a number of additional new questions. As such, we hope that our paper will generate more research on this important topic.
