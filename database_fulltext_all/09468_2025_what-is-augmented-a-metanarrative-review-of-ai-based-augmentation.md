# What Is Augmented? A Metanarrative Review of AI-Based Augmentation

Inès Baer,<sup>1</sup> Lauren Waardenburg,<sup>2</sup> Marleen Huysman <sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, The Netherlands, i.b.baer@vu.nl <sup>2</sup> ESSEC Business School, France, waardenburg@essec.edu <sup>3</sup>Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, The Netherlands, m.h.huysman@vu.nl

## Abstract

The widespread implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) in organizations has given rise to an increasing focus on augmentation in the academic and public discourse. While the verb “to augment,” defined as a process to make something greater or more numerous, is often used in IS research, it lacks a discussion of what the targets of such a process could be. In other words: What is augmented? Our paper builds on the literature of five research disciplines in which augmentation is a particularly prevalent topic—i.e., computer science, information systems, economics, management, and philosophy. Accordingly, we identified four metanarratives that represent four distinct targets of AIbased augmentation—the body, cognition, work, and performance—that build on unique human-AI configurations and bring to the fore specific augmentation tensions. Using these insights, we formulate avenues for further IS research on AI-based augmentation.

Keywords: Augmentation, Artificial Intelligence, Human-AI Interaction, Metanarrative Review Method

Guy Paré was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on September 24, 2022, and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

The implementation and use of artificial intelligence (AI)—commonly defined as technological systems capable of performing functions associated with human intelligence, such as learning, speech, planning, and problem-solving (Russell & Norvig, 2016)—is experiencing renewed interest following the launch of generative AI tools such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT and DALL-E. Such increased interest typically comes with discussions on the implications of these technologies on humans, including a new wave of automation anxiety (Mokyr et al., 2015). In these discussions, the buzzword of augmentation as benefiting humans is often used to contrast automation with statements such as “AI is augmenting humans, not replacing them” (e.g., De Cremer & Kasparov, 2021; Lakhani, 2023). In this more optimistic augmentation perspective, AI is claimed to be complementary to humans, and augmentation is presented as a human-centric path that society and organizations should follow (e.g., Davenport & Kirby, 2016; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).

Interestingly, despite the popular use of the term augmentation in IS literature and despite it even being called “the key managerial issue of our time” (Berente et al., 2021, p. 1440), existing research has failed to provide an agreed upon definition for this term and dig deeper into the nuances behind it. In broad terms, augmentation is often described as interaction or collaboration between humans and AI (e.g., Berente et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2022; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). However, such descriptions emphasize the generic mechanism through which augmentation can be achieved (i.e., by productively combining humans

To address our research question, we conducted a metanarrative review of the augmentation literature. As this method is particularly appropriate when a topic has been differently conceptualized, theorized, and empirically studied in various communities of research, our analysis includes the main disciplines that have engaged theoretically with the topic of AI-based augmentation: computer science, management, information systems, philosophy, and economics. Going beyond the boundaries of these traditional disciplines, we identify different metanarratives—i.e., “overarching story lines” (Greenhalgh et al., 2009, p. 732) with a shared set of concepts, theories, and preferred methods—on augmentation.

In particular, we distill four metanarratives that represent four distinct targets of AI-based augmentation: the body, cognition, work, and performance. In each metanarrative, we unpack the underlying theoretical concepts that provide a more precise answer to what is augmented with AI, such as skills, productivity, or decision-making. We also point out how each metanarrative differently conceptualizes humans and AI, through which we describe the human-AI configurations underlying each augmentation target. In addition, we discuss so-called “counternarratives” that highlight tensions within and between the augmentation targets. Based on our insights regarding the augmentation targets, the augmentation configurations, and the augmentation tensions, we formulate avenues for further IS research on the interrelationships between augmentation targets, the sociotechnical dynamics of augmentation targets, and ways to systematically capture and measure them.

## 2 Augmentation from a Mechanistic Point of View

According to the Merriam-Webster Dictionary, the verb “to augment” is defined as a process of making something greater, more numerous, larger, or more intense. In the context of information systems, the term augmentation was coined in 1962 by computer scientist Douglas Engelbart concerning the emergence of the first universal computers. Since then, the term has been used by scholars in a variety of fields and has more recently gained renewed popularity with the increased attention to AI (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).

In broad terms, augmentation is often described as collaboration and interaction between humans and AI. For example: “Augmentation means that humans collaborate closely with machines to perform a task” (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021, p. 193); “In augmented intelligence methods, machines are taking actions and making decisions in collaboration with humans i.e., there is collaborative human-machine decision making” (Hassani et al., 2020, p. 143); “Human-ML augmentation, where humans and technology work together to perform organizational tasks jointly” (Teodorescu et al., 2021, p. 2).

These descriptions focus on the complementarity between the strengths of humans and those of AI, claiming that effectively combining both would lead to a whole that is greater than the sum of its parts (Davenport & Kirby, 2016; Dellermann et al., 2019a; Teodorescu et al., 2021; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018). This links to the common imagery around AI-based augmentation. For example, when searching Google images for “artificial intelligence augmentation,” the resulting images tend to depict a hybrid human-AI brain, connected human-robotic hands, or humans and AI interacting with each other often represented by bidirectional arrows (see Figure 1 for an example).

Augmentation is thus often considered from a mechanistic point of view, as a combination of humans and AI into a productive mechanism. This includes notions of human-AI configurations (e.g., Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020), the assemblage of humans and AI into an integrated unit (Rai et al., 2019), humans and AI being considered as components or parts of a hybrid system (Dellermann et al., 2019a, 2019b), or human-AI structures that need to be assembled, crafted, stabilized, and maintained (Baptista et al., 2020).

According to the Oxford English Dictionary and the Cambridge Dictionary, a “mechanism” can be defined in two different ways, either as a set of parts that work together in a system or “machine,” or as a process or method for achieving something. While the literature mostly uses the first meaning of mechanism to define augmentation (as a hybrid human-AI system or machine), the use of the second meaning (as a process with an outcome) is less apparent. If combining humans and AI aims to create a whole that is more than the sum of its parts, we must ask the question: More of what? Reflecting on the dictionary definition of augmentation introduced earlier (i.e., making something greater, more numerous, larger, or more intense), we ask: What is this something? Or, in other words: What is augmented with AI?

In sum, we conclude that the current literature refers to augmentation as a generic mechanism of human-AI configuration but disregards the variety of underlying targets behind this mechanism and its theoretical implications.

## 3 Literature Review Methodology

To address our research question, we conducted an indepth, integrative review of multiple disciplines (Cronin & George, 2023) that discuss AI-based augmentation. Integrative reviews aim to synthesize knowledge across different research traditions to identify higher-order themes and propose a new research program. The specific method we used is the metanarrative review, which aims to make sense of a heterogeneous body of literature based on different worldviews and epistemological positions (Greenhalgh et al., 2005, 2009; Paré et al., 2015). This method is particularly appropriate when a topic, construct, or phenomenon (in our case, AI-based augmentation) has been differently conceptualized, theorized, and empirically studied in different research communities. It thus helped us cut through the complexity and ambiguity of the augmentation literature and unpack common metanarratives, where each metanarrative corresponds to a distinct storyline with a unique set of concepts, theories, terminology, and methods.

Our methodological framework draws inspiration from foundational works on the metanarrative review method (Greenhalgh et al., 2005; Greenhalgh et al., 2009), but it does not intend to directly replicate this method in a different context. As all literature review methods represent “ideal profiles” (Paré et al., 2015), our review diverges in certain aspects from the procedural guidelines established for the metanarrative method (Greenhalgh et al., 2005). While our activities align largely with the prescribed procedure for the metanarrative method, we deviate in two regards (italicized in our descriptions in Table 1). First, in the planning phase, our research was not triggered by or conducted in relation to a sponsoring body or external steering group. While this helped us to maintain the much-appreciated academic freedom, it also implied that we would not determine our potential outputs with a funder or client. Therefore, we did not have regular review meetings with an external party. However, we did schedule multiple feedback sessions with academic peers by, for example, presenting our work at academic conferences and research seminars and submitting our work to the academic review process of this journal.

Second, we fundamentally deviate from the prescribed procedure in the synthesis phase. While an initial version of our manuscript included narratives on AIbased augmentation grouped according to discipline (i.e., economics, management, information systems, computer science, and philosophy), we realized that grouping the narratives according to discipline was not generative. The problem we encountered was that the different types of literature were largely divided regarding the target of augmentation that appeared through our coding process and that the core concepts we identified in relation to the augmentation targets crossed the boundaries of the five disciplines we included in our literature review. We therefore decided to depart from discipline-based (or paradigm-based) metanarratives and, instead, focused on target-based metanarratives. To do this, we coded all underlying concepts (see Table 2) and kept track of those that often appeared together, which helped us to identify four over-arching metanarratives: (1) augmenting the body, (2) augmenting cognition, (3) augmenting work, and (4) augmenting performance. Each metanarrative brings together insights from multiple disciplines. For instance, the metanarrative “augmenting work” consists of insights from management scholars and labor economists. Since all disciplines have their own “language,” we took a more active role in generalizing the language used in each metanarrative for the purpose of clarity. To stay as close as possible to the more “traditional,” discipline-based procedure for developing metanarratives, we included a description of the most dominant disciplines in each metanarrative and described the main differences between their respective perspectives on the metanarrative.

Finally, apart from describing a general procedure, Greenhalgh et al. (2005) also established five core principles for conducting a metanarrative review. In Table 3, we outline how our decisions regarding our research design relate to those five core principles. While our research largely aligns with these principles, we deviate from the principle of historicity, which emphasizes the centrality of a historical “plot” in which different metanarratives build upon each other. The nature of our research into the “what” of AI-based augmentation, which turned out to vary widely between disciplines, prevented us from taking such a historical perspective across the metanarratives. While we initially tried to map the sources for the different metanarratives across a timeline, we realized that there is no analytical value to comparing timelines across narratives. We did, however, include the time of publication of each source for constructing each separate metanarrative, which helped us better understand how each storyline grew over time and to include this in the metanarrative descriptions.

## 4 Metanarratives on AI-Based Augmentation

While AI-based augmentation is generally described as a fruitful combination of humans and AI to enhance “something,” the target of that enhancement appears to vary significantly between (and sometimes within) research communities. In this section, we present an integrative account of the four main metanarratives we identified concerning AI-based augmentation: (1) augmenting the body, (2) augmenting cognition, (3) augmenting work, and (4) augmenting performance. In Table 4, we synthesize the key conceptual, theoretical, and methodological differences between the four metanarratives on AI-based augmentation.

## 4.1 Augmenting the Body

The first augmentation metanarrative we discuss concerns the use of technology to augment the human body. This includes augmenting physical functions (e.g., fine motor skills, strength), sensory functions (e.g., visual perception, auditory perception), and brain functions (e.g., memory, attention), with the intent to overcome the biological limitations of individuals and help them reach superior capabilities (Bavalier et al., 2019; Daily et al., 2017; Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019; Raisamo et al., 2019; Xia & Maes, 2013). The use of AI to augment the body can therefore help to “greatly exceed” what is attainable by “unaugmented” humans (Bostrom, 2008; Gladden, 2019) and it can compensate for human impairments (Daily et al., 2017; Raisamo et al., 2019). For example, object recognition technology and large language models can generate descriptions of images for the visually impaired. Studies on augmenting the body emphasize that AI is unique in its ability to go beyond the routine manual domain (e.g., robotic limbs enhancing physical strength) towards entirely new capabilities (e.g., large language models enhancing visual or auditory perception), becoming more and more personalized (Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019).

One of the earliest and most-cited (>3000) studies on augmenting the body through technology is Licklider’s (1960) study on man-computer symbiosis, in which he unpacks the cooperative interaction between humans and electronic computers and brings the “mechanically extended man” to the fore. Following this early work, most research was focused on augmenting the body through hardware such as robotics, prostheses, or implants (Raisamo et al., 2019). While this metanarrative was off to a slow start for the first 30 years, in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when the focus shifted to the algorithms that are embedded in hardware, there was a rise in scholarly interest in what this meant for the human body, including the human brain (e.g., Bostrom, 2003, 2008; Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Skagestad, 1993). The more recent advances in machine learning then led to a surge in publications on AI-based bodily augmentation after 2015 (e.g., Alicea, 2018; Batin et al., 2017; Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019).

The body augmentation narrative can be mostly found in the disciplines of computer science and cognitive science, and philosophy. The discourse in computer science and cognitive science is mainly about more drastic augmentation of the human species and society (“humans 2.0”), including merging between humans and AI. The aim is to achieve continuous technological progress and innovative human-machine combinations that will “think as no human brain has ever thought” (Licklider, 1960, p. 4) and “achieve what was previously unattainable for the individual” (Gerber et al., 2020, p. 291). Philosophers, on the other hand, mainly consider technology as a tool to be used by humans that can serve as an extender of their biological functions, i.e., a brain extender such as a smartphone (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019) or body extender such as robotic arms (Besmer, 2015; Ihde, 1979). In this case, the focus is less on invasive and more on external technologies that are used temporarily so that the augmentation effect is lost when the tool is not used (Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019). In philosophy, the research aims less to strive to achieve augmentation but rather to reflect on or make sense of what this would mean for human identity and society.

Body augmentation can be temporary, using an external device (e.g., smartphone) or a device that is more closely integrated with bodily processes (e.g., augmented reality glasses) or more lasting when the technology is physically integrated into the body, as is the case for implants or prostheses (Besmer, 2015; De Preester, 2011). Consequently, scholars (mainly in the field of cognitive and computer science) discuss how the human brain can merge with AI, either temporarily or lastingly, into a mutually beneficial human-machine symbiosis (Gerber et al., 2020; Grigsby, 2018; Licklider, 1960). To achieve such a symbiotic relationship, a hybrid “brain-computer interface”

needs to be optimized towards a common goal (Gerber et al., 2020), where both sides must learn to understand each other and “seamlessly” or even “intuitively” react to each other “in real time” (Grigsby, 2018; Skinner et al., 2014). This “tightly coupled” human-machine system can be considered as a single unit, with the result being that humans and machines become codependent over time (Gerber et al., 2020; Licklider, 1960). Human-machine symbiosis remains mostly in the realm of the future but current examples are moving in this direction, such as augmented reality devices building on brain-computer interfaces (Putze, 2020; Hurst, 2023). Even if the device is not directly connected to the brain but to eye movements, the information that is thereby revealed on brain activity qualifies as an early-stage brain-computer interface (Miseviciute, n.d.). Finally, scholars contributing to this narrative also reflect on how AI might lastingly modify the human condition and evolve the human species (biologically) or human society (culturally) into “humans 2.0” (Raisamo et al., 2019) or “posthumans” (Bostrom, 2008). This includes visions of immortality, as individual memory and even personality can be transferred to machines (Batin et al., 2017; Kurzweil, 2005).

This metanarrative generally takes a positive stance toward the possibilities for AI to enhance and improve the human body and brain. For example, Bostrom (2003, p. 493) claims that the aims of augmentation through AI are the “radical extension of human health span, eradication of disease, elimination of unnecessary suffering, and augmentation of human intellectual, physical, and emotional capacities.” However, some publications also point towards implications that run contrary to this positive augmentation narrative, thereby initiating what we call a counternarrative. Some have argued that while AI might lead to the augmentation of some abilities, it tends to reduce others at the same time (Ihde, 1979; Kiran, 2015). For example, Sergeeva et al. (2020) argue that surgeons using robotic arms gain fine motor skills and augmented vision but lose the feeling of touch with the patient’s tissue. The result of this can also be that humans become overly dependent on AI, meaning that their bodily abilities in the absence of technology decrease (Lindebaum et al., 2020). Such effects have already occurred in the domains of navigation and medicine (Cabitza et al., 2017; Newell & Marabelli, 2015; Panesar et al., 2020). Another debate occurs on the issue of human agency, free will, and bodily agency, as human actions are increasingly influenced by AI systems. For example, smart wearables collect data to quantify diverse aspects of the human body (including sleep cycles, steps, and body temperature) and already today influence when people move, drink water, or have intercourse (Crawford et al., 2015; Xue, 2019).

## 4.2 Augmenting Cognition

The next metanarrative we identified focuses on augmenting cognition, by building on a productive combination of human cognition and machine cognition. While body augmentation includes the augmentation of brain functions, cognitive augmentation considers the broader cognitive system, expanding beyond the physical boundaries of the brain (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019). In organizations, this cognitive system is distributed between human agents, information artifacts, and their interactions (Asatiani et al., 2019). The literature underlying this metanarrative also commonly talks about “intelligence augmentation” (or IA) as a contrasting ambition to “pure” artificial intelligence (Hassani et al., 2020; Jain et al., 2018; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; Paul et al., 2022; Rouse & Spohrer, 2018; Zhou et al., 2021). Intelligence is described as the ability to accomplish complex goals by applying cognitive processes, such as learning, reasoning, and knowledge application, to decision-making and problem-solving (Dellermann et al., 2019a), which artificial intelligence is said to be able to replicate and even outperform.

Scholars in this metanarrative typically argue that AI uniquely augments cognition because it self-generates knowledge based on learning from data, instead of humans needing to codify their own knowledge explicitly into the technology. While with previous technologies it was mostly the developers that augmented the knowledge encoded in the technology, with AI, end-users are also continuously augmenting the technology (by providing data and feedback), often even without knowing it. The new, more detailed, more rational, or more accurate insights generated by AI (Wilson & Bataller, 2015) can be used to augment organizational decision-making (Shrestha et al., 2021), e.g., hiring decisions (Van den Broek et al., 2021), and problem-solving, such as solving customer inquiries (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). Apart from generating new knowledge, AI can also disseminate knowledge or abilities from the best-performing content creators to the rest of the organization (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023).

Similar to the body metanarrative, studies on augmenting cognition build on a long research tradition, starting in the 1950s (Ashby, 1956). Particularly Engelbart’s (1962) book on augmenting human intellect was highly influential (almost 1700 citations) in shaping the initial discussions. While these earlier studies still focused on the cognition of an individual human, with some similarities to body augmentation, this metanarrative evolved to consider the augmentation of the broader cognitive system. The cognitive extension thesis by Clark and Chalmers (1998) was instrumental, stating that humans outsource cognitive functions to some external artifacts—or “cognitive extenders”—thereby creating a cognitive system that goes beyond the human organism. In IS, the focus then shifted to organizational cognition, i.e., the distributed cognition between several human and artificial agents in an organizational setting (Boland et al., 1994). Research on augmenting cognition took particular flight after 2015, when the emergence of machine learning algorithms brought the relationship between human and artificial cognition more and more to the fore (e.g., Holzinger, 2016; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018).

This narrative resides mainly within information systems and management research but also in computer science. Computer scientists often describe technology as more rational and potentially superior to humans and tend to aim for more technological autonomy and artificial general intelligence (AGI). Augmenting cognition in this discipline is mainly focused on improving the technology or algorithm itself, with humans acting at the service of the technology. Here, a distinction is made between human-in-the-loop computing, where humans augment the AI, and human-out-of-the-loop computing, where AI autonomously augments itself (Holzinger, 2016). For example, Holzinger (2016, p. 119) describes human-in-the-loop computing as “algorithms that can interact with agents and can optimize their learning behavior through these interactions, where the agents can also be human (emphasis added).” Some computer science scholars (e.g., Gorban et al., 2018) take this argument to the extreme, arguing for keeping humans out of the loop as far as possible, with the exception of the (temporary) intervention of AI developers themselves or of the “best experts” (p. 4), given that most humans do not like to engage with AI and that human intervention generally leads to “unavoidable mistakes” (p. 12). This argument emphasizes that the overall intelligence of human society (or of an organization) can be augmented, without augmenting the intelligence of individual humans.

In contrast, the information systems and management communities tend to argue in favor of human superiority and oversight (Teodorescu et al., 2021). They focus their narrative on human-in-the-loop computing and the various active roles and tasks humans must execute to augment AI systems, such as actively training the learning algorithm, sustaining it, supervising and controlling its output, and explaining the results to others (e.g., Teodorescu et al., 2021; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018). Apart from augmenting the algorithm, they focus on how humans can learn from AI and build a “hybrid intelligence,” which defines augmentation as combining the complementary strengths of human and artificial intelligence to achieve superior results while both continuously coevolve, learn from each other, and augment each other’s cognition (Dellermann et al., 2019a; Rai et al., 2019). They also emphasize what organization and business processes are necessary to support this hybrid intelligence (Wilson & Daugherty, 2018). Some IS scholars also take a more sociotechnical perspective, considering what relationships among humans, technology, and their environment may lead to intelligence augmentation (Paul et al., 2022).

At the core of this metanarrative are the learning loops between human and artificial intelligence, where humans learn from AI because it generates new insights and presents them in a distinct way and AI learns from humans via interactions and datasets (Dellermann et al., 2019a; Benbya et al., 2021; Rouse & Spohrer, 2018; Wiethof & Bittner, 2021). This mutual learning is what leads to superior overall intelligence. In addition, some scholars focus in particular on the superior cognition that can come from humans “augmenting the algorithm” (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020), i.e., improving the AI’s learning, performance, and accuracy (Gennatas et al., 2020).

The emerging counternarrative here is that complex, sophisticated machine learning models might not always have better cognition than simpler, codified expert systems (e.g., Ben-David & Frank, 2009). In the case of large language models, AI often “hallucinates” information that does not correspond to any real knowledge (Nah et al., 2023). Here, humans can make choices on the degree of creativity or randomness that they accept from the model (a parameter also referred to as “temperature”). There is also the argument that the more accurate we might make an AI model (e.g., by using a lot of different data or a more sophisticated algorithm), the less interpretable or explainable it might become (Mori & Uchihira, 2019; Rueda et al., 2024) and therefore less usable in practice—known as the accuracy/explainability trade-off. In addition, the focus on augmenting overall cognition might also be detrimental to human cognition, especially the unique human knowledge that is complementary to AI. Scholars have argued that humans start mirroring the thinking processes of AI systems and that even though individual accuracy increases, the thinking diversity and therefore the combined intelligence of the group might actually decrease (Fügener et al., 2021). Finally, AI might not always need humans to learn from but can increasingly augment its own performance by generating synthetic training data that it can learn from (Stafford, 2023).

## 4.3 Augmenting Work

A more recent metanarrative focuses on how AI is augmenting work, with underlying concepts such as employment, tasks, skills, and work satisfaction. In this metanarrative, scholars focus on positive employment outcomes, with the aspiration of AI acting as a catalyst for new work opportunities, producing significantly more opportunities than it destroys (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Davenport & Kirby, 2015). Besides measuring the impacts of AI on overall work opportunities, scholars are also studying the change in the nature and content of work, arguing that some human tasks and skills can be substituted by AI, whereas other tasks and skills will likely be complemented by AI (Agrawal et al., 2019a; Autor, 2015; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017).

This metanarrative builds on insights from labor economists studying the dynamics between human labor and technology (e.g., Goldin & Katz, 1998; Keynes, 1930; Simon, 1965). This community started using the term “labor augmentation” in this context following the more recent advances in machine learning, leading to a particular interest in how this would impact human employment (e.g., Acemoglu & Autor, 2012; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018, 2019). Studies looking at the types of workers that are more likely to be receptive to work augmentation typically found that technological progress has historically augmented the employment of high-skilled (or rather high-educated) workers (e.g., Acemoglu & Autor, 2012) and workers focusing on abstract rather than routine tasks (Autor et al., 2003; Autor, 2015). More recently, scholars have developed specific task models to predict the impact of AI, in particular machine learning and generative language models, finding that the occupations most likely to be affected were quite different from those impacted by previous technologies, moving higher up the skills ladder and into nonroutine domains such as creative work (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Felten et al., 2023). In recent years, there has been a surge in studies analyzing the qualitative changes in the nature of work after introducing AI in organizations, including new human-AI work configurations (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023; Lebovitz et al., 2022).

The disciplines that have mainly contributed to this metanarrative are labor economics and organization research. In labor economics, scholars are mainly interested in the macroeconomic effects of technological progress, i.e., the increase or reduction in the demand for human labor and employment (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018; Autor, 2015; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Felten et al., 2023; Korinek & Stiglitz, 2021). They measure and predict the substitution versus complementarity/augmentation effects of AI and determine the key drivers of these effects (e.g., education level, nature of the task). Studies in this field are based on an economic model where economic production output is created using different kinds of so-called “factors of production,” mostly distinguishing between (human) labor inputs and (technological) capital inputs (Autor & Dorn, 2013; Autor et al., 2003), and where technological progress can be labor-augmenting or capitalaugmenting (Korinek & Stiglitz, 2021). For example, intelligent navigation assistants allow more humans to work as drivers and are therefore considered laboraugmenting, whereas self-driving cars would be capital-augmenting (Korinek & Stiglitz, 2021). Generative AI might also make the creative industries more accessible to lower-skilled workers, who are now able to generate content with the help of AI (De Cremer et al., 2023; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023), thereby addressing the talent shortage that many countries are facing.

Organizational scholars, on the other hand, are more interested in the nuances and qualitative (or hidden) changes in the nature of work and organizations. They study the implications of AI on human work in practice, while work is being performed (e.g., Anthony, 2021; Waardenburg et al., 2022). Most scholars in this field take a less technology-deterministic stance, rather considering the organizational context and how it influences the implications of the technology. In addition, organizational scholars focus on how new work practices and new roles emerge when human workers engage with the technology, to critically judge its output and integrate it into their own decisionmaking, neither blindly accepting nor blindly refusing it (Burton et al., 2020; Lebovitz et al., 2022). For example, generative AI is expected to lead to the emergence of new roles in the organization, such as prompt engineers and content curators (Bouschery et al., 2023; De Cremer et al., 2023).

Scholars taking on the work augmentation narrative typically argue that repetitive tasks can be delegated to AI so that workers can take on more sophisticated and fulfilling tasks, resulting in increasing task variety and sophistication (Davenport & Kirby, 2016; Leyer & Schneider, 2021; Plastino & Purdy, 2018; Raj & Seamans, 2019a). For example, AI-powered chatbots can handle routine customer inquiries, allowing human customer support agents to focus on more complex customer issues that require empathy, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills. Such augmentation has been shown to increase the work satisfaction and retention of workers (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). In this narrative, human workers remain superior to AI and make the final decisions (Leyer & Schneider, 2021; von Krogh, 2018). The discourse is that augmentation allows workers to build on their unique human strengths and expand their expertise by making use of the insights generated by AI but retaining their own professional judgment (Davenport & Kirby, 2016; Lebovitz et al., 2022; Tschang & Almirall, 2021).

While the narrative of work augmentation focuses on the positive work implications of AI, we can also see an increasing counternarrative in recent literature. For example, some scholars argue that there are instances where the overall employment implications are not positive for workers and AI is actually “augmenting automation” (Tschang & Almirall, 2021). While the main argument is that AI complements high-skilled workers and raises skill requirements, increased reliance on AI can also lead to deskilling as it takes away opportunities for human workers to exercise their professional judgment and develop their expertise, which particularly impacts less experienced workers (e.g., Faraj et al., 2018; Mayer et al., 2020). This may make them more complacent and disengaged and diffuse their sense of responsibility (Lindebaum et al., 2020), which can lead to work becoming less meaningful after introducing AI (Smids et al., 2020). For example, in the case of chatbots, when AI systems suggest a personalized script for every conversation and the agents simply read out these scripts, this can lead to a reduction in perceived meaning at work (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). Indeed, research on AI in practice has shown that professionals may lose some of their professional authority and autonomy (Mayer et al., 2020; Strich et al., 2021) and spend more time on microtasks (Parker & Grote, 2020), ghost work (Gray & Suri, 2019; Kellogg et al., 2020; Watson-Manheim & Klein, 2019) or “babysitting robots” (Alekseev, 2020). In addition, AI could impede collaboration and break knowledge ties between human workers (Anthony et al., 2023; Retkowsky et al., 2024).

## 4.4 Augmenting Performance

A final, less established, and more emerging metanarrative focuses on using AI to augment economic value and performance, comparing “algorithm-augmented performance” to performance without using AI (Allen & Choudhury, 2021). Superior performance and value can be achieved by augmenting productivity or augmenting innovation. This metanarrative has emerged only in the last 10 years, following the new promises in the performance augmentation of recent machine learning progress.

Primarily two disciplines have joined this metanarrative: management (in particular, strategy) and economics. While strategy scholars focus especially on the role of (firm-level) operations and business processes for performance augmentation (Johnson et al., 2022), economists are more concerned with measuring largescale augmentation effects on the economy, such as augmented growth, productivity, and patent creation (Raj & Seamans, 2019b; Wang et al., 2021).

AI can augment performance by improving productivity, either by making existing processes more efficient (achieving the same output with less input, in terms of financial resources or time) or more effective (better accuracy, quality, customer experience) (Brynjolfsson et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Ryder, 2017; Sampson, 2020). For example, large language models can help organizations be more productive, allowing them to synthesize information (Bouschery et al., 2023) and generate high-quality content more quickly (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023; Shan & Qiu, 2023). These so-called “productivity effects” are particularly benefiting organizations by increasing customer satisfaction and reducing costs (Agrawal et al., 2017; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). While AI’s main added value lies in its superior efficiency, human judgment is still required to ensure the effectiveness of AI results (Agrawal et al., 2019a).

Apart from productivity effects, AI can benefit business performance by augmenting innovation and generating new revenue streams (Brynjolfsson et al., 2019; Cockburn et al., 2019). This includes the innovation of products (e.g., new drug discovery enabled by AI), services (e.g., prediction services), and business models (e.g., platform models based on smart matching) (e.g., Johnson et al., 2022). As a “general-purpose technology,” AI is expected to enable a wide range of complementary innovations, similar to former technological revolutions such as electricity and personal computers (Goldfarb et al., 2023). In particular, the recent progress in generative AI has sparked an interest in the AI-augmented innovation process, where transformer-based models can generate original, out-ofthe-box ideas and content (Bilgram & Laarmann, 2023; Bouschery et al., 2023; Sison et al., 2023; Vartiainen & Tedre, 2023). In the innovation process, AI is particularly augmenting the exploration phase, i.e., expanding the detection of potential problems to solve and expanding the generation of ideas while leaving humans responsible for exploitation, i.e., supervising the output and selecting the desired, innovative solutions (Bouschery et al., 2023).

There are also beginnings of a counternarrative to the narrative of performance augmentation—in particular, augmented productivity. Some scholars have argued that the productivity effects of AI have led to a “productivity paradox,” which can have different explanations, including measurement difficulties and time lags that typically occur with general-purpose technologies (Brynjolfsson et al., 2019). Another argument is that we are developing “the wrong kind of AI,” with too much focus on augmented productivity and insufficient focus on augmented innovation (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020). In contrast to the narrative that AI is augmenting innovation, there is also an argument that machine learning replaces the diversity occurring in human processes with a more homogeneous process selected based on conformance with historical data, ignoring context and more extreme (out-of-the-ordinary) cases (Balasubramanian et al., 2022). This relates to increased convergence and loss in the diversity of human behavior when using AI systems, which can be highly detrimental to business innovation (Fügener et al., 2021).

## 5 Synthesis

Building on our metanarrative review, we offer insights into what is augmented with AI. We specify four different, what we call, “augmentation targets”: the body, cognition, work, and performance. Existing research typically considers augmentation from a mechanistic perspective, e.g., as a “configuration,” “assemblage,” or “hybrid system” (Dellermann et al., 2019a, 2019b; Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Rai et al., 2019) of humans and AI working together to create “something” that is better than the sum of its parts. By asking what is intended to be augmented, our study emphasizes that AI-based augmentation does not represent one generic concept or mechanism but includes a multitude of distinct targets and underlying mechanisms.

By unpacking the metanarratives representing the four augmentation targets, we show that each target is grounded in a different conceptualization of both humans and AI (see Table 5). In other words, the four augmentation targets build on four distinct human-AI “configurations,” i.e., assembled arrangements or relations between human(s) and AI with a certain division of roles between them (Baptista et al., 2020; Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020). These configurations further refine our insights into how augmentation is achieved and how this varies depending on the augmentation target.

Scholars engaging in the body augmentation metanarrative conceptualize humans as acting bodies with inherently limited physical, sensory, and/or brain functions, improvable with the help of AI. The individual human body is the augmentation target, with AI as either an embedded or external device with superior capabilities. Depending on the level of embeddedness, the combined configuration of the human body and AI can be considered a single unit or “human-AI symbiosis.” In cognitive augmentation, human and artificial intelligence are conceptualized as intelligent agents and decision makers. Here, both are configured as parts of a mutually reinforcing cognitive system, with mutual learning loops, where the final output is augmented cognitive potential, including augmented knowledge, learning, decisionmaking, and problem-solving. While augmenting the body is mainly targeted at the abilities of individual humans (e.g., workers, managers, or customers in an organizational context), augmenting cognition goes beyond the individual toward the practice or organizational level. The work augmentation metanarrative conceptualizes humans as organizational agents with specific tasks and skills, and AI as autonomous organizational agents or tools assisting human workers. Their augmentation configuration is a task model where work tasks are distributed between humans and AI, depending on their complementary skills, and where AI’s progress is influencing the nature and volume of available work tasks, particularly enabling new work opportunities. In performance augmentation, humans and AI are conceptualized as factors of productivity and innovation, whose configuration and respective contribution to economic value depend on performance comparisons between both. To reach augmented productivity, AI mainly targets superior efficiency, while human judgment is required to ensure effectiveness. To augment innovation, AI mainly allows the expansion of the exploration stage, while humans remain central to the exploitation of innovations.

Finally, while the discourse on augmentation tends to have a very optimistic tone, emphasizing the positive implications of AI for the body, cognition, work, and performance, the counternarratives presented in this paper emphasize tensions both within and between the augmentation targets. Within the augmentation targets, the counternarratives show that the commonly discussed tension between automation and augmentation (e.g., Raisch & Krakowski, 2021) takes on different nuances depending on the augmentation target. In body augmentation, the increased autonomy of AI can mean a reduction in the autonomy and agency of humans, as AI systems increasingly influence and dictate bodily behavior. The cognitive augmentation narrative discusses the limitations of autonomous, human-out-of-the-loop AI systems and risks such as hallucinations occurring in more autonomous AI. From the perspective of work augmentation, increasingly autonomous AI tools can reduce opportunities for human workers to apply and develop their skills and negatively impact work satisfaction. In the performance augmentation narrative, the question is raised whether we are developing “the wrong kind of AI” by focusing too much on automation and productivity tools, and too little on innovation.

The counternarratives also point towards tensions occurring between the augmentation targets. They show, for instance, that augmenting an organization’s cognition—e.g., its decision-making—with intelligent technologies can act in tension with augmenting individual bodily abilities of workers as they become overly dependent on AI, with the result that their own abilities in the absence of technology decrease (e.g., Lindebaum et al., 2020). In this case, humans may start mirroring the cognitive processes of artificial intelligence, thereby losing their uniquely human and complementary advantage (e.g., Fügener et al., 2021). Another tension emerges when performance augmentation favors investments in automation technologies rather than technologies augmenting human work practices or creating new work opportunities (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020; Tschang & Almirall, 2021).

Synthesizing the insights from the metanarrative analysis into distinct augmentation targets, configurations, and tensions helps us better understand how to move forward when studying AI-based augmentation. Below, we use these insights to formulate research avenues for IS scholars.

## 6.1 A Relational Perspective on Augmentation Targets

By unpacking the different augmentation targets, our study opens up opportunities to further study how these targets exist in relation to each other. While the counternarratives presented in this paper mainly highlight potential tensions within and between targets, we unpack how the four metanarratives’ underlying premises, values, and worldviews can sometimes lead to conflicts, productive dependencies, or mutually accelerating effects. Using our understanding of the metanarratives, we offer conjectures—preliminary suppositions based on current yet incomplete information available (Grover et al., 2020)—that can guide future, empirical research into deeper analyses of the relations between the augmentation targets and describe how such relations emerge and with what organizational consequences. In addition, we briefly discuss an overarching potential point of tension between economic and ethical goals that emerges through synthesizing the four augmentation targets and their underlying values.

## 6.1.1 Conflicting Premises and Values

Body vs. cognition: The body augmentation metanarrative focuses on surpassing biological limitations through physical or sensory integration of AI. However, the augmenting cognition emphasizes enhancing individual and organizational decisionmaking, knowledge, and problem-solving. As such, while cognitive augmentation tends to stress human oversight, particularly in decision-making, body augmentation leans toward biohybrid configurations that may bypass traditional human agency. This difference can lead to conflicts about the scope of augmentation, i.e., whether AI should temporarily enhance cognitive abilities or more lastingly enhance bodily ones. Moreover, while cognitive augmentation can lead to tools that help humans augment their bodily functions, focusing on augmenting an organization’s overall cognition can also conflict with augmenting individual bodily abilities as human workers become dependent on AI, with the result that in the absence of technology, their own abilities decrease.

Work vs. performance: In the workplace, enhancing performance and productivity could lead to prioritizing efficiency-focused AI systems, which may be at odds with the values central to work augmentation, such as worker autonomy, task variety, and meaningful engagement. Moreover, augmenting work prioritizes maintaining the relevance of human skills, while augmenting performance may lead to favoring investments in automation technologies rather than technologies augmenting human work practices.

## 6.1.2 Productive Dependencies

Cognition depends on the body: Cognitive augmentation, including the augmentation of the knowledge, decision-making, and problem-solving of an organization, builds on a complementary and mutually reinforcing combination of human and artificial intelligence. Cognitive augmentation, therefore, depends on the augmentation of bodily abilities (such as memory, attention, visual, and auditory senses) of human workers within organizations, which are needed, for example, to complement, control, and train AI.

Work depends on cognition and the body: The human-AI work configurations that enable work to be augmented depend on the augmented cognitive processes of knowledge generation, decision-making, and mutual learning between humans and AI. In addition, these augmented work configurations depend on body augmentation, as humans need to increase their own abilities to be able to offer skills that are complementary to AI in the workplace. For example, an augmented human ability, such as managing emotions, can translate to an augmented skill at work, such as resolving difficult customer queries.

Performance depends on work, cognition, and body: Body augmentation can enhance the individual productivity of workers, thereby directly supporting organizational performance goals. For example, surgeons using robotic arms benefit from improved precision, serving both bodily capabilities and broader organizational productivity. Performance gains can also be achieved by enhancing the organization’s cognitive processes, including augmented knowledge, problem-solving, and decision-making. Finally, performance augmentation depends on effective work configurations between humans and AI in order to best combine the efficiency and exploration capabilities of AI, and the effectiveness and exploitation capabilities of humans.

## 6.1.3 Mutually Accelerating Effects

Cognition, work, and performance: When augmenting cognition leads to improved organizational decision-making, this can facilitate optimized work roles and, in turn, enhance performance. This enhanced performance allows workers to engage more in innovative, higher-level tasks, giving them more opportunities to refine the decision-making and problem-solving skills essential for complex tasks. Thus, augmenting cognition can serve as a catalyst for augmenting work and performance by informing strategic decision-making in such a way that it enhances worker productivity and innovation. This cycle illustrates how augmenting cognition, work, and performance can mutually reinforce and strengthen each other.

The body and cognition: In scenarios where AI increasingly integrates into the human body (e.g., wearable brain-computer interfaces), there can be a synergy that not only enhances physical and sensory capabilities but also helps to augment cognition. Such integration can extend a form of “extended cognition,” where physical and mental enhancements mutually reinforce each other. This progression can shift the focus from temporary augmentation tools to more embedded, permanent enhancements.

## 6.1.4 Economic vs. Ethical Goals

The differing values behind each of the four metanarratives reveal a fundamental tension between ethical and economic objectives in AI-based augmentation. For example, augmenting the body or work aims to empower humans, enhancing their capabilities and autonomy while aligning with ethical priorities like human-centrism. However, augmentation focused on performance tends to prioritize cost-efficiency and productivity, which can lead to increased automation and a reduction in human input and agency at work. In addition, when body augmentation is reoriented toward performance optimization, it becomes less about individual empowerment or inclusivity and more about maximizing organizational productivity, leading to a devaluation of the human element. Cognitive augmentation—especially in the form of AI-driven decision-making tools—often puts more emphasis on algorithmic accuracy and objectivity than on critical human engagement and oversight. When cognitive augmentation targets the algorithm rather than the human workforce, this may come at the expense of human agency or the preservation of human skill development. These tensions emphasize the need for responsible AI frameworks that can balance ethical and economic goals, respecting the diverse values associated with the four augmentation targets.

In sum, while each augmentation target has distinct premises, values, and areas of tension due to counternarratives, they also relate to each other in such a way that they are part of a shared conversation that can advance our understanding of the role of AI in society. Continuous negotiations across the different narratives can both challenge and mutually accelerate the broader goals of AI implementation and use, which can potentially lead to richer and more balanced configurations of human-AI collaboration.

## 6.2 Unpacking the Sociotechnical Dynamics of Augmentation Targets

By uncovering the distinct human-AI configurations underlying the augmentation targets (see Table 5), we emphasize the importance of future research accounting for both the social and the technical dynamics, as well as their interactions, when studying AI-based augmentation. More specifically, we argue for a sociotechnical approach to studying augmentation targets.

Taking a sociotechnical approach is particularly important since our analysis of the human-AI configurations shows that all metanarratives so far have maintained a more traditional human-AI dichotomy. Some scholars have emphasized that such a dichotomy is increasingly difficult to uphold because humans are deeply entangled in all of AI’s processes (e.g., Gehman et al., 2024; Glaser et al., 2024). We encourage future research to go beyond the boundaries between humans and AI towards a more integrative perspective that examines how the broader sociotechnical system of, for example, humans, data, routines, decisions, and their interactions is augmented.

Moreover, a sociotechnical approach can also help us better understand how augmentation targets are established in and through practice. For example, future research could examine the “configuration work” (Baptista et al., 2020) that is required to create, stabilize, and improve the distinct human-AI configurations corresponding to the different augmentation targets. Also, IS scholars have recently started to examine the different practices underlying AI development, implementation, and use (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2022; Teodorescu et al., 2021; Van den Broek et al., 2021). Our study points out that insights into what is intended to be augmented can help us better understand such practices and their consequences for both AI and organizations. For example, two studies on augmented decision-making, namely Burton et al. (2020) and Lebovitz et al. (2022), led to significantly different insights when digging deeper into the augmentation target. Both studies define augmented decision-making as the human decisionmaker incorporating AI results into their own judgment, neither blindly refusing nor blindly accepting the AI’s recommendations. However, the focus of Burton et al. (2020) is primarily on augmenting the decisions themselves, which corresponds to augmenting cognition. In contrast, Lebovitz et al.’s (2022) study centers on human expertise as the augmentation target, which corresponds to augmenting work. This difference in focus also leads to different results; whereas Burton et al. (2020) emphasize the need for cognitive alignment between humans and AI to overcome barriers to augmented decision-making, Lebovitz et al. (2022) point out the need for humans to engage in critical judgment in order to enhance their professional expertise. This example not only highlights the importance of clarifying the augmentation target when developing theory on the practices of AI development, implementation, and use; it also emphasizes that not being explicit about the target might lead to ambiguity or unintended consequences when managing AI (Mayer et al., 2020).

To study AI-based augmentation from a sociotechnical perspective, we encourage embedded, ethnographic research to deeply examine how augmentation targets are established over time, how such targets influence further AI development, and with what organizational consequences.

## 6.3 Systematically Capturing and Measuring Augmentation Targets

Finally, our study also encourages future research that captures and measures AI-based augmentation within organizations. Recent IS research has introduced the concept “AI orientation” to identify “a firm’s overall strategic direction and goals associated with introducing and applying AI technology” (Li et al., 2021, p. 1604), meaning that managing AI requires “goal-directed action” (Berente et al., 2021, p. 1435). While, at first, AI orientation seems to come close to our identification of the four augmentation targets, this concept has been operationalized as a one-dimensional measure, as more or less AI orientation adopted by an organization (Li et al., 2021). Based on our review, we argue that such AI orientation and underlying goals are not singular and therefore cannot be considered as taken-for-granted or unquestioned starting points. We encourage future research to broaden the scale of AI orientation by uncovering the various augmentation targets that can be pursued by organizations.

While our review looks at how AI-based augmentation has been conceptualized, we offer an initial outline for these targets. We still face a lack of a comprehensive understanding of the augmentation targets as established in practice, which requires empirical data. We encourage future research to systematically capture the wide range of augmentation targets pursued by organizations when implementing AI. For this, IS scholars could benefit from developing specific measures to capture different augmentation targets. Scholars could for example apply natural language processing techniques to organizational discourse to identify and measure the targets underlying AI-based augmentation. Such novel methods and measures could help identify which augmentation targets are pursued to what degree by organizations and how this has evolved over time. While the performance augmentation narrative is less established in academic literature, for instance, this does not mean that the same is true in organizations adopting AI. Future research could also examine how the augmentation targets change depending on contextual elements such as the size, sector, or geography of the organization or the type of AI technology implemented.

## 7 Limitations and Boundary Conditions

It is worth noting several boundary conditions of our study, which also open up opportunities for future research. First, we made specific choices when selecting the literature for our review. For analytical clarity, which becomes even more important when performing a literature review that crosses disciplinary boundaries, we decided to limit our review to papers that specifically referred to and theorized augmentation. While we are convinced that we obtained a thorough understanding of the different metanarratives, some works might have been left out of the review if the authors used a term other than augmentation to describe the same phenomenon or if the description of augmentation did not add anything new to our understanding of the underlying targets. In addition, we only considered metanarratives that discussed augmentation in relation to both humans and AI. We therefore did not consider metanarratives such as data augmentation, which is purely technical and does not theorize about the human, or medical augmentation, which is not related to AI. We also acknowledge that the augmentation literature is constantly evolving, and other metanarratives and underlying concepts could emerge in the future. Our paper intends to open a discussion on “what is augmented” with AI, and we hope that further research will explore even more augmentation targets.

Furthermore, while in this study, we focused on AI as an umbrella term for multiple emerging technologies, from embodied AI to generative AI, we agree with recent works that push for a more nuanced perspective that includes the specifics of the technology under consideration (e.g., Huysman, 2020). As research on AIbased augmentation continues to advance, we encourage future research to open up the technology by being more specific about the algorithms used, for example, and unpacking their potentially different consequences for AIbased augmentation.

## 8 Conclusion

Through our review, we have identified four distinct metanarratives on AI-based augmentation: the body, cognition, work, and performance. Each metanarrative represents a distinct augmentation target, as well as underlying human-AI configurations, and augmentation tensions. To arrive at these insights, we used an approach for conducting literature reviews that is relatively novel to the IS field—the metanarrative review method. We found this method to be particularly suited for reviewing the heterogeneous and multidisciplinary body of literature on AI-based augmentation. The metanarrative method not only allowed us to improve conceptual clarity on augmentation; more importantly, it also allowed us to explore the nuances and complexities behind this concept in a structured manner. Our decision to move away from a discipline- or paradigm-based analysis—as foreseen by the traditional metanarrative approach— towards a target-based analysis, allowed us to identify higher-arching themes that link the different disciplines engaging with AI-based augmentation, thereby helping to blur the boundaries between fields. As such blurring of boundaries is likely one of the key consequences of introducing AI into organizations (e.g., Dwivedi et al., 2019; Kusters et al., 2020), our article emphasizes the important role that the metanarrative review method can play in IS research to improve the understanding of the multidisciplinarity of AI.

## About the Authors

Inès Baer is a PhD candidate at the KIN Center for Digital Innovation at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Netherlands. Her research explores the multidisciplinary questions that emerge when developing and managing artificial intelligence in organizations. She currently studies this research topic in the area of public employment services.

Lauren Waardenburg is an assistant professor at ESSEC Business School in Cergy, France. Her main research interests are the role of technology in occupational emergence and how technology is reshaping work and organizing, focusing on the interplay between the physical and the digital. She has a specific interest in using ethnography as a research method for studying technology in practice.

Marleen Huysman holds a Chair in Knowledge and Organization at the School of Business and Economics, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, where she is the founder of the KIN Center for Digital Innovation. Heading the AI@Work research group, her ethnographic research focuses on knowing practices during the development and use of intelligent technologies in the workplace.
