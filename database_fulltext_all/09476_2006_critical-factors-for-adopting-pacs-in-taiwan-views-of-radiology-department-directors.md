# Critical factors for adopting PACS in Taiwan: Views of radiology department directors

I-Chiu Chang <sup>a,1</sup>, Hsin-Ginn Hwang <sup>a,2</sup>, David C. Yen <sup>b,\*</sup>, J.W. Lian <sup>c</sup>

National Chung Cheng University Department of Information Management, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of DSC and MIS Miami University Oxford, Ohio 45056, USA <sup>c</sup> National Central University Department of Information Management, Taiwan

Received 10 November 2004; received in revised form 10 August 2005; accepted 27 August 2005 Available online 20 October 2005

## Abstract

To cope with the dramatic changes and fierce competition, healthcare industry is experiencing major transformation in its information technology base. Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS), an integral part of comprehensive electronic medical records, has been recognized as a strategic information technology tool to improve a hospital’s competitiveness. Though many studies have investigated the critical factors concerning adoption of information systems, little attention has been focused on the healthcare industry and specifically on PACS. This study incorporates the views of Radiology Department directors and finds that the critical factors for adopting PACS in medical centers and regional hospitals of Taiwan are high-level manager support, governmental policy, and the expected benefits of using PACS. @2005.F1s D \_LA 11

Keywords: Electronic Medical Record (EMR); Information Technology (IT); Healthcare industry; Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS)

## 1. Introduction

The healthcare industry is experiencing a major transformation in its information technology base [32]. By nature, hospitals are in an information-intensive industry and hence they would gain great benefit by adopting IT applications, ranging from medical systems to administration systems. In other words, proper and correct adoption of IT can significantly affect the quality and performance of medical services provided by a hospital. However, not all hospitals adopt IT without any hesitation. The issue of <sup>b</sup>what factors promoting the adoption of IT in a healthcare setting<sup>Q</sup> becomes an important question for all healthcare administrators.

In the past, competition among hospitals in Taiwan was not intense and the operating costs of hospitals were not a concern. In 1995 the Taiwan Bureau of National Health Insurance (BNHI) established national health insurance and set a fixed reimbursement for health benefits. Since then, the BNHI has continuously decreased the reimbursement due to a weak economy. Consequently, hospitals are facing fierce pressure from competition to survive. To increase their competitiveness, some hospitals employ IT to improve the quality of patient care and productivity of hospital staff.

Currently, with the advances of computer technology in image processing and network communications, adopting Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) to process digitized medical images is growing rapidly. PACS is different than the adoption of some other types of information systems. PACS is somewhat unique because it is complicated by nature, requires heavy capital investment, and needs to be integrated with other instruments to process data from various hospital information applications to maximize its productivity. These unique features make the adoption of PACS a challenging task.

Most of the research related to PACS focuses on technology, with little emphasis on management issues. This research reviews related papers on IT adoption in business and follows the advice of an expert panel, composed of both academics and industry professionals, to design the study. Through questionnaires and hypothesis testing, this study identifies the critical factors for adopting PACS. Brief follow up interviews with two medical centers were used to obtain an insight into the PACS adoption. Furthermore, based on the study results, some suggestions are made for hospitals that may adopt PACS, for governments to set related policies, and for ongoing academic research to shed light on PACS management issues.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. The picture archiving and communication system

A comprehensive electronic medical records (EMR) system is a viable solution for optimizing patient management and providing high quality care while maintaining costs under economic restrictions [24]. Due to costly and complex installations of PACS, the early development of EMR was limited to supporting text documents and clinical data in electronic format, except medical image data. By integrating PACS and hospital information systems (HIS), a comprehensive EMR with patient-centered data model can be constructed to provide effective and timely medical care. Also, the information systems of a digital hospital such as digital therapy records, long distance treatments, Internet virtual hospitals, and medical e-commerce are all based on PACS [21].

To synthesize previous definitions [14,20] and the EMR perspective, this research defines PACS as <sup>b</sup>a medical image management information system, which processes medical images and integrates different equipment through a network<sup>Q</sup>. The main functions of PACS are to capture, transmit, store and retrieve, display, and analyze medical images [14].

A PACS requires a capital investment of several million US dollars [14]. To promote PACS adoption, Japan has established standards such as a <sup>b</sup>small-size PACS<sup>Q</sup> with less than four, a <sup>b</sup>medium-size PACS<sup>Q</sup> with 5–14, and a <sup>b</sup>large-size PACS<sup>Q</sup> with up to 1300 image display terminals [15]. Taiwan, however, has no official standard but roughly classifies PACS as <sup>b</sup>mini<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>full<sup>Q</sup>. A mini PACS is similar to small- and mediumsize PACS in Japan that handle digitized X-rays, digitized ultrasonic, or laparoscope systems that serve one or more departments. A full PACS serves the entire hospital and is similar to the large-size PACS in Japan.

Whereas PACS adoption begins in the 1980s in Japan [15], the US, and other developed countries [14], the earliest adoption of PACS in Taiwan was in 1992 [17]. At that time, commercial PACS were well established in many developed countries. Therefore, most PACS projects in Taiwan were implemented via cooperation between a hospital project team, often led by the director of the radiology department, and foreign PACS suppliers. This cooperative strategy is usually the more expensive implementation strategy [14].

## 2.2. The factors affecting IT adoption

Key factors that influence the adoption of IT in business can be categorized into three dimensions: the organizational dimension, the environmental dimension, and the characteristics of IT itself [27].

Organizational dimension includes organizational strategy, structure, management procedures, and personnel factors [27]. Raymond [25] focused on the size of the organization, maturity, resource investment, decision time, and organizational information system structure. Rogers [26] found that <sup>b</sup>over centralization and formalization<sup>Q</sup> inhibit organizational innovation. Grover and Goslar [11], and Zmud [34] came to a similar conclusion. Choe and Weill & Olson [3,31] indicate that the support of high-level managers, past experience in adopting IT, user satisfaction, and organization policy are all important factors for evaluating IT as an investment.

Davidson and Chismar [6] concluded that organizational factors influence the development of information systems in hospitals. In his research, organizational structure contains attributes such as the size, degree of centralization, and degree of formalization of the organization. Also, the attitude of doctors is a key factor that affects the adoption of information systems [13]. Sobol et al. [29] listed ten major obstacles for hospital computerization including related knowledge, manager recognition, and other internal issues.

Other than the internal environment, the external environment is an important factor for adopting IT [11,23]. Both Globerman [9]and Ettlie [7] indicated that the greater the competition between companies, the more likely the adoption of innovation. Peng [23] indicated that one of the main reasons not to adopt IT was the <sup>b</sup>lack of a governmental assistance plan<sup>Q</sup>. Cheng [2] also pointed out the importance of government policy to clinic computerization. Hill [12] found that change in medicare reimbursement policy was a driving force in the adoption of accounting and information systems in hospitals in the United States. Mogel [19] and Lemke [18] both pointed out that government policy played an important role in stimulating PACS innovation. Furthermore, due to the government PACS Reimbursement Act, the utilization of PACS in Korea climbed considerably from 2000 to 2002 [15].

The characteristic of information was one of the factors that influence IT adoption [25,27]. Tornatzky and Klein [30] found that innovation characteristics such as benefits, compatibility, and complexity have an effect on the adoption of innovation. Benefits of innovation mean the advantages to be gained from the innovation adoption [30]. The benefits of an information system [23] and issues in design, planning, and installation of IT [29] actually influence the type of IT to be employed. Additionally, Rogers [26] indicated that with higher compatibility, users are more likely to adopt innovations. He [26] also defined compatibility as <sup>b</sup>the degree to which an innovation is perceived as consistent with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of potential adopters<sup>Q</sup> (p.224). Based on this definition, Tornatzky and Klein [30] derived two interpretations of compatibility as <sup>b</sup>cognitive compatibility<sup>Q</sup> and the <sup>b</sup>operational compatibility<sup>Q</sup> (p.33). Grover [10] further combined the two interpretation and defined information systems compatibility as <sup>b</sup>compatibility of an IOS (inter organizational systems) with the company’s values, information base, experience and technological infrastructure<sup>Q</sup> in the information systems adoption.

## 3. Research methodology

Based on related research and the opinions of the expert panel, this study proposes a research model and associated hypotheses about factors affecting the adoption of PACS in medical centers or regional hospitals in Taiwan. After the questionnaire was designed, the directors from radiology departments of two medical centers performed a pre-test to refine the questionnaire. A factor analysis was utilized with the survey data to extract useful factors to perform a discriminate analysis in separating the adopters and non-adopters of PACS. Finally, brief follow up interviews with two medical centers were used to obtain an insight into the PACS adoption.

An expert panel was formed to provide face validity of this research. The expert panel included one medical information consultant, one director each of the MIS department and the radiology department from a medical center, one PACS vendor, and the authors of this research. The medical informatics consultant has extensive consulting experience with PACS and is also a professor in the field of medical informatics at a national university. Both directors were involved in the planning and implementing of early PACS projects in Taiwan, and consequently they have accumulated comprehensive experience in PACS-related research. The PACS vendor has extensive experience with one of the largest PACS companies in the world and is also the representative of that product in Taiwan. In addition, he has participated in all the bidding processes for acquiring PACS in Taiwan.

## 3.1. Research model and hypothesis

The research model for this study is shown in Fig. 1. The factors that influence the adoption of PACS can be divided into three dimensions: the organizational dimension, the environmental dimension, and the recognition of PACS characteristics.

The variables in the research model are derived from the research of Cheng, Grover, Grover & Golslar, and Peng [2,10,11,23]. Three groups of hypotheses are presented as follows.

(1) The organizational dimension of a hospital affects the adoption of PACS.

H1a. Hospitals with a lower degree of centralization tend to adopt PACS.

H1b. Hospitals with a lower degree of formalization tend to adopt PACS.

H1c. Hospitals with high-level manager support for technology tend to adopt PACS.

(2) The environment dimension of a hospital affects the adoption of PACS.

H2a. Hospitals with a high degree of business competition tend to adopt PACS.

H2b. A hospital that perceives the government policy as important tends to adopt PACS.

(3) A hospital’s recognition of the PACS characteristics affects the adoption of PACS.

H3a. A hospital that perceives the cost of PACS as low tends to adopt PACS.

H3b. A hospital that perceives compatibility between PACS and hospital information systems tends to adopt PACS.

H3c. A hospital that perceives the benefits of PACS tends to adopt PACS.

## 3.2. Research scope

There are three operating types of medical radiology services in Taiwan: (1) a radiology department within a medical center or regional hospital, (2) a radiology lab in a local hospital or clinic, and (3) a free-standing lab. Medical centers and regional hospitals are responsible for caring for more critical patients thus, their facilities are more advanced than the other two types. Furthermore, the investment in capital and human resources to install PACS is usually not affordable by small hospitals, clinics, or free-standing labs. For this reason, this study chooses the first operating type and performs the survey with the director of the radiology department within medical centers or regional hospitals in Taiwan.

According to the Department of Health of Taiwan, there were 68 qualified medical centers, regional hospitals, and candidates for regional hospitals at the time this research was conducted. Consequently, they were all included in the study. These hospitals either adopted or did not adopt PACS. Regardless of the size of PACS, the adoption decision is made largely by the directors of the radiology department who are mostly medical doctors (MD) with a specialization in radiology. Some of the decision makers are doctors in general practice, while a few are radiology technicians. These directors actually play two important roles in the development of PACS. The first role is as decision maker for adopting PACS, and the other role is as the PACS user. Therefore, it is assumed that they have a good understanding of the issues surrounding the adoption of PACS.

## 3.3. Questionnaire

As with development of the research model, the questionnaire used in this study includes consultation with the expert panel and findings from related research questionnaires. Most of the questionnaire items (see Appendix A) are either translated or modified from Grover [10], Peng [23], or Cheng [2]. Due to language and culture differences, some of the questions, which are not suitable for this research, were discarded. The expert panel inspected the questionnaire to guarantee the effectiveness of its contents. A five-point Likert scale was used to measure each variable. Scores of 1 to 5 represent totally disagree to totally agree.

Prior to mailing the questionnaire, one of the authors conducted a brief telephone call with each of the 68 hospitals to determine the name of the radiology department director. Studies have also shown that making prior notification calls [5] and mailing questionnaires to the specific subject can improve the response rates of mail surveys. At the same time, the status of PACS development in each hospital was checked since DOH had no official number of PACS adopters and non-adopters.

## 3.4. Follow up interview

In order to further examine the research findings, two follow up interviews were conducted. Two hospitals were selected for their similarities as well as differences based on the suggestions of Glaser and Strauss [8] about theoretical sampling. Face-to-face, using both structured and open-end questions were conducted with one high-level manager for each hospital, the director of the radiology department or information systems department, as well as PACS project-related participants. For each interview, one researcher asked the questions and at least two assistants kept the minutes.

Excluding the result of the pre-test, 66 questionnaires were sent out and 35 useable questionnaires were returned, which translates into a response rate of 53.03% for this research. ## 4.1. Analysis of reliability and validity

When examining the validity of an instrument, generally two aspects of validity are used: content validity and factorial validity. Zikmund [33] defined content validity as <sup>b</sup>Professional agreement that a scale logically appears to be accurately reflecting what was intended to be measured (p. 282)<sup>Q</sup>. In this study, the scales were first derived from theory-based studies, then reviewed by an expert panel. Therefore, there is professional agreement about the questionnaires content. Additionally, discriminant validity focuses on how one can empirically differentiate one construct from other constructs [16]. Exploratory factor analysis was employed to validate discriminant validity in this study. The results (See Appendix Table A.1) indicate the presence of eight factors which confirmed the research model. The factors account for 51.55%, 51.17%, and 50.11% of the variation in the organizational, the environmental, and the PACS characteristics dimensions, respectively.

The reliability of an instrument describes the consistency of the instrument in measuring the same phenomenon over time. In this research, the Cronbach’s coefficient a was computed for each variable to test for reliability. According to Nunnally [22], the index is valid between 0.5 and 0.6, while Churchill [4] suggested the index be greater than 0.7 to be reliable. In general, if the index is smaller than 0.35, the test should be abandoned. The Cronbach’s a of this research is between 0.41 and 0.71. The low value of 0.41 may be due to the exploratory nature of the research and lack of related items for reference. The small sample size is also an important factor. The expert panel values this factor and suggests the factor stay in the study. A comparison of Cronbach’s a from this study and previous studies is shown in Appendix Table A.1.

## 4.2. Hypothesis testing results

The value of Wilks’ Lambda (0.622) indicates that the research model of this study is significant ( p = 0.1). In addition, transforming Wilk’s Lambda into an exact F-ratio assesses the statistical significance of the discriminant function [28]. A Chi square test is used to examine the usefulness of the discriminant function. Since there are eight factors used in the discriminant function, the degree of freedom is 8, and the observed significance level is 0.101. Therefore, the discriminant function is useful for classifying adopters and nonadopters of PACS ( p =0.1).

The contribution of each factor in the discriminant function is based on its discriminant loadings. In general, this value has to be greater than 0.3 to be significant. Among the eight factors, support of the high-level manager, government policy, and benefits of PACS are significant (See Table 2). Therefore, from the viewpoint of the radiology department director, these factors have a significant effect on the decision to adopt PACS.

## 4.3. Follow up interview

Two adopters from the non-respondent group were used for follow up interviews. Both cases are medical centers and represent different stages of PACS diffusion in Taiwan. Rogers [26] suggested <sup>b</sup>adopter distributions follow a bell-shaped curve<sup>Q</sup> (p. 260), the first 2.5% are the innovators and the next 13.5% accounts for the early adopters. . .<sup>Q</sup>. In our study, case A was the first adopter of PACS in Taiwan, therefore is an innovator that fell in the first 2.5% of 68 hospitals and Case B was an early adopter that fell in the 13.5% of 68 hospitals.

Case A, which began its PACS development in 1992, is a public medical center with 3000 beds, and is supported by the government through the Veterans Affairs Commission. It was the first operational PACS site in Taiwan and, therefore, has become a benchmark for hospital PACS development in Taiwan. Their development strategy for PACS was to form a project team with both MIS and radiology department directors as leaders and then cooperate with vendors for the adoption.

The PACS development history of Case A included four phases. The first phase was devoted to planning, designing, and implementing a mini-PACS. Computed Tomography (CT) was the major connected modality and only four PACS workstations were provided initially. In phase two, more modalities were included, such as Magnetic Resonance Image (MRI) and Sonography (SONO), and the system scale was moved from mini to full PACS. The third phase was user oriented and integrated with other hospital information systems. From phase three to the present, hospital-wide-filmless is the major mission of the PACS system.

Case B is also a public medical center with 1661 beds, supported by the Ministry of National Defense, and started to develop PACS in 1997. Their PACS development outsourced because of the maturity of PACS technology and the fact that the number of PACS vendors had doubled since 1992. Case B formed a project team with the director of the radiology department as leader of the project and also followed the planning, design, and implementation process of PACS.

Case A raised six critical factors including information technology, user, systems function, management support, vendor, and external environment given that it was the first in Taiwan to adopt PACS. Case A needed to consider educating internal customers (user), developing system functions, and dealing with external vendor simultaneously. From the external environment view, government policy was their major concern because digital healthcare record-related laws in Taiwan were not legislated then. However, Case A foresaw the benefits of PACS, valued its contribution, and then decided to become an innovator in the area.

## 5. Discussion

Both adopters and non-adopters agreed on the following three aspects: (1) the degree of centralization and formalization within the hospital, (2) the degree of competition in the medical industry, and (3) the impact of cost and compatibility of PACS on the decisions to adopt PACS. Both groups thought differently about high-level manager support, government policy, and the benefits of PACS while adopting PACS, and therefore those three variables can be used to distinguish PACS adopters from non-adopters.

The comparison of results obtained from this and previous adoption studies is shown in Table 2. The results of the organizational dimension hypotheses tests agree with findings from previous research on factors such as high-level manager support and degree of formalization, however the findings disagree on the degree of centralization. Regarding the environmental dimension, the results of hypotheses tests agree with previous research on the government policy factor and disagree regarding competition in the same business. The results of the PACS (i.e., innovation) characteristics dimension disagree with the previous research [10] on systems compatibility. And lastly, the cost of PACS cannot be used to distinguish adopters and non-adopters.

The possible reasons why the findings of this study vary from prior studies are firstly, previous studies surveyed multiple organizations in multiple industries. Unlike for-profit organizations, Taiwan hospitals have been consistently high in their degree of centralization. Both adopter and non-adopter perceived similar degrees of centralization of their hospital. All hospitals perceived similar competition within the healthcare industry mostly caused by the reform of NHI. Secondly, due to the high margin of profit, PACS venders provided total solution such as gateways and on-site helps to link PACS to hospitals information systems, therefore, hospitals perceived indifferently in PACS compatibility. Also the information systems adopted in the previous studies were different from PACS which bring in different benefits. PACS adopters perceived and received different rewards under the reimbursement policy by the time this research conducted.

The follow up interview of two cases in different stage of technology adoption revealed that the variables both cases concerned about are high-level manager support, PACS benefits, and the impact of government policy, which agrees the survey results. The innovator, Case A, had more critical factors to be concerned with, such as technology, user, and vendor issues. Case B, as an early adopter, did not consider the above factors critical any more. The different critical factors explained the special concerns needed at the specific stage of technology diffusion.

In summary, the respondents reported consistently high in degree of hospital centralization, low in degree of hospital formalization, high in degree of competition in the health care business, high in PACS compatibility with existing systems, and high cost of PACS. These findings suggest that the above five variables should not be used as discriminant variables in future hospital related studies in Taiwan until further investigations are conducted. ## 6. Conclusion

This study identifies critical factors while adopting PACS considered by medical centers and regional hospitals in Taiwan using questionnaires to radiology department directors. The research hypotheses examine mature constructs most often evaluated in for-profit organizations where decisions regarding the adoption of technology are made largely by MIS department managers [35]. The results of this research confirm some findings from previous for-profit organizations studies in the support of high-level mangers, government policy, and the benefits of PACS as three critical factors affecting the adoption of PACS.

Both adopters and non-adopters of PACS reported extremely high competition in the medical industry. The adopters tended to use PACS as a tool to gain competitive advantage. Though both groups recognized the heavy monetary investment of PACS, cost did not hold back the adopters because they foresaw the benefits of PACS. The vendor on the expert panel gave an unofficial estimate under the current insurance reimbursement system that adopters could typically break-even on an investment in PACS in two to three years, which suggests the benefits of PACS were not a primary consideration of non-adopters. Regarding government policy, adopters foresaw the future and reacted to the changing environment as the cases indicated.

Hospitals are different from most for-profit organizations where doctors are the major decision makers instead of MIS managers [1]. Doctors’ expertise is in the medical area instead of management operation, and hence hospitals tend to be consistently high in centralization and low in formalization. Therefore, the degree of centralization and formalization did not affect the adoption of PACS.

By the time this research was conducted, the PACS diffusion was in the <sup>b</sup>early majority<sup>Q</sup> stage, adopters reached about 40%. In other words, with increasing competition among PACS vendors the PACS system became trialable, observable, and no longer complicated. The survey results revealed that most of the development strategies of PACS were either outsourcing or cooperating with the vendors extensively. Case B also indicated that with high marginal profit some PACS vendors even provided several on-site employees to assist the hospital to link the PACS with existing hospital systems.

## 6.1. Suggestions

There are 60% of the research hospitals in Taiwan delaying their development of PACS, since PACS costs about three to six million US dollars to fully implement in an entire hospital. This study suggests non-adopters review the expected benefits of PACS and start with mini PACS in suitable departments to establish a model and/or prototype for future development of PACS within the entire hospital. On adoption strategy, a PACS project team should be composed of the radiology and MIS directors or different experts who keep good contact with the vendors. Digital hospital is the future goals of Taiwan government. The information systems of digital hospital can be listed as digital therapy records, long distance treatments, Internet virtual hospitals, and medical e-commerce, which are all based on PACS [21]. Without PACS, none of the above future function can be achieved. The above issues can be used to gain highlevel management support.

The research results indicate that governmental policy is a perceived as critical factor by adopters while adopting PACS in hospitals. However, the Taiwan government’s attitude is relative weak compared to that of PACS well developed countries; for example, Korea. The Korea government not only nurtured the domestic PACS companies to develop their own brand of PACS but also encourage the PACS usage by establishing reimburse incentive. Therefore, this study suggests government should take stronger position to provide financial aid, construct complete PACS company information database, educate and train non-adopters, and establish related policies to promote PACS for hospitals in Taiwan.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research directions

Though mail surveys with a return of about 30% are often considered <sup>b</sup>satisfactory<sup>Q</sup> [5] the respondents of this study may not represent the population of interest because they might tend to favor the technology and therefore were willing to mail back the questionnaire. Also, without DOH provided official adoption status of PACS in each hospital, the self-reported status collected in this study may be biased. This study using a static cross-sectional approach may not reveal the dynamics of the adoption processes. Therefore, more rigorous study is needed to further explore issues uncovered by this study. For instance a longitudinal study would provide more insight into the adoption process. At the time of this research, the innovation diffusion of PACS in Taiwan was at the early majority stage. As more adopters join in the diffusion, a study to investigate the critical factors that affect the implementation of PACS may be necessary. Meanwhile, uses technology acceptance, cost analysis, and the cross-culture aspects of PACS, are issues to be explored.
