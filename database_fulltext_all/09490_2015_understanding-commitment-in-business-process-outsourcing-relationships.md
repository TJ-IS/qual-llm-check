UNDERSTANDING COMMITMENT IN BUSINESS PROCESS OUTSOURCING RELATONSHIPS

## Abstract

Because of cost savings and efficiency gains in past business process outsourcing (BPO) projects, firms increasingly consider outsourcing business processes that are knowledge-intensive and strategic oriented. However, success of these strategic BPO projects entails strong commitment from a client and its service provider to leverage interfirm resources and maintain close collaboration to improve a client’s business processes. Despite the growing acce ce of BPO practices, the formation of its commitment remains unclear. To address this knowledge gap, this research uses the relational view as the theoretical lens to explain how a client’s commitment is influenced by the relational value from outsourcing relationships. Relational values are characterized as knowledge-based capabilities, process-based capabilities, and governance. The proposed model and hypotheses are largely supported by the empirical data from 167 firms. The results show that commitment is positively affected by a service provider’ task-knowledge coordination, process alignment, process flexibility, and a client’s behavior control over the service provider. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications.

Key words: commitment, knowledge, outsourcing, process, governance, relational view

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Business process outsourcing (BPO) refers to the delegation of one or more IT-enabled business processes to an external supplier, who is responsible for managing the processes to achieve the client firm’s defined and measurable set of performance goals [1-3]. For example, banks may outsource their credit card application processing to a third-party vendor to simultaneously minimize the processing cost and improve customer experience. The global spending on BPO has significantly increased in the past decade [4-6]. Some of the benefits of BPO are access to growing trend and the purported benefits of BPO, the reported failure rates of BPO are high [1, 9, 10]. Studies conclude that one notable reason behind these failures is challenges associated with managing outsourcing relationships [10, 11].

While prior IT outsourcing (ITO) literature has examined outsourcing relationship management in general, there are crucial distinctions between BPO and ITO that require additional research efforts to better understand how to manage BPO relationships [4, 5, 8, 12, 13]. ITO is the sourcing of information technology services through external third parties. Some of these services may include help desks, IS planning and management activities, data centers,

In contrast, BPO objectives are more diverse and can go beyond the technically focused projects in ITO. For example, BPO objectives tend to emphasize a range of business performance improvements that span from cost reduction, rapid delivery, and convenience to flexible enablement, business transformation, and innovation [13]. Second, the knowledge required to be integrated between a client and a service provider is different between BPO and IT outsourcing. Knowledge integration in IT outsourcing is usually “goal-oriented” and focuses on the provider’s knowledge and expertise in reaching a client’s goal such as performance of IT infrastructure and operations, and IT applications development. In contrast, BPO emphasizes “process-oriented” knowledge sharing that requires not only a client’s context-specific knowledge essential to perform BPO but also a service provider’s knowledge about how to manage and execute the business process [3, 4]. Third, BPO relationships usually do not have well defined start and end points. Long-standing relationships and effective governance mechanisms (e.g., behavior control) to ensure partners’ contribution are required to fully realize BPO goals and associated benefits [8, 9, 17]. Finally, ITO outcomes depend, in part, on the technical and methodological capabilities and knowledge sharing between a client and a service provider. In contrast, BPO success largely depends on knowledge sharing and interfirm learning on how to improve business processes and support business strategic goals.

In their comprehensive review of ITO and BPO body of research, Lacity et al. [12, 13] identify promising BPO research opportunities based on gaps that exist in ITO and BPO literature. They suggest that new studies should reflect the shift in BPO relationships and outcomes from a short-term to a long-term orientation. In particular, BPO clients increasingly expect service providers to not only offer short-term benefits (e.g., lower costs, valuable service on back office processes), but also support their long-term goals (e.g., technological innovations and process maturity). Lacity et al. [13] also note that recent BPO projects (e.g., business analytics) require more knowledge intensive activities compared to other BPO activities (e.g., human resources, marketing, and supply chain functions) that were examined in previous studies. These knowledge process outsourcing projects support a client’s long-term and strategic goals related to innovation and competitive actions [4, 8]. For example, BPO usually entails strategic processes (e.g., customer analytics) to facilitate innovation and business transformation, which require ongoing knowledge contribution from both a client (e.g., BPO requirements) and a provider (e.g., business domain, knowledge for process improvement) [4]. Achieving these knowledge process outsourcing requires the client and the provider to commit to long-term relationships.

From a theoretical perspective, Lacity et al. [12] suggest that transaction cost economics logic that seems to highlight a short-term orientation of outsourcing offers diminishing marginal insights to outsourcing problems. Their conclusion is derived from the evidence that “findings have been repeatedly examined and found to have no significant effects on … BPO outcomes … because there are simply no patterns to find.” ([13], p. 241). The current BPO practices among businesses and a critical review of outsourcing research seem to suggest that researchers need to draw on a long-term orientation to theorize BPO outsourcing relationships and partnerships as a basis to understand outsourcing outcomes.

Both ITO and BPO research has highlighted the importance of satisfaction and commitment in outsourcing success [4, 9, 11]. While satisfaction tends to be more transitory [18-20], commitment is more stable and reflects a more comprehensive assessment of a client’s willingness to put efforts into developing and maintaining lasting relationships with a provider [11, 21]. The unique characteristics of BPO relationships suggest that outsourcing outcomes (e.g., satisfaction, project performance) and their antecedents in prior ITO research may not fully capture the spirit of BPO performance [5, 12]. Since ITO focuses on short-term benefits from IT services, ITO studies mainly draw on theoretical arguments from transaction cost economics and the resource-based view to examine antecedents of commitment from a cost reduction and IT related resource capability perspectives respectively. In contrast, BPO emphasizes the importance of strategic management and relationship-specific resources to commitment [9, 10, 13]. Commitment is particularly important for successful BPO because the project requires a combination of a client and its provider’s capabilities to collaboratively implement the outsourced process. On the flip side, the lack of commitment tends to increase a client’s costs (e.g., switching costs and redeployment costs) and reduce a provider’s business opportunities [22, 23]. In addition, previous research suggests that a provider’s understanding about a client’s business domain and the integration of a provider’s services into a chain of other functions in a client firm are also significant to BPO success [1, 3]. Thus, BPO requires knowledge supply chain between the client and the service provider (e.g., knowledge sharing and knowledge integration between them, client’s domain knowledge). This knowledge-related capabilities help improve BPO performance and increase commitment [3, 8,13]. Overall, this logic and related empirical evidence highlight the importance of commitment and relationship-specific resources in BPO performance [12, 13].

Research has suggested that a set of individual, organizational, economic, social, and strategic factors is associated with BPO commitment [7, 10, 11, 22]. Accordingly, outsourcing research has examined the influence of a number of antecedents on commitment. Some of these antecedents are strategic management variables (e.g., absorptive capacity, capability), economic variables (e.g., relationship-specific investments or resources), and relational governance (e.g., behavior control, interaction and alignment of actions between the client and the service provider) [9, 10, 12, 23, 24]. While prior research has investigated these factors separately, we take an integrated view and empirically evaluate these variables together. In particular, this study focuses on how relational value generated form the client and service provider relationship influences commitment. The novelty of our theoretical approach is the comprehensive assessment of antecedents of commitment that offers a nuanced understanding of how relationship-specific capabilities influence commitment that underlies the success of BPO outcomes.

Building on outsourcing literature [12, 13] and the relational view [25], this study develops a theoretical model of factors that influence BPO commitment and tests it empirically. The relational view provides a useful lens to study how business processes can be improved by leveraging relationship-specific resources (or capabilities). We argue that a client’s commitment depends on whether the outsourced task is performed effectively. We draw from the key dimensions of the relational view—knowledge-sharing routines, relation-specific assets, complementary capabilities, and effective governance— to identify meaningful antecedents of commitment in BPO relationships. These antecedents are a client’s absorptive capacity, a client’s behavior control, a service provider’s task-knowledge coordination, a service provider’s process alignment, and a service provider’s process flexibility.

We surveyed 167 firms and analyzed the data using hierarchical regression analyses. The results largely support the proposed hypotheses. Our study contributes to outsourcing literature in general and BPO literature in particular by providing a theoretical framework and empirical evidence that deepens our understanding about how outsourcing relationships can be improved by leveraging relationship-specific resources. We also advance the relational view argument by extending the conceptualization of relationship-specific capabilities and evaluating them in the context of BPO relationships.

## 2. THEORY BUILDING AND HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

## 2.1. Outsourcing Literature

Outsourcing research has primarily focused on outsourcing decisions and outsourcing outcomes [12]. Outsourcing decisions range from the most fundamental make-or-buy decision to more nuanced decisions regarding sourcing specific IT functions or strategic processes [14, 15]. Outsourcing outcomes are measured in a number of ways, including a client’s general perceptions of the success, quality of relationships, project or business performance, and resource and IT management capability [26, 27]. While outsourcing decisions emphasize the process of making decisions (why, what, and which) and the primary units of analysis have been the IS function or the transaction, outsourcing outcomes capture the performance-based perspective on outsourcing implementations using different levels of analysis (e.g., project, firm, and client and service provider relationship) [12, 28]. In their survey of outsourcing research, Lacity et al. [12] called for more research to understand BPO performance. This study is a step in that direction by focusing on commitment as an important construct to understand outsourcing outcomes.

ITO researchers generally classify ITO outcomes into three categories—performance (e.g., a client’s project performance, IS performance, business performance, or a client’s satisfaction), relationship quality (e.g., trust, prior client/service provider working relationship), and capability (e.g., providers’ human resource management capability, a client’s relationship management capability, technical and methodological capability of both clients and providers) [12]. Compared to ITO, measures of BPO outsourcing outcomes are more diverse. These include a client’s performance improvements (e.g., cost reduction, profits), relationship quality and relational governance, effective knowledge sharing, trust, commitment, capability (e.g., business process management capability of both the client and the provider), and client/provider alignment [13]. BPO reflects a client’s outsourced process that incorporates an integrated sequence of events that occur over time to help the client achieve a particular goal [1, 11]. Outsourcing studies note that the extent to which outsourcing outcomes can be successfully achieved relies on the efficiency and effectiveness of the coordination of interdependent activities between a client and a provider [3]. Others consider knowledge sharing and collaboration between partners as key factors affecting outsourcing outcomes [9]. Still, others emphasize the importance of governance structure in outsourcing outcomes. Governance can be formal control (e.g., contracts), informal control (e.g., relational governance) or a combination of both [8, 23].

In the past, outsourcing projects primarily focused on costs savings [12, 13]. As outsourcing practices become more complex with an increasing emphasis on innovation and transformation of business processes, more recent outsourcing studies put less emphasis on transaction attributes but highlight the importance of relationship and partnership in BPO [1, 10, 13, 29]. This is because relationships play a key role in leveraging the provider’s capabilities and facilitating the alignment of interfirm processes for outsourcing in general and for BPO in particular [10, 11]. Success of BPO depends on the degree of collaboration between outsourcing partners and the efforts put into relationship management. A deteriorating relationship can incur high financial costs, operational disruption, and loss of business opportunities [23].

Outsourcing relationships have been examined from a number of perspectives, including an economic perspective (e.g., transaction cost economics), a strategic perspective (e.g., resource dependency theory), and a social perspective (e.g., social exchange theory) [9, 10, 19, 23]. For example, Bharadwaj et al. [1] examined a successful outsourcing relationship from both an economic and a strategic perspective using transaction cost economics and resource-based view theories. Drawing on both economic and social perspectives, Goo et al. [23] investigated factors that influence commitment in ITO, including formal contract factors (e.g., service level agreement) and relational governance factors (e.g., relational norms).

Among the measures of outcomes associated with outsourcing relationships, commitment is conceptually different from other measures such as satisfaction or trust. Satisfaction emphasizes a client’s backward evaluation of a provider, thus it tends to be more transitory. Trust reflects a client’s belief about a service provider’s benevolence, integrity and honesty and whether its outsourcing requirements will be met through a service provider’s future actions [11, 32]. In contrast, commitment incorporates both belief and behavior and reflects a client’s intention to stay in the relationship and willingness to put in effort to maintain the relationship [4, 31]. In other words, commitment reflects a client’s “stickiness” and loyalty to a target provider [20, 23, 30]. Trust was found to influence mitment in outsourcing relationships [11, 23]. However, trust is more salient when a new outsourcing is formed while commitment plays a key role in positive relational outcomes such as relationship durability and cooperation.

Commitment in BPO is a “forward looking” measure and more enduring over time, which is a necessary and sufficient condition for a successful BPO relationship to realize desirable strategic benefits. For example, Zhou et al. [30] point out that commitment is more action oriented, reflecting a client’s stability and loyalty to a target service provider even when the level of satisfaction is low. Goo et al.’s [23] research shows that commitment in outsourcing reflects not only a client’s belief on its providers’ goodwill and stability of the outsourcing relationship, but also the client’s input into the relationship (e.g., the client’s investment of capital and effort). Thus, compared to trust or satisfaction, commitment represents a long-term assessment of outsourcing outcomes and is suitable for the BPO context that requires close cooperation and stable relationships to realize the benefits [1]. Despite its importance, few studies consider commitment as a measure of BPO outcomes, according to the extensive review of BPO studies from 1996-2011 by Lacity et al. [13]. Given the lack of understanding about commitment in BPO, this study fills this gap by using relational view to examine antecedents of commitment.

Previous studies report the influence of various relationship-specific factors on commitment. Goo et al. [10] found that outsourcing relationships rely not only on the client’s knowledge management capability but also on its ability to control the provider’s opportunistic behavior. Others report that outsourcing commitment relies on various interorganizational relationship management mechanisms [23]. Specifically, Goo et al.’s [23] study draws on both transaction cost economics (e.g., contractual terms associated with change characteristics of service level agreement) and relational governance (e.g., mutual value creation, conflict resolution) to explain how interorganizational processes and a service provider’s behavior can be managed to improve commitment. Still, others build on the resource-based view theory and suggest that both a client’s capability (e.g., outsourcing management capability) and a provider’s capability (e.g., process management capability) play a key role in outsourcing relationships [1]. Together, these studies contribute to our understanding about the key determinants of commitment to outsourcing. They generally agree that the extent to which outsourcing relationship can be improved depends on how well the partners leverage interfirm resources and manage the sourcing process (e.g., knowledge sharing, capability of the client and the provider to improve outsourcing processes).

With the growing interest in BPO and the dearth of empirical research to understand commitment in BPO, Lacity et al. [12, 13] called for a more granular exploration of the influence of client-provider relationships on outsourcing performance. To fill these gaps and respond to Lacity et al.’s [13] call, this study aims to investigate the antecedents of BPO commitment from a relationship perspective, emphasizing how relationship-specific capabilities affect commitment.

## 2.2. Theoretical Framework

The relational view suggests that firms gain competitive advantage not only from resources (e.g., knowledge) and capabilities that are owned and controlled by themselves, but also from idiosyncratic interfirm processes or collaboration with partners [25]. Previous research has found the relational view to be useful in explaining performance improvement from relationship-specific processes and capabilities in different settings, including relational contracting, working partnerships, cross-licensing agreements, value co-creation in new product development, and logistics processes [4, 32, 33]. In an IS context, the relational view has been used to explain co-creation of relational value in interorganizational IT capabilities development and strategic ITO [4, 5, 34]. Our research extends Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework to examine how a client’s commitment is influenced by interfirm capabilities created by the client and the service provider.

The relational view suggests that four factors are critical to generate relational value—relationship-specific assets, interfirm knowledge-sharing routines, complementary capabilities, and effective governance [25]. Relationship-specific assets emphasize the specific investments (site specificity, physical specificity, process-specific knowledge) that can be utilized to improve firm performance. Interfirm knowledge-sharing routines reflect interfirm communication, information sharing, and collaboration. Complementary capabilities emphasize that a firm’s resources be utilized in conjunction with the complementary resources (or capabilities) of its partners. Finally, effective governance seeks to provide structural safeguards for managing the relationship.

We apply the four resources of relational value in the context of BPO. We propose a knowledge-process-governance framework, in which we posit that the success of managing BPO relationships depends on three dimensions of relational capabilities--knowledge-based capabilities, process-based capabilities, and governance. Table 2 shows how we map the four resources of relational value from Dyer and Singh [25] to variables that are meaningful in a BPO context. Knowledge-based capabilities relate to relationship-specific assets and interfirm knowledge-sharing routines in Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework. Relationship-specific assets that are important to outsourcing relationships are human asset specificity and relation-specific skills. These assets and skills are manifested in know-how accumulated through relationships. Dyer and Singh [25] posited that the development of relation-specific skills shapes and is shaped by a regular pattern of interfirm knowledge-sharing routines. They noted that “cospecialization increases as … partners develop experience working together and accumulate specialized information, language, and know-how” (p. 662), allowing them to communicate effectively and efficiently with less error. Interfirm knowledge-sharing routines is defined as “a regular pattern of interfirm interactions that permits the transfer, recombination, or creation of specialized knowledge” ([25], p. 665). In particular, absorptive capacity in relation to a specific partner plays a key role in the development of overlapping knowledge bases and interaction routines between partners. In this study, knowledge-based capabilities refer to BPO partner’s ability to recognize and assimilate knowledge that is required to handle BPO tasks. This includes both a client’s knowledge on how to facilitate strategic processes and a service provider’s process-specific knowledge accumulated through long-standing relationships (e.g., joint learning, coordination between client-specific knowledge and BPO tasks) [8, 13].

In BPO, both the client and the service provider should have the needed knowledge to jointly solve the outsourced business problems. As BPO tends to be used for strategic reasons (e.g., production capacity planning) rather than for routine transactions (e.g., call center), a client should have enough process domain expertise to better manage the outsourced processes (e.g., a client’s ability to acquire and assimilate domain knowledge) as well as effective communication plans with a service provider and strategies to motivate a service provider’s behavior [3, 8, 17]. The service provider’s knowledge and its ability to coordinate the outsourced task play a key role in dealing with the outsourced processes that have different levels of complexity. In particular, this type of knowledge is required to improve outsourcing functions and perform process transition [1, 35]. Thus, we theorize that these knowledge-related capabilities reflected in a client’s absorptive capacity and a provider’s task-knowledge coordination influence BPO commitment.

Process-based capabilities relate to complementary capabilities in Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework. To achieve superior performance, it is important that a partner’s distinctive resources are combined with those from others, creating a synergistic effect whereby the combined resources are more valuable, rare and difficult to imitate. In this study, process-based capabilities are defined as a service provider’s ability to improve outsourced processes by leveraging a combination of complementary resources from BPO partners. For example, a service provider’s process alignment capability to coordinate among downstream value chain activities (e.g., marketing management), upstream value chain activities (e.g., logistic management), and other activities in the client firm (e.g., financial planning) is necessary for a successful implementation of a client’s improved business processes [8, 34]. In addition, a service provider’s flexibility to cope with changes in a client’s requirements and in the environment enhances BPO success. To perform the outsourced task, the service provider needs to have both an understanding of the client-specific processes and the capability to integrate the client’s outsourced process into its services [3, 9, 10]. This study conceptualizes process-based capabilities as the service provider’s process alignment and process flexibility that requirements for services.

Finally, governance maps directly to effective governance in Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework. Governance shapes the willingness of partners to engage in value-creation activities that are beneficial to the relationship. In this study, governance refers to a client’s control mechanisms over a service provider to influence its behavior and to promote a client’s interests. Outsourcing research has emphasized the importance of governance to motivate the service provider to act in a manner consistent with the client’s expectations [17, 24]. We use a client’s behavior control to capture the extent to which a client believes that its provider is willing to comply with the guidelines and procedures for undertaking outsourced activities.

Overall, knowledge-based capabilities focus on “what” capability to capture knowledge that is needed to address the uncertainty of BPO tasks, while process-based capabilities reflect “how” to enhance BPO performance [8-11]. Control serves as the underlying mechanism to influence the service provider’s actions and behaviors to achieve BPO objectives [17, 24]. ## 2.3. Hypothesis development

## Knowledge-based capabilities: Client’s absorptive capacity and service provider’s task-knowledge coordination

Drawing on Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework, knowledge-based capabilities are conceptualized as a client’s absorptive capability and a service provider’s ability to perform task-knowledge coordination with the client. Previous research views knowledge sharing routines as capabilities to create value and facilitate interfirm interactions that permit the transfer, recombination, or new knowledge creation [36]. In particular, knowledge sharing and knowledge creation generate value through interorganizational learning in outsourcing and supply chain management [33].

Knowledge sharing engenders collaborative participation of both the client and the service provider that promotes mutual understanding [4, 5, 9].

Prior work in IT outsourcing consistently shows that, to achieve successful outcomes, both the client and the service provider should contribute the knowledge needed to perform the outsourcing activities. For example, Rustag et al. [17] found that a client’s technical knowledge helps improve communication with service providers and enhance the level of success. Similarly, others point out that a client’s understanding about the service provider’s IT expertise for improving outsourcing processes facilitates interaction [9]. Still, others report that a client’s knowledge and capability are essential to appraise outsourcing processes and monitor the impact of BPO [1]. In addition to a client’s knowledge, studies on BPO also highlight the importance of a supplier’s knowledge management capabilities to achieve task-knowledge coordination [3, 10]. These capabilities include an understanding the client’s business domain, appropriate interfaces with the client’s proprietary applications, and an integration of outsourced applications with other functions in the client’s firm [9, 27].

Drawing from evidence in the outsourcing literature, we use a client’s absorptive capacity and a service provider’s task-knowledge coordination to evaluate the role of interfirm knowledge sharing in BPO’s commitment. We define absorptive capacity as a client’s ability to recognize and assimilate valuable knowledge from external resources and to apply it to BPO [37, 38]. A service provider’s task-knowledge coordination refers to the extent to which the service provider understands the client’s business domain and has the ability to manage interactions and to relate the client’s domain knowledge to the BPO tasks [1, 35].

A client with a high degree of absorptive capacity is more likely to leverage the service provider’s expertise to achieve the BPO goal [8, 9]. Besides, the client’s in-depth understanding of its needs encourages the service provider to engage in communication, leading to more knowledge sharing and collaboration [1]. Thus, we anticipate that a client with a higher degree of absorptive capacity shapes positive behaviors of a service provider and leads to a client’s higher commitment towards BPO. Thus, we propose H1.

## H1: Client’s degree of absorptive capacity positively affects its BPO commitment.

To achieve high BPO performance, a service provider needs to understand the client’s business domain and requirements of the outsourced processes [3]. Task-knowledge coordination enables the alignment between business knowledge and technical knowledge and helps the service provider understand “who knows what” and relate the required expertise that is critical to the execution of BPO [1, 9, 35]. Studies on interfirm relationship management report that relationship-specific asset investment plays a key role in positive outcomes including financial performance and competitive positioning [5, 33, 34]. Relationship-specific investment may include strategic knowledge transfer across firm boundaries, partners’ knowledge sharing, and coordination between idiosyncratic strategic information and interfirm processes.

Outsourcing literature has highlighted the importance of task-knowledge coordination to improve interaction processes and relationship outcomes [1, 3]. For example, Han et al. [3] provided empirical evidence to support that outsourcing partners’ ability to coordinate knowledge and the outsourced task through information sharing and collaborative participation plays a crucial role in outsourcing success. Likewise, others pointed out that outsourcing performance can be improved through a service provider’s capability in addressing knowledge interdependencies between a client’s domain knowledge and a service provider’s tasks [9, 39, 40]. BPO studies show that a service provider’s capability in coordinating between customized processes catering to the client’s requirements and domain knowledge through the development of expertise unique to a BPO task relate to a successful relationship [1, 41]. In addition, a service provider’s task-coordination capability reflects its willingness and effort to contribute to the BPO task and client-provider relationship by providing a feasible solution to BPO. Thus, we hypothesize that a service provider’s task-knowledge coordination capability increases the client’s willingness to be committed in the relationship, leading to H2.

## H2: Service provider’s task-knowledge coordination positively affects a client’s BPO commitment.

## Process-based capabilities: Service provider’s process alignment and service provider’s process flexibility

Process-based capabilities refer to the capabilities embedded in the partnership that can be subsequently appropriated to improve the outsourced processes. These capabilities closely map to complementary capabilities that seek to improve interfirm business processes through joint capabilities [25]. Complementary capabilities emphasize the ability to coordinate the partners’ capabilities to collectively increase the relational value and subsequent performance.

Extending prior work, this study theorizes process-based capabilities through two mechanisms: service interfirm processes can be coordinated and managed [1, 34]. For example, prior work has viewed process alignment as important mechanisms for improving collaboration between partners and coordinating processes that are distributed across partners [41]. This is because process alignment facilitates the mutual adjustment of actions between partners and enables the discovery of how processes can be enhanced. Process flexibility reflects the service provider’s ability to capture emerging opportunities by adjusting its offered services and its collaboration with the client [8, 41, 42].

Studies have recognized the role of process alignment in improving interorganizational relationships in general and outsourcing performance in particular [23, 34]. Process alignment enables the collaborative participation between the client and the service provider such as solving problems together, synchronizing outsourced tasks, clarifying task outputs, and integrating outputs back into the client’s value chain [8, 9]. Process alignment also enables joint learning, working together in creating boundary-spanning problem-solving processes, and facilitating process improvement [3, 34]. For example, a client is more likely to commit to a service provider who is willing to develop joint learning and intensive communication and who has the ability to align the outsourced process with the client’s business domain and proprietary applications. According to the relational view, process alignment reflects a service provider’s ability to manage outsourcing relationship and leverage interfirm resources [25]. Empirical findings support that a client’s desire to continue a relationship and confidence in the stability of the relationship rely on outsourcing performance and joint capabilities, which are induced by a service provider’s process alignment [5, 23, 34]. For example, the success of an outsourcing task with knowledge-intensive characteristics such as financial planning and logistics management relies on intense communication and joint-learning between a client and a service provider. Besides, completing the outsourcing task also requires the service provider to align its problem-solving processes with localized knowledge that resides in the client [3, 34]. Therefore, the extent of coordination between the client and the service provider is critical to encourage mutual adjustments of plans and processes and enable the improvement of work processes. Thus, we propose H3.

## H3: Service provider’s process alignment positively affects a client’s BPO commitment.

Prior work on outsourcing has emphasized the importance of a service provider’s flexibility in addressing the uncertainty of the outsourcing task and in strengthening the relationship with the client [10, 22, 43, 44, 45]. The service provider should be able to process the outsourced task in a flexible way such that the client’s change of contractual or functional aspects and design features can be addressed in a timely manner. Process flexibility reflects a service provider’s ability to respond to shifts in a client’s preferences, technological and requirement changes, and market opportunities [1, 8, 34]. Strategic management research reveals that one of the underlying reasons for a client to outsource is to leverage the service providers’ capabilities to exploit opportunities in the market and to reduce competitors’ threats [46]. Outsourcing studies suggest that outsourcing outcomes are affected by the extent to which a service provider is able to use resources in a flexible way. These include a service provider’s domain understanding (e.g., knowledge about clients’ procedures and applications, and client-based market opportunities), adaptability (e.g., ability to adapt processes to meet changes), change management (e.g., ability to help the client manage the change prompted by market opportunities), and flexibility of using resources [13, 47, 48]. Others also found that the service provider’s flexibility in providing functional and technical services (e.g. interoperability and modularity of the outsourced applications) and modifying contractual parameters positively affected the client’s commitment to collaborate with the service provider [22]. As process flexibility seeks to provide relationship-specific services and helps the client gain competitive advantage, we posit that a client will form positive perception toward the service provider and have more confidence on the outsourcing relationship if the client perceives that the service provider has process flexibility, leading to H4.

## H4: Service provider’s process flexibility positively affects a client’s BPO commitment.

## Governance: Client’s behavior control

Governance plays a critical role in creating relational value through its influence on transaction costs and partners’ willingness to engage in value-creation initiatives [25]. Literature on outsourcing has broadly classified governance as formal and informal control [17, 23, 24]. Formal control mechanisms are well documented in contracts (e.g. outcome control and behavior control), while informal control is usually implicit (e.g., social control and relational norms) [12, 28]. Formal control is viewed as an essential ingredient to successful outsourcing relationships [17]. Recent outsourcing research reveals that a greater number of studies emphasize formal control mechanisms than informal mechanisms due to the difficulty in forging shared norms and values. Outcome control focuses on a client’s pre-specification of desired outputs provided by the service provider [24]. Behavior control refers to the extent to which the service provider achieves the objectives of BPO according to a set of rules and procedures that the client expects the service provider to follow [24].

Outcome control does not pay attention to how a service provider achieves the outsourced processes. Since the emphasis of BPO is to transform and improve business processes, this study focuses on behavior control rather than outcome control for two reasons. First, research has found that firms are more likely to outsource non-critical and less complex business processes to outside vendors [49]. Therefore, it is likely that clients may have a set of rules, methods, and procedures for a vendor to follow. Second, research suggests that “interfirm interaction during the process of exchanging behavior control evaluation information can foster social relations and strengthen the socialized acceptance of shared values and beliefs” ([24], p. 98), thus strengthening the client and service provider relationship. Since behavior control plays a key role in building a sustainable relationship, we expect that a client’s commitment will increase if a service provider is willing to comply with the client’s prescribing procedures for reaching BPO goals [13, 17, 23].

Behavior control seeks to reduce the negative effect (e.g., the service provider’s opportunistic behavior) caused by bounded rationality and uncertainty in outsourcing relationships [10, 23]. Besides, behavior control also reflects the service provider’s willingness and ability to handle the outsourced task based on the client’s expectation. Thus, the more a client perceives that a service provider’s behavior is controlled, in terms of its adherence to the methods and procedures prescribed by the client, the more likely that the client has the confidence in the stability of the outsourcing relationship and has the desire to maintain the relationship with the service provider [17, 24]. This premise is consistent with the findings reported in prior outsourcing research. For example, some studies found that the evaluation information obtained through direct monitoring and a service provider’s self-report (e.g., periodic meeting and ongoing documentations of work processes) played a key role in providing the client and the service provider with shared expectations about appropriate behavior, which in turn promoted solidarity in the relationship [23, 50]. Hence, we present H5.

## H5: Client’s behavior control positively affects its BPO commitment.

## 3. METHOD

This study aims to explore the influence of interfirm capabilities on BPO commitment. We draw on the relational view to identify the key factors that affect commitment. Five independent variables were selected to examine their impact on a client’s BPO commitment. This study used a firm level of analysis to investigate BPO relationships in Taiwan. A survey method was conducted to test the proposed hypotheses. We designed the questionnaire by choosing the measurements that have been validated in prior research.

## 3.1. Sample and data collection

We identified 700 firms with outsourcing experience through the assistance of Market Intelligence & Consulting Department under the Institute for Information Industry in Taiwan. Similar to previous outsourcing and organizational studies [10, 23], we use a key informant methodology for our data collection. Following prior work on interfirm relational value creation [23, 33], this study uses a single-respondent approach to collect data from client firms. Senior IT executives were chosen as the key informants due to their knowledge about outsourcing processes. Of the 700 distributed surveys, 233 responses were received. We discarded 66 responses, including 38 with missing data and 52 from respondents who have less than 5 years outsourcing experience. To evaluate common method biases that may occur in questionnaire-based studies eliciting responses for the predicator and criterion variables from the same source, we used the technique of counterbalancing question order and psychological separation of measurement suggested by Podsakoff et al. ## 4. DISCUSSION

This study seeks to understand how key relational value affects commitment in BPO relationships. Drawing on the relational view and the outsourcing literature, we examine three types of capabilities from a client and a service provider—knowledge-based capabilities, process-based capabilities and governance. The results confirm that these capabilities and the governance mechanism influence a client’s commitment. Our results are consistent with prior outsourcing studies that three key antecedents play an important role in outsourcing outcomes--“knowledge” (e.g., a client and service provider’s knowledge sharing) [13, 56], “process” (e.g., coordination of interdependent processes) [3] and “governance” (e.g., behavior control) [17, 24]. It is important to note that these antecedents have been considered separately in previous studies that focus on either a control perspective or a capability perspective of an individual outsourcing partner (a client or a service provider). In contrast, this study takes a comprehensive perspective and examines the influence of antecedents related to knowledge, process, and governance capabilities on interfirm outsourcing relationship management.

Our work contributes to the development of the BPO relationship model that encompasses the influence of interfirm resources. The theoretical model provides a perspective for BPO partners to leverage their interfirm resources through knowledge sharing, collaboration, and resource combination (e.g., process alignment). In other words, relational value can be enhanced by combining a client’s and a service provider’s resources (i.e., knowledge, knowledge-sharing routines, and interfirm interactions), and by governing a service provider’s behavior. Positive BPO outcomes are likely to be realized when outsourcing relationships are well managed.

Four of five hypotheses are supported, which offers strong evidence to strengthen many of our theoretical arguments. First, our results show that the service provider’s task-knowledge coordination is the strongest predictor of client’s commitment. Task-knowledge coordination focuses on knowledge sharing and learning by collaborating with outsourcing partners. This implies that the client treats the service provider’s knowledge management capabilities as the critical capability that motivates the continuance of outsourcing relationships. Our conceptualization of task-knowledge coordination emphasizes the service provider’s capability in facilitating information sharing, increasing interactions, and leveraging the client’s domain knowledge or tacit knowledge. These findings deepen our understanding about how interfirm knowledge-sharing based on Dyer and Singh’s [25] framework can be implemented in BPO.

Second, our results suggest that process-based capabilities play an important role in strengthening BPO commitment. Prior work on interfirm partnership views coordinated interfirm activities as a key factor to improve performance [33, 34]. Process-based capabilities in terms of a service provider’s process alignment and process flexibility make the knowledge sharing and collaboration feasible. Third, commitment is also driven by the client’s ability to manage the service provider’s behavior. In addition to the service provider’s capability and knowledge in processing the outsourced task, the extent to which the client has confidence in the stability of the relationship relies on its ability to monitor and control the service provider’s behavior.

Finally, the only unsupported hypothesis is the influence of a client’s absorptive capacity on commitment—positive but insignificant. It is plausible that the conceptualization of a client’s absorptive capacity does not precisely capture the spirit of learning from external resources and applying the learned knowledge to BPO. The other possible explanation is that the importance of absorptive capacity in explaining commitment is relatively weaker than other factors. This is because, compared to the other four capabilities associated with the client and service provider relationship, absorptive capacity is a relatively broader concept and may apply beyond an sourcing relationship. For example, prior work has found that a firm’s absorptive capability influenced organizational agility and enterprise system assimilation [51, 71, 72]. In addition, absorptive capacity was recognized as a multidimensional construct that involve potential capacity reflected in knowledge acquisition and assimilation and realized capacity reflected in knowledge transformation and exploitation [51]. Future research may want to embrace the multidimensional nature of the absorptive capacity concept and theorize how various dimensions of absorptive capacity influence BPO relationships. Future studies may also investigate factors that moderate the relationship between absorptive capacity and commitment such as product complexity or environmental turbulence [3, 8].

## Theoretical implications

Overall, our empirical findings have provided strong support for theorizing BPO commitment from the relational view. Prior work has offered a limited understanding of how interfirm capabilities can be leveraged to improve outsourcing outcomes in the BPO context [9, 13]. This study contributes new knowledge by proposing a knowledge-process-governance framework that explains how three types of interfirm resources, knowledge-based capabilities, process-based capabilities, and governance, affect commitment. Although prior outsourcing studies emphasized the importance of using a long-term orientation to examine outsourcing success [12, 13], empirical research that provides a comprehensive assessment of how BPO commitment can be achieved by leveraging relationship-specific resources remains lacking. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first empirical studies that focuses on BPO commitment and its antecedents from a relational view. Our theoretical model provides an encompassing and theoretically grounded framework on how a client and a service provider may co-develop strategies to increase BPO commitment through their knowledge coordination, process alignment, process flexibility, and governance mechanisms. Our results provide a new perspective on the role of the client and the service provider, and how their knowledge, processes and control should be managed together to increase outsourcing commitment.

This study also contributes to the relational view literature by broadening the conceptualization and measurement of resources that extend beyond firm boundaries to facilitate collaboration between outsourcing partners. Our study answers the call by Lacity et al. [13] for BPO research to focus on a long-term orientation and strategic management. Besides, our work extends prior work on outsourcing commitment [11, 23] by focusing on the antecedents that allow outsourcing partners to work together to leverage their relationship-specific resources. Our work draws on the relational view [25] and investigates the role of both a client and a service provider in improving BPO outcomes. In particular, our theorizing draws upon the characteristics of outsourced processes and how relationship-specific resources can be better utilized to improve the success of BPO relationships. Our study offers fine-grained knowledge about how the key antecedents derived from Dyer and Singh’s [25] theory influence commitment in the context of BPO. The high level of variance explained by these antecedents indicates that this study provides a more holistic and integrative understanding of the relationship between relational value and outsourcing outcomes through commitment.

## Practical implications

From a practical perspective, understanding the formation of commitment helps both the client and the service provider to leverage its key resources and capabilities to improve outsourcing relationships and outcomes. Our results suggest that service providers seeking to increase the collaboration and strengthen the outsourcing relationship should focus their efforts on task-knowledge coordination, process alignment and process flexibility to improve outsourcing performance and a client’s commitment. From a client’s point of view, effective governance through control over a service provider’s behavior can improve long-term BPO relationships.

Although the effect of a client’s absorptive capacity on commitment is not significant, this does not imply that the client’s ability to learn new knowledge, assimilate it and apply it to outsourcing is not important. Rather, a client’s absorptive capacity that is specific to outsourcing management may be helpful in enhancing BPO commitment. A client’s absorptive capacity reflects its ability or intention to acquire, assimilate, and exploit knowledge related to IT applications or processes [13, 71, 72]. A client firm that has a higher degree of absorptive capacity is likely to keep current with IT and process innovation and continue to experiment with new IT or IT-enabled processes. The client’s process knowledge and process innovation enable it to have productive knowledge sharing and interfirm learning with its service provider.

Service providers can use our findings to develop actionable strategies to improve BPO relationships with clients through task-knowledge coordination. Task-knowledge coordination reflects a provider’s willingness and effort to invest in relationships with clients. Task-knowledge coordination in BPO often entails the coordination between various types of knowledge such as client-specific knowledge about business processes, a service provider’s knowledge about business process improvement and IT-enabled business process improvement, and the integration of a client’s and service provider’s knowledge and BPO tasks. BPO tasks may involve relationship-specific routines, standard operating processes to execute outsourced tasks, and processes that are idiosyncratic to a client. A service provider that can perform task-knowledge coordination well will be able to strategically lock clients into the BPO relationship.

A service provider should work closely with its client by taking advantage of a client’s complementary resources. A service provider’s capability to leverage complementary resources relies on its ability to develop complementary BPO processes by considering how the IT capability, operating procedures, and business processes between the client and the service provider can by aligned. In particular, a service provider should work with a client to mutually adjust their plans and actions to match the BPO tasks and relationships. A service provider’s process flexibility allows it to effectively reduce uncertainty caused by shifts in a client’s preferences, technological change, and changes in market opportunities. For example, due to business and technological uncertainties in BPO [4], a service provider should have the flexibility to make adjustments in response to uncertainties and grasp client-based opportunities, such as accommodating changes in the client’s business requirements and rich interaction routines to reconsider BPO decisions (e.g., delays in BPO completion, quality problems), share risks, and respond to unforeseen contingencies.

Finally, the risk of opportunism and uncertainty in collaboration must be controlled and managed. Behavior control reflects a client’s formal control over its service provider through explicitly prescribing procedures. Some of the initiatives that a service provider can use to show the client that it adheres to the prescribed procedures are weekly progress reports or ongoing documentation of work processes.

## Limitations and future research

This research has a few limitations. First, it is plausible that some other factors beyond those included in this study may exert influence on commitment. For example, commitment may be evaluated from other perspectives such as an economic perspective or a social perspective. However, it is important to note that most outsourcing studies that rely on an economic perspective mainly focused on understanding the choice of governance mode in economic activities. As a result, relationship-specific investment that emphasizes the development and management of assets (e.g., knowledge, service, customized business processes) that are important to outsourcing relationships has been overlooked. Second, while this study focuses on how BPO partners should leverage interfirm resources (knowledge-based and process-based capabilities) to improve commitment, extant literature has suggested that resources and capabilities exert varying degree of influence on value creation [57]. To address this gap, future work may consider different mechanisms of how resource structuring and capability building affect commitment. Third, it is possible that environmental characteristics and process complexity may moderate the relationship between process-based capabilities and commitment. This is because environmental turbulence and process complexity may affect the influence of resource management on performance. Fourth, we collected data to validate the influence of relational value on BPO commitment from a client’s perspective. We recognize that a client and service provider dyad approach would give us triangulated evidence of the knowledge-based, process-based and governance capabilities that underlie the BPO relationships. However, we believe that this should not be a major problem since our study focuses on perceptions of clients on a service provider’s capabilities. Finally, while cross-sectional surveys have been adopted by most empirical studies on interfirm value creation [1, 4, 5, 23, 41], these surveys are limited in establishing causality. Future work may use longitudinal survey and an in-depth process-oriented approach to offer rich causal explanation on the formation of commitment.

## 5. CONCLUSION

The goal of this study is to answer the research question—what types of resources or capabilities are critical to commitment in BPO relationship? This research views commitment as one of the important outsourcing outcomes. It extends Dyer and Singh’s [25] relational view and proposes the knowledge-process-governance framework to examines the influence of knowledge-based capabilities, process-based capabilities and governance on BPO commitment. The proposed model and hypotheses are largely supported by the data, showing that commitment is affected by the service provider’s task-knowledge coordination, the service provider’s process alignment, the service provider’s process flexibility, and the client’s behavior control. Our results provide insights into how collaboration between the client and the service provider can be enhanced by leveraging the resources and capabilities from the interfirm relationship.
