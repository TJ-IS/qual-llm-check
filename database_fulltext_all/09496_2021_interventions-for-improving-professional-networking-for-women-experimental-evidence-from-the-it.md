# INTERVENTIONS FOR IMPROVING PROFESSIONAL NETWORKING FOR WOMEN: EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FROM THE IT SECTOR<sup>1</sup>

Sofia Bapna Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {sbapna@umn.edu}

Russell J. Funk

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {rfunk@unm.edu}

Professional networks are vital for individuals’ career advancement. Research demonstrates, however, that women are often disadvantaged in their access to such networks. Using a randomized field experiment at an IT conference, we found that women had worse networking outcomes than men. Relative to men, women met 42% fewer new contacts, spent 48% less time talking to them, and added 25% fewer LinkedIn connections. We theorize that in fields where women are underrepresented (e.g., IT) two networking barriers—search and social—differentially affect men and women. We designed and experimentally tested interventions for reducing these barriers. The search intervention was designed to facilitate locating diverse contacts and information. The social intervention was designed to facilitate helping behavior and connecting across social boundaries. Our findings indicate that the search intervention increased the number of new contacts women met by 57%, the time they spent talking with them by 90%, the number of LinkedIn connections they added by 29%, and their odds of changing jobs by a factor of 1.6. The social intervention also increased the time women spent talking to new contacts by 66%. However, the interventions did not improve men’s outcomes. Our results show that simple interventions can help women expand their networks and find jobs.

Keywords: IT workers, women in IT, gender gaps, social network analysis, networking, randomized field experiment; career mobility

## Introduction

Over the past few decades, social scientists have shown that professional networks are vital for career advancement (Podolny and Barron 1997; Cross and Thomas 2008). Professional networks are important because they help individuals meet relevant and influential people (Burt 1992; Wuchty et al. 2007; Dahlander and McFarland

2013), identify resources and opportunities (e.g., job leads) (Granovetter 1995; Yakubovich 2005; Wolff and Moser 2009), and acquire new knowledge and skills (Allen 1977; Hansen 1999; Durbin 2011).

Against this backdrop, however, studies have also revealed evidence of gender inequality in access to valuable social networks (Ibarra 1992, 1993; Burt 1998; Singh et al. 2010). For example, studies report that relative to men, women have fewer collaborators (McDowell et al. 2006), are frequently excluded from informal corporate networks (Ahuja 2002; Trauth et al. 2009; Armstrong et al. 2018), often have close colleagues who are disproportionately women (Kleinbaum et al. 2013), tend to have fewer indirect and bridging connections (Singh et al. 2010), and tend to be less connected to mentors, an important source of career advice (Ahuja 2002). In sum, women’s professional networks tend to be structured in ways that may hinder their career advancement.

Women’s professional network disadvantage is likely to be exacerbated in settings like information technology (IT), where they are outnumbered by men and where they disproportionately occupy lower-status jobs. Although IT is among the fastest-growing sectors in the United States, with employment rising from 450,000 in 1970 to 4.6 million in 2014 (Beckhusen 2016), only 25% of IT professionals are women, and women’s representation in IT has declined from its peak of 31% in 1990 (Beckhusen 2016). Compounding these disadvantages in representation, research reports that women in IT continue to face persistent stereotypes regarding their aptitude (Ahuja 2002; Michie and Nelson 2006; Cheryan et al. 2013), which is likely to further inhibit women’s ability to build their professional networks.

Although prior research has documented systematic differences in men’s and women’s networks, less is known about the factors that contribute to these differences. One possibility, suggested by social network theory, is that women may face greater barriers when networking. Understood as “individuals’ attempts to develop and maintain relationships with others who have the potential to assist them in their work or career” (Forret and Dougherty 2001, p. 283), networking is a critical means by which individuals meet new people and identify opportunities (Forret 2014; Casciaro et al. 2014; Ingram and Morris 2007).

Motivated by these observations, this study addresses three related research questions. First, do women in IT experience worse professional networking outcomes than men? Second, if professional networking outcomes are worse for women, what are the factors that contribute to these gender differences? Finally, can women’s professional networking outcomes be improved?

Building on insights from theories of social networks, we posit that two categories of barriers—search and social— differentially affect women’s networking. Search barriers encompass the challenges of identifying and locating potential contacts who can provide useful information (Singh et al. 2010). These kinds of contacts are often located in distant parts of an individual’s network (i.e., beyond his or her immediate network neighborhood) (Burt 2000). Social barriers encompass the challenges of interaction, particularly across social boundaries (e.g., gender) (Ibarra 1992, 1993; Burt 1998; Piskorski 2014). We theorize that both search and social barriers may be higher for women than men, especially in settings like IT, where women are the minority and where they occupy lower-status positions.

In such settings, search barriers are likely to be greater for women because women’s networks only partially overlap with men’s networks (Aldrich 1989) and women tend to be less connected, less central, and have fewer indirect and bridging connections than their male counterparts (Ibarra 1992). Social barriers are also likely to be greater for women due to the combination of individuals’ preference for interacting with those that are similar to themselves (Ibarra 1992), negative stereotypes regarding the respective minority group (Kanter 1977; Younkin and Kuppuswamy 2017; Greenberg and Mollick 2017), and ambiguity about the social norms associated with interacting with individuals of the opposite gender (Piscorski 2014).

To examine the research questions posed above, we conducted a randomized field experiment in partnership with a nonprofit organization that hosts IT events in the area of data analytics. We designed two interventions aimed at reducing search and social barriers and examined their effects on networking outcomes and job change after the event. The networking outcomes we evaluated include the number of new contacts met, time spent talking with new contacts, and the number of connections added on LinkedIn (an online professional networking platform). The interventions entailed exposing subjects to other conference participants through an email that was sent prior to the conference by the conference organizer. The design of the search intervention was motivated by prior research on social networks (e.g., Granovetter 1995; Burt 1998). In this intervention, individuals received nonreciprocal contact lists (if A was on B’s list, B was not necessarily on A’s), presented as “networking recommendations.” Through this intervention, subjects were indirectly connected—via the recommendations of their recommendations—to many other conference participants (since an individual’s direct recommendations could introduce them to their own recommendations), which may facilitate locating diverse contacts and useful information. The design of the social intervention was motivated by prior research on the social psychology of groups (e.g., Levine et al. 2005; Tajfel et al. 1971). In this intervention, individuals received reciprocal contact lists, presented as “networking introductions.” Through this intervention, subjects were members of small groups, which may facilitate helping behavior and connecting across social boundaries.

We found that at, baseline, on multiple dimensions, women experience significantly worse professional networking outcomes than men. Relative to men, women met 42% fewer new contacts, spent 48% less time talking to them, and added 25% fewer contacts on LinkedIn. Our findings also indicate that lowering search and social barriers is effective at improving women’s networking outcomes, with the former intervention offering the most consistent benefits. Reducing search barriers increased the number of new contacts women met by 57%, the time they spent talking with new contacts by 90%, the number of connections women added on LinkedIn by 29%, and women’s odds of changing jobs by a factor of 1.6. Lowering social barriers also increased the time women spent talking to new contacts by 66%. However, the interventions did not improve men’s outcomes. We demonstrate that these results are robust to many alternative model specifications, including network autocorrelation models (NAMs) and models with clustered errors, which help mitigate concerns about violations of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

Our study contributes to the literature on IT workers, particularly research on gender inequality. Studies in this area have identified gender gaps in the compensation (Levina and Xin 2007; Truman and Baroudi 1994), advancement (Langer et al. 2020; Igbara and Baroudi 1995), and mobility (Joseph et al. 2015; Joseph et al. 2007) of male and female IT workers. Additionally, as discussed above, prior work indicates that compared to men, women in IT tend to systematically occupy disadvantaged positions in professional networks (Ahuja 2002; Trauth et al. 2009; Armstrong et al. 2018). Existing literature has devoted less attention, however, to identifying how women may be able to expand their professional networks, which may be important for reducing women’s professional network disadvantage. We fill this gap in prior work by theorizing and experimentally evaluating the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing barriers to women’s networking. We show that that simple, low-cost interventions can be effective at improving networking for women. Finally, our findings add to research on IT workers by suggesting that a novel factor—professional networking barriers—may be important for understanding women’s career trajectories in IT and, consequently, gender gaps in representation, particularly because valuable information such as job leads flows through professional relationships.

In addition to contributing to research on IT workers, our findings add to the literature on gender differences in social networks. Although our study is not the first to examine gender differences in networking, most prior research in this area has been qualitative in nature (e.g., using ethnography or interviews) and primarily focuses on differences in men’s and women’s networking behaviors (e.g., gendered networking practices) (Berger et al. 2015; Xu and Martin 2011; Benschop 2009). We extend this literature by exploring differences in networking outcomes, which helps paint a more complete picture of gender differences in networking. Additionally, existing research has generally been directed at characterizing differences in the structure of men’s and women’s professional networks and theorizing about the mechanisms that may contribute to women’s disadvantaged network positions (Ibarra 1992, 1993; McDowell et al., 2006; Kleinbaum et al. 2013). Far less work has considered potential mechanisms for helping women build their networks. Our study therefore makes a contribution to the literature by providing a deeper understanding of two barriers—search and social—that may contribute to gender differences in networking outcomes. In particular, by using an experimental approach to explore the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing these barriers, we are able to offer insight into the types of interventions that are likely to be most effective at moving the needle on particular networking-related outcomes.

## Search and Social Barriers

## Search Barriers

Search barriers encompass the challenges of locating potential contacts who can provide useful information. For example, a person looking for a job may be trying to identify which employers are in need of his or her skills. According to Burt (1992, p. 64), “everything else constant, a large, diverse network is the best guarantee of having a contact present where useful information is aired.” This is because people tend to live in clusters of individuals with whom they have strong relations, and within which information circulates rapidly; ties that connect an individual to other clusters (i.e., ties that span “structural holes” between groups) tend to be the source of nonredundant and therefore useful information such as job leads (Burt 2000).

Building a diverse network of contacts may be particularly challenging for women in settings like IT, where they are outnumbered by men and where they disproportionately occupy lower-status jobs. First, in such settings, women are generally less connected (Ahuja 2002; Armstrong et al. 2018), less central, and have fewer indirect and bridging connections (Ibarra 1992), making it harder for them to connect with individuals in other clusters who are likely to be sources of nonredundant information. Second, since women in such settings tend to occupy more peripheral network positions, they may be less visible to others (Podolny 2001) and may therefore be less likely to benefit from unsolicited introductions, which may be a useful source of information. Third, women’s network connections tend to span a “female world” in which their contacts only partially overlap with those of their male counterparts (Aldrich 1989). Consequently, in settings that are predominantly male, women are likely to have less accurate cognitive maps of “who knows what” within their fields<sup>2</sup> and are likely to have fewer contacts that can provide useful information (Singh et al. 2010) such as job leads. In summary, in fields where women are the minority and where they occupy lower-status positions, their disadvantaged network positions (low centrality, low number of indirect and bridging connections, and mostly female connections) are likely to limit their ability to network effectively.

## Social Barriers

Social barriers encompass the challenges of interaction, particularly across social boundaries (e.g., gender) (Ibarra 1992, 1993; Burt 1998; Benschop, 2009; Xu and Martin, 2011; Piskorski 2014; Berger et al. 2015). In fields, like IT, where they are a minority, women may face higher social barriers when networking, which may in turn cause them to interact with fewer people. First, research suggests that people prefer to interact with others who are similar to themselves (Ibarra 1992). In professional fields where women are the minority, such tendencies may negatively affect women’s networking by constraining the set of potential contacts with whom they interact. Second, research suggests that members of the majority group (men) may consciously or unconsciously avoid socializing with members of the minority group (women) because women are often viewed with negative stereotypes or generalizations (Kanter 1977; Mehra et al. 1998; Brands and Kilduff 2013). To overcome stereotypes regarding their technical or management capabilities or prioritization of family over careers (Ahuja 2002; Trauth et al. 2009; Joseph et al. 2015), gender biases (Ahuja 2002), and gender-role expectations (Igbaria and Baroudi 1995), women typically need to work harder than men to demonstrate their legitimacy to potential contacts (Burt 1998; Trauth et al. 2009). Third, ambiguity over norms of interaction with the opposite sex may narrow women's networking. A recent poll by the New York Times finds that many men and women are wary of one-on-one interactions with those of the opposite sex (Miller 2017). Empirically, Piscorski (2014) observes that there is very little crossgender communication on social networking platforms for job seekers. He posits that the reason underlying this behavior is the fear that such messaging may be viewed as a normative violation or an unintended expression of romantic interest. In fields where women are underrepresented, such cautionary behavior is likely to affect women more than men because it limits their interactions with members of the opposite sex, who constitute a large proportion of the profession (and therefore potential contacts). In summary, in fields where women are outnumbered by men, sex-based homophily, stereotypes regarding the minority group, and ambiguity over norms of interacting with those of the opposite gender are likely to limit women’s ability to network effectively.

## Experimental Setting, Measures, and Methods

To address our research questions regarding the existence and extent of gender gaps in networking and the effects (and possibility) of reducing search and social barriers, we conducted a randomized field experiment in partnership with a nonprofit organization that hosts data analytics events. We chose this context because networking events such as conferences and mixers are conducive to relationship formation (Feld 1981) and are common both within and across organizations. In particular, the weak and diverse ties that result from networking events may be more valuable sources of information (e.g., on job opportunities) than close contacts (Granovetter 1995).

Subjects in our study were individuals who registered to attend a daylong conference—TheConference (name disguised)—organized by our partner. 1,161 individuals registered for TheConference, of which 311 (27%) were women and 850 (73%) were men. This gender distribution corresponds closely to the larger IT sector, where Census Bureau data show that 25% of jobs are held by women (Beckhusen 2016). Individuals who registered to attend TheConference had 717 distinct job titles and belonged to companies with 607 distinct names.<sup>3</sup> 216 individuals were senior managers, of which 41 (19%) were women and 175 (81%) were men. This distribution is also consistent with the larger IT sector, where senior leadership is predominantly male (Ashcraft et al. 2016).

## Interventions for Reducing Search and Social Barriers

## Random Assignment to Treatment and Control Groups

After exclusions,<sup>4</sup> the subject pool of 958 individuals (264 [28%] women, 694 [72%] men) was randomly assigned to one of three groups (cf. Hasan and Bagde 2012, 2015)—a Control group (n = 312; 86 women, 226 men), a Search List treatment group (n = 323; 89 women, 234 men) and a Social List treatment group (n = 323; 89 women, 234 men). Individuals in the Control group received a generic email prompting them to network with others at TheConference. The goal of the Search List and Social List treatments, described below, was to reduce search and social barriers, respectively.

## Reducing Search Barriers

The Search List intervention was designed to lower search barriers by minimizing intermediaries between subjects and other conference participants. Individuals in this treatment condition received an email from the conference organizer with a list of 16 target contacts, presented as “networking recommendations.” Targets were randomly selected from among those in the same treatment condition and were not reciprocal—if A received B as a recommendation, B did not necessarily receive A. Put differently, B received a list of 16 recommendations that did not necessarily overlap with A’s recommendations. Thus, A was one hop (i.e., network “step”) away from 16 people (A’s direct recommendations) but two hops away from about 256 individuals (the individuals who appeared on the lists that were sent to A’s direct recommendations). Through their recommendations—and the recommendations of their recommendations—subjects were indirectly connected to many other conference participants (e.g., A’s recommendations could introduce them to their own recommendations). Thus, through this intervention, the network distance between individuals in the Search List condition is likely to be reduced. The left-side panel of Figure 1 shows the network structure of recommendations for the Search List treatment condition.

The design of the Search List treatment was motivated by prior research on social networking (e.g., Granovetter 1995; Burt 1998), which suggests that useful resources tend to flow through fortuitous interactions with weak ties. Relative to the network structure of those who network organically (our Control condition), the network structure for the Search List treatment is likely to be more conducive to accessing nonredundant contacts (since individuals can benefit from their direct recommendations and the recommendations of their recommendations), who can offer valuable information benefits (Burt 1998). This is true particularly for women, whose contacts may only partially overlap with those of their male counterparts (Aldrich 1989).

## Reducing Social Barriers

The Social List intervention was designed to lower social barriers by enhancing in-group social identity and therefore facilitating interactions between subjects and other conference participants. Individuals in this treatment condition received an email from the conference organizer with a list of 16 target contacts, presented as “networking introductions.” Unlike in the Search List treatment, where recommendations were nonreciprocal, introductions in the Social List condition were reciprocal—if A was introduced to B, then B was introduced to A. Targets were randomly selected from among those in the same treatment condition. The 323 individuals in the Social List treatment were thus randomly assigned to 19 groups of 17 individuals each. The treatment email informed subjects that everyone in their group (i.e., the other individuals in the email) had been introduced through a similar message. The email also suggested that recipients connect with people in their group and let them know that the conference organizer recommended they meet.

The design of the Social List treatment is motivated by research in the social psychology of groups. This work demonstrates that group membership facilitates helping behavior (Levine et al. 2005) and improves individuals’ confidence (McMillan 1996) by enhancing in-group social identity (Levine et al. 2005), even among people with no prior interactions and in groups based on trivial criteria (Tajfel et al. 1971). Thus, through this intervention, the social barriers that individuals face when networking across social boundaries are likely to be lowered. Specifically, the Social List intervention should increase subjects’ likelihood of meeting and helping people who differ from themselves (e.g., on gender) by enhancing ingroup social identity. Thus, the intervention is likely to alleviate some of the segregating effects related to individuals’ tendencies to network with those that are similar to themselves (i.e., sex-based homophily) (Ibarra 1992), the majority group’s avoidance of socialization with members of the minority group because of negative stereotypes and biases (Kanter 1977), and the fear of signaling romantic interest (Piscorski 2014). The right-side panel of Figure 1 shows the network structure of introductions for the Social List treatment condition. The differences between the Search List and the Social List interventions are summarized in Appendix A, Table A1.

## Implementation of the Experiment

Treatment and control group emails were sent simultaneously, four days before the conference. The emails included a link to a web application through which individuals could search for and message other conference participants. Recommendations and introductions included targets’ names, job titles, and companies. The subject line of the emails was, “Important information about your upcoming day at TheConference.”

Figure 2 shows the email sent to subjects in the Control group. Figures 3 and 4 show the top section of the emails sent to the Search List and Social List treatment groups, respectively. In the full treatment emails, 16 targets are listed, using the same format as targets one through six in Figures 3 and 4.<sup>5</sup> Below the list of targets, the emails included the conference date, location, and instructions for search and messaging; this information is identical that provided in paragraphs two and three of the main body of the email in Figure 2.

Individuals in the subject pool included 174 senior managers (33 [19%] women, 141 [81%] men), 13 founders, and 44 students. To ensure balance on these characteristics across target contact lists, we first randomly assigned the women such that there were 89 in each of the treatment groups and 86 in the Control group. Second, we randomly assigned founders such that there were four founders in each of the treatment groups and five in the Control group. Third, we randomly assigned students such that there were 14 in each of the treatment groups and 16 in the Control group. Following this, the remaining subjects were randomly assigned to one of the three groups. Thus, we guaranteed that subjects’ target lists were balanced on these observable characteristics. Specifically, we ensured that target lists did not include more than one founder, one student, five women, and two individuals from the same organization. Balancing helped to alleviate concerns that our results may be affected by imbalance in the targets—e.g., more women in the target lists for individuals in the Social List or Search List group could drive greater homophilous networking in the treatment with more women.

Randomly assigning target contacts (that are balanced on observable characteristics) helped identify a baseline effect of the Search and Social List interventions. If a matching algorithm had been used to identify targets (rather than random assignment), we might have observed different (perhaps better) results. To confirm that subjects were assigned to groups randomly, we used t-tests that compared group means on observable characteristics. ## Dependent Variables

Our dependent variables capture networking outcomes and job change. These variables were created using data from a post-conference survey and from LinkedIn.<sup>8</sup> Following its conferences, the event organizer asked attendees to complete a web survey. Two networking outcomes—Number of New Contacts Met and Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each New Contact—were collected through this survey,<sup>9</sup> which was sent three days after TheConference.

The survey received 238 responses<sup>10</sup> (25%, 62 women and 176 men), which is consistent with response rates reported in recent information systems publications (e.g., Addas and Pinsonneault 2018; Benitez et al. 2018). Two additional outcomes—Job Change and Number of LinkedIn Connections—were collected from LinkedIn. Nearly all subjects—896 (93.5%, 246 women, 650 men)—had LinkedIn profiles.

Job Change is used to examine the effects of our treatments on subjects’ job changes. We collected career histories 12 months before TheConference and 12 months after. Using these data, we created a 25-month panel in which observations are subject × month combinations. Job Change was set to 1 if the subject began a new position in the focal month, and 0 otherwise.

The outcome Number of LinkedIn Connections (collected five months after the conference) was used to corroborate results from the survey. Finding similar results for the Number of New Contacts Met (from the survey data) and the Number of LinkedIn Connections (from the LinkedIn data) added confidence to the survey-based findings. Additionally, concerns that our survey results may be affected by response bias were alleviated because 93.5% of subjects had LinkedIn profiles. Moreover, t-tests (Table 1) indicate that the number of people with LinkedIn profiles across the three groups (293 in Control, 304 in Search List, and 299 in Social List) is not statistically different. Our dependent variables and the time frame for collecting the data for each dependent variable are summarized in Appendix A, Table A3.

## Model Estimation

The dependent variables for our survey-based outcomes— Number of New Contacts Met and Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each New Contact—are counts, meaning that they are neither continuous nor dichotomous and are limited to discrete nonnegative numbers. Although linear regression models are often applied to count outcomes, estimates from such models can be inconsistent or inefficient (Long et al. 2006). We therefore used negative binomial regressions, which are preferable over Poisson models due to overdispersion in our dependent variables. Turning to LinkedIn-based outcomes, for individuals with more than 500 connections, LinkedIn indicates that the person has “500+” connections, rather than giving the actual number. Because Number of LinkedIn Connections is right-censored at 500, we used Tobit regressions.<sup>14</sup> We logged Number of LinkedIn Connections prior to the model estimation. For models of Job Change, given the binary dependent variable and the panel data structure, we estimated logit regressions with subject random effects and standard errors clustered by subject.<sup>15</sup> To facilitate interpretation of the multiple interaction terms, we analyze Job Change for men and women separately.

## Results

## Gender Differences in Networking

We expected that women would have worse networking outcomes than men. To evaluate gender differences in networking outcomes, we compared men and women in the Control group. Figure 7 summarizes these results. Women underperformed men on the Number of New Contacts Met (Table 4, Model $1 \colon \beta _ { F e m a l e } = - 0 . 5 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each New Contact (Table 4, Model $2 \colon \beta _ { F e m a l e } = - 0 . 6 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The gap was substantial. Relative to men, women met 42% fewer new contacts and spent 48% less time talking to them. Additionally, we found that five months after the event, the predicted Number of LinkedIn Connections was 25% lower for women than for men (Table 4, Model 3: $\beta _ { F e m a l e } = - 0 . 2 5 , p <$ 0.05). Finally, following the event, we did not find that the odds of changing jobs were significantly different for men and women (Table 5, Model 1: $\beta _ { P o s t - c o n f e r e n c e \times F e m a l e } = - 0 . 2 5 ,$ $p = 0 . 3 8 )$ . When interpreting this result, it is useful to recall that this test relies only on subjects in the Control group— a small subset of our data—and that the baseline rate of Job Change for both men and women is small (2.3% of person × month observations). Our sample for this test may be insufficiently powered to identify differences between genders on Job Change within the Control group.

Figure 8 provides evidence relating to our prediction that reducing search and social barriers would improve women’s networking outcomes. ## Effect of Treatments on Men

Although our primary focus in this study is on women, for completeness we also analyzed the results for men. Starting with the Search List intervention, the coefficients for Search List in Table 4, Models 1, 2, and 3 are not significant, indicating that for men in the Search List group, the number of new contacts met, the average time spent talking to new contacts, and the number of connections on LinkedIn five months after the event were not significantly different from those of men in the Control group. Regarding the Social List intervention, the coefficients for Social List in Table 4 Model 1, and Model 3 are not significant, indicating that for men in the Social List group, the number of new contacts met and the number of connections on LinkedIn five months after the event were not significantly different from those for men in the Control group. Consistent with our theorizing, assuming that men in IT on average face lower networking barriers than women, organic networking in the Control group (wherein men may gravitate towards others based on homophily or may be introduced to others via common contacts) may enable men to find more relevant contacts, thereby leading to longer conversations.

## Why Women in the Search List Group May Have Benefitted More Consistently

Our results indicate that women in the Search List group benefitted more consistently than women in the Social List group. When interpreting this finding, recall that although the targets assigned in both the Social List and Search List groups were random, targets for subjects in the Social List group were reciprocal while targets for subjects in the Search List group were nonreciprocal. The reciprocal nature of the introductions made in the Social List group implies that as a result of our treatment, any individual in the Social List group was one hop (i.e., network “step”) away from 16 individuals, i.e., those to whom they were introduced (Figure 1, right panel). This is because if individual A was introduced to individual B, then B was also introduced to A.

In contrast, the nonreciprocal nature of the recommendations provided in the Search List group implies that as a result of our treatment, any individual in the Search List group was one hop away from their 16 direct recommendations and two hops away from about 256 individuals (Figure 1, left panel). This is because if individual A received individual B as a recommendation, B did not necessarily receive A. In other words, B received a list of 16 recommendations that did not necessarily overlap with A’s recommendations. Thus, A was one hop away from 16 people (A’s direct recommendations), but two hops away from about 256 individuals (the individuals who appeared on the lists that were sent to A’s direct recommendations). Through their recommendations—and the recommendations of their recommendations—subjects in the Search List group were indirectly connected to many other conference participants. Thus, in this intervention, an individual’s direct recommendations could introduce them to their own recommendations. By design, this was not possible in the Social List intervention, since the introductions made in the Social List intervention were reciprocal.

In summary, the distance between any two individuals in the Search List group—regardless of whether either was directly recommended to the other—is lower than the analogous distance between any two individuals in the Control group or the Social List group. Relative to the network structure of organic networking (our Control group) and that of the Social List group, the network structure of the Search List group is likely to be more conducive to accessing nonredundant contacts, who can offer valuable information benefits (Burt 1998). This is true particularly for women, who are likely to occupy more peripheral network positions in fields like IT.

Consistent with this view, Huffman and Torres (2002) find that women receive lower quality job leads than their male counterparts because women have significantly more women in their job-tip provider pools than men. Similarly, McDonald (2011) finds that individuals with a larger proportion of women in their occupational networks receive less job-related information, while individuals with a larger proportion of men in their occupational networks tend to have access to more unsolicited job information. Since the Search List intervention gives women access to more nonredundant contacts (16 1-hop connections, and 256 2-hop connections who can be reached via their 1-hop connections) than the Social List intervention (16 1-hop connections), this could explain why women in the Search List group benefit more than do women in the Social List group.

## External and Internal Validity of Our Findings

## Robustness Tests

## Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption

The SUTVA requires that the response of a particular individual depend only on the treatment to which he or she was assigned, not the treatments of others. Network experiments often involve complicated relational structures that may violate the SUTVA (Bapna and Umyarov 2015). Recall that through the Social List intervention, subjects were members of small groups. Thus, responses may be correlated among group members. To mitigate this concern, we estimated negative binomial models that cluster standard errors based on the group to which each individual was introduced.<sup>16</sup> The results for Number of New Contacts Met, Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each New Contact and Number of LinkedIn Connections are shown in Table A7 of Appendix A, Models 1, 2, and 3, respectively.<sup>17</sup> Across the three models, the coefficient estimates for the Social List × Female interaction are consistent with those of our main model (Table 4, Models 1, 2, and 3, respectively) in their direction and significance.<sup>18</sup>

To further evaluate the sensitivity of our findings to the SUTVA, we analyzed our data using NAMs (Doreian 1989, 1990; Ravindran et al. 2015). NAMs are a family of statistical models that are specifically designed to account for interdependence in relational data (Mizruchi et al. 2006; Dow 2007). They are widely used in social network analysis and are based on spatial autocorrelation models developed in geography. The NAM hypothesizes that an observation’s outcomes and errors depend on the other observations to which it is linked. Formally,

$$
\mathrm{y} = \rho_ {1} \mathrm{Wy} + \mathrm{X} \beta + \varepsilon , \varepsilon = \rho_ {2} \mathrm{W} \varepsilon + \mathrm{v},
$$

where W is an adjacency matrix that captures the interdependence among observations and v is a normally distributed error term. ρ<sub>1</sub> and ρ<sub>2</sub> are conceptually analogous to correlation coefficients (although they are not necessarily bound to the range 0-1) that capture the strength of interdependence. We estimate NAMs using maximum likelihood and log count outcomes to meet the distributional assumptions of the model.<sup>19</sup>

We estimated NAMs using two different weight matrices, each of which helps to account for a different potential source of nonindependence. In a first set of models, we created the weight matrix (W<sub>T</sub>) using path distances from the network of introductions and recommendations (Mizruchi et al. 2006). This weight matrix helps to account for potential nonindependence resulting from the assignment of subjects in the same treatment conditions. In a second set of models, we leverage advances in network autocorrelation models (Dow 2007) to introduce a second weight matrix (W<sub>S</sub>) based on the similarity of subjects on visible (to other conference participants) demographic characteristics.<sup>20</sup> The characteristics we incorporate include age, gender, job status (visible via job titles on name badges), and industry (visible via organization names on name badges).<sup>21</sup> This weight matrix helps to account for potential nonindependence resulting from the interaction of subjects (potentially across treatment conditions) based on factors not related to the treatment conditions (cf. Leenders 2002 for a discussion of this approach to defining the weight matrix). According to sociological research, the most powerful predictor of social interaction may be similarity of social attributes (e.g., age, gender) (McPherson et al. 2001; Kossinets and Watts 2009; Dahlander and McFarland 2013). All else being equal, people generally prefer interacting with others who are similar to themselves, and these preferences help account for the significant homophily observed in many social networks.

Therefore, building on this line of research, we predict that if the treatments primed people to engage in more social interaction—including with subjects in other (treatment) groups—subjects would have been most likely to engage in such social interaction with people similar to themselves in social attributes.

Models 1-5 of Appendix A, Tables A8 (W<sub>T</sub>) and A9 (W<sub>T</sub> and W<sub>S</sub>) show the results of the NAMs described above for our five dependent variables (Number of New Contacts Met, Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each New Contact, Number of LinkedIn Connections, Job Change for women, and Job Change for men). The findings are substantively consistent with our main models (Table 4, Models 1, 2, and 3; Table 5, Model 2; Appendix A, Table A4, Model 1, respectively). We do not find either ρ<sub>1</sub> or ρ<sub>2</sub> to be significant in any of the NAMs for our five dependent variables. These results help to alleviate concerns regarding SUTVA violations in our analyses.<sup>22</sup>

## Additional Analyses

## Social List Treatment—Identification as a Group

In the discussion above, we drew on the social psychology literature to theorize that the Social List intervention would work to create helping behavior and feelings of obligation because individuals were members of small groups. To help examine whether our treatment had the proposed effect, we conducted an additional analysis. Sociological theory suggests that senior managers have higher social status by virtue of their position (cf. Blau, 1964). Individuals in higherstatus positions should be relatively more valuable as prospective contacts at conferences and other networking events. However, this implies that senior managers are also likely to have more individuals reaching out to them, thereby reducing their time and feelings of obligation to carry on longer conversations with unfamiliar people (Mayhew and Levinger 1976). If the Social List intervention succeeded in creating a group identity, then we may predict—based on the social psychology literature—that the Social List treatment will counteract these propensities for individuals in senior management positions who received the treatment, thereby leading them to have longer conversations.

In an analysis of senior managers (Appendix A, Table A10) we observe a positive and statistically significant coefficient for Social List × Senior Management, which indicates that senior managers who received the Social List treatment spent more time talking with others at the event. In addition, we do not find any effect of the Search List treatment—in which there was no group identity—on time spent talking to new contacts for individuals in senior management positions. Specifically, the coefficient on the Search List × Senior Management interaction is not significant (Appendix A, Table A10).

## Networking with Existing Contacts

To determine whether networking with new contacts (due to our treatments) may have come at the expense of networking with existing contacts, we examined the effect of our treatments on subjects’ interactions with their existing contacts. To do so, we gathered responses to two additional survey questions: (1) How many existing contacts did the individual meet? (2) How many minutes on average did the individual spend talking to each existing contact? Responses to these questions correspond to the variables Number of Existing Contacts Met and Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each Existing Contact, respectively. The survey questions can be found in Appendix A, Exhibit A1.

The coefficient for Female is significant and negative in Model 1 of Appendix A, Table A11, indicating that among those in the Control group, women met significantly fewer existing contacts than men. In terms of effect size, relative to men, women met 54% fewer existing contacts. Additionally, the coefficient for Female is not significant in Model 2 of Appendix A, Table A11, indicating that among those in the Control group, the average time spent talking with existing contacts was not significantly different for men and women.

In Models 1 and 2 of Appendix A, Table A11, the coefficients for Search List, Social List, Search List × Female, and Social List × Female are not significant, suggesting that relative to the Control group, the treatments did not significantly affect Number of Existing Contacts Met and Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each Existing Contact for either men or women. We also tested and confirmed that there is no significant difference between the two treatment groups (Search List and Social List) on Number of Existing Contacts Met and Average Minutes Spent Talking to Each Existing Contact. Together, these results suggest that networking with new contacts (because of our treatments) did not compromise interactions with existing contacts. The results also confirm that women in our setting were less connected than men.

## Conference Participants’ Goals with Respect to Networking

We show (details in Appendix B, “Conference Participants’ Goals with Respect to Networking”) that meeting people, whether new contacts or existing ones, was the networking goal of 69% of our respondents, suggesting that the conference offered an important outlet for building social connections.

## Manipulation Check, LinkedIn Connections, Clicks on Links in the Emails

Through a manipulation check (details in Appendix B, “Manipulation Check”) we verified that the treatment emails were likely to be interpreted by subjects as intended. We also show (details in Appendix B, “LinkedIn Connections”) that the effects of our interventions remain perceptible over longer durations (i.e., 14 months after the event.). Finally, we found (details in Appendix B, “Clicks on Links in the Emails”) that prior to the conference, people in the Control group were more likely to attempt to identify individuals with whom to network. In contrast, individuals in the Search List and Social List groups were less likely to do so, potentially because individuals in the treatment groups felt that the conference organizers had identified networking leads for them.

## Discussion

Professional networks are vital for individuals’ career advancement because they help people meet relevant and influential contacts, identify resources and opportunities (e.g., job leads), and acquire new knowledge and skills. Against this backdrop, however, previous research has also shown evidence of inequality in access to valuable professional networks, particularly for women (Ibarra 1992, 1993; Burt 1998; Singh et al. 2010). These gender differences in access are likely to be exacerbated in settings, like IT, where women are far outnumbered by men and where they disproportionately occupy lower-status jobs. Motivated by these observations, our study evaluates whether women in IT experience worse professional networking outcomes than men. Further, we explore the factors that contribute to gender differences in professional networking and examine whether women’s professional networking outcomes can be improved. Using a randomized field experiment at an IT conference, we confirmed that women experienced worse professional networking outcomes than men. Specifically, we found that relative to men, women met fewer new contacts, spent less time talking to them, and added fewer connections on LinkedIn.

We theorized that in fields where women are a significant minority and disproportionately occupy lower-status positions, women face greater search and social barriers to networking than men. We then introduced two interventions that were designed to lower these barriers. The search intervention was motivated by prior research on social networks (e.g., Granovetter 1995; Burt 1998). In this intervention, individuals received nonreciprocal contact lists (if A was on B’s list, B was not necessarily on A’s), presented as “networking recommendations.” Through this intervention, subjects were indirectly connected—via the recommendations of their recommendations—to many other conference participants (any individual in the Search List group was one hop away from their 16 direct recommendations and two hops away from about 256 individuals; moreover, an individual’s direct recommendations could introduce them to their own recommendations), which is likely to facilitate locating diverse contacts and thus useful information.

The social intervention was motivated by prior research on the social psychology of groups (e.g., Levine et al. 2005; Tajfel et al. 1971). In this intervention, individuals received reciprocal contact lists (if A was on B’s list, B was also on A’s), presented as “networking introductions.” Through this intervention, subjects were organized into small groups (any individual in the Social List group was one hop away from their 16 reciprocal introductions), which may facilitate helping behavior and connecting across social boundaries. For women, reducing search barriers increased the number of new contacts they met by 57%, the time spent talking with new contacts by 90%, the number of connections added on LinkedIn by 29%, and the odds of changing jobs in the 12-month period after the event by a factor of 1.6. Lowering social barriers increased the time women spent talking to new contacts by 66%. The interventions did not improve men’s outcomes. In summary, women in the Search List group benefitted most consistently.

Our study is not without limitations. First, we do not have the data to show that the control and treatment groups had an equivalent number of LinkedIn connections before the conference. Second, because of data constraints, we were unable to track exactly with whom subjects connected. Finally, our results are based on a single conference, which could potentially raise concerns regarding the generalizability of our findings to the broader IT sector (a detailed discussion on external validity can be found in Appendix A). Notwithstanding these limitations, our study makes several contributions to research and practice.

We make four contributions to the literature on gender and IT workers. Prior research has explored differences between male and female IT workers with respect to compensation (Levina and Xin 2007; Truman and Baroudi 1994), advancement (Langer et al. 2020; Igbara and Baroudi 1995), and mobility (Joseph et al. 2015; Joseph, et al. 2007). First, we extend this literature by identifying the existence of significant gender differences in professional networking outcomes. Using data from LinkedIn to triangulate our findings from survey responses, we find that women experience significantly worse professional networking outcomes than men on multiple dimensions. These include number of new contacts met, time spent with them, connections added on LinkedIn, and job change after the conference.

Second, our study draws on prior work that identifies the obstacles faced by women in IT (e.g., Ahuja 2002; Armstrong et al. 2018; Trauth et al. 2009) to theorize about two barriers—search and social—that may contribute to professional networking differences between men and women, and evaluates the effect of interventions designed to reduce these barriers. We find that women who received the search intervention benefitted most consistently, which suggests that locating nonredundant contacts (who are often a source of useful information) is a major hurdle for women but one that can potentially be addressed. Third, the study demonstrates that simple, low-cost interventions may help to improve women’s networking outcomes. Perhaps most notably, our search intervention increased women’s likelihood of job change, a vital measure of whether the networking contacts met provided useful information.

Finally, our findings suggest that a novel factor— professional networking barriers—may be important for understanding women’s career trajectories in IT. The fast pace at which IT professionals change jobs<sup>23</sup> suggests that networking is critically important for IT professionals career success. Moreover, there are striking differences between male and female IT workers—25% of IT jobs (versus 47% in all occupations) are occupied by women (Beckhusen 2016), the attrition rate in IT is higher for women than men (Hewlett et al. 2008), and the IT workers who tend to remain in the profession throughout their careers are predominantly men (Joseph 2012). Despite efforts to promote equality in representation, the proportion of jobs going to women in IT has declined over the past 25 years (Beckhusen 2016). Within this context, identifying the extent of the gaps in networking outcomes between women and men—and gaining a deeper knowledge of the reasons for these gaps—may be important for understanding women’s careers in IT because valuable information such as job leads flows through professional relationships. To the extent that women face higher barriers in connecting with other professionals, as indicated by our study, gender gaps in networking may also translate to gender gaps in representation.

Our study also makes several contributions to the literature on social networks. First, although gender has become an increasingly important topic in the literature on social networks, most prior work has focused on either characterizing differences in the structures of men’s and women’s professional networks, or theorizing about the mechanisms that lead women to systematically occupy comparatively disadvantaged network positions (Ibarra, 1992, 1993; Burt 1998; Kleinbaum et al. 2013). Much less is known about the factors that may be most helpful for women seeking to build their professional networks. Within this context, we add to the existing literature on social networks by theorizing how two barriers—search and social—may contribute to gender differences in networking outcomes and examining whether interventions aimed at reducing these barriers may be beneficial for women. Further, by considering multiple interventions side by side (i.e., Search List and Social List), our study offers insight into whether some types of interventions may be better for facilitating certain types of networking outcomes over others. While introducing women to small groups of new potential contacts seems to be primarily helpful for increasing the time women spend with new contacts, offering nonreciprocal recommendations appears to benefit a broader range of outcomes, including the number of new contacts women met, the average time they spent with new contacts, the number of connections they added on LinkedIn, and even their odds of changing jobs. Future research may benefit from keeping these findings in mind when attempting to design new interventions that build on and improve those presented here or by adapting our interventions to new contexts.

Second, existing research specifically on men’s and women’s professional networking is largely qualitative or theoretical in nature, with a particular emphasis on gender differences in networking behaviors (Xu and Martin 2011; Benschop 2009). For example, studies in this stream have leveraged interviews and ethnographic observations to show that, in networking interactions between men and women, gender often affects the interaction in both implicit and explicit ways (Berger et al. 2015; van den Brink and Benschop, 2013). Our quantitative study adds to this literature by considering networking outcomes, thereby laying the foundations for a more complete understanding of gender differences in professional networking.

Finally, our study has implications for policy makers and managers in IT seeking to foster growth in women’s professional networks as a conduit to catalyzing women’s career advancement in the field. Specifically, we found that the search intervention developed in this study provided consistent benefits and offers a simple, low-cost solution that can be easily implemented.

## Assessments of Internal and External Validity

A strength of randomized controlled experiments, such as ours, is that they facilitate causal inference by eliminating extraneous factors and resulting endogeneity (Karahanna et al. 2018; Brewer 1985). However, the external validity (or generalizability) of our study—i.e., the extent to which the results of our study can be generalized to other people—relies on the representativeness of conference participants (our subjects) to the broader set of IT professionals. Several factors mitigate concerns regarding the generalizability of our study. First, our data come from a randomized field experiment with real conference participants, which alleviates the “student subjects” concern of laboratory experiments (Karahanna et al. 2018). Second, of the individuals who registered to attend the conference in our study, 216 individuals were senior managers, of which 41 (19%) were women and 175 (81%) were men. This distribution is consistent with the larger IT sector, where senior leadership is predominantly male (Ashcraft et al. 2016). Third, the conference in our study had 1,161 registered attendees, of which 311 (27%) were women and 850 (73%) were men. This gender distribution corresponds closely to the larger IT sector, where Census Bureau data show that 25% of jobs are held by women (Beckhusen 2016). Fourth, our field experiment is conducted at a general interest conference, as opposed to a niche-focused conference, the latter of which tend to be specific to certain software, roles, or firm types. The broad nature of the conference in our study increases the generalizability of the findings. For illustration, as noted previously, individuals who registered to attend the conference had 717 distinct job titles. Additionally, individuals who registered for the conference belonged to companies with 456 distinct names (607 before cleaning the names) and these companies ranged from Fortune 500 firms to startups with fewer than 5 employees. Nevertheless, while these considerations alleviate concerns regarding the generalizability of our findings, we emphasize that our study is most likely to generalize to active IT professionals, i.e., IT workers who attend professional events such as conferences.

Similarly, we also believe our study has strong internal validity, understood as the extent to which the effects observed in the study are due to the treatments, rather than some other factors that might influence the relationship between cause and effect. A strength of randomized field experiments, such as ours, is their high internal validity (i.e., demonstration of causality) (Karahanna et al. 2018). Still, a potential concern regarding the internal validity of the study is that rather than resulting from the Search List and Social List interventions, the effects we observe may be because the interventions increased the awareness of network among those in the treatment groups. In other words, those in the treatment groups may be primed to network while those in the control group are not. However, this concern is mitigated because we compare the networking outcomes for those in each of the treatment groups to those in the Control group, and our study design increases awareness of network among subjects in the Control group as well. Figure 1 shows the email sent to subjects in the Control group. The email notes that the conference organizer strives to organize events that facilitate quality connections among attendees. The email also includes a link to a web application through which subjects could search for and message other conference participants. Thus, awareness of network is not exclusively heightened in the treatment conditions. Moreover, if the reason that the interventions were effective is because of heightened awareness regarding networking in the treatment conditions, then this would not explain why the Social List intervention was not as effective as the Search List intervention. The interventions for the Social List and the Search List groups look very similar (see Figures 3 and 4 of the paper), yet we find that the search intervention increased the number of new contacts women met by 57%, the time they spent talking with them by 90%, the number of connections women added on LinkedIn by 29%, and women’s odds of changing jobs by a factor of 1.6. In contrast, the social intervention only increased the time women spent talking to new contacts by 66%. Finally, a different but common concern with internal validity is that if subjects knew that their behavior is being observed, it might alter their behavior, and thus affect the outcomes recorded. However, such actor-observer bias (Jones and Nisbett 1971) was eliminated in our experiment because subjects were unaware that they were part of a study.

## Additional Analyses

## Response Bias in the Survey

We examined whether our survey results may be affected by response bias. Specifically, a potential concern is that people who responded to the survey may be systematically different from those who did not. To evaluate this possibility, we checked whether certain groups of people were more likely to respond to the survey than others. Using t-tests, we identified whether the likelihood of responding to the survey varied by gender, being a senior manager, being a student, or belonging to either the control group or one of the two treatment groups. Results of the t-tests are found in Appendix A, Table A6. None of the groups compared are significantly different from each other, which reduces concerns regarding response bias.<sup>24</sup> Our analyses of data from LinkedIn, particularly the analysis of Number of LinkedIn Connections, corroborate our results from the survey for Number of New Contacts Met, and thus further addresses concerns regarding response bias because 93.5% (293 in Control, 304 in Search List, and 299 in Social List) of the individuals in our study have LinkedIn profiles. t-tests (shown in Table 1) indicate that there is no statistical difference in the number of people with LinkedIn profiles (Has a LinkedIn profile) across the three groups.

## Job Change Analyses

We conducted several additional analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings on women's job changes to alternative model specifications. First, a small number of subjects (n = 8) reported only the start year (and no start month) for one or more job changes during the study window, and therefore we could not record these changes in our subject × month panel (Model 1, Tables B1 and B2). Model 2 in Tables B1 and B2, shows that our findings are robust to dropping these subjects. Second, as noted earlier, individuals with very few LinkedIn connections did not appear to actively use the platform. Model 3 in Tables B1 and B2 estimates the job chang models including only subjects with more than 50 LinkedIn connections. Results from these models are consistent with the findings from our main models (Model 1, Tables B1 and B2). Third, we considered an alternative modeling strategy in which we begin our analysis only in the post-conference period. Although this approach discards valuable pre-conference data (half of our subject × month observations are lost), it does not require the Post-conference indicator or its interactions, and therefore may be easier to interpret. Results using this strategy, shown in Model 4 of Tables B1 and B2, are consistent with those of our main models (Model 1, Tables B and B2). For completeness, Appendix A, Table A4, Models 2-4, shows the results of the robustness tests described above for Job Change for men. The results from these models are consistent with that of our main model of Job Change for men, shown in Appendix A, Table A4, Model 1.

## Email Opens

Using data from the event organizer, we examined whether the likelihood of opening the study-related emails (shown in Figures 2, 3, and 4) differed across the three groups. This analysis helps to provide additional evidence that our treatment and control groups were balanced. We created a binary variable, Opened the Email, which was set to 1 if the subject opened the email and 0 otherwise. For the logistic regression (Model 1, Table B3), the average marginal effects (AMEs) indicate that being in the Search List and Social List groups do not significantly affect the probability of opening the email. This indicates there is no statistical difference between the two treatment groups and the control group in terms of their likelihood of opening their respective study-related emails. We also test and confirm that individuals in either of the treatment groups were not significantly more likely to open the email than those in the other treatment group.

## Conference Participants’ Goals with Respect to Networking

We also sought to better understand participants’ goals with respect to networking at the conference. Following prior work (Ingram and Morris 2007), conference attendees were asked about their networking goals at the event (the exact survey question used is shown in Appendix A, Exhibit A1). 229 individuals (57 [25%] women, 172 [75%] men) responded to the question. As shown in Figure 5, the most common goal was to make new connections. 119 respondents said they wanted to make new connections; 71 said they wanted to strengthen existing connections; and of these, 33 said they wanted to do both. The black portion of the first two bars of Figure 5 identifies the portion of respondents who wanted to make new connections and to strengthen existing connections. In summary, meeting people, whether new contacts or existing ones, was the networking goal for 69% of the respondents, suggesting that the conference was an important outlet for building social connections.

## Manipulation Check

To verify whether the treatment emails would be interpreted by potential subjects as intended, we conducted a manipulation check using two surveys, both of which were administered to workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). In the first survey, we provided the text sent to individuals who received the Social List email and asked respondents to indicate the degree which to they agreed to the following questions:

1. The conference organizer has made introductions (via email) between you and all the other people on the list

2. You feel that you and the 16 people listed are members of a group that have all been introduced to one another

In the second survey, we provided the text sent to individuals who received the Search List email and asked respondents to answer the same two questions noted above. Responses to both questions were recorded on a 5-point scale from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.” Responses to the first question were used to define the variable Made Introductions; responses to the second question were used to define the variable Members of a Group.

In both surveys, we included questions to evaluate whether respondents on AMT were carefully reading the prompts rather than randomly selecting answer choices. Subjects were asked to indicate the degree which to they agreed to the following statement: “This is a survey about a conference-related email.” Responses were recorded on a 3-point scale and included “Agree,” “Disagree,” and “Neither Agree nor Disagree” As a further check, the order in which the answer choices were provided was such that “Agree” was displayed at the top of the list, followed by “Neither Agree nor Disagree” and then “Disagree” For the prior two questions (i and ii above) the order was “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”. If individuals were randomly selecting answers to the survey questions without reading them, then they would likely answer this question incorrectly. The exact questions and survey text used in the two surveys can be found in Exhibit B1 below.

To further ensure that we received high-quality responses, we limited participation in the survey to AMT workers who met the following qualifications:

1. They were located in the United States.

2. They were qualified as “Masters” by AMT. AMT includes built-in technology to identify high-performing workers and monitors their performance over time. Workers who have demonstrated excellence over a wide range of tasks are awarded “Masters” qualification by AMT.

3. Their human intelligence task (HIT)<sup>25</sup> approval rate (%) for all requestors HITS was >= 98%

4. Their number of HITS approved was greater than or equal to 50

50 people were asked to respond to each survey (100 respondents total). We found that one person failed the attention check as they responded “Disagree” to the attention check question. The rest of the 99 respondents responded “Agree.” We excluded the responses from the individual who failed the attention check. We used a Mann-Whitney (two-sample Wilcoxon Rank-Sum) test to compare the two groups of respondents. The Mann-Whitney statistic indicates the likelihood that a member of one group will score higher than a member of the other group (Conroy 2012). Table B4 reports the results of these tests. Test 1 in Table B4 indicates that the probability of an observation in the Social List Email group having a true value for Made Introductions that is higher than an observation in the Search List Email group is 70.2% (p < 0.01). Additionally, Test 2 in Table B4 indicates that the probability of an observation in the Social List Email group having a true value for Members of a Group that is higher than an observation in the Search List Email group is 66.5% (p < 0.01). These tests confirm that, in comparison to those who saw the Search List email, those who saw the Social list email tended to feel that i) the conference organizer has made introductions (via email) between them and all the other people on the list ii) they and the 16 people listed were all members of a group that have all been introduced to one another.

## Clicks on Links in the Emails

As shown in Figures 2, 3, and 4, treatment and control group emails included links to a web application through which the recipient could search for (based on name, job title, and company) and/or message others who were planning to attend the conference. We checked to see whether individuals in the treatment or control groups were more likely to click on these links. The binary variable Clicked on Links in the Email is set to 1 if a subject clicked on a link in the email, and 0 otherwise. For the logistic regression (Table B3, Model 2) the average marginal effects (AMEs) indicate that being in the Search List and Social List groups significantly and negatively affect the probability of clicking on links in the email. This indicates that people in the treatment groups were less likely to click on the links (to the web application) in the email than those in the control group. We also tested and confirmed that individuals in either of the treatment groups were not significantly more likely to click on the links in the email than those in the other treatment group. Finally, we checked to see whether the number of times individuals clicked on the links in the emails were significantly different across the groups. Like the logistic model, the results of a negative binomial model (Table B3, Model 3) show that the coefficients for Search List and Social List are significant and negative. Together, these results suggest that, prior to the conference, people in the Control group were more likely to attempt to identify individuals with whom to network. In contrast, individuals who received recommendations and introductions (that is those in the Search List and Social List groups, respectively) were less likely to do so, potentially because individuals in the treatment groups felt that the conference organizers had identified networking leads for them.
