## a b s t r a c t

We propose a decision analytics approach that leverages adaptive learning in the re<sup>fi</sup>nement of service operations. We aim to integrate service design and service pricing with downstream operational decision-making related to service provision. This approach involves: collecting consumer data and establishing consumer behavior models; integrating consumer behavior models with models for service operation decision-making; and iteratively evaluating service designs based on service delivery performance that evolves over time due to learning. We discuss how this approach enables service providers to set time-differentiated prices and evaluate the impact on transportation network performance. We use agent-based simulation to illustrate the application of our approach to the operations of a public rail transportation <sup>fi</sup>rm in a European urban setting. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that knowing the impacts of consumer responses in service operations is essential for devising cost-effective and value-bearing service designs. Our approach can support service providers who wish to adjust their pricing, consumer demand and capacity management models, and to develop more effective market forecasts of performance through adaptive learning, in the presence of “big data” from consumers and operations.

## 1. Introduction

The traditional approach to product design involves the targeting of the mass market to attract the largest number of consumers. With the increased availability of rich consumer information through the use of advanced ITs, <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to understand more about consumer demand and to make more informed decisions about the design of customized services for speci<sup>fi</sup>c consumer segments. Firms recognize the importance of understanding how the value propositions and the accompanying value realization process need to be managed for services offered across different market segments. Access to improved information also has changed the nature of consumer behavior though. This change requires <sup>fi</sup>rms to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll consumers' new needs and requirements through new service innovations. As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms are striving to design and differentiate their product and service offerings.

To tailor services to small groups of consumers, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to develop adequate capabilities to understand the heterogeneous preferences and levels of willingness-to-pay of these consumers in their markets. This enables them to design and price their services more effectively. At the same time, they need to assess the costs of producing these service offerings. Services are fundamentally targeted at consumers, involving exchanges between a provider and the consumers. Recent research has challenged the effectiveness of customer centricity and has argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms have dif<sup>fi</sup>culties taking advantage of it [6].

Senior executives with whom we have discussed these issues identify two reasons why <sup>fi</sup>rms have not been able to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from consumer behavior-based service design and innovation. First, <sup>fi</sup>rms have limited data and technological capabilities to enable them to implement services in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way. In addition, models that are used for service demand forecasting typically assume exogenous demand and ignore the consumers. A senior manager of marketing that we interviewed commented: “We've talked about customer-based services for years. But it's always on the aggregated level. Our model does not allow customers to be treated differently. Detailed customer data provide an opportunity to <sup>fi</sup>nally move to a more detailed understanding. Customers make decisions about what to buy and how much to pay. We don't make these decisions, but need to anticipate their behavior changes.” Recently, researchers have been working on models that incorporate individual consumers' choices on the demand side for services [17].

The second reason for the inability of <sup>fi</sup>rms to bene<sup>fi</sup>t is that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to integrate marketing and operational decisions to design service offerings to capture pro<sup>fi</sup>table consumer responses in the ever-changing marketplace. Another senior executive whom we interviewed clearly articulated this problem: “In reality, developing different services is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. It requires that the entire organization works together – marketing, new product development, operations – which are separate functional units in most organizations. The decisions about new product launch, pricing, promotion, and operations are made in silos. There are limited communications between the departments when these decisions are made. It's very hard to make pro<sup>fi</sup>table value propositions without understanding impacts.” These comments suggest reasons why <sup>fi</sup>rms' decision support tools are not advanced enough to help managers make consumer behavior-based decisions that take into account the commercial and operational aspects of service provision.

This article answers two research questions. First, how should <sup>fi</sup>rms design their service offerings in view of their new capacity to develop a richer, information-led understanding of consumer preferences and discover their willingness-to-pay? Second, given the importance of designing new services to meet consumer needs, how can <sup>fi</sup>rms leverage model-based decision support to create value in services through adaptive learning and achieve consumer behaviorbased service innovations? We examine how <sup>fi</sup>rms leverage their information and decision support capabilities to learn about the choice behavior of consumers over time. We will emphasize adaptive learning in service operations, which involves understanding consumer behavior changes with respect to services, and to integrate models of consumer behavior with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's operational decisions. We will also evaluate service designs as organizational learning occurs.<sup>2</sup>

Based on our assessment of the prior literature, we sought to develop a model-based decision support approach that <sup>fi</sup>rms can use to leverage their information capabilities to create value through technologydriven and behavior-based service designs that rely on learning. In our model, service providers – as economic agents – perform adaptive learning, and adjust their decisions to enhance <sup>fi</sup>rm performance as new information becomes available over time. The theory of adaptive learning [33] suggests that service providers can utilize the available decision-related information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, and learn the value of an appropriate pricing strategy over time to meet their objectives. This means that if service providers do not know the “true” demand model through the use of available information, they will be able to learn over time about the parameter values they need to more fully understand what it is. As a result, their expectations will become nearly identical to those that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the parameters of the true model, and they will be able to make better demand and performance forecasts as a result.

We will apply our approach to the operations of a major European public transportation service provider that is implementing nationwide electronic ticketing enabled by smart cards and mobile technologies. We will use a math programming model, a consumer choice experiment and simulation to perform our evaluation. The decision analytics that we employ are intended to explain how adaptive learning in service operations occurs. This implementation has potential to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer travel experiences and the service provider's efforts to achieve service innovations.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we lay out our theory and modeling approach. In Section 3, we demonstrate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the approach by applying it to the operations of a public transportation service provider. We also discuss the collection of primary empirical data, and the use of a choice model, as a basis for evaluating the effectiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's pricing strategy on its service operations using a simulation. Section 4 summarizes the study, highlights the contributions, discusses its limitations, and offers some thoughts about future research.

## 2. Decision analytics for adaptive learning in services operations

Services are targeted to consumers and are relationship-based, and this requires a shift in emphasis within the <sup>fi</sup>rms that deploy them. They need to emphasize business processes and organizational units to consumers, and segments in markets, as a basis for solving key operational and organizational design problems. Prior research has demonstrated the effects of such services on <sup>fi</sup>rm activities. The work includes a variety of issues: achieving better organizational effectiveness [31], fostering trust [28], increasing consumer satisfaction [11], and improving competitiveness through detailed modeling [16]. Today, <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from giving attention to value enhancement and price recovery, and emphasizing willingness-topay, rather than improved internal ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and productivity bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [32]. Instead, productivity-led management has become more of an “old school” view.

Service systems are value-creation networks composed of consumers, producers, technology, and organizations. We propose a model-based decision analytics approach to achieve adaptive learning in service organizations. We compare the proposed and traditional approaches in Table 1. Our approach consists of: collecting consumerlevel data to produce information on patterns of demand; modeling consumer behavior relative to the service offerings they choose to consume; integrating the impacts of the consumer behavior model with a model of service operation choices; and evaluating the effects of service designs on service operations through adaptive learning. Next, we will describe our approach in more detail, prior to offering an illustration involving public rail transportation industry for an urban area.

## 2.1. Collecting consumer-level data to produce information on demand patterns

Firms have been collecting data to facilitate their decision-making for a long time. The question is not about whether to collect data; instead, it is about what data to collect. Today, there are many new opportunities to capture historical and contemporary data about consumers, all the way up to online real-time data on them in a variety of service settings. Traditionally, companies such as the airlines have primarily focused on collecting and organizing their data based on booking class, fare codes, origin–destination information, and other key airline ticket variables. The practical advantage of collecting such service-based data is that <sup>fi</sup>rms can use sales transactions to understand market demand. This has imposed a strong assumption that their services do not change much over time, however. In reality, this hardly is true: services and market conditions change all the time. Airlines modify their schedules, equipment and prices on a daily basis, route by route. The related market conditions also change over time. For example, there may be more competing airlines that offer new alternatives, and they may change their prices too.

To deal with these problems, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to spend more time collecting consumer-level data rather than only service-level data. This is because consumer-level data enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to understand how consumers react to different service attributes in the space of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's service offerings. For example, the airlines frequently experiment with the presentation of a large number of service options online. They obtain consumer choice data to understand what in<sup>fl</sup>uences their decisions, and what their preferences are. They also obtain information on how consumers react to different changes in service attributes and levels, how they respond to competitors' service offerings, and how much they are willing to pay at a particular point in time and under a particular market condition. This contrasting view may challenge the thinking of senior managers and requires a shift from models of requests for products or services to models of consumer behavior. With the new approach, <sup>fi</sup>rms should not ask: “How many consumers should we accept or reject?” Instead, they should ask: “Which alternatives should we make available to consumers to pro<sup>fi</sup>tably in<sup>fl</sup>uence the choices they make?” [32,37].

Collecting such data has become economical and operationally feasible with current IT and techniques. Research and business practice, particularly in marketing, have previously used data from scanners and loyalty programs to understand consumer behavior, such as satisfaction and retention [20]. Recently though, research has begun to explore the usage of innovative data sources to explain various consumer behaviors. This includes studies leveraging online clickstream data to explore consumer bidding behavior [18], using smart card data to understand travel behavior changes to pricing strategies [21], and assessing mobile transaction data to explain consumer usage behavior associated with user-generated content [14]. It also includes evaluating detailed e-mail data to decipher information worker productivity [3], testing the effectiveness of viral product designs in creating peer in<sup>fl</sup>uences and social contagion using randomized trail Facebook data [4], and exploring Google search trend data to predict future housing market sales [39]. The increased data availability has enabled researchers and <sup>fi</sup>rms to move from product and service-based forecasting to consumerbased forecasting, and this has been heavily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the availability of micro-data, as well as online, mobile, and environmentally-sensed real-time data.

## 2.2. Modeling consumer behavior for the services consumed

Consumers exhibit systematic responses to <sup>fi</sup>rm service offerings, and <sup>fi</sup>rms are responsible for anticipating these responses when making their service design and service pricing decisions. Consumer demand depends on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's service offerings. We assume that each consumer has an ideal service speci<sup>fi</sup>cation de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the service characteristics and based on the price they are willing to pay. This is different from the assumptions of prior research in which consumers are viewed as preferring a lower-priced service for a given speci<sup>fi</sup>cation [26]. We also assume that the choices that consumers make about service offerings depend on the combination of a number of different service attributes. Firms change prices and service attributes for different service designs to in<sup>fl</sup>uence market demand. Thus, consumer demand is a function of service price and other attributes. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c function can be estimated empirically based on random utility models of consumer choice [27].

Providing consumers with services involving bundled attributes will attract consumers if the features that are offered are appropriate. By monitoring how the market reacts to new service offerings, as we will discuss in greater detail in an illustration later, <sup>fi</sup>rms can gain insights into how customers value new service attributes. Service providers also need to recognize that there is a cost side of the equation when they support consumer efforts to maximize utility. This allows them to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their service designs for high pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and value, and satisfy consumers at different levels of demand, while controlling their costs.

2.3. Integrating the impacts of consumer behavior with service operations

## 2.3.1. Modeling background

To evaluate the impacts of different service designs on service operations, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to understand not only the decision processes of consumers, but also the interaction points between consumer demand and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's service operations where capacity is concerned. This is common for industries such as transportation, utilities and telecommunications, where capacity constraints sometimes limit a <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to supply services at the level that consumers demand. This is especially common for public <sup>fi</sup>rms, where the interest in performance in pure pro<sup>fi</sup>tability terms needs to be counter-balanced with concerns about the creation of social welfare. Apart from the natural differences in consumer willingness-to-pay, differences in <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>tability exist because they have different abilities to generate demand and control costs.<sup>3</sup>

We also need to recognize that the level of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that <sup>fi</sup>rms can obtain is constrained. Often, it is too expensive to produce enough capacity to accommodate peak demand. On the other hand, if service providers offer too little capacity, their business activities won't match the value creation and revenue opportunities with service production that arise from consumer demand. This may lead to less-than-best profitability or, for public organizations, they may not create an appropriate amount of social welfare.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm has a <sup>fi</sup>nite set of service designs that it will be able to offer, and a decision analyst can get an idea of their range by enumerating the possible combinations of service attributes and levels. The <sup>fi</sup>rm's goal is to select a service design that will be accepted by consumers, and is the most valuable compared to other alternatives. In meeting this goal, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will need to take into consideration consumer responses to new service design options and the cost of providing them.

## 2.3.2. The model

Next, we will set up a model that addresses this kind of problem setting.<sup>4</sup> We will explain it in the context of the public rail transportation in an urban setting. One of the major issues in this industry is the peak load problem, in which many people travel during the peak hours in the morning and afternoon, despite the limited capacity of the available rolling stock. This causes excess demand, bottlenecks in service provision and diminished consumer satisfaction. Rail service providers adjust their services to alleviate these problems with better service designs, scheduling, pricing, and capacity options.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm's objective function involves maximizing the expected total value (Expected Total Value) of train services at different scheduled times t subject to being able to produce a cost-effective capacity level in the marketplace during the peak hours of demand, with a social welfare penalty assessed when the scheduled services do not cover demand. Expected Total Value is the summation of the individual expected values (Expected Value ) of all scheduled trains that depart at time t=1 to T during the peak hours of service.<sup>5</sup> The decision variable is time-dependent service prices (Price<sub>t</sub>) for a set of the scheduled services.<sup>6</sup> The model that we propose for this setting is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Maximize   Expected   Total   Value } = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \text { Expected   Value } _ {t} \\ = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \text { Price } _ {t} \cdot \text { Expected   Load } _ {t} - \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \delta \cdot \text { Capacity } _ {t} - \text { Weight } _ {t} \\ \times \left(\sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \text { Expected   Demand } _ {t} - \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \text { Capacity } _ {t}\right) ^ {\theta}. \end{array}
$$

In the objective function, the <sup>fi</sup>rst term is the <sup>fi</sup>rm's revenue, which is created by the service price (Price ) multiplied by the expected load of a train (Expected Load ) at some scheduled time t. Expected Load is the number of people that can board a scheduled train when capacity is available for them. We assume that all scheduled peak hour trains have the same <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity and cannot carry more people than that capacity limit allows. However, it is possible that there is a higher demand in the market at time t than the maximum capacity can accommodate. Thus Expected Load is the maximum of the capacity and the sum of current expected demand (Expected Demand ) and excess demand (Excess ) for $\mathrm { i t . } ^ { 7 }$ Further, Excess =|Expected Demand +Excess −Capacity |. So whatever demand is not served with the available capacity will be added to demand in the following period. In other words, people might be left standing on the train platform, and this may go on to the point where not everyone is completely served during the peak hours of service. Expected demand for a given train (Expected Demand ) is the total number of consumers who choose to consume the services offered at time t based on the time-differentiated prices that the <sup>fi</sup>rm sets for its scheduled trains during the peak hours. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects the endogeneity of consumer choice in the expression of demand at different price levels.<sup>8</sup>

The second term in the objective function is the total allocated <sup>fi</sup>xed cost during the service hours.<sup>9</sup> The typical interpretation of the variable Capacity , in terms of number of passenger seats, is that it is a decision variable driven by expected demand. However, since we are modeling peak hour train services, we assume all scheduled trains will be built to have the greatest feasible capacity, which is based on the standard length of a station platform along its route.<sup>10</sup>

The cost δ associated capacity denotes the allocated <sup>fi</sup>xed cost of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's operations for one unit of capacity for providing its services. Decision-makers need to spread total <sup>fi</sup>xed costs over the service operations capacity based on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's estimate of expected consumer demand. Typically, it is desirable for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to achieve a level of capacity that matches market demand. This allows the <sup>fi</sup>rm to do better in allocating its <sup>fi</sup>xed costs in as spread out a fashion as possible, based on as full utilization of capacity as possible.

The last term in the objective function is a penalty function, involving a subtraction from the expected value created by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which is zero when the capacity is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to handle expected demand in the optimization, and positive when the demand exceeds the capacity. The parameter Weight is a constraint weighting factor (similar in function to a Lagrange multiplier) that the <sup>fi</sup>rm assigns for a train scheduled for a given time t. The weight is a parameter that managers can vary depending on how much emphasis they put on reducing the expected value a given train achieves based on the assignment of capacity and the selected price when the expected demand is not met. Regulators can also assess it if the need arises.<sup>11</sup>

There is a chance that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be unable to serve the demand in the market when it is greater than the available capacity. As a result, some people will need to wait for the next available train when demand for a train at a given time is greater than its capacity to carry them. This congestion externality results in dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed consumers and reduces social welfare [36]. We represent this as a quadratic cost with the exponent θ≥1, which also ensures the concavity of the objective function.<sup>12,13</sup> The peak period of service operation is when the congestion externality costs of assigning inadequate capacity to move passengers probably reach their highest point.

The challenge is to reshape the distribution of demand over time that occurs during the peak hours, essentially pushing down the observed distribution to <sup>fl</sup>atten the peak and fatten the tails, even if some demand is pushed out to non-peak hours or not served. The common practice of <sup>fi</sup>rms to even out peak demand concentration is to increase service prices during peak hours, and to lower prices for offpeak period operations (see Fig. 1). This may be subject to time-varying drift and stochasticity. The drift may be driven by seasonal effects, changing economic conditions, the persistent effects of changes elsewhere in a region's transportation system, and so on. ## 2.3.3. Implementing a new service design

This optimization problem is only a starting point for managing a public transportation service provider's effort to achieve high performance in expected value and expected social welfare terms, however. Optimization doesn't really guarantee optimal performance. Expected demand and assigned capacity based on the prices that a <sup>fi</sup>rm selects may not closely match realized demand and required capacity. This is because expected demand for public goods like public transportation is stochastic. Although demand is generally viewed as exhibiting stationarity over time with known parameters, a stable economy and other stability factors in place, it still is appropriate for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to monitor the performance of its operations in terms of the difference between the expected value and the realized value of its service design and operations. Our emphasis from here on will be on the difference between expected and realized value, which will be based on the difference between expected and realized demand for a set of service designs and a set of prices for peak period train services. Our goal is to illustrate an adaptive learning approach to improve performance, by iteratively minimizing the difference between expected and realized demand in the presence of stochasticity.

When the expected number of consumers reaches a certain level such that it generates a large revenue bene<sup>fi</sup>t to offset the allocated costs and any externality penalty, the <sup>fi</sup>rm should consider implementing a scheduled train service to maximize expected value. We propose:

Proposition 1 (The Baseline Service Choice Proposition). A firm should implement a scheduled train at time t only if its expected revenues exceed the allocated costs and the externality penalty of providing this service, based on the following expected value condition being true;

2.4. Evaluating service operation designs with an adaptive learning theory perspective

## 2.4.1. The theory of rational expectations and adaptive learning

The theory of rational expectations [29] suggests that economic agents will base their expectations and subsequently make their decisions on the true structural model of the economy. There are two key assumptions on which the theory of rational expectations is based. Economic agents form their beliefs based on a given set of information. And their expectations will be conditioned on all of the information that is available. The theory has been popularly applied in various contexts and to various kinds of economic agents. They include: developing an understanding of output and in<sup>fl</sup>ation in macroeconomics [24,25,34], IT investment and technology adoption decision-making [5], and diffusion of products involving durable goods [30].

Rational expectations theory is based on a strong assumption. It requires economic agents to have full knowledge of the structure of the relevant models and their parameter values. This has been pointed out by a number of studies. The 2011 Nobel Prize winner in Economics, Thomas Sargent [33], has argued that this interpretation may be a little too strict. Instead, he suggested a weaker assumption about prior knowledge and information, captured in an alternative perspective called adaptive learning theory. With a basis in the boundedly-rational decision-making of Simon [35], adaptive learning theory assumes that economic agents, including individuals as well as managers in <sup>fi</sup>rms [12], initially may not know the exact information they need to predict the relevant outcomes in the economy that concern them. Nevertheless, they are able to learn over time, and as a result, they are also able to update their expectations about the relevant parameter values on the basis of newly-received information.

A <sup>fi</sup>rm's expectations in our context will be conditioned on the available information. It will exploit all of the possible opportunities to pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and develop service designs that create an advantageous position relative to the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoffs associated with service innovation. It is typically dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to deal with all the information that is available to develop a perfect service design right from the beginning though. The theory of adaptive learning suggests that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will utilize the available decision-related information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently over time. Managers will learn about the relevant consumer behavior-related parameter values, and this will support their effort to improve their service designs to meet their business and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability objectives. By allowing learning, managerial expectations in a <sup>fi</sup>rm will become nearly identical to those that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the parameters of the “true” model of market demand. This perspective differentiates these ideas from more standard interpretations in operations management of re<sup>fi</sup>nement-based improvements. This also will have the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect of increasing the likelihood of redesign of existing service and operational capabilities so they come nearer to the optimal design for joint services, pricing, and operational decisions.

## 2.4.2. Service updates based on adaptive learning

Next, we will discuss the incorporation of adaptive learning on the part of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The need for recalculation of expected value over time is due to the fact that price, expected demand, and allocated capacity may need to be updated based on new information the <sup>fi</sup>rm receives. The <sup>fi</sup>rm still seeks to maximize total expected value each period its services are offered and consumed. It's just that adjustments may be necessary to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune prices to ensure appropriate demand and capacity for its scheduled services.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision involves how to price one service over time among many. It wishes to obtain an appropriate level of realized demand (Realized Demand) and maximize realized value (Realized Value) in the process. This choice is based on consumer responses, adjusted for the costs of delivering the service.

With rational expectations and adaptive learning in mind, learning and updating will occur in the following way. At the beginning of period t, a <sup>fi</sup>rm may start out with an expectation for demand (Expected Demand ) of how many consumers will choose a speci<sup>fi</sup>c service design, based on the assigned price (Price<sub>t</sub>). The <sup>fi</sup>rm then allocates capacity (Capacity ) to run the service based on its expectation of market demand for the scheduled time. Using our proposed objective function, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will compute the expected value (Expected Value<sub>t</sub>) for a given scheduled train over a period of time in which learning occurs.

During the period in which train operations are conducted, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will learn from receiving more information regarding how consumers actually responded to the chosen service design based on the prices and costs allocated for capacity, which results in the realized demand (Realized Demand ) for individual trains in the scheduled period. Then it can calculate the realized value (Realized $V a l u e _ { t } )$ of providing each train service in the schedule, recognizing the stochasticity that is inherent in the outcome that is observed. This realized value will deviate from the expected value of operating a given train, since the parameters of the demand function will not be available as perfect information. Nevertheless. based on the service designs and the realized total value for all the trains, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be able to derive demand across the different prices and demand levels during the peak hours across the period of study. The study period may be a month or quarter, so there is time for the effects of stochastic drift to play out.

In the next period, the same demand function can be used as a basis for assigning prices, with knowledge of <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity and a <sup>fi</sup>xed schedule during the peak hours of operation. The <sup>fi</sup>rm will decide if it makes sense to continue the same services or to adjust to new service designs to re<sup>fl</sup>ect changes that may have occurred. This may result in an adjustment to expected demand for each of the trains in the schedule, so that the new value estimates for the trains will be in synch with their realized levels of demand, based on some targeted values (Target ) for improvement for each of its trains.

The need for a period-to-period updating is due to the different set of choices of consumers and different expected number of consumers that will choose the service, as well as the <sup>fi</sup>rm's expectations about the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost components. These need to be updated over time based on new information that the <sup>fi</sup>rm receives about the changing demand that consumers express, and adjustments from service offerings updates. This process of adaptive learning on the part of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, as depicted in our model, involves a multi-period decision-making setting and may span a longer time horizon. When more information becomes available over time, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will wish to use the new information to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune its service designs, and adjust its expectations about its future forecast for demand and service provision. Updated information on the expected value of future bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will serve as continuing feedback. We expect to see the <sup>fi</sup>rm implement a new service that will enable larger bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to maximize value, based on:

Proposition 2 (The Adaptive Learning and Service Design Update Proposition). Under adaptive learning, a firm will implement a new service for a scheduled train at time t in its peak hour schedule only if the difference between its expected value and its realized value exceeds a minimum targeted level. This can be expressed by Expected Value −Realized Value >Target .

## 2.4.3. An illustration of the comparison process for service updates

To explain how the <sup>fi</sup>rm will update its services in each period, we offer the following illustration with an 07:30 peak hour morning train. At the beginning of the period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will select a price for the scheduled 07:30 train in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period $( \mathrm { e . g . , } P r i c e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , }$ with the subscripts representing departure time and period of evaluation now). Its choice will be based on a consumer survey or an experiment, or based on what its marketing and operations managers know about the market. It will then form an estimate of expected demand (Expected $D e m a n d _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , 1 } )$ for the scheduled train. Based on this, it will allocate the <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity at the scheduled time, reassess the price that should be charged, and calculate the expected value (Expected $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , }$ ). During the period of observation, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will receive more information and learn about consumer behavior. The <sup>fi</sup>rm will be able to observe the realized value (Realized Value ) of providing this train service.

In the second period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will estimate expected value (Expected $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 } , \ 2 )$ , based on the newly-estimated demand for the price and service design combination for a train that departs at a speci<sup>fi</sup>c time, with an allocated capacity. The <sup>fi</sup>rm will identify the expected value (Expected $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , 2 } )$ for the second learning period based on the realized value from the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of operation (Realized $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , 1 } )$ , and a desired improvement level that it wishes to achieve (Target). It will use the realized demand (Realized $D e m a n d _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , 1 } )$ in the prior period as the expected demand (Expected Demand ) of the second period, as a basis for selecting the new price associated with the scheduled service design $( P r i c e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , 2 } )$

At the end of the second period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will obtain the realized value (Realized $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , \ 2 } )$ for delivering the re-priced scheduled train service. It will assess realized demand (Realized $D e m a n d _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , }$ <sub>2</sub>), and this will be used to form an expected value for the next period of evaluation (Expected $V a l u e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , \ 3 } ) .$ . If further improvement to match expected value and realized value in the second period is needed, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will select and implement another new service in the market in the third period. This process can be iterated as necessary, and also can be expanded to cover all of the trains in the peak hour service time schedule to permit summation to expected total demand, and realized total demand.

Through learning, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be able to gradually eliminate errors from its decision rules and adjust its forecasts. This is an iterative process that will continue until the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to reach a satisfactory result wherein all interested consumers are able to choose services offered that maximize the value they take away based on what they receive in return. When this occurs, the theory of adaptive learning views the situation as if all acts of learning are complete. As a result, a <sup>fi</sup>rm will have no incentive to further adjust and make service design and service provision changes in its operations, since it will no longer be easy to improve the potential net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the <sup>fi</sup>rm can obtain by providing its services for consumers. We expect this to persist so long as the <sup>fi</sup>rm continues to hold stable beliefs about the operational and economic environments, and there are no exogenous shocks. In most business operations, however, a better assumption is to assume that stable equilibria rarely persist very long, and instead require constant attention and operating parameter adjustments and re<sup>fi</sup>nements by managers.

## 3. Adaptive learning in public transportation service operations

We now will use the approach that we developed in the previous section, and apply it to the service operations of a major public rail transportation company in Europe to demonstrate how it engages in adaptive learning. Our discussion will correspond to the analysis that we described in the previous section.

First, we collected data in the context of a large-scale, <sup>fi</sup>rmsponsored <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment using the stated choice experiment method at the individual level of the consumer to understand what in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer choices of travel service offerings, and how consumers make tradeoffs among different service attributes. The data collected include how consumers make choices and their characteristics, as well as the service design options that the <sup>fi</sup>rm considers. Second, using these data, we were able to establish behavioral models that supported our subsequent examination of how consumers choose among different travel service design options. Third, we apply an agent-based simulation to the travel decision-making process of individual consumers. We include the movement of trains using the timetable and transportation network information of the public rail transportation provider. This allowed us to integrate considerations of service design with the related service supply. Fourth, we used the simulation to evaluate the impact of new travel service options on service demand, revenue production, and service operation over time.

3.2. Collecting consumer-level data to produce information on demand patterns

To address the business challenges and practical requirements of spreading out demand concentration of peak hour travels in urban rail transit, we <sup>fi</sup>rst need to understand how consumers respond to different travel services. Consumer choice theory suggests that the perceived value of different service attributes can vary dramatically from one person to the next. A viable means for setting prices is to do so according to the value or willingness-to-pay that a consumer places on the service attributes. One way to implement this is to offer different levels of the attribute that are designed to appeal to different types of consumers. Then consumers can make their own decisions about how to segment themselves. The service attribute level they choose reveals the value they place on the level. This allows a <sup>fi</sup>rm to gauge consumers' willingness-to-pay for a bundle of non-price attributes.

We have chosen to use a stated choice experimental method to collect consumer-level data. It is based on the premise that any product or service can be described by its characteristics or attributes, and the extent to which a decision-maker values or expresses utility depends upon the nature and levels of these characteristics. In such an experiment, hypothetical choice situations are presented to decision-makers, who are asked to state their preferences among the different alternatives [15,22]. This method has been used in a wide spectrum of research and industry domains, such as marketing [23] and transportation [10].

Together with the service provider, we conducted a series of stated choice <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments and carried out the data collection in three ways. First, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed service attributes and speci<sup>fi</sup>ed some appropriate service attribute levels based on current service designs and internal discussions with the service provider's management staff. Apart from service pricing, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of relevant service restrictions, which management believes to be important attributes for consideration. These provide a basis for the <sup>fi</sup>rm for pricing individual trains in the scheduled peak hour train services. Based on our <sup>fi</sup>eld work, we determined that the appropriate service attributes and their levels are: Price (Level 1 to Level 4), Time Window Starting in the Morning (6:30, 7:00, 7:30, 8:00), Time Window Ending in the Morning (8:00, 8:30, 9:00, 9:30), Time Window Starting in the Afternoon (15:30, 16:00, 16:30, 17:00), and Time Window Ending in the Afternoon (17:00, 17:30, 18:00, 18:30).

Second, once the attributes and levels were <sup>fi</sup>nalized, the number of alternatives in each choice set and the number of choice sets were speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. Each choice set consists of two alternative services. Service A is more expensive and without restrictions, and Service B is less expensive but has restrictions on time validity. Our intent is to allow a respondent to choose either of the service alternatives or the nochoice option. All experimental designs follow a standard fractional factorial design as in Louviere et al. [22], which leads to higher quality parameter estimates.

3.4. Integrating the impacts of consumer behavior with service operations

We have examined consumer responses for different service designs. Nevertheless, we still do not know how these new services will affect the concentration of train travelers at peak load times. Nor do we know how they will affect operations in terms of revenue and capacity utilization, where operational constraints must be taken into account. To understand these effects, we need to model the interactions between the decisions consumer make that will affect the <sup>fi</sup>rm's service operations.

Considering the variety of consumer decisions that are made and the complexity of the interactions among service designs and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's operations, we chose to use an agent-based simulation. It overcomes some of the problems with approaches that involve the estimation of demand in isolation from consumer behavior, and only are founded on historical market trends [19]. 3.5. Evaluating service operation designs with an adaptive learning theory perspective

We now will use the simulation to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the demand and service design choices through adaptive learning for one train among those in the scheduled services during the peak hours of demand. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of learning, for example, based on one month of observations, we select a service design for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c scheduled train based on the assessment of market responses to different services. This might apply to the service price (say Price , simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed from $P r i c e _ { 0 7 : 3 0 , }$ earlier), for example, or some other aspect of the service under the control of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We calculate the expected demand (Expected Demand<sub>1</sub>) and the expected value (Expected Value ) to be used in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of learning, to initialize the simulation. During the <sup>fi</sup>rst learning period, we will receive more information regarding how consumers respond to the selected service design and their willingness-to-pay. At the end of <sup>fi</sup>rst period of learning, we obtain the realized value from the service's implementation (Realized Value ). To obtain this, we have to run the simulation to derive the realized demand (Realized Demand ) of implementing this service design. This acquired parameter will then be included in the choice decision rules of consumers in the next period of learning (now Expected Demand ), and any other appropriate updates to the new decision rules will be made. We also allocate <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity once again. We then use the simulation to evaluate if service design re-<sup>fi</sup>nement will yield an improvement in expected value (now Expected Value<sub>2</sub>).

To illustrate this process, we will use the same four service designs as discussed earlier as inputs for the simulation. We then calculate the expected value (Expected Value ) of implementing the service. In the second period, we adjust the decision rules of consumers and obtain a new set of performance indicators after the service update. Next, we select a new Service 2, simulate the train's operation, and obtain another set of results. We then assess the improvement in expected value terms. We proposed a decision approach for <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve adaptive learning in service operations. We presented our approach and its application in the public rail transportation industry in a European city.

## 4.1. Summary and contributions

With the increased availability of consumer information, more and more <sup>fi</sup>rms are developing new service offerings to capture the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table consumer responses. Creating consumer value may be constrained by the costs of production, however. Some service attributes are more expensive to produce than others because of <sup>fi</sup>rms' limited operational capacity. Thus, knowing the impacts of service operations in view of the expected consumer responses is essential for devising pro<sup>fi</sup>table service designs.

Our key contribution is the proposed decision analytics approach that supports <sup>fi</sup>rms in designing service offerings, setting service prices, and evaluating service designs based on their operational performance through the theoretical lens of adaptive learning. We considered urban rail transit, where capacity considerations limit the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to supply services at the level that consumers demand during peak hours. We sought to maximize value creation through the use of a penalty function whose force is based on an assigned weight that represents the loss in social welfare for demand exceeding capacity, so that not all rail passengers are fully served. This is in keeping with other research that views capacity, revenue generation and social welfare as key considerations in the effective provision of public services.

We considered revenue expansion through enhanced services and service delivery while leveraging consumer behavior, rather than emphasizing the traditional discussion of how IT improves service ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We proposed a formal process for using modeling and empirical analysis for: collecting individual consumer-level data; modeling consumer behavior; integrating a model of proposed behavior with a <sup>fi</sup>rm's operational decisions; and developing market forecasts of performance through adaptive learning.

This research contributes to the IS literature in several additional ways. The proposed approach advances our understanding of how to conduct consumer behavior-based evaluations of services. It suggests how to integrate service designs with service operations in the presence of micro-data from consumers. We also combined a stated choice experiment and simulation to study the interaction between consumers and services offered to them.

Due to the complexity of the interactions between the service designs and service operations, analytical modeling was ineffective for understanding this problem setting. Simulation permitted us to control the experimental conditions through multiple replications to achieve strong internal validity. To mitigate external validity concerns, we derived consumer decision rules from data collected through stated choice experiments. We also utilized <sup>fi</sup>eld study knowledge from service providers in setting parameters such as the present transportation network, train timetable and operation, and baseline service pricing.

There are a number of relevant managerial implications. Current IT and systems provide new opportunities for service providers to retrieve real-time consumer information, automatically analyze customer behavior—in the style of near to real-time analytics, respond directly to consumer requests, and provide customers with a highly customized experience. They can also go beyond this, to learn from what they do. This increases the need for developing solid learning and optimization routines to facilitate decision-making and guide future planning efforts.

## 4.2. Applicability, limitations and future research

We applied our models and analytics to issues in the public rail transportation industry. Our approach can be applied across domains and to companies that are examining a range of emerging technologies to develop service innovations. This goes beyond the <sup>fi</sup>rm and consumer setting to business-to-business environments. For example, in situations where service providers structure IT services contracts, managers are often faced with the task of choosing contractual parameters such as service levels, pricing, quality, and contract duration. Decisions regarding parameter selection are important because they affect consumers' willingness-to-pay for the related services. Our approach suggests that the more service providers learn about the contractual parameters, the more they are able to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from contracting. Further, the more they learn how their partner values the contractual terms, the better they will be able to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their parameter selection and generate higher willingness-to-pay in the long run. This will affect the risk position of a portfolio of service contracts and overall pro<sup>fi</sup>tability.

There are some limitations of this study and some future avenues for research. First, our method provides support for <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in view of consumer behavior responses toward service designs, but it does not incorporate multiple train services that are available at the same time. We can relax this assumption in future research and analyze how multiple service offerings will affect <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

Second, there are more variables that affect consumer behavior than the ones we modeled in our empirical application. For example, consumers can self-select their preferred services based on their departure time <sup>fl</sup>exibility, including <sup>fl</sup>exibility in their work schedules, their time with their families, and connecting transit to other stations. The more <sup>fl</sup>exible a consumer's activities are, the more likely he will be to choose a cheaper product that has more associated restrictions. To offer a richer explanation, future research may wish to consider the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of <sup>fl</sup>exibility on departure time choice. In addition, consumers may choose when to buy products in response to <sup>fi</sup>rms' pricing practices. Other relevant issues for future research include manipulating product availability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchase timing, valuation uncertainty, and other consumer learning effects.

Third, while the use of a discrete choice model is an attractive tool to understand consumer behavior, there are many other alternatives that may be effective. For example, future research can use data mining to facilitate categorization and segmentation of smart card data to understand <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained level of consumer choices in terms of departure time, travel mode, travel route, and product selection. A <sup>fi</sup>rm also might conduct conjoint analysis to understand the tradeoffs between different attributes of the service. Or it could apply other experimental and statistical methods that may be helpful for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to know more about its current as well as future consumer preferences and behavior for possible service design and pricing changes. The analysis methods should determine the data to be collect ed, but also be selected based on how expensive and feasible the data are to capture.

Further, future research should consider using data about revealed consumer choices from actual behavior, especially near to real-time data involving changes in consumer behavior in view of the changes that are occurring to service offerings. There are many rich data sources available to gather customer data. We are currently exploring new ways to conduct this kind of research through the new process of experiment-driven societal-scale data analytics with <sup>fi</sup>rms in the transportation, the <sup>fi</sup>nancial services and retail telecommunications industry. Some other examples are smartdust systems involving micro-electro-mechanical environmental sensors [38] and affective bioanalytics systems, and other sensing technologies that allow organizations to collect a large amount of consumer and social media data.

## Appendix A. A description of the simulation

We present below an overview and describe the details and assumptions of the simulation (Fig. A1).

## Population generation

A person, as an agent, is created with a set of demographic characteristics, such as home location, age, gender, and education. A person also has the possibility of working for an employer, which has a name and an of<sup>fi</sup>ce location. We use standard geographic coordinates – latitude and longitude – to de<sup>fi</sup>ne location.

## Activity schedule construction

Travel demand is derived from demand for conducting activities at speci<sup>fi</sup>c times and locations. We assume a person plans his travels based on his activity plan. An activity describes what a person wants to do, when and where he wants to be during the day. We assume that information on activity type, sequence, location, and timing are known to each person. Each person has a 24-hour activity plan that consists of a number of activities that they would like to conduct. From this activity plan, a person could construct his daily activity and journey plan that includes both activities and the associated journeys. Starting from an empty daily activity and journey plan, activities are added according to their start time and tolerance. If the adjacent two activities are not in the same location, this person will plan a journey to reach the location of the next activity. A routine is also designed to check for time overlaps. For example, if a journey cannot <sup>fi</sup>nish before the start time of the next activity, either the adjacent activities or the journey is shifted in time.

Fig. A1. Conceptual overview of simulation.

## Journey planning

If a journey is required between two activities, the person will need to use the journey planning module. It is a process of searching for a set of alternative journeys between the given origin–destination (OD) city-pair within the start and end time of these two activities. As a result of this process, a set of possible journeys is generated. These journeys may vary in cost, departure time, arrival time, transport mode, and service class.

## Journey selection

Among these alternative journeys, we assume a person will choose the journey that permits him to arrive at the destination the earliest, without taking travel cost into account. This is because the value of travel time-savings is known to be the most important factor for consumers of public transportation services. They consider the value associated with saving time, since it can be allocated to other activities. We used a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of Dijkstra's algorithm to obtain the journey with the earliest arrival time. Instead of searching for the shortest path, our implementation uses arrival time as the weight of each link (rather than distance) and identi<sup>fi</sup>es the journey with the earliest arrival time giving by the timetable information. Further, we also include the available transportation mode to determine the minimal arrival time and the order of the carriers that are used by each traveler.

## Service selection

We assume that a person is presented with a number of service options for each alternative journey. We adopt the discrete choice model to model this service selection process. It describes how a person makes a choice from a set of mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive alternatives that are available. This person calculates the utility of each alternative as a function of the observed service attributes, and the unobserved random part. This randomness could be caused by unobserved attributes, unobserved taste variations, measurement errors and imperfect information. The theory of random utility suggests that the person will have a higher probability to choose the service that has the greatest utility among all available alternatives. As a result of the journey selection and service selection process, a journey with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c route, departure and arrival time, and travel cost is selected.

## Schedule execution

Until now, for each required journey between activities, a person has to choose the journey with the earliest arrival time and selects the design option with the greatest utility. This process iterates until the daily activities and journal plan are fully constructed. A person then executes his plan: conducts an activity in one location, makes a journey by traveling to another location for the next activity, and so on.

## Service operation

When making a journey, a person will need to board the chosen scheduled carrier. If the demand exceeds the capacity, the person will board the next available carrier. The supply simulation consists of a transport network assignment, transportation vehicle movement and a timetable. A transportation vehicle offers scheduled transportation services according to timetable information on a given transportation network.

## Appendix B. A decision analytics approach for adaptive learning

We think of the multi-period decision-making analytical process that we have described in terms of the following steps. (1) Service providers will collect new information regarding consumer willingness-to-pay under the service strategy that is currently in place. (2) They will estimate micro-level behavioral models based on the newly-collected data. If the newly-estimated model is better than the current model, the service provider will update the behavior estimation model to re-<sup>fl</sup>ect this. Otherwise, they will continue to analyze new consumer willingness-to-pay information based on methods that have been successful to date. At the same time, the service providers will collect market information that is available to them. They will choose appropriate services based on the information they obtain. (3) They will study the interactions between consumer responses and service operations in light of the chosen service designs. After new performance indicators related to the services become available to them, they will compare them against the previous levels of performance. (4) They will further adjust their expectations about the future demand forecast, the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of their pricing and service choices, as appropriate, and see what comes out in terms of business performance. Typically, the process will be iterative in that service providers will go back to (2), and collect more new information, and see what further iterative adjustments are appropriate. See Fig. B1.

![](/api/attachments/4XMY8FJ3/fulltext/images/b6cf1f94bd3d51d2ffd044efe338e9bf7f804e544e1789f40f1431b4df201479.jpg)  
Fig. B1. An adaptive learning approach for service design decision analysis.

This decision and learning process will involve an ongoing process and span a longer time horizon. Information on prior performance indicators serves as a means of providing continuous feedback to update current expectations about performance. Through learning, service providers will be able to mitigate the effects of past errors in their decision rules, and they can adjust their forecasts to understand the basis for identifying more stable and recognizable patterns in the future. This process will not <sup>fi</sup>nish until all interested consumers are able to purchase the travel services that maximize the value they take away based on what they receive in return.
