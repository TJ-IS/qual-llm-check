# IS outsourcing management competence dimensions: instrument development and relationship exploration

Zhengzhong Shi<sup>a,\*</sup>, A.S. Kunnathur<sup>b</sup>, T.S. Ragu-Nathan

<sup>a</sup>College of Business Administration, North Dakota State University, Putnam Hall, P.O. Box 5237, Fargo, ND 58105, USA <sup>b</sup>College of Business Administration, The University of Toledo, USA

Received 28 December 2003; received in revised form 17 July 2004; accepted 9 October 2004 Available online 8 December 2004

## Abstract

In this paper, we discuss our investigation of IS outsourcing management competences, their relationships and measurement. First, three theoretical perspectives were applied to explain the nature of the IS outsourcing phenomenon. Second, IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and their measures were generated, based on the literature. Third, data was then gathered to refine measures empirically and test proposed competences and their relationships. The theoretical development and empirical testing filled the gap in IS outsourcing management competence research resulting in important implications for IS outsourcing practitioners.

Keywords: Empirical research; IS outsourcing management competence; Knowledge management; Transaction costs; Agency theory; Interorganizational process

## 1. Introduction

In research studies, determinants [2,44,64] and best practices of IS outsourcing [3,25,40,57,37,38,46] have been investigated and discussed. IS outsourcing related costs, risks, and pitfalls have also been considered [7,16,29]. Consequently, in practice, firms need to have the necessary IS outsourcing management ability to evaluate the determinants and implement best practices that can reduce costs, contain risks, and avoid pitfalls in IS outsourcing. They should be able to accurately measure IS service performance both before they sign the contract and throughout the process [66]. Thus, a set of IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and their relationships may work as credible guides in helping firms understand the necessary IS outsourcing management needs and develop them subsequently [12,56,65].

While many case studies and some surveys exist, e.g., [10,42,43], apparently no survey based IS outsourcing management competence research has been performed. This paper is intended to fill the gap.

## 2. Theoretical perspectives on IS outsourcing

## 2.1. An inter-organizational process based perspective

Dwyer et al. [15] proposed a model for a sellerbuyer relationship developmental process consisting of four stages: awareness, exploration, expansion, commitment, and dissolution. Ring and Van de Ven [55] view ‘‘the development and evolution of a cooperative IOR as consisting of a repetitive sequence of negotiation, commitment, and execution stages, each of which is assessed in terms of efficiency and equity.’’ An IS buyer–supplier relationship is an interorganizational relationship (IOR) involving all the stages and dynamics. This process perspective helps clarify the fact that various stages of IS outsourcing may require different sets of skills and routines and thus helps identify the scope of the IS outsourcing management competence.

## 2.2. A knowledge management based perspective

In the information intensive and knowledge based post-industrial society [28], a view of the firm as an institutional mechanism to integrate knowledge is insightful and meaningful [23]. This is similar to combinative capability in [30] in the sense that both focus on a firm’s capability to flexibly and efficiently assemble a diversity of knowledge for more effective and efficient production in order to achieve competitive advantage. Outsourcing is a part of the way that firms assemble knowledge from suppliers [52]. Thus, IS outsourcing can be seen as a mechanism to integrate IS knowledge from IS vendors [41]. Further, IS outsourcing may encourage the generation of new ways to use IS for better organizational performance through positive interactions, with some chaos between the buyer and the vendor [48]. Finally, some academics have concerns regarding the potential loss of internal know-how through IS outsourcing [67].<sup>1</sup> We believe that a knowledge management-based perspective of IS outsourcing can help determine ways of avoiding loss of important knowledge and nurturing an organizational learning capability.

## 2.3. An economics based perspective

Two related streams, transaction costs economics (TCE) and agency theory, have been used while investigating the buyer–supplier relationship. TCE is focused on the organizational boundary, while agency theory has been focused on the contract between the two involved parties [17]. Factors in the transaction cost model (TCM), such as demand and technological uncertainty, and asset specificity [68], and those in agency theory, such as non-separability of performance among team members [1] and task programmability (i.e., knowledge of a production process) [18,50], are influential in choosing the IOR structure and process [45].

An IS buyer–supplier relationship will certainly be impacted by the transaction costs and these factors. Grover et al. found that asset specificity of transactions needed to be considered for a high level of IS outsourcing effectiveness [24]. Lacity and Hirschheim [35,36] pointed out that the measurement uncertainty may generate opportunistic behaviors on the part of an IS vendor. Aubert et al. [5] applied a TCM including both asset specificity and uncertainty to analyze the level of IS outsourcing. Thus, an economic perspective inspired us to pursue an answer to: what IS outsourcing management competences are necessary to deal with these TCE and agency theory related factors?

## 3. IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and their relevance to theoretical perspectives

Four IS outsourcing management competences, informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring, and vendor development, have been conceptualized in [19] and these competences are a part of the IS services delivery capabilities. The following paragraphs will review their definitions and establish their relevance to theoretical perspectives.

## 3.1. Informed buying and its relevance to theoretical perspectives

Informed buying is the capability of IS purchasing personnel to manage the IS sourcing strategy that meets the interests of business. Feeny and Willcocks propose that informed buying involves analyzing the external IS services market, selecting a sourcing strategy to meet business needs and solve technology issues, and leading the tendering, contracting, and services management processes. It is clearly a conceptualizing competence responsible for analyzing IS demand and supply, finding the fit between IS supply and demand, and conveying this fit analysis to users, managers, and executives so that all the involved parties can fully understand and innovatively embrace IS demand–supply interactions to ensure IS outsourcing success.

As to its relevance to theoretical perspectives, informed buying is clearly related to different stages of IS outsourcing process in the sense that it describes the quantity and quality of information and knowledge available at each stage for decision making and implementation. Second, informed buying is information and knowledge intensive in itself and thus demands effective knowledge management for better internal and external IS supply and demand knowledge integration and creation. Third, since decisions such as which functions will be outsourced and to whom will be made in informed buying, TCE and agency considerations, such as asset specificity, uncertainty, non-separability, and task programmability, have to be taken into consideration in IS outsourcing decision making.

## 3.2. Contract facilitation, contract monitoring, and their relevance to theoretical perspectives

## 3.2.1. Contract facilitation

Since many users within a business receive IS services from multiple external and internal supply points in detailed and lengthy agreements, it is necessary to have a mechanism through which these services can be coordinated and synchronized, conflicts between users and suppliers be resolved in a collaborated fashion, lessons learned from one buyer–supplier relationship can be documented and applied to other situations, and both excessive user demands and cost overruns by vendors can be managed and limited. Feeny and Willcocks propose that contract facilitation should act as this kind of mechanism to ensure the success of IS outsourcing. Fundamentally, contract facilitation is an actionoriented competence with a purpose to manage contract negotiation, execution, and conflicts resolution. It is a response to the bounded rationality of human beings that results in incomplete contracts [27], which demands continuous adjustments from all involved parties.

## 3.2.2. Contract monitoring

Feeny and Willcocks indicate that while contract facilitation works to make things happen, contract monitoring is the capability of a firm to protect its business’ contractual position over time. They propose that ‘‘effective contract monitoring means holding suppliers to account on both existing service contracts and developing performance standards of the services market.’’ Hence, it is critical for IS outsourcing management to ‘‘develop service level measures and reports, specify escalation procedures, include cash penalties for nonperformance, determine growth rate, and adjust charges to changes in business.’’ All these performance and payment structure and processes are to help justify any actions taken to hold IS suppliers be accountable. Fundamentally, contract monitoring is an action-oriented competence that provides the necessary performance measurement process and final performance information for the diagnosis of problems and the prescription of solutions in managing IS buyer and supplier behaviors.

## 3.2.3. Their relevance to theoretical perspectives

First, contract facilitation and monitoring are certainly an important part of an inter-organizational process, which are closely related to negotiation, commitment, and execution stages and the exploration, expansion, commitment stages. Second, contract facilitation and monitoring will generate many interaction opportunities at individual, team, and organizational levels between IS suppliers and buyers and thus will establish a field for knowledge creation and integration. For example, innovative concepts and practices on how to use contracts to regulate both IS suppliers and buyers’ behaviors to create win–win situations in IS outsourcing may be generated and applied in the contract revision and enhancement processes. Third, on the one hand, contract facilitation is clearly related to organizational boundaries design (e.g., managing excessive user demand and cost overruns by vendors), which is a transaction costs model concern. On the other hand, contract monitoring is related to performance measurement, which is an agency theory based concern.

## 3.3. Vendor development and its relevance to theoretical perspectives

Feeny and Willcocks describe vendor development as ‘‘organizations looking beyond existing contractual arrangements to explore the long-term potentials for suppliers to create win–win situations in which the supplier increases revenues by providing services that increase business benefits’’. To realize the long term potentials in IS outsourcing, a long-term oriented risks and benefits sharing system (i.e., creating win–win situations and growing together) and an appropriate context for this sharing system (i.e., mutual understanding of business processes) are necessary to encourage both the IS supplier and the IS buyer to collaborate with each other for better IS outsourcing performance. Existing IS outsourcing research have already demonstrated that the quality of relationships with IS vendors has significant impacts on IS outsourcing success. Fundamentally, vendor development is a both future and action oriented competence dimension and is related to the long-term success of both the IS supplier and the IS buyer.

As to vendor development’s relevance to theoretical perspectives, first, it is certainly an interorganizational process to benefit both the supplier and the buyer. Existing research in operations management field such as Krause and his colleagues’ papers [32–34] on supplier development antecedents, processes, and consequences have already demonstrated the importance of the inter-organizational process perspective on vendor development. Second, the mutual understanding of business processes and motives and the exploration of new outsourcing potentials and opportunities in vendor development require inter-firm learning and provide opportunities for knowledge exploitation and creation. Third, since vendor development has a purpose to grow together with vendors, it will certainly impact continuous contract revisions to redefine organizational boundaries, improve the performance measurement system, and enhance the inter-firm production economics.

In summary, as demonstrated in Fig. 1, IS outsourcing management competences developed from previous IS outsourcing research can be explained from the inter-organizational process, knowledge management, and economic perspectives.

## 4. Research methodology

A rigorous methodology has been followed to implement this research. Items were generated based on previous IS outsourcing research and field interviews were conducted to explore their content validity and motivate this current research. A pretest and a pilot test were also administrated to further enhance the quality of the survey questionnaire. A large-scale survey was then implemented. Nine rather than four IS outsourcing management competence dimensions were derived from data analysis. Second-order IS outsourcing management competence factors were then proposed based on literature review and measurement model testing. Relationships among these second-order factors were also investigated.

## 4.1. Item generalization

Measurement items for informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring, and vendor development were generated, based on literature. Due attention was paid to content validity by making sure that measurement items covered the content domain of a theoretical construct [11].

## 4.2. Field interviews

Field interviews were conducted to explore and verify the content domain and refine measurement items. Two IS directors and one quality director from two automobile suppliers agreed to have four interviews with researchers. The two companies are identified as D-Corporation and A-Corporation. Their customers are OEMs such as Ford, GM, and DaimlerChrysler. They have very high purchasing costs; D-Corporation has an annual purchasing cost of about US \$8 billion. Each interview lasted from about 1 h to over 2 h and measurement items (see Appendix A) were used to explain the meaning of IS outsourcing management competence dimensions at an operational level. Among many IS outsourcing decisions, directors emphasized that decisions, such as which IS functions needed to be outsourced and which needed to be kept in house, had long-term impact. Further, they had two serious concerns:

1. the effectiveness and efficiency of the IS outsourcing services, and

2. the capability of the firm to absorb new technol ogies after outsourcing.

The first concern entailed managing sourcing strategy, vendor selection, contract management, and vendor development. The second was related to the first concern, since effective organizational learning over a long time depends on the structure and management of the IS outsourcing process.

IS outsourcing management competences are definitely required for these, since IS outsourcing management competences directly address sourcing strategy management, vendor selection, contract management, and vendor development and shape the mechanisms, procedures, boundaries, and future directions for IS outsourcing. Informed buying allows the firm to develop its knowledge on IS demand, IS supply, and their interactions. Informed buying is the prerequisite for having quality IS outsourcing services. Contract facilitation and monitoring captures the capability of the firm to negotiate and execute the contract and facilitate its evolution for efficient operations. Vendor development captures the ability of the firm to create an informal, intellectual, and social atmosphere to balance the formal and legal contractual atmosphere and manage non-routine operations. It impacts both the contract execution efficiency and the learning effectiveness throughout outsourcing. In summary, these interviews validated the IS outsourcing management competences and enhanced the design of measurement items.

## 4.3. Pretest

A pretest was implemented to refine measurement items by presenting the questionnaire to two dissertation-stage Ph.D. candidates, two IS professors, one operation management professor, and one IS director. They made many comments and the quality of the survey was improved.

## 4.4. Pilot study

This was used to enhance the reliability and validity of measurement items and refine the design. Five hundred names were randomly selected from a name list provided by Applied Computer Research, Inc; it contained entries for 3925 IS executives. Surveys were administered and phone calls were made to all the potential respondents. There were 31 responses, 29 of which were useful. Corrected item total correlation (CITC) was used to purify the measurement items, and Cronbach alpha was used to test the reliability of the measurement items [58]. Items are deleted iteratively if their CITC scores were below 0.5. A higher than 0.70 for alpha was also pursued in maintaining or deleting items [49]. All four competences had good CITCs and Alphas and all 24 items were kept for the large scale study.

## 4.5. The large scale study

## 4.5.1. Data collection, data, and methods

The list contained 3425 IS executives names after the pilot study. Two sets of surveys were administered in the US, each having several waves of surveys with 2 or 3 weeks in between. Three hundred phones calls were made to find why IS directors and managers had not responded: typical reasons were company policy not to answer surveys, lack of time, etc, due to travel, retirement, time constraints, quitting job, change of departments, vacations, and undeliverable addresses.

There were 82 useful responses from the first set of mailings and 123 from the second set of mailings; thus 205 useful responses were received. One hundred ninety eight were returned without responses. The effective response rate was thus 205/ $( 3 4 2 5 - 1 9 8 ) = 6 . 3 5 \%$ . While this was low and thus a limitation of the study, there were enough for statistical analysis. For example, Sethi and King [60] used 185 sample points to show the extent to which IT applications provide competitive advantage using structural equation modeling.

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the firms and people responding to the study; 64% were from manufacturing, 17% from services, and 15% from other industries. As to firm size, 26% had 1000 to 2499 employees and 33% over 2500 employees. Sales for 82% were larger than \$100 million. Nineteen percent were CIOs, 35% IS directors, 21% IS managers, 11% vice presidents. Thus, respondents were likely to be aware of or be directly involved in IS outsourcing.

As to non-response bias, two tests were conducted to compare the first set of respondents with the second set on firm size and sales. The second set was treated as non-respondents [4,39]. It was found that the two chi-square values (9.10 for the firm size test and 5.63 for the sales test) were less than the critical value 22.3 (0.95 confidence of chi-square test with 13 degrees of freedom); this implied that there were no statistical differences between the first and second set of respondents in terms of firm size and sales. We concluded that respondent bias did not exist in the data.

## 4.5.2. Large study results

Table 2 shows results after the exploratory factor analysis using SPSS. All the factor loadings are greater than 0.4, which are significant with a sample size around 200, and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) is 0.89, which is outstanding [26]. It was found that there are five competence dimensions. First, contract facilitation items were split into two groups. After an examination of all its items, it is clear that CONF1-3 are from the user’s point of view and CONF4-5 are from the IS function’s point of view. Thus, two dimensions were derived from this competence. Reliability analyses showed that both CONF1-3 and CONF4-5 had good reliabilities.

Second, vendor development items were also been split into two. VENM1, 4, and 5 were focused on fostering a long-term relationship with vendors by creating win–win situations, increasing mutual understanding, and growing together. VENM2 and 3 together with contract monitoring items formed a different dimension with a focus on vendor management at the operational level. All contract monitoring items measured whether there are processes to ensure that IS suppliers were held accountable on both existing and evolving standards and functionality in the IS service market. Thus, these contract monitoring actions, together with annual meetings to develop new IS outsourcing and reports highlighting IS supplier achievements against industrial and contractual standards (i.e., VENM2 and 3), constitute an operational level of vendor management system including developing standards, measuring performance, and enforcing contracts. Thus five competence dimensions were derived from the data analysis.

## 4.6. Convergent validity

The convergent validity criteria require that there be one latent variable underlying a set of measurement items and LISREL can be used to test for it [8,9,13,21,59]. If one dimension has fewer than four items, the convergent validity will be tested with other dimensions, since there will not be enough degrees of freedom for structural equation model estimation when tested individually. Models are respecified only when there are strong statistical and substantive reasons. The exact modification procedure followed to derive a set of relatively parsimonious and pure indicators is shown in Table 3. Based on model fit indices and loadings, it is easy to see that all models have good model fit indices and show high levels of convergent validity after model respecification.

The AVE for each construct, measuring the ratio between the variance captured by a construct and the measurement error [22], was also calculated (see Table 4). All the AVEs are well above 0.5, which means that each latent variable explains more than half of the variance in its measurement items: thus good convergent validity is demonstrated. In summary, after checking measurement models and AVEs for convergent validity, nine meaningful IS outsourcing management competence dimensions had been derived.<sup>2</sup>

## 4.7. Composite factor reliability

Nine composite factor reliabilities were calculated (Table 5). All reliabilities are greater than 0.8, implying that all the measurement items are sufficient to represent their respective latent factors [58].

## 4.8. Discriminant validity

This refers to the uniqueness and independence of the measures, i.e., the extent to which the measures are distinctively different from each other. Discriminant validity among any two of the nine dimensions was tested by comparing the chi-square values of the original (the model with the correlation between the two factors free of control) and constrained model where the two factors are united as one construct (the correlation between the two factors set as 1). If the chisquare value of the constrained model is much larger than that of the original model, then the two dimensions are different from each other and cannot be united as one dimension. And the two dimensions meet the discriminant validity criterion.

As indicated in Table 6, all statistics demonstrate that discriminant validity were good for all pairs of dimensions. In addition, it was found that each dimension’s AVE was larger than the square of their correlations with other factors, suggesting that items share more common variance with their respective constructs than any variance that the construct shares with other constructs [20] and thus discriminant validity was demonstrated again.

## 4.9. Predictive validity

If a construct has a significant relationship with a performance criterion, then it is said to have good predictive validity. The underlying rationale is based on proposition that the conceptual meaning of a construct should be determined not only by its definition and measure but also by its relationship to other constructs. Our study used IS performance effectiveness to evaluate the predictive validity of all dimensions of IS outsourcing competence. Table 7 shows that all IS outsourcing management competence dimensions had significant relationships with IS performance.

## 4.10. Relationship exploration and empirical examination

After nine IS outsourcing management competence dimensions had been identified and their measures developed, it was beneficial to investigate the relationships among them in order to better understand the underlying theoretical meanings. To achieve this goal, a co-variation model was tested first to verify the existence of relationships among these nine dimensions and it laid a foundation for further investigation of second-order competence factors. Then, three second-order competence factors were proposed based on literature review and the second-order factor model. The development of the second-order factors was based on both statistical and substantive analyses. Further, in this paper, the importance of substantive analysis with the support of statistical analysis was emphasized.

It was proposed that the first second-order factor consist of three first-order dimensions: (1) IS sourcing competence based on business needs and technological standards (F1), (2) the understanding of both internal and external IS service supply options (F2), (3) IS outsourcing leadership through appropriately tendering, leading, and servicing management (F3). The second-order factor, informed buying, focuses on the competence of IS purchasing personnel to analyze IS supply, IS demand, and their interactions, and fully convey analytical results to all parties throughout the IS outsourcing process.

We proposed that the second of the secondorder factor consist of (1) existing contract execution (F4), (2) contract development and enhancement (F5), and (3) IS supplier accountability for evolving IS markets (F6). These first-order dimensions clearly described the required competence in the whole life cycle of IS outsourcing contract management. First, with an existing contract, efficient execution is the prerequisite for any degree of IS outsourcing success. Second, IS outsourcing contract development and enhancement is inevitable, because any IS outsourcing contract is most likely an incomplete contract representing only a limited view of the dynamic IS outsourcing process. Further, as IS personnel’s understanding of IS outsourcing is deepening, they are becoming more capable of developing new and enhancing existing contracts. Third, to benefit from these contract developments and enhancements, holding IS suppliers to be accountable for new developments in technology and standards is clearly required. In summary, this second-order factor, contract management, focuses on contract development, execution, and evolvement.

It was proposed that the third second-order factor consisted of (1) contract facilitation from users’ point of view (F7), (2) contract facilitation from IS function’s point of view (F8), and (3) strategic IS vendor development (F9). These first-order dimensions measure how well the relationships among IS function, users, and IS vendors are managed so that all parties in the process collaborate with each other to solve problems, resolve conflicts, enhance mutual understanding and trust, create win–win situations, and grow together as a community. This factor, relationship management, has been found to be critical for IS outsourcing success by Grover et al. and Lee and Kim. Relationship management is playing a crucial role in balancing a focal firm’s reliance on formal and legal procedures (contract management, e.g., formal bargaining, formal legal contract, and role interactions) with informal and inter-personal norms (e.g., informal sense making, psychological contract, and personal interactions in the relationship management). This balance is the foundation for all parties to negotiate, commit to, and execute an intellectual IS outsourcing process in a cooperative and economical manner.

## 4.10.3. Relationships among second-order competence factors

The three second-order factors are inter-related. First, informed buying is the knowledge basis for both generating a substantive guiding framework, which can be used to design and manage a contract and create a shared knowledge base for managing IS vendor, user, and IS staff relationships. Second, contract management provides the structure and process based on which IS vendor, user, and IS staff relationships are managed. On the other hand, it also generates opportunities, such as contract revision and enhancement, for involved personnel to experiment their innovative IS outsourcing management ideas to enlarge the knowledge base for better informed buying. Third, through establishing an appropriate social and intellectual atmosphere, such as mutual understanding of business processes, relationship management not only facilitates the exploitation and exploration of an existing IS outsourcing contract (i.e., contract management), but also helps IS outsourcing management better understand IS supply, IS demand, and their interactions, and fully communicate their insights to all involved parties for better decision making (i.e., informed buying). Finally, the continuously interacting relationships among informed buying, contract management, and relationship management are forming a spiraling cycle for better decision making and implementation throughout the IS outsourcing process. ## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Contributions to research

This paper added to understanding of IS outsourcing phenomenon by applying inter-organizational process, knowledge management, and economic perspectives. It also contributed to instrument development for a set of nine first-order IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and their relationships with three second-order IS outsourcing management competence factors. These first-order competence dimensions and second-order competence factors captured the necessary organizational capabilities to plan and execute various IS outsourcing activities throughout an inter-organizational process for both economic and intellectual benefits.

A side effect was that the instrument development substantiated competence-based theory by responding to a critique of its lack of operationalization. Giovanni Dosi and Teece described competence perspective as ‘‘a differentiated set of skills, complementary assets, and organization routines which together allow a firm to coordinate a particular set of activities in a way that provides the basis for competitive advantage in a particular set of market or markets [14]’’. However, while Williamson does not ‘‘attempt a sustained critique. . .,’’ he does ‘‘pose two related concerns: obscure and often tautological definitions of key terms; and failures of operationalization’’ [69]. Thus, by developing IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and their operationalized measurement items here, a competence-based perspective has been substantiated because an instance of the abstract competence has been empirically operationalized and verified.

## 5.2. Contributions to practitioners

For practitioners, the development and empirical confirmation of IS outsourcing management competence dimensions and factors provide an overview of the different IS outsourcing management competences. This will help them find their missing competences and allow them to develop them. Insight into the continuously interacting relationships among informed buying, contract management, and relationship management helps managers realize the incomplete nature of any contract due to bounded rationality, changing business needs, and evolving technologies and thus motivates management to continuously improveme and learn better ways of managing IS outsourcing.

## 5.3. Research limitations

Limitations here include:

1. the use of only one key informant in each responding organization,

2. the low response rate, the fact that the demographics may influence the generalizability of the findings,

3. the ordering of the measurement items not being totally randomized with possible common method bias because ‘‘respondents may be sensing the inherent constructs via the ordering of questionnaire items and they may respond accordingly’’ [63]. However, this effect may not be significant as our measurement items reflect different aspects of one latent variable, which is somewhat different from the TAM example. We have calculated all the nine dimensions’ Cronbach Alphas and only three of them are above 0.9 and none of them are above 0.95. Nevertheless, it is necessary to have future research by randomizing measurement items and/or using different data gathering methods to reduce or eliminate the common method bias to further advance this stream of research.

## 6. Conclusion

As IS outsourcing becomes more critical for both traditional industrial firms and new Internet players, firms need to understand IS outsourcing management competences and how to develop them. For management to achieve such goals, we believe that our set of IS outsourcing management competence first-order dimensions, second-order competence factors, and their relationships and measurement provide a credible framework for real actions.
