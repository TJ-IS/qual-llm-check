# Why the first provider takes it all: the consequences of a low trust culture on pricing and ratings in online sourcing markets

David Gefen<sup>1</sup> and Erran Carmel<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management, LeBow College of Business, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Kogod School of Business, Management of Global Information Technology, American University, Washington DC, U.S.A.

‘Trust matters, especially in emerging markets where the rule of law is weak’. (The Economist, 2011, p. 13)

## Abstract

Electronic markets are ruled by price and reputation, and, at least in the case of Online Sourcing Markets (OSM), also by preference for providers the buyer already contracted with. OSM are online markets for software development. Adding Fukuyama’s notion of a low trust culture, an argument is advanced why buyers in OSM may give absolute preference to providers with whom they had previous contracts, presenting a special case of neoclassical contracting. Examining all the transactions in one calendar year at a leading OSM supports this proposition. All it took to be given the tender was to be the only bidding provider with at least one successful previous contract with the buyer, rendering pricing and ratings immaterial to bid choice. Only when none of the bidding providers had previous successful projects with the buyer did pricing and rating affect bid choice. The proposition is also consistent with the buyers’ comments about their providers. Implications are discussed about how a low trust culture affects OSM behavior. ## Introduction

Research shows that electronic markets are governed by relative price (Gefen & Carmel, 2008) and affected by reputation (Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006). This is what transaction costs economics, TCE, calls classical contracting (Williamson, 1985). Classical contracting is about choosing among providers based primarily on price as it deals with nonspecific standardized low asset specificity goods or services with many alternative comparable providers. At least in the case of Online Sourcing Markets (OSM), buyer loyalty is also critical in determining which bid is chosen (Gefen & Carmel, 2008). OSM are online markets for software development services. Buyer loyalty is the preference for providers the buyer previously contracted with. This places OSM closer to what TCE calls neoclassical contracting. Neoclassical contracting typically deals with high asset specificity where the goods or services cannot be easily used for other purposes (Williamson, 1985). This neoclassical preference for a long-term relationship identified in OSM echoes such preferences in other IT outsourcing contexts (Gulati, 1995; Kalnins & Mayer, 2004; Gefen et al, 2008; Rai et al, 2009).

This paper argues that there is another key consideration in an OSM that might explain the seemingly neoclassical contracting behavior that occurs in OSM, even without assuming the unlikely case that most tenders are of a high asset specificity nature, which is what TCE would require for this to occur. This additional factor is that OSM behave in a manner that is characteristic of low trust cultures, and that is why buyers are reluctant to engage with unknown providers. As discussed by Fukuyama (1995), low trust cultures are cultures where the legal system and historical precedent created a business atmosphere where people are afraid to trust strangers. As a result, people in low trust cultures prefer to do business within the family and those they already know when it concerns more than just a straightforward economic exchange. In contrast, in high trust cultures the legal framework creates an environment where there is reasonable protection from opportunistic business parties, and so people do not refrain outright from doing business with strangers even in transactions requiring long-term cooperation. By extension to electronic markets as exemplified by OSM, if the market creates a low trust culture, even unintentionally, then buyers who have the option to do so will refrain from contracting with providers they do not already know from previous contracts. This is an important issue because online markets typically allow providers to reinvent themselves and erase their past with the perhaps inadvertent consequence of creating the same kind of consequences as a low trust culture creates.

This proposition was tested in the context of an OSM where, as typical in many other electronic markets, providers can disappear and then reappear easily if they wish to hide their history – and do so with impunity and with no repercussions, liabilities, waiting period, or cost. The presumed resulting low trust culture created by allowing providers to reincarnate themselves should, this study argues in Hypothesis 1, increase buyers’ reluctance to deal with unknown providers. This was supported in examining all the tenders posted in one calendar year in a leading OSM: Buyers backed out of their tenders considerably more frequently when no provider they knew from previous contracts bid on their tender. Continuing this line of logic, it was hypothesized, Hypotheses 2–5, that when providers the buyer previously contracted with bid, the buyer would choose these providers regardless of bid pricing or provider ratings. These hypotheses were supported to the extreme. In every one of the 800 þ tenders where such a provider bid, it was always such a provider who was given the tender. Buyer comments attached to their evaluations of the providers also show that the driving issue for buyers in this OSM was identifying a reliable provider, not relative price or the ratings others gave.

The study thus presents a new theoretical perspective, that of a low trust culture, why there will be cases where buyers might show absolute buyer loyalty to providers they know, and render pricing and ratings immaterial. This expands the TCE perspective – which implicitly assumes a high trust culture – by showing that such dominant buyer loyalty preference can be related to other aspects beyond the idiosyncratic asset specificity reason brought by TCE. This manifestation of absolute buyer loyalty also presents a context that questions the implied textbook answer that pricing is always important (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005). Likewise, that ratings are significant mostly only when there are no bids from providers with previous contracts with the buyer sheds new light on the importance of ratings which hitherto were treated in a manner implying that they too are always important. On a practical level, the results show the unexpected consequences of managing electronic markets in such a way that allows providers to reincarnate themselves and erase their past.

## Theory and proposition development

## The OSM context of this study

OSM are online markets where buyers post tenders for small software development projects and where providers then bid on these projects. In recent years OSMs have commonly been labeled crowdsourcing. Buyers are not obliged to choose the cheapest, or indeed any, of these bids. In contrast to online product markets such as eBay, where the product being bid on already exists and its quality can be reasonably assessed, in an OSM the software being provided does not yet exist when the bid is made. This additional uncertainty makes choosing a trustworthy provider a crucial issue in OSM (Snir & Hitt, 2003; 2004). To facilitate the choice of such a provider, OSM, much as other online markets do, allow buyers to rate providers, in this case on a 1–10 scale. The particular color coded scale of the OSM studied in this paper is shown in Figure 1. Only ratings of 7 and above are ‘good’ ratings.

Importantly, OSM are geared towards monetarily protecting the buyer. The buyer is under no obligation to accept the lowest or any other bid, and indeed in 43.27% of the cases in the year studied in this paper they did not choose any bid at all. Once the buyer chooses a bid, an escrow account is set up, the buyer pays the amount to the escrow account, and the provider begins the project. When and only when the project is completed to the satisfaction of the buyer is the provider paid from this escrow account. In a case of a disagreement between the buyer and the provider, an arbitrator may be invited to resolve the issue if the buyer wished to do so.

During the period of this study OSMs, and specifically the OSM we studied, vWorker, allowed anybody to register as a buyer or a provider. There was no identification or certification verification, no background checks, it was possible to have multiple aliases, and providers could easily present themselves as something they were not. Registration took minutes. There were internal certification exams providers could take, but these were not enforced and rarely used. No internal punishment or suspension mechanisms existed. ## A transaction costs economics perspective of OSM

TCE is probably the most cited theory used to study IT outsourcing and has been widely used in other disciplines as well to discuss why companies should buy products or services in the market rather than manufacture or provide them in-house (Lacity et al, 2011). The application of TCE to IT outsourcing has, however, yielded mixed results. These mixed results have been suggested to result from previous studies not including all the relevant aspects of TCE, inaccurately applying TCE precepts, and because TCE applies better as a normative theory (Alaghehband et al, 2011). It is also possible that TCE just does not fully capture the unique aspects of IT outsourcing (Lacity et al, 2011) such as the critical role of trust (Gefen et al, 2008; Lacity et al, 2010) and its related levels of risk in the marketplace (Gefen & Pavlou, 2011). The proposed addition in this study of treating OSM as a low trust culture environment is in line with the need to include additional aspects beyond only those captured by TCE.

In TCE contract governance is based to a large extent on economic rationale determined by asset specificity, frequency, and uncertainty – assuming bounded rationality and human agent opportunism (Williamson, 1985). These governance decisions occur in the context of the buyer preferring a governance type that reduces its costs in its make-or-buy decision. Transaction costs that are specifically relevant to OSM include search costs in identifying a trustworthy provider and building and managing a trusting relationship. In this section we shall discuss asset specificity. Frequency is represented through the key independent variable: the number of previous contracts between a buyer-seller pair. Uncertainty is the core of the argument we present that OSM because of the way they are managed create what seems to parallel low trust cultures, that is, they are a high uncertainty market for all transactions. The uncertainty argument will set the stage why for all transactions, without assuming anything about their level of asset specificity, OSM behave as neoclassical contracting.

Asset specificity is how unique an asset is to a specific context. When asset specificity is low, such as with standard goods and services available from many providers with minimal distinguishing features that may give one provider an edge over the others, relative price determines which provider is chosen. Hiring a Cþþ programmer to do basic coding, a typical type of assignment in a contract in OSM, should be a low specificity service with price being what counts in choosing a provider. This is what TCE calls classical contracting. This type of contracting is geared towards short-term relationships with one-time, well-defined transactions. In such cases, having had previous contracts with the buyer might give the provider an advantage (because custom code is inherently specific to the buyer it is written for and so knowing this code is advantageous to the provider), but probably not an overriding advantage in the small scale code dealt with in this OSM. The legal oversight in classical contracting is geared at enforcing the contract and protecting the monetary investment (Williamson, 1985). This is how many electronic markets treat transactions, including the OSM studied in this paper.

In contrast, a high specificity asset is one where the goods or service are unique. (TCE does not assume, and neither do we, that asset specificity is a dichotomous variable, being either low or high. Rather, it is presented this way, as in Williamson (1985), only as a matter of simplifying the story. Asset specificity is a matter of degree.) Knowing the inside workings of a company and doing systems analysis based on this knowledge would be a high specificity service asset. In such cases there is a preference for long-term relationships and writing the contract in such a way that it will encourage long-term orientation by all parties involved. An example of such long-term legal orientation is to make arbitration and conflict resolution an integral part of the contract so neither party can take unilateral action. This is what TCE calls neoclassical contracting. Here, the importance of pricing is moderated by the degree of asset specificity (Williamson, 1985). OSM do not operate this way. OSM permit buyers not to pay and allow them to forego arbitration when they are dissatisfied.

But the picture may be more complex than assuming that OSM should be understood as only low asset specificity provider service markets. After all, arguably, for buyers, identifying reliable providers should be a high informational transaction cost. It takes an OSM buyer a lot of ex ante costs in time and effort to identify a good provider, and this is done in the context of nonnegligible ex post risks and costs if a wrong choice is made. Since this information is useless except in the context of the specific OSM, it is consequently a high specificity information asset to the buyer. TCE would suggest that buyers who invested in obtaining this high specificity information should utilize it on a long-term basis. This adds a neoclassical contracting aspect to OSM, even if the OSM itself is managed to support primarily classical contracting. Note that we are not arguing for high asset specificity on account of the OSM dealing with software development because there is no evidence to claim so in the data. OSM contracts typically deal with very small projects that do not require detailed knowledge of the buyer or its operations.

## Pricing and buyer loyalty in OSM

As might be expected considering OSM are managed through classical contracting but have an element of neoclassical contracting, both pricing and contractual history have been shown to be crucial determinants of bid choice in OSM (Gefen & Carmel, 2008). This is not surprising also in view of the perhaps comparable context of products research where it has been shown that customers prefer providers they know (Jacoby, 1975;

Wernerfelt, 1991) and are willing to pay more for brands they are accustomed to (Krishnamurthi & Raj, 1991). Indeed, allowing that having previous contracts with the provider builds trust in electronic markets, as it does in other markets (Ganesan, 1994; Gulati, 1995), this willingness to pay more to providers the buyer trusts has been noted also in electronic markets (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004).

While both pricing and the contractual history between the buyer and the provider affect bid choice, the moderation effect of this contractual history on the importance of pricing has not been studied in OSM or electronic markets. Possibly related research on products does, however, show a moderation effect with past contracting making buyers less price-sensitive – although pricing is presumed to be always important (Starr & Rubinson, 1978; Wernerfelt, 1986; Krishnamurthi & Raj, 1991; Wernerfelt, 1991; Krishnamurthi & Papatla, 2003; Choi et al, 2006). Likewise, that provider ratings affect customer choice in electronic markets is also not new (Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006), although how this importance is moderated by the contractual history between the buyer and the provider is unknown.

## An alternative low cultural trust perspective

That there may be situations where contractual history might negate the importance of pricing and ratings altogether was not considered by previous research, as it is neither part of classical nor neoclassical contracting. In fact, the implied textbook answer is that pricing is supposed to be always important (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005). There may be situations, however, such as exemplified in OSM, where this will not be the case because buyer behavior will be based on identifying an appropriate provider and not on pricing and ratings at all. The key theoretical and practical issue at hand is that a market can create a low trust culture when its mechanisms do not provide adequate enforcement and contractual frameworks. In TCE terms this could be understood as markets operating in very high uncertainty, albeit as a matter of the overall market rather than the specific transaction as in TCE.

In low trust cultures people stick to their family, friends, and those they know when doing more than trivial one-time business transactions. In these cultures people try to refrain from doing business with strangers because they concentrate on controlling their risks. It is a market where if I do not know you and trust you personally then I do not transact with you. Only in a high trust culture, where the market does provide adequate protection do people feel comfortable to transact with strangers. As a result, markets flourish in high trust cultures, but stagnate in low trust ones (Fukuyama, 1995). As Fukuyama describes it, whole countries and regions can be in a high or a low trust culture because of their legal systems and history.

This is an important distinction because the way OSM operate may have inadvertently created what parallels such a low trust culture. In an OSM buyers and providers are not protected from opportunistic reincarnating parties, with the exception of monetary protection to the buyers. Note that research on previous contracting cited above and contract theory itself (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005) deal with a market governed by a strong legal system that provides reasonable enforcement and legal framework to all parties involved – the prerequisites of a high trust culture (Fukuyama, 1995). This includes enforced identification verification and the legal continuity of all parties in a contract. This implied assumption of a high trust culture business environment would seem to be an implicit assumption also in TCE as described by Williamson (1985). A vendor in a traditional market, for example, must be legally registered, if only for taxation and regulatory purposes, a process that deliberately takes time and costs money. Such a vendor cannot easily reinvent itself under a new name within minutes and erase the past because it acquired a bad reputation. This difference is a key distinguishing factor in electronic marketplaces including OSM, making them in effect conceptually parallel to low trust cultures.

To understand what high and low trust cultures mean, one must first reexamine what trust is all about. Trust is the willingness to rely on another person or company in contexts where the trusting party is vulnerable to possible opportunistic behavior from the trusted party (Mayer et $^ { a l , }$ 1995). Trust is a key issue in many contractual business relationships (Ganesan, 1994; Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005) and a determining force in e-commerce (McKnight & Chervany, 2002; Gefen et al, 2003), electronic markets (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), and presumably OSM (Gefen & Carmel, 2008). Trust is crucial because all contracts are in essence incomplete to some degree, and each party needs therefore to rely on the other beyond the letter of the contract not take advantage of the situation (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005). Trust is typically built when the trusted party lives up to expectations (Blau, 1964; Mayer et $^ { a l , }$ 1995; Gefen et al, 2003). This is why business familiarity, that is, having had previous successful contracts with the other party, builds trust (Gulati, 1995). When trust is broken people respond with distrust, controlling and avoiding risks associated with the behavior of others (McKnight et al, 2004; Pavlou & Gefen, 2005).The broad application of rating systems in electronic markets shows that the industry too recognizes the importance of finding a trustworthy partner.

The first hypothesis sets out to test whether indeed OSM behave in a manner characteristic of a low trust culture where people are reluctant to do business with strangers. If OSM do behave so then buyers should withdraw from fewer tenders when at least one of the bidding providers had previous successful contracts with the buyer who posted the tender. This is because such a provider should be less of an unknown stranger to the buyer, and hence the buyer should be less reluctant to transact in this case. A successful previous contract in the OSM studied is one where the buyer gave the provider a rating of seven or above, and is defined as such in this study. The other hypotheses will build on this hypothesis to argue that if OSM were to be viewed through the lenses of TCE alone the results would not be as extreme as would be expected based on a low trust culture perspective.

H1: Buyers will withdraw their tenders less often when at least one of the bidding providers on the tender had previous contracts with the buyer who posted the tender.

## Why buyers in an OSM should prefer providers they already know

Treating an OSM as a low trust culture should also result in a strong tendency to prefer providers the buyer already knows from previous contracting. Paralleling the logic of Fukuyama (1995), such providers should be preferred because they are not as much total strangers as those the buyer does not know at all. Allowing that OSM are low trust cultures where buyers try to avoid doing business with total strangers, it follows that buyers should choose providers they already know at least somewhat from previous contracting. Moreover, such providers may possibly even be trusted to some degree based on business familiarity achieved through previous contracting, as in other outsourcing contexts (Kalnins & Mayer, 2004).

This kind of strong tendency to prefer providers the buyer had previous successful contracts with, could be understood also based the buyers’ high information costs in identifying reliable providers or by assuming that in some cases the provider has a unique understanding of the buyer’s needs. This would suggest a shift from classical to neoclassical contracting, or even various types of relational contracting with their joint investment by buyer and provider and unified governance structures. Relational contracting is not explicitly supported by the institutional mechanisms of OSM; however, lasting relationships are built as a result of OSM use. Arguing for a neoclassical contract perspective based on TCE, while possible, is rather unlikely for several reasons. First, this is an imperfect application of TCE because the contract in an OSM is about monetarily protecting the buyer, a classical contract, rather than creating a longterm relationship, which is a characteristic of neoclassical contracting. Second, TCE, although not suggesting any clear cut distinguishing lines, would suggest that the hypothesized dominant advantage given to providers with previous successful contracts with the buyer should occur primarily with idiosyncratic assets and recurrent transactions. An idiosyncratic asset requires even more specialized knowledge that each of the parties should have about the other than in the case of high asset specificity. This is unlikely to be the case in the majority of tenders, which deal with small coding projects. And, according to TCE this strong preference should only occur when there are recurrent transactions, something that is hard to claim as prevailing in this OSM based on the frequency of recurrent contracts in Table 4. In other words, the low trust culture argument, resembling to some extent the uncertainty aspect of TCE, requires fewer assumptions on the data than arguing for the hypothesis based on asset specificity.

Sticking to providers the buyer knows from previous contracts is also rational considering the high search costs of identifying adequate providers, on the one hand, and the little expected benefits in savings that may arise from switching to another provider, on the other. The importance of these costs is magnified considering the low median contract price in Table 1. Selecting among many bids involves a time-consuming examination of the ratings and recommendations of the bidding providers. The potential saving of a few dollars that may be achieved by switching to another provider may simply not be worth the time it takes to search for an alternative provider once a reliable provider is already known based on past satisfactory contracts. Indeed, posted buyer comments attached to their ratings of providers they previously contracted with show this tendency to stick with providers who delivered reliably in the past without mention of relative price. Moreover, the risk that an unknown provider may not deliver reliably is a real threat considering that 15% of the tenders in this OSM are not fulfilled. Continuing to contract with a provider who has delivered satisfactorily in the past should reasonably, although not perfectly, assure the buyer to some extent that the provider will be able to deliver again as promised. Furthermore, although the structure of the interaction and payment system at the OSM where the data were collected was explicitly geared to financially protect the buyers, encountering an unsatisfactory provider could still be a hassle that buyers should rationally choose to avoid. Apart from anger, there are timeconsuming activities the buyer needs to redo, such as reposting the tender, searching for another provider and agreeing on the new contract (Carmel & Tjia, 2005). Accordingly, buyers should prefer providers they know, allowing as in H1 that OSM create a low trust culture. H2 examines whether this relationship is indeed so. Practically, this means that having successful previous contracts with the buyer should give a provider a dominant advantage over other bidders. The hypothesis discusses identifying the chosen provider, not the chosen bid, because in some of the tenders providers with previous contracts bid several times, typically lowering their price in subsequent bids.

## H2: Being the only bidding provider with previous successful contracts with the buyer who posted the tender will place the provider at a dominant advantage.

If H2 holds, or likewise as in H1 if OSM behave as low trust cultures, then other factors mentioned in the literature as affecting bid choice should be significant only when no providers with successful previous contracts with the buyer are bidding. This is because the overriding concern buyers have in such cases is choosing a provider they know, especially given the low median tender price in the OSM studied. The next set of hypotheses examines this. To begin with, bidding higher than the average on a tender should reduce the chances of the bid being chosen while bidding lower should increase these chances (Dans, 2002; Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005). On the other hand, if the logic of H1 and H2 holds, then pricing should be a minor consideration. The next hypothesis tests this.

## H3: The negative effect of charging a price premium on bid choice will not be evident when there are bids from providers with previous successful contracts with this buyer.

Likewise, the number and average of previous provider ratings are known to affect buyer choice in other online auctions and selling settings (Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Wu et al, 2012). More numerous and higher average ratings should give a provider an advantage, although whether this applies to OSM is not known as it was not part of Gefen & Carmel (2008). Based on the logic of H1 and H2, however, and presenting previous research in a new limiting light, this too should apply only when no providers with previous successful contracts with the buyer are participating. The next hypotheses examine this.

H4: Higher average provider ratings will not give the provider an advantage in bidding when there are bids from providers with previous successful contracts with this buyer.

H5: A higher number of provider ratings will not give the provider an advantage in bidding when there are bids from providers with previous successful contracts with this buyer.

## Controls

All else being equal, the more providers there are competing on a tender, the smaller the chance of any one of them winning it. To account for this, How Many Providers are Bidding on this Tender was added as a control. Replicating Gefen & Carmel (2008), if the Provider is Abroad, that is in another country than the buyer was also added as a control because this provider might be at a disadvantage because of cultural and linguistic issues. In other outsourcing contexts being abroad may add legal, cultural, and business risks (Rottman & Lacity, 2004; Beulen et al, 2006). Also added was the Number of Previous Exams the Provider Passed. Exams are certifications and should give a provider who passes them an advantage. Exams passed was not part of Gefen & Carmel (2008). Also included was the actual Bid Price so its effect could be compared with price premium.

## Data collection

## Data description

The data for this study were provided by vWorker.com, one of the largest OSM. In this type of market a buyer posts a tender describing the work that needs to be done, and then providers (also known as coders) post bids on this tender. Both buyers and providers must be registered at the site. Registration takes minutes. It is not checked against any national or tax payer identification. All projects are fixed price. No post-hoc negotiations on price are allowed. Communication is in English. Payments are in U.S. \$.

The data analyzed included all the tenders, all the bids, all the transactions on these tenders, all the provider ratings and exams, and country of origin of both buyer and provider from the founding of the company in May 2001 until the end of 2005.All these data were provided with a relative timestamp. This allowed the retrieval of the number of previous contracts between the specific buyer and provider as well as the average and number of each provider’s ratings and the number of exams passed by the provider by the timestamp of each particular bid. Although the hypotheses were tested only on all the tenders posted in 2005 and their associated bids, the calculation of the number of previous contracts as well as the ratings and exams up to the timestamp when the bid was made were calculated from 1 May 2001, when vWorker.com, originally called Rent A Coder, started. The data were initially analvzed to calculate these previous cumulative values with SQL . Price premium was calculated as the relative price of the amount in the bid to the average amount in all the bids on the same tender.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Price   Premium } = ((\text { Bid   Price }) - (\text { Average   Price   of } \\ \text { all   the   bidsin   this   tender })) / (\text { std.   of } \\ \text { the   bids   in   this   tender }). \end{array}
$$

In all, 99.21% of the providers had no previous contracts with the buyer. In 2009, 95% of the buyers were buyers who had previously bought at the site and it is likely that in 2005 the number of such buyers was also quite as high. Two exclusions were made: tenders that drew only one bid were excluded because in such cases there was no competition, and tenders that were ultimately not paid to any bidder. ## Qualitative data analysis

To verify that the underlying assumptions of preference for providers the buyer already knows are borne out in the market, we also examined the comments that buyers made about the providers. These comments are attached to the numeric rating that buyers give providers to explain their numeric rating. Two typical examples of these comments are ‘Pablo is the best!! He goes above and beyond of what you expect him to do and he is very patient. I highly recommend him’ and ‘Another job well done. I will be using Katy for all future web projects’. To categorize the themes and classify them, a subset of 100 of these comments was read by two raters and key themes were identified, then 500 postings were rated for these themes. The most frequent themes were: ability (466 postings, 93% of total), would use the same coder again (118 or 24%), speed (105 or 21%), being responsive (68 or 14%), and would recommend to others (54 or 11%). The inter-rater agreement levels on these themes were: Ability 94% agreement, would use the same coder again 96%, speed 88%, being responsive 96%, and would recommend to others 96%. We also looked for whether the comments hinted at the bid being reasonably priced or of price comparisons. A hint at such appeared only once (0.2% of total) – and even then in conjunction with having outstanding ability. In all, the results show that, at least in retrospect, what the buyers appreciated was deliverable quality. It was not cost cutting or ratings. This arguably supports the tendency to prefer providers the buyer already knows from previous contracts. These results are in accordance with observations in other online markets, albeit with products, that it is quality that rules the market, not low price (Reichheld & Schefter, 2000).

## Hypotheses 2–5

The remaining hypotheses were tested with logistic regression and replicated with linear regression to test for multicollinearity. Initially, in accordance with the wording of the hypotheses, only tenders where at least one bidding provider had previous contracts with the buyer were examined. This is shown in Table 3. A close examination of these data is shown in Table 4. As a post-hoc analysis all the tenders, including those where none of the bidding providers had previous contracts with the buyer were examined. This is shown in Table 5. The post-hoc analysis was done because examining only tenders where at least one provider had successful previous contracts with the buyer, Table 3 shows that pricing and rating were unimportant as hypothesized, seemingly contradicting previous findings that pricing and rating should be important. Combined, Tables 3–5 show that being the only bidding provider with successful previous contracts with the buver is all it takes to be given the tender, supporting the hypotheses, but that pricing and rating are significant when all the data, that is, including tenders where no bidding providers had previous contracts with the buyer, are analyzed.

The data analysis to test Hypotheses H2-H5 is shown in Table 3. Table 3 Column 1shows that the number of previous contracts with the buyer is by far the most important determinant of which bid is chosen, although seemingly price premium and average previous ratings are also significant. Column 1 shows that the number of previous contracts, price premium, and the average and number of previous ratings combined with the controls correctly classified all the chosen bids and overclassified 110 of the not chosen bids as chosen ones. A closer examination of the data shows, however, that while price premium, and the average and number of previous ratings are indeed significant predictors of which bid is chosen, Table 3 Column 2,they contribute nothing to the correct classification of the bids beyond what the number of previous contracts alone does, Table 3 Column 3. As Column 3 shows, the number of previous contracts alone also classifies correctly all the chosen bids and over classifies the 110 of the not chosen bids as chosen ones. In other words, although price premium and average previous ratings are significant, they contribute nothing to correctly classifying the chosen bids beyond what the number of previous contracts alone does. The results of the linear regression, Table 3 Column 4, support this.

Only the number of previous contracts with the buyer (b ¼ 0.868, Po0.001) is a significant determinant with a b greater than 0.01 in the linear regression. Indeed, a crosstabs of the number of previous contracts with the number of chosen bids, Table 4, shows that every one of the tenders where at least one bidding provider had previous contracts with the buyer was given to such a provider. Not a single contract was given to a provider with no previous contracts with the buyer in this subset. Table 3 Column 4 also shows there is negligible multicollinearity as all the VIF are between 1.007 and 1.243.

The results support the hypotheses. It was categorically only a matter of having had previous contracts that determined the chosen provider in every instance in the data where such a provider bid. As to the over classified 110 bids, a close examination of the data showed that these occurred because some providers bid more than once and because on some tenders more than one bidding provider had previous contracts with the buyer. There was not even much competition among providers with previous contracts. Among the bids made by providers with previous contracts on the same tender only five came from another provider with previous contracts with this buyer. All the other bids were by the same provider placing another bid, mostly at a lower price, on the same tender. Pricing and ratings, whether from the buyer or anyone else, made no difference.

As to why pricing and ratings were significant in Table 3 Column 1, the reason may be that providers with at least one previous contract with the buyer also bid lower and had better ratings, although technically the linear regression identified no problem with multicollinearity. The data show that the ratio of price to the average bid price on this tender was 0.947 (std. 0.505)

among providers with previous contracts compared with 1.109 (std. 0.757) among those without. And, average previous coder rating for providers with at least one previous contract with the buyer was 9.634 (std. 0.572), compared with 6.023 (std. 4.440) among those without.

Among providers with at least one previous contract with the buyer 99.999% of the ratings were above 7.0 and 99.985 above 8.0. In all, there were only four cases where a provider with at least one previous contract with the buyer had a previous average below 7. Of these four, in three cases the other bidding providers had even lower ratings, and in the other case the provider was bidding at a ratio of 0.42 of the average price while the one bidder with a higher rating was bidding at 2.16 the average, presenting an extreme case. These higher than 7 average ratings mean that all the data analyzed satisfied the caveat in the hypotheses that only previous contracts that were successful should be included in the analysis. There was no data to allow for an examination of the implied reciprocal part of the hypotheses that unsuccessful previous contracts do not have such an effect. One might suspect that with the ease of setting up new accounts providers who did poorly in the past may have just decided to reincarnate themselves as new providers, meaning that poor previous performance cannot be identified.

## Post-hoc analyses

The results in Table 3 beg the question of whether pricing and rating are very important determinants of which bid is chosen at all. To test this we replicated the analyses in Table 3 with the entire data set, Table 5, that is, without limiting the analyses only to the segment of the data with tenders where at least one bid came from a provider with previous contracts with the buyer. As Table 5 Column 1 shows, when analyzing all the data, and not only the segment of interest to the hypotheses, pricing and rating are significant as are the number of previous contracts, although these independent variables mainly classified which bids were not chosen. The linear regression, Table 5 Column 4, supports this too. The VIF values indicate no significant multicollinearity. The analysis was then repeated applying down-sampling to force an equivalent number of cases in each category. Some references on logistic regression suggest it is better to do so (King & Zeng, 2001; Liu et $^ { a l , }$ 2006; Ruiz-Gazen & Villa, 2007; Strobl, 2008; Emam et al, 2010). The down-sampling was done by choosing all the tenders that had at least one provider with previous bids with this buyer and choosing randomly from among the tenders with bids from only providers who had no previous tenders with this buyer. This was done so there would be approximately the same number of tenders in each of these two categories. Apart from a higher Nagelkerke $R ^ { 2 } ,$ , the results showed that all the independent variables except the Number of Previous Contracts were insignificant in predicting which bid would be chosen.

OSM are different. A key feature of an OSM is the ease and speed and lack of market supervision with which buyers and providers can disappear and reappear under new names when they want to reinvent themselves to hide their history. In contrast to traditional markets, in an OSM there are no authentication, registration fees, required continuity of legal obligations, or licensing and certification required to reincarnate oneself. This lack of market supervision could, extrapolating from Fukuyama (1995) on how the lack of a strong legal supervision tradition in other markets, make also OSM buyers reluctant to contract with strangers. This reluctance should result in an extreme preference not to do business with providers the buyer does not already know. This reluctance is over and above the TCE argument about asset specificity, although it does somewhat resemble the TCE argument about uncertainty also playing a key role in contract governance. Viewed this way, the study of OSM, as an exemplar of low trust culture markets, provides insight to a case where the TCE assumed high trust culture does not hold. In such cases, buyers behave as if controlling uncertainty is their primary concern.

With the caveat that this OSM deals with small contracts, and that therefore drawing conclusions to other larger markets does require additional research, the results could have some additional potentially interesting implications. Markets are about monetary exchange, and so relative pricing and premiums should be a dominant consideration. That is a key aspect of contract theory (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005) and has been shown to apply to other contexts where the importance of previous contracting was not examined (Dans, 2002). That pricing is less important in an OSM may in theory be because of high adverse selection risk in choosing which OSM provider to contract with (Snir & Hitt, 2003), and, presumably, knowing the bidders should reduce this adverse selection risk (Gefen et al, 2008). Moreover, with low cost projects as in this OSM it may not be worth the transaction costs of searching for another trustworthy provider once one has been identified. To date, these claims about adverse selection lacked empirical evidence. The data in this study are, to the best of our knowledge, the first to empirically support this adverse selection argument and show its actual strength. If there is a perception of adverse selection risk among the buyers then the buyers should back out of tenders more often when they do not know any of the bidders. The data not only show this excepted behavior, but also show that this is perhaps the most critical issue.

As the qualitative analysis of their comments indirectly shows, buyers cared more about deliverable quality than about cost saving. Their behavior also revealed a reluctance to switch providers. This revealed buyer behavior would seem to challenge some of the basic assumptions about the importance of price premiums (Bolton & Dewatripomt, 2005) and ratings (Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006). Adding the theoretical aspect of low trust cultures could be the basis for expanding those views by suggesting another distinct way of thought among buyers in the market, one that is not based on relative costs and risks but on sticking to a satisfying, even if not optimal, choice.

The dichotomous results concerning the importance of having had previous contracts with the buyer may indirectly support but also qualify the proposition made by Gefen & Carmel (2008) that having had previous contracts creates trust also in OSM settings. It should be added here that although their claim was grounded in theory, there was nothing in their data to support their proposition. Relying on provider ratings, data they did not have, may support and qualify this trust proposition. In Table 3, when the provider is somewhat known to the buyer through previous contracts, provider ratings, arguably indicting some aspects of provider trust worthiness, are immaterial. In contrast, in Table 5 where in most of the tenders there are no such providers, these ratings are significant. This may suggest that external indicators of provider trustworthiness are important, but only earlier on in the joint contracting history of a particular pair of buyer and provider when the provider is not yet known to the buyer. Once the buyer knows the provider there is no more need for such external indictors. Likewise, although Tables 3 and 5 indicate that being abroad is a disadvantage for a provider, Table 4 shows if the provider had previous contracts with the buyer being abroad was immaterial. These results could be interpreted as showing that such external indicators are important but only until the first contract is signed. Direct experience is what counts.

## Implications for practice

A key challenge for OSM managers is that buyers often post tenders and then withdraw them. Showing how serious this challenge is, in the data studied 43.27% of the tenders were not awarded to any bidder. This is a serious problem considering many OSM make their money by charging a percentage on paid tenders. One way of addressing this problem may be to rethink how the OSM are managed in order to create a high trust culture. The current way OSM are managed seems to create an apparent low trust culture with its typical high backing out percentage. It may be necessary to rethink how to encourage a high trust culture instead, for example by making it costly for providers to enter or reenter the market.

The results also seem to confirm, at least with these data, the long-known dictum: existing customers are the best customers. As Table 3 Column 3 shows, having had previous contracts is really all it took to correctly classify all the chosen bids, although some bids were classified as chosen when they were not because of double bidding by the same provider. Adding pricing and ratings, Column 1, added nothing to the classification. This supports the long-held practice of providers to invest in the early stages of the relationship, perhaps even at a loss, because it creates subsequent opportunities. This is, after all, an effective defensive marketing strategy in traditional markets (Roberts, 2005). In fact, Roberts (2005) asserts that an incumbent company defending its position may be better advised not to compete by lowering its prices, but rather should play on customers’ perception that switching may entail risk. Indeed, eBay product buyers are willing to pay more to sellers they trust (Ba & Pavlou, 2002), and do so at least partly because this reduces their risk perception (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). This strategy may be even more effective in an OSM. After all, the data show that if the provider is the only one who has previous contracts with the buyer then this provider will be given the contract regardless of pricing and ratings.

The results also suggest that the ratings obsession in various marketplaces should be brought into context. What really counts is having had previous contracts with the buyer that singularly determines which bid will be chosen, Table 3 Column 3. To the extent that these results can be generalized to other settings, this puts previous research and current practice on ratings in a new and more humble perspective. Ratings and price are important, but, as Table 5 implied, only initially until the buyer identifies trustworthy providers.

## Provider behavior

Interestingly, providers with previous contracts with the buyer bid lower and also had better and more numerous previous ratings. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, it is not primarily because of better pricing or ratings that these providers are awarded the tenders. But it does beg the question why should these providers not charge more than market price. There are several explanations why this may be so. First, the providers may not know about this market behavior. After all, they do not have the transaction data we have. Not knowing this, the provider does have an incentive to bid lower. Second, the same logic of a low trust culture may apply to the providers too. Not all buyers are good customers. And so, it would be less risky for a provider to bid on tender if this tender was made by a buyer the provider already knows. Less risk means the provider can afford to bid lower, hoping to increase its chances of being given the tender, especially given the previous point that the provider does not know, and cannot know based on the information it has, of this market behavior. Alternatively, the bidding provider cannot be certain that there will not be bids from other providers who have previous contracts with this buyer. Being uncertain, it would be counterproductive to charge a premium even by experienced providers who may realize that buyers prefer providers they had contracted with before.

## Limitations and future research

These are archival data. Although there are many benefits to such data, including the lack of common method and post-hoc recollection biases, it also means the model is constrained to only those constructs that are already available in the data or can be derived from it. Additional constructs of interest, such as measures of uncertainty, trust, and perceived risk, cannot be analyzed with these data. One is forced to rely on theory in making the argument about these data. There is also the ever present issue of generalization and external validity. The data truly represent what happened at this particular OSM since these represent the entire population of transactions, but the findings cannot be unconditionally generalized to other contexts. This is especially an issue when it comes to applying the results to other contexts of outsourcing because the context here is different from typical outsourcing contracts such as those described by previous research (Sabherwal, 1999; Bahli & Rivard, 2003; Ethiraj et al, 2005; Goo et al, 2007; Dibbern et al, 2008). Moreover, as the median price is low, there may be problems in generalizing the results to other more expensive markets. Thus, the results should be treated as relating to the specific case where entry costs are nonexistent, tender costs are low, and transaction costs in identifying trustworthy providers are high. Applying the results to other contexts requires additional research.

Another limitation, and possibility for future research, relates to the conceptual level. Loyalty as studied in marketing deals with both attitude and behavior. Considering we analyzed archival data with revealed behavior only, additional research is needed to extend the conclusions to the attitudinal aspects of loyalty. Another avenue future research could take is to examine whether buyers’ risk attitude plays a moderating role on their behavior. In other marketplaces, buyers’ risk attitude moderates the effect of rating reviews volume and valiance, as well as of product price, on their willingness to pay (Wu et al, 2012). Conceivably, the same might apply in OSM too.

## Conclusion

Pricing is a central consideration in awarding contracts in TCE and in contract theory. These theories implicitly assume there is a supportive legal system in the background that corresponds to what Fukuyama calls a high trust culture. OSM are different. OSM create what parallels a low trust culture. Studying this OSM low trust culture reveals an absolute preference for providers who had successful previous contracts with the buyer, trumping the cards and determining thereafter which bids are chosen regardless of pricing and the ratings. This low trust culture perspective adds a new theoretical perspective to the study of electronic markets.
