# REVEALING OR NON-REVEALING: THE IMPACT OF REVIEW DISCLOSURE POLICY ON FIRM PROFITABILITY $^{1}$

Xinxin Li
Department of Operations and Information Management, School of Business, University of Connecticut,
2100 Hillside Road, Storrs, CT U.S.A. {xinxin.li@uconn.edu}

Collecting and displaying product reviews written by consumers is a common practice for many retail websites. These websites, however, differ in their choice to reveal aggregate review statistics on the product list displayed while consumers browse or search to make initial product selections. This study proposes an analytical model to examine the conditions in which revealing average star ratings on the product list is more profitable than not revealing this information. Noting that consumers differ in their valuations of products sold on a firm's website, this study finds that if a firm sets prices to maximize total profits, it suffers less profitability from disclosing average star ratings if two products do not differ significantly in their average value and consumers' valuation of the low-value product is more heterogeneous. If products sold on the website instead are priced by third-party sellers that seek to maximize each product's own profit, it is less profitable for the firm to reveal average rating information when the two products do not differ significantly in average value and consumers' valuation of the high-value product is more heterogeneous. These results suggest guidelines that retail websites can use to evaluate their information revelation policies, based on three important factors: the difference in the average value of different products, relative heterogeneity in consumers' valuation of different products, and the firm's ability to coordinate prices across products.

Keywords: Online product reviews, analytical model, information revelation policy

## Introduction

Consumer-generated product reviews are a standard feature of many retail websites, detailing the experiences and opinions of consumers who have purchased a product and thus help prospective buyers gauge the value of those products for their own purchase decisions. Companies also can collect and aggregate consumers' numerical ratings to create an average star rating, which offers a convenient proxy of consumers' average assessments of the value of the product. Although collecting and displaying these product reviews is a common practice, retail websites differ in how they reveal the review information at different stages of consumers' shopping experiences. Some websites show the average star rating next to the name or picture of each product in a product list, displayed while consumers explore the product menu or search for certain products. Figure 1 shows an example, using the list of toys that result from a search for “preschool toys” on Walmart.com. This list offers review highlights for each product, including the average star rating and number of reviews. But other websites withhold these average ratings until consumers access a specific product page, which also features all the available product reviews, as depicted in Figure 2 for a list of dresses on Gap.com. Only the prices appear in the product menu, without any review information, but if we were to click on a product to view its page, we could read all the individual reviews posted, as shown in the callouts. Which of these two information revelation policies for displaying product reviews is more profitable?

Typically, the initial stage involves viewing webpages that display many products per page, then the choice stage entails viewing individual product pages. When consumers confront a list of products displayed on a retail website, they pick a subset for their next step, closer examination, which maximizes the likelihood of finding a product that ultimately offers them the highest net utility. This decision depends on the information presented at the time of the selection. Limited screen space means that a site cannot present all of the individual reviews on the product list, but it might reveal average star ratings. Unlike individual reviews, average star ratings do not allow readers to gauge the reasons for the reviewers' preferences, such that they could get an accurate estimate of their own valuations, but these averages still affect consumers' expectations of the average value of the products. Therefore, the question of whether to reveal average star ratings on the product list can be critical, in that it may cause some products to appear more attractive than others and give them a greater chance of entering consumers' consideration set and eventually being purchased.

To address this critical question, we develop an analytical model and examine the conditions in which it is more profitable for a firm to reveal average star ratings on the displayed product list, before consumers enter individual product pages to view the detailed product reviews and make purchase decisions. In our main model, a firm sells two experience products to a market of consumers with heterogeneous preferences. It sets the prices for both products to maximize its total profit. The reviews, by previous buyers of each product, are posted on the respective product's own page, and reading the detailed reviews would enable prospective buyers to estimate their own valuation of the product before purchasing. In all cases, consumers encounter both products in a product list that results from browsing or a search, along with their prices. If the firm reveals average ratings on its product list, consumers also learn the average rating for each product, then decide which page to visit first. We also extend this model to an alternative situation in which two separate third-party sellers price and sell the two available products, aiming to maximize their own profit, and the firm simply provides its website as a platform and earns a commission on any completed sales.

If the firm owns both products and sets their prices, it is less profitable to provide average star ratings on the product list, as long as the two products do not differ significantly in their average value and consumers' valuation for the lower value product is more heterogeneous. However, the result differs if the products are priced by separate third-party sellers. If each product is priced to maximize its own profit, it is less profitable for the firm to reveal average star ratings on the product list, as long as the two products do not differ significantly in average value and consumers' valuation for the higher value product is more heterogeneous. These results highlight three important factors to determine whether it is profitable to reveal aggregate review information early in consumers' shopping processes: the difference in the average value of the products, relative heterogeneity in consumers' valuations of the products, and the firm's ability to coordinate prices across products to maximize its total profit. In practice, firms already follow different review disclosure policies; our model provides clear guidelines they can use to evaluate the profitability of their policy.

In turn, this study extends research into online product reviews, which thus far has been primarily empirical. Prior investigations examine the relationship between online product reviews and product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Dellarocas et al. 2007; Duan et al. 2008; Godes and Mayzlin 2004; Liu 2006), investigate moderators of this relationship (Forman et al. 2008; Lu et al. 2013; Zhu and Zhang 2010), note biases in reviews (Hu et al. 2009; Li and Hitt 2008, 2010), and identify factors that might influence review posting behavior (Goes et al. 2014; Hu and Li 2011; Moe and Schweidel 2012; Moe and Trusov 2011). Across various product categories (e.g., books, movies, beers, restaurants, video games, digital cameras, bath and beauty products), extant literature offers various metrics and empirical support for the relationship between online product reviews and consumers' purchasing behavior. Our analytical model contributes by adding to the relatively sparse analytical work in this research stream. That is, existing analytical studies address how firms use online product reviews and their effects on firms' profits in different market conditions. For example, Dellarocas (2006) analyzes sellers' incentives to boost the ratings of their products artificially, arguing that even manipulated reviews can be informative. According to Li et al. (2011), information provided by online product reviews influences firms' pricing strategies for products purchased repeatedly. Kwark et al. (2017) study how firms can use the upstream pricing scheme as a strategic tool to benefit from online product reviews. Yet none of these studies consider the firms' actual review disclosure policy. Rather, Chen and Xie (2008) examine an online seller's strategic decision to supply consumer-created (i.e., product reviews) versus seller-created product information. Their focus is on the decision to offer a product review feature; we instead examine whether it is more profitable for retail websites that offer product reviews to disclose the aggregate review statistics on the product list, before consumers visit individual product pages to access more detailed review information.

Sun (2012) has highlighted how ratings variance can influence subsequent demand, beyond the effect of the mean rating value, such that when the mean is low, greater variance in the ratings has a positive impact on sales. We build on this evidence about the importance of variance, but unlike Sun's study, we explicitly examine review disclosure policies and the role of different forms of review information (aggregate statistics versus individual reviews) in consumers' shopping processes. Furthermore, Sun focuses on a monopoly setting, without product competition.

Finally, our study relates to general information literature, beyond the product review context (e.g., Gu and Xie 2013; Guo and Zhao 2009; Kuksov and Lin 2010; Mayzlin and Shin 2011), to which it offers two main contributions. First, rather than a simple choice (revealing versus not), we investigate the practice of disclosing partial information and its effects on how full information gets revealed later. Second, in our model, the information revelation policy applies to all products sold on the website, rather than limiting each seller to a single product, for which it decides independently whether to reveal information.

## Main Model

## Model Setup

Assume that a firm sells two experience products on its website, Product 1 and Product 2, and their prices are $p_1$ and $p_2$ , respectively. The value of each product varies for each consumer. For example, a book may be loved by some readers but hated by others for diverse reasons, such as its subject matter, writing style, genre, plot, or character development; a digital camera similarly may satisfy the needs of some consumers but not others, depending on their skill levels and picture-taking needs. We use $v_{ij}$ to represent the value of Product $j$ ( $j = 1, 2$ ) to a consumer of type $i$ . The utility of Product $j$ to a consumer of type $i$ is $v_{ij} - p_j$ . $v_{ij}$ follows a uniform distribution $[\overline{v}_j - a_j, \overline{v}_j + a_j]$ where $\overline{v}_j$ represents the average value of Product $j$ , and $a_j$ captures the variation in the value of Product $j$ for different consumers. The firm knows the realizations of $\overline{v}_j$ and $a_j$ . Consumers know the joint probability density distribution of $\overline{v}_j$ and $a_j$ , denoted as $f(\overline{v}_j, a_j)$ with means of $E[\overline{v}_j] = v^e$ and $E[a_j] = a^e$ , as well as the distribution of $v_{ij}$ given $\overline{v}_j$ and $a_j$ (i.e., $[\overline{v}_j - a_j, \overline{v}_j + a_j]$ ), but they do not know the realizations of $v_{ij}, \overline{v}_j$ , or $a_j$ for a specific product before their purchase, unless they can access existing consumers' reviews for the product. This setup is similar to the one used by Sun (2012), who models the variation in the value of a product using heterogeneous misfit costs.

Product reviews can help consumers estimate their valuations of the products before purchase. Individual product reviews reveal both product valuations and the preferences of the consumers who provide the valuations, so potential buyers can adjust the differences in reviewers' preferences to estimate their own $v_{ij}$ . In contrast with individual product reviews, average star ratings do not reveal the reasons for reviewers' preferences, but they signal the average value of the products (i.e., realization of $\overline{v_{j}}$ ). Therefore, we assume

1. Before accessing any product review information, a consumer of type $i$ expects Product $j$ 's value to be $E[v_{ij}] = E[\overline{v_j}] = v^e$ .

2. After viewing average star ratings, consumers observe the realization of $\overline{v}_{j}$ , so a consumer of type i expects Product j's value to be $E[v_{ij}] |AR = \overline{v}_{j}$ , where |AR indicates that the expected value is updated on the basis of the Average Rating.

3. After viewing individual reviews posted on Product j's page, a consumer of type observes how consumers with different preferences value the product, according to which she estimates her own valuation of the product: $E[v_{ij}]\|IR = v_{ij}]$ , where $|IR|$ indicates that the expected value is updated on the basis of Individual Reviews. $^{2}$

Consumers finally make purchase decisions, depending on their expectations of the utility of a product, which equals the expected value of the product minus price. Each consumer buys at most one unit of one product; if a consumer does not buy any product, her utility is 0.

Consumers may discover products in two different ways. One group of consumers is only interested in a particular product, say Product s; they visit its product page directly, read the individual reviews, and then decide whether to purchase Product s, according to its expected value $E[v_{is}]|IR = v_{is}$ and price $p_{s}$ . These “concentrated consumers” are not aware of or interested in other options offered on the website; they purchase Product s if $v_{is} - p_{s} \geq 0$ . But another group of consumers do not have a specific, preselected product in mind and thus peruse the product menu or search the website to explore different options before selecting and viewing a particular page in detail. These “explorative consumers” see the products in the product menu or search result and the related prices; they also might encounter average ratings posted on the product list. In this case, they update their beliefs about the expected value of Product j to $E[v_{ij}] | AR = \overline{v}_{j}$ . If the average ratings are not posted, their expected value of Product j is $E[v_{ij}] = v^{e}$ . They choose the product that has the highest expected net utility and visit its product page. If two products have equal expected net utility, they first visit the product that appears on the top of the product list they obtained by browsing or searching. $^{3}$ On the product page, they can read individual product reviews and estimate their own value for the product they visit first, say Product f. They will purchase Product f if $E[v_{if}] | IR - p_{f} \geq 0$ , that is, if $v_{if} - p_{f} \geq 0$ . If $v_{if} - p_{f} < 0$ , a fraction 1 - k ( $k \in (0, 1]$ ) of these consumers visits the other product page, $^{4}$ say Product l, and purchase Product l if $E[v_{il}]\mid IR-p_{l}\geq0$ , that is, $v_{il}-p_{l}\geq0$ .

We compare the firm's profits under two possible review disclosure policies. With a revealing policy, the firm reveals the average star ratings for products on the product list, whereas with a non-revealing policy, it does not reveal average star ratings on the product list. The adoption of either policy only affects explorative consumers, because concentrated consumers only focus on the product in which they are interested, such that they are not affected by information displayed on the product list.

Without loss of generality, we can assume $\bar{v}_{1} > \bar{v}_{2}$ , such that Product 1 has a higher average value than Product 2. Product 1 is not necessarily the first product shown on the product list though; either product may appear at the top of the list. For ease of exposition, we present our comparison results using the rescaled parameters $\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ , $\frac{a_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ , and $\frac{a_{2}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ , which reflect the ratios of $\bar{v}_{1}$ , $a_{1}$ , and $a_{2}$ to $\bar{v}_{2}$ —equivalent to normalizing $\bar{v}_{2}$ to 1, but we retain $\bar{v}_{2}$ in the results for clarity.

To keep the model simple and tractable without affecting the key insights, we make several assumptions. First, we normalize the market size to 1 and the marginal cost of production to 0. Second, we assume that the market is equally split into concentrated and explorative consumers, and concentrated consumers are equally split into those interested in Product 1 and those interested in Product 2. Third, to allow for the possibility that some consumers receive negative value from using a product (e.g., a pair of shoes that fit poorly can hurt the wearer's feet), we assume that $\frac{a_j}{\bar{v}_j} > 1$ , so $\overline{v_j} - a_j < 0$ .

This assumption ensures that the market will never be fully covered, which simplifies the presentation of the results. A similar assumption appears in previous studies (e.g., Sun

2012). Fourth, to focus on the interesting scenario in which neither product is completely dominated by the other, and both products earn sales on the website, we assume that $\frac{a_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}} < 2$ and $\frac{a_{2}}{\overline{v}_{2}} < 2$ , which rules out the corner solution in which a firm chooses to sell only one product on its website. Fifth, in the main model, we present the results for the middle values of k and $\frac{a_{2}}{\overline{v}_{2}}$ (namely, $k = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{a_{2}}{\overline{v}_{2}} = \frac{3}{2}$ ) but our sensitivity analysis suggests that the results are robust to all values of $k \in (0,1]$ and $\frac{a_{2}}{\overline{v}_{2}} \in (1,2)$ . Details about the sensitivity analysis are in Appendix D.

## Comparison between Revealing Policy and Non-Revealing Policy

## Revealing Policy

Under a revealing policy, explorative consumers observe the average ratings of both Product 1 and Product 2 on the product list. They set their expected value of Product j to be $E[v_{ij}]\left|AR=\overline{v}_{j}\right.$ . They choose to visit the page of the product with a higher expected net utility $\overline{v}_{j}-p_{j}$ or the one on the top of the product list if the expected utilities of the two products are equal. Once they visit the product page, they can read detailed consumer reviews and estimate their own value for the visited product, say, Product f. They purchase Product f if $E[v_{if}]\left|IR-p_{f}=v_{if}-p_{f}\geq0\right.$ . If $v_{if}-p_{f}<0$ , half of the explorative consumers will visit the page of the other product, say, Product l, and purchase it if $E[v_{il}]\left|IR-p_{l}=v_{il}-p_{l}\geq0\right.$ . Concentrated consumers instead visit their product of interest and estimate its value by reading consumer reviews. They purchase the focal product, say Product s, if $E[v_{is}]\left|IR-p_{s}=v_{is}-p_{s}\geq0\right.$ . We thus derive the following profit function for the firm under a revealing policy:

$$
\pi^ {1} \left(p _ {1}, p _ {2}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \left(\frac {3}{4} p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}} + \frac {1}{4} p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}} \right. & i f \bar {v} _ {1} - p _ {1} > \bar {v} _ {2} - p _ {2} o r i f \\ \left. + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}}\right)\right) p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}}\right) & \bar {v} _ {1} - p _ {1} = \bar {v} _ {2} - p _ {2} a n d \\ & \text { Product   1   is   on   the   top } \\ & \text { of   the   list } \\ \left(\frac {1}{4} p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}} + \frac {3}{4} p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}} \right. & i f \bar {v} _ {1} - p _ {1} <   \bar {v} _ {2} - p _ {2} o r i f \\ \left. + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}}\right)\right) p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}}\right) & \bar {v} _ {1} - p _ {1} = \bar {v} _ {2} - p _ {2} a n d \\ & \text { Product   2   is   on   the   top } \\ & \text { of   the   list } \end{array} \right.
$$

The firm selects $p_{1}$ and $p_{2}$ to maximize its profit. The browsing behavior of explorative consumers right after viewing the product list when the firm conducts optimal pricing is described in Lemma 1; the derivation of the optimal prices and profit is in Appendix A.

Lemma 1. If a firm reveals average star ratings on the product list, the following behaviors occur, assuming the firm's optimal pricing:

(1.1) If $a_{1} < a_{2}$ and $\frac{v_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}} < \overline{Q}_{1}$ , the firm distorts Product 2's price from its monopoly level to induce explorative consumers to visit Product 2's page first. $^{6}$

(1.2) In all other cases, explorative consumers first visit Product 1, but if $\frac{\bar{v}_1}{\bar{v}_2} < \frac{\frac{24a_1}{\bar{v}_2} - \frac{23a_2}{\bar{v}_2} + 26 + 1 / \frac{a_2}{\bar{v}_2}}{24}$ , the firm needs to lower Product 1's price to maintain its lead in attracting initial visits.

Lemma 1 suggests that under a revealing policy, explorative consumers usually visit the product with a higher average value $\bar{v}_j$ (i.e., Product 1) first, unless the high-value product has a relatively small advantage in average value $\left(\frac{\bar{v}_1}{\bar{v}_2} < \overline{Q}_1\right)$ and is less heterogeneous in consumer valuation ( $a_1 < a_2$ ). In this case, the firm finds it profitable to induce explorative consumers to visit the low-value product (Product 2) first, by decreasing Product 2's price from its monopoly level and simultaneously increasing Product 1's price. The intuition is as follows: Because Product 1 has a higher value on average, selling Product 1 is typically more profitable for the firm. Therefore, the firm generally has an incentive to encourage explorative consumers to visit it first. However, if this high-value product has a relatively small advantage in average value $\left(\frac{\bar{v}_1}{\bar{v}_2} < \overline{Q}_1\right)$ and is also less heterogeneous ( $a_1 < a_2$ ), Product 1 can be less profitable than Product 2. In that case, the firm finds it profitable to induce explorative consumers to visit Product 2 first, by making its price offer more appealing.

## Non-Revealing Policy

Under a non-revealing policy, explorative consumers do not observe average star ratings for either product when they see the product list. Their expectation of the value of Product j is therefore $E[v_{ij}] = v^{e}$ . They first visit the page of the product with higher expected utility $v^{e} - p_{j}$ , with its lower $p_{j}$ , or else the page of the product shown on the top of the product list if the expected utilities of the two products are equal. $^{7}$ Once they visit the page, they read the detailed consumer reviews and assess their own value of the visited product, say, Product f. They purchase Product f if $E[v_{ij}]|IR - p_{f} = v_{if} - p_{f} \geq 0$ . If $v_{if} - p_{f} < 0$ , half of these explorative consumers visit the product page of the other product, say Product l, and purchase it if $E[v_{il}]|IR - p_{l} = v_{il} - p_{l} \geq 0$ . Concentrated consumers again will visit their preferred product and estimate their value for it by reading consumer reviews, then purchase if, for this Product s, $E[v_{is}]|IR - p_{s} = v_{is} - p_{s} \geq 0$ . We derive the profit function of the firm with this non-revealing policy as follows:

$$
\pi^ {2} \left(p _ {1}, p _ {2}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \left(\frac {3}{4} p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}} + \frac {1}{4} p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}} \right. & \text { if } p _ {1} <   p _ {2} \text { or   if } p _ {1} = p _ {2} \\ \left. + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}}\right)\right) p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}}\right) & \text { and   Product   1   is   on   the } \\ & \text { top   of   the   list } \\ \left(\frac {1}{4} p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}} + \frac {3}{4} p _ {2} \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}} \right. & \text { if } p _ {1} > p _ {2} \text { or   if } p _ {1} = p _ {2} \\ \left. + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {\bar {v} _ {2} + a _ {2} - p _ {2}}{2 a _ {2}}\right)\right) p _ {1} \frac {\bar {v} _ {1} + a _ {1} - p _ {1}}{2 a _ {1}}\right) & \text { and   Product   2   is   on   the } \\ & \text { top   of   the   list } \end{array} \right.
$$

The firm selects $p_1$ and $p_2$ to maximize its profit. The browsing behavior of explorative consumers, right after viewing the product list under the firm's optimal pricing, is described in Lemma 2; the derivation of the optimal prices and profit is in Appendix B.

Lemma 2. If a firm does not reveal average star ratings on its product list, the following behaviors occur, assuming the firm's optimal pricing:

(2.1) If $a_1 < a_2$ and $\frac{\overline{v}_1}{\overline{v}_2} < \overline{Q}_2$ , the firm distorts Product 2's price from its monopoly level to induce explorative consumers to visit the product page of Product 2 first.

(2.2) In all other cases, explorative consumers first visit Product 1, but if $\frac{\overline{v}_1}{\overline{v}_2} > \frac{\frac{23a_2}{\overline{v}_2} - \frac{24a_1}{\overline{v}_2} + 22 - 1 / \frac{a_2}{\overline{v}_2}}{24}$ , the firm needs to lower Product 1's price to maintain its lead in attracting initial visits.

Lemma 2 suggests that under a non-revealing policy, similar to Lemma 1, explorative consumers usually visit Product 1 first, except when the product with a higher average value $\overline{v}_{j}$ (Product 1) has a relatively small advantage in average value $\left(\frac{\overline{v}_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}}<\overline{Q}_{2}\right)$ and is also less heterogeneous in consumer valuation $(a_{1}<a_{2})$ . In this case, the firm finds it profitable to induce explorative consumers to visit the low-value product (Product 2) first. The intuition is similar to that for Lemma 1, except that the region in which explorative consumers first visit the low-value product is larger for Lemma 2 than for Lemma 1 $(Q_{2}>Q_{1})$ . It is easier for the firm to induce explorative consumers to visit the low-value, more profitable product when the average ratings that favor the product with a higher average value are not revealed.

## Profit Comparison between Revealing and Non-Revealing Policies

A direct comparison of the optimal profits in Lemmas 1 and 2 suggests the following proposition; the derivation is in Appendix C.

Proposition 1. Revealing average star ratings on the product list is less profitable for the firm than not revealing the average star ratings if $\frac{\overline{v}_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}} < \overline{Q}$ and $a_{1} < a_{2}$ .

Sometimes, less information can be better than more information. Specifically, we find that disclosing average star ratings on the product list is usually better (or at least equally profitable) for the firm, except if the advantage of the high-value product in its average value is relatively small $\left(\frac{\overline{v}_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}}<\overline{Q}\right)$ and the low-value product is more heterogeneous in consumer valuation $(a_{1}<a_{2})$ . With these two conditions, it is less profitable for a firm to reveal average star ratings on the product list, even if it is free to do so.

If the advantage of the high-value product in terms of average value is not too small $\left(\frac{\overline{v}_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}}\geq\overline{Q}\right)$ , or the high-value product is (weakly) more heterogeneous in consumer valuation $(a_{1}\geq a_{2})$ , it is (weakly) more profitable for the firm to induce explorative consumers to visit the high-value product first, regardless of whether average star ratings are revealed. In this case, either selling the high-value product to explorative consumers yields (weakly) higher profit than selling the low-value product to these consumers, or else selling the low-value product to explorative consumers is more profitable but too costly for the firm to extort prices to induce them to visit the low-value product first. Because the high-value product is eventually the product that explorative consumers visit first, revealing average ratings helps; it directs traffic to the product with a higher average.

If the advantage of the high-value product is relatively small $\left(\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}<\overline{Q}\right)$ , and the low-value product is more heterogeneous in consumer valuation $(a_{1}<a_{2})$ , selling the high-value product to explorative consumers yields lower profit than selling the low-value product to them, because even though the average value of the low-value product is lower, a greater fraction of explorative consumers are willing to pay a high price for it, ultimately yielding higher profit for the firm. For example, in an extreme case, if $a_{2}$ is so big that $\bar{v}_{2}+a_{2}>\bar{v}_{1}+a_{1}$ , then a higher fraction of explorative consumers pay a price between $\bar{v}_{1}+a_{1}$ and $\bar{v}_{2}+a_{1}$ for Product 2 than for Product 1. Regardless of whether average ratings are revealed, the firm thus has an incentive to induce explorative consumers to visit the low-value product first. However, it is more costly (or even unprofitable) for the firm to do so if it provides review statistics on the product list, because the average ratings favor the product with a higher average (i.e., Product 1), likely directing explorative consumers to Product 1's page. By not revealing the average ratings, the firm can induce explorative consumers to visit Product 2 with less price distortion than if it had revealed the average ratings. It is more profitable for the firm not to reveal the average ratings in this case.

Our result thus provides guidelines for retail websites to evaluate the profitability of their review disclosure policy. Which policy is more profitable ultimately depends on its ability to direct traffic to the more profitable products sold on its website, and the profitability of a product is determined by both the average value and heterogeneity in consumers' valuations. In practice, these average values and the heterogeneity in consumers' valuations vary not only across products within a category but also across categories, and their relative importance differs across categories as well. In some categories, consumers' valuations of products are barely dispersed, and products mainly differ in their average values, but in others, consumers have very distinct opinions and widely dispersed valuations. We typically observe higher variance for products with a lower average value (Clemons et al. 2006; Sun 2012),

so we can use $\frac{a_{2}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ to proxy for the overall magnitude of heterogeneity in consumers' valuations in a product category and numerically derive the region in which a non-revealing policy is more profitable for the firm for all values of $\frac{a_{2}}{\bar{v}_{2}}\in(1,2)$ , as we show in Figure 3. The result is summarized in Corollary 1.

Corollary 1. The greater the heterogeneity in consumers' product valuations for the low-value products in a product category, the more likely a non-revealing policy is to be more profitable for the firm.

Corollary 1 suggests that withholding average star ratings from the product list is more profitable for product categories in which consumers differ significantly in their tastes and preferences. When consumers disagree significantly on product value, the variance in consumers' valuations is so large that the average value may be only a weak predictor of a product's overall profitability. Then products with a lower average value have a greater chance to be more profitable in these product categories, compared with in product categories in which consumers have similar preferences. A revealing policy that encourages consumers to visit the products with higher average value first thus is likely to be less profitable for firms in these categories.

## Model Extensions

In this section, we extend our model to examine the robustness of our result to alternative model assumptions. In our main model, we assume that the firm owns the two products sold on its website and is able to coordinate the prices of the two products to maximize its total profit. In practice though, some websites do not own the products but simply provide a platform for others to sell their products and earn a commission on sales transacted through the site. These platforms do not set prices; rather, the two products would be priced by two separate, third-party sellers, each of which attempts to maximize its own profit. The result is likely price undercutting that would not occur if the two prices were set by a single firm. Because the price determination directly affects the prices of the two products, which in turn directly and indirectly (through explorative consumers' browsing behavior) affect the platform firm's profit, in a first model extension, we examine when it is more profitable for a platform firm to disclose average star ratings on product lists. If the website reveals average ratings on the product list, we assume it does so for all products sold on its website. Both sellers know the realizations of $\bar{v}_{j}$ and $a_{j}$ . In this extension, most of our assumptions are the same as in the main model, with two exceptions. First, Product j's price $p_{j}$ is now set by Seller j, not by the website firm. The firm collects commissions from both sellers as a fixed $\beta$ portion of the total revenue of the two products. Second, in this extension, pure strategy equilibrium does not exist, and the mixed strategy equilibrium is intractable for k < 1, so we derive the equilibrium for the case of k = 1 with the consideration that the results of our main model are insensitive to the value of k. The result is summarized in Proposition 2, and the derivation is in Appendix E.

Proposition 2. If products are priced by two separate third-party sellers (in the case of k=1), it is less profitable for the website or platform firm to reveal average star ratings on the product list if $\frac{\overline{v}_{1}}{\overline{v}_{2}}<\overline{Q}_{E}$ and $a_{1}>a_{2}$ .

This prediction differs from the result in the main model, suggesting that the website firm's ability to coordinate prices (to avoid price undercutting and maximize total profits) plays an important role in determining whether disclosing or withholding average star ratings on the product list is more profitable. If products are priced by separate third-party sellers, it is less profitable for the firm to reveal average ratings on the product list if the high-value product has a relatively low advantage in average value ( $\frac{\overline{v}_1}{\overline{v}_2} < \overline{Q}_E$ , similar to the result in the main model) and is also more heterogeneous in consumer valuation ( $a_1 > a_2$ ), different from the result in the main model). Because the advantage of the high-value product in average value is relatively low ( $\frac{\overline{v}_1}{\overline{v}_2} < \overline{Q}_E$ ), the two sellers likely engage in a price war, and the firm's profit depends critically on how aggressively the two sellers undercut prices (whereas in our main model, the firm sets all its prices to maximize the total profit). Two competing forces are at work here. The seller of the low-value product is more aggressive if average ratings are revealed on the product list than if the average ratings are not revealed, because average ratings signal its value disadvantage. The opposite is true for the seller of the high-value product. When the high-value product is more heterogeneous in consumer valuation $a_{1} > a_{2}$ , the former force dominates, thus favoring the non-revealing policy.

We also investigate the robustness of our result to other alternative model assumptions. First, we add a positive marginal cost of production. Second, we vary the size of the group of concentrated consumers relative to explorative consumers. Third, we allow explorative consumers to exhibit different preferences for the two products, based on product attributes that can be observed before purchase. Fourth, we examine the situation in which the firm pools the prices of the two products under a non-revealing policy to eliminate possible signaling effects of differential pricing. Fifth, we adopt an alternative approach used in prior studies (e.g., Xu et al. 2011) to model the search behavior of explorative consumers. The results of these model extensions appear in Appendices F–J. Our main result is robust to these alternative model assumptions, as long as the size of the group of explorative consumers is not too small and the unobservable attributes have significantly more influence on consumers' purchase decisions than do attributes that can be observed before purchase.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Online product reviews are becoming an indispensable feature that consumers expect on every retail website. The reviews assist consumers in estimating the value of products with which they have no experience, such that they affect purchase decisions. In an early stage of their shopping process, consumers glance at different options on the product list that they have brought up by searching or browsing, then select those options that are likely to yield the highest utility to examine further. At this stage, a website can influence consumers' expectations of the different products that ultimately will match their preferences and thus influence initial product selections by disclosing or withholding average star ratings in the product reviews on the product list.

Our model in turn provides guidelines for assessing the profitability of different review disclosure policies and suggests three important factors to consider: the difference in the average value of different products, relative heterogeneity in consumer valuations of different products, and the firm's ability to coordinate prices between products sold on its website. If a firm is able to coordinate the prices of two products to maximize their total profit, the first two factors determine the profitability of each product, as well as the magnitude of price discount the firm may have to offer to direct early traffic to the low-value, more profitable product. In this case, it is less profitable to provide average ratings on the product list if the two products do not differ much in their average value and the consumers' valuation of the product with the low average value is more heterogeneous. But if the products are priced by separate third-party sellers seeking to maximize their own profit, the firm cannot coordinate prices for the products sold on its website, so the first two factors determine how aggressively each seller undercuts price in a price war. In this case, it is less profitable for the firm to reveal average ratings on the product list if the two products do not differ significantly in average value and consumers' valuation of the product with high average value is more heterogeneous.

Our results have direct implications for the review disclosure policies of different retail websites. Contingent on the characteristics of their products and profit model, disclosing review highlights on a product list that results from consumer browsing or searching can be more or less profitable than withholding this information. For a website that sells multiple categories of products, the net effect for the entire site needs to be considered, to determine which review disclosure policy is more profitable overall. Marketing research, such as product trials, consumer panels and surveys, and historical purchase data about similar products, can help estimate the average product value and heterogeneity in consumer valuation for different product categories.

Our results also have implications for the general practice of relying on information disclosures to influence consumers' purchase decisions. Although the Internet empowers consumers with an unprecedented amount of information to facilitate their decision making, consumers are limited by the amount of information they can process. Releasing or withholding highlights of information that they cannot process entirely is a tool that firms can leverage to steer consumers first toward options that are more beneficial for the firms. Retail websites may face extreme competitive pressures to collect and disseminate information to help consumers make informed decisions, but they have the freedom to choose how to organize and present this information. Our study provides an example of how firms might use different information revelation policies to increase their profitability, and from this perspective, it relates to research on opaque selling strategies (e.g., Fay and Xie 2008; Shapiro and Shi 2008). In an opaque selling situation, a consumer cannot observe the value of the product until the purchase is completed. Firms typically use opaque selling to price discriminate among consumers with different price sensitivities. Adding to this line of research, our study suggests an alternative way firms could use their information revelation policies to affect consumer purchase behavior. A key difference is that in our model, withholding or revealing review information only affects consumers' search behavior in an early stage of their shopping process; uncertainty about the product's value is fully resolved before the purchase. Therefore, information revelation policies do not act as a price discrimination strategy here but instead as an invitation to consumers to discover products that might appeal only to a small niche market and that might be overlooked.

For consumers, the displays of average ratings in product lists also might not be beneficial in all cases. First, consumers may pay higher prices when they receive more information about product value, as a result of firms' strategic pricing. Second, to the extent that average ratings cannot represent all the heterogeneity in consumer valuation, observing this information in the initial stage of product search may mislead some consumers into examining and possibly buying a less valuable product. A complete examination of consumer surplus requires a thorough analysis of consumers' search behavior, which is beyond the scope of this study and instead should be addressed with further research.

Finally, several limitations of our study suggest further research options. First, our model includes two products and their prices on the retail website. We did not examine the extended cases of more products or upstream competition in the supply chain. Future research should check the robustness of our results in these settings. Second, our model focuses on one type of information, online product reviews, but in practice, firms can display other types of information on the product list, such as product pictures at different angles or selective product features. Our model applies to products for which unobservable attributes are more important than those that can be observed before purchase; we hope further research investigates information revelation policies for other types of products, especially if the firm can select multiple types of information to disclose. Third, in considering the effects of revealing versus withholding review statistics in a product list, we assumed the posted reviews were informative. The means for collecting reviews and the characteristics of the consumers who post them may affect this informativeness, a question that can be investigated in future research. Fourth, we could not derive closed form solutions for all the results and obtained some of them numerically. This limitation may be addressed by future research as well.

The profit of the firm that owns the website under the revealing policy is $\pi^1 = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}\Bigl (\pi_{S1}^1 (p_1) + \pi_{S2}^1 (p_2)\Bigr)$ .

Seller 1 selects $p_{1}$ , and Seller 2 selects $p_{2}$ simultaneously to maximize their own profits. The first-order condition and the negative second-order derivatives suggest that the profit-maximizing prices are $p_{1} = \frac{\bar{v}_{1} + a_{1}}{2}$ and $p_{2} = \frac{\bar{v}_{2} + a_{2}}{2}$ . Note that $\bar{v}_{1} - p_{1} = \bar{v}_{2} - p_{2}$ can never be the equilibrium, because the seller of the product not shown on the top of the list could always lower its price by a very small amount ( $\varepsilon$ ) and increase its profit. We thus derive the equilibrium by analyzing two cases.

Case E1. If the profit-maximizing prices for (E1) and (E3) fall in the region of $\bar{v}_{1}-p_{1}>\bar{v}_{2}-p_{2}$ , that is, if $\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}>1+\frac{a_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}-\frac{a_{2}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ , Seller 2 will compare $\pi_{S2}^{1}\left(\frac{\bar{v}_{2}+a_{2}}{2}\right)_{(E3)}$ and $\pi_{S2}^{1}\left(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}+\frac{\bar{v}_{1}+a_{1}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)_{(E4)}$ to decide if it is profitable to deviate by undercutting Seller 1 to induce explorative consumers to buy Product 2. Given $1+\frac{a_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}-\frac{a_{2}}{\bar{v}_{2}}<\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}<\frac{a_{1}}{\bar{v}_{2}}$ , $\pi_{S2}^{1}\left(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}+\frac{\bar{v}_{1}+a_{1}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)_{(E4)}$ is always larger. Therefore, Seller 2 will undercut Seller 1 to attract consumers to visit Product 2, and Seller 1 will respond by lowering Product 1's price to prevent it from happening. The resulting mixed strategy equilibrium can be derived as follows. Let $pn_{1}$ be the lowest price of Seller 1 that Seller 2 is willing to undercut. Depending on whether there is probability mass at $\frac{\bar{v}_{2}+a_{2}}{2}$ in Seller 2's price support, there are two possible equilibriums:
