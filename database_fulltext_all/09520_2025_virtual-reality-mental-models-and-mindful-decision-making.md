## Abstract

This research investigates virtual reality presentation (VR), revealing its impact on users’ mental models and mindful decision-making. We identify two fundamental features of VR: interactive viewing and depth cues. Through the lens of mental model theory, we explore how these two features shape users’ mental models and subsequently influence their mindfulness in decision-making. Two lab experiments were conducted to examine our research model. Study 1 examines interactive viewing and depth cues separately to assess their distinct impacts on the key components of users’ mental models—perceived control and acquired information. Study 2 employs an integrated VR presentation technology (i.e., Meta Quest 2) to explore the overall effect of VR presentations on users’ mental models and mindful decision-making. Results largely support our hypotheses. This research highlights the unique effects of VR presentation and its features, emphasizing enhanced user control and information acquisition in fostering high-quality mental models that increase mindfulness in users’ decisions in the online environment. These findings have significant implications for both research and practice of VR presentations.

Keywords: Virtual Reality, Interactive Viewing, Depth Cues, Mental Model, Perceived Control, Mindfulness, Mindful Decision-Making

## 1 Introduction

The concept of virtual reality (VR), as an approach to immerse users in a 3D virtual world (Fishkin et al., 2000; Walsh & Pawlowski, 2002), emerged in the early 1990s (Delaney 2016). Nonetheless, VR has become costeffective and spread widely in different domains only recently, with the unprecedented advances in computer hardware, software, and infrastructure. Digital devices such as Microsoft HoloLens, Meta Quest (formerly Oculus Quest), and Apple Vision Pro are becoming popular and competing for a potentially huge market of virtual reality. According to an analyst report, the market size of virtual reality headsets worldwide was 13.52 billion

USD in 2024 and is expected to grow 30.8% from 2025 to 2034 (Precedence Research, 2025). To embrace the fast expansion of the online virtual world, Facebook rebranded itself as Meta to capitalize on the idea of the “metaverse,” which was believed to be the future version of the internet (Bobrowsky & Needleman, 2022). Hence, it is safe to predict that we will see much more online content presented using VR technologies in the years to come.

Despite the compelling potential of VR presentation in the online context, research on its presentation mechanisms and how they influence user behaviors is still nascent. VR can be thought of as a user interface to enmesh users within a 3D world (Fishkin et al., 2000; Walsh & Pawlowski, 2002). Due to technological limitations, prior studies have primarily focused on 3D experience on 2D presentation devices (e.g., computer monitors), whose effects rely heavily on users’ ability to build a 3D mental model based on the presented content in 2D (Klein, 2003; Nah et al., 2011). Only recently, with the help of head-mounted devices, have researchers begun to examine direct VR presentation (i.e., without processing the 2D pictures to build 3D mental models) and its effects on user behaviors (Breves & Greussing, 2021; Kim & Ko, 2019).

In this paper, we extend this line of research by offering a systematic and in-depth study of VR presentation and its effect on users’ mindful decision-making. Specifically, in the online environment, this research aims to bridge two theoretical gaps. First, it attempts to enhance our understanding of VR presentation and its features. This research identifies and summarizes two fundamental features of VR presentation—interactive viewing and depth cues—and investigates their working mechanisms. Specifically, interactive viewing enables users to move objects in the virtual space, such as by changing the position and angle (Suh & Lee, 2005), while depth cues provide an immersive environment (Lee et al., 1986). Given the distinct mechanisms of these two VR presentation features, we aim to study their impacts respectively and jointly.

Second, this research studies how VR presentation affects users’ mental models and subsequent mindful decisionmaking, a crucial topic in the online environment (Thatcher et al., 2018; Vitharana et al., 2016). A mental model is an internal representation (i.e., in users’ minds) of an external reality, such as a target system or a product in an online shop (Norman, 1983; Staggers & Norcio, 1993). According to mental model theory (Johnson-Laird et al., 1999; Norman, 1983), users’ mental models are affected by how the external realities are presented (e.g., VR presentation) (Staggers & Norcio, 1993), and mental models shape users’ behaviors and task performance, such as mindful decision-making (Balijepally et al., 2015). Unlike heuristic, automatic, or impulsive decisionmaking, mindful decision-making refers to a person being more open to new information and paying greater attention to details and comparisons during the decision process (Langer, 1989). Research suggests that mindful decision-making leads to better outcomes, such as higher user satisfaction and more innovative use of technology (Thatcher et al., 2018). This is particularly important in online contexts, where users often act as “cognitive misers,” relying on heuristics or social norms to inform their decisions (Duan et al., 2009; Liu & Goodhue, 2012). While these strategies may save cognitive effort, they frequently lead to suboptimal decisions that users later regret (Sun et al., 2016; Zou et al., 2023). The consequences of such poorly informed choices can be significant, not only reducing consumer satisfaction but also negatively impacting business outcomes. By fostering mindful decision-making—where users carefully evaluate information and consider alternatives—better, more informed decisions can be achieved. Therefore, understanding how different forms of online presentation can encourage mindful decisionmaking is of great theoretical and practical importance. In sum, this study aims to address the following research question: How does VR presentation (with features including interactive viewing and depth cues) influence users’ mental models and subsequent mindful decisionmaking in the online environment?

To approach this research question, we developed a contextualized research model based on the mental model theory (Johnson-Laird et al., 1999; Norman, 1983). We posit that high-quality mental models are essential for VR presentations to influence mindful decision-making. In particular, our research model shows that VR presentations positively influence users’ perceived control and acquired information, thereby enhancing mental model quality and resulting in higher levels of mindfulness in decision-making.

We conducted two controlled lab experiments to examine this research model. Study 1 explores how the two distinct VR presentation features (interactive viewing and depth cues) may influence users’ perceived control and acquired information. Study 2 complements and extends Study 1 by investigating how VR presentation, which integrates the two features, influences users’ mental models and, eventually, mindful decision-making. The findings largely support our hypotheses. Specifically, our findings indicate that interactive viewing and depth cues are distinct mechanisms that influence users’ mental models. Compared to static 2D presentation, displaying content with interactive viewing could significantly improve users’ sense of control. Interestingly, neither of the two stand-alone features enhances users’ information acquisition. A significant increase in users’ acquired information was observed only when the two features were integrated, as in the VR presentation (Study 2). In addition, we found that perceived control and acquired information were two key factors shaping users’ mental models, which positively influenced their subsequent mindful decision-making.

This research contributes to IS research in several ways. First, it advances our understanding of VR presentation by distinguishing between two fundamental features, interactive viewing and depth cues, and revealing their distinct and joint impacts on users. Second, this study develops a model that examines the drivers of users’ mental model quality when information is presented using VR technology. Third, this research sheds light on mindful decision-making, an increasingly prominent aspect in the online domain. By demonstrating the positive relationship between mental model quality and mindful decision-making, this study enriches our understanding of the drivers behind mindful decisionmaking processes in the online context, especially with the help of the latest VR presentation technology.

## 2 Conceptual Background

The key difference between traditional 2D presentation and VR presentation is that VR presentation can immersively and directly present a target object in three dimensions (3D) as if users were examining the object in the real world (Liu et al., 2019). As a result, users can easily build a mental model (i.e., mental representations of external realities) of the presented object, which helps them shape their reasoning and decision-making (Johnson-Laird, 2001; Lim et al., 1997). In this research, relying on mental model theory, we posit that users’ mental models are the key underlying factor through which VR presentation influences mindful decision-making. Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework of this research, which organizes the following discussions.

## 2.1 Virtual Reality (VR) Presentation

Virtual reality presentation primarily utilizes 3D presentation, which is for displaying spatial information about the depth of an object (Wheatstone, 1838). VR users value VR’s capability as an extension of reality (Nardon & Aten, 2012). VR can take the form of 3D displays with interactive viewing and tactile response (Capps et al., 1999). It can also take the form of an immersive system, utilizing large video projections that offer depth cues and motion tracking to create the illusion of 3D (Lanier, 2001). In general, compared with 2D presentations, VR presentation is characterized by its immersive interactive viewing and depth cues (Mütterlein, 2018; Nah et al., 2011; Walsh & Pawlowski, 2002). In recent years, VR has been commonly realized through head-mounted displays (e.g., Apple Vision Pro), pushing these VR to a higher level than previous instantiations.

## 2.1.1 Interactive Viewing

Interactive viewing in VR is a form of immersive interaction that allows users to explore objects from different angles (Seymour et al., 2021). This feature also enables users to move objects in the virtual space by, for example, changing their position and angle (Suh & Lee, 2005) to simulate real-life experiences with products and services (Klein, 2003). In addition, head-mounted displays afford mobility (Davis et al., 2009) and enable head tracking so that the user’s view changes naturally as they move their head (Seymour et al., 2021). By offering these interactive viewing capabilities, VR helps users build a more comprehensive, three-dimensional mental model.

There are two widely adopted types of interactive viewing in online contexts: multiple-angle pictures (Figure 2a) and rotatable views (Figure 2b). For example, an online shop can provide users with several product images taken from different angles, as in Figure 2a. Users can choose which image to view and zoom in/out to check product details. Alternatively, rotatable views organize product images in certain orders so that the product can be rotated to be examined from various angles successively, i.e., the angles change gradually from one to another (e.g., the 360-degree view of a camera in Figure 2b). In both cases, users interact with the product by changing their viewing angles (Schlosser, 2006).

## 2.1.2 Depth Cues

Depth cues refer to “the information from which the human brain perceives the third visual dimension” (Mehrabi et al., 2013, p. 91). These cues are essential for conveying spatial relationships between objects and are often enhanced by creating a fully immersive environment that blocks out immediate physical surroundings (Mütterlein, 2018)(Mütterlein, 2018). In typical 2D presentations, such as those on computer monitors, the flat nature of the display and the lack of binocular disparity limit the ability to convey depth, making it harder for users to perceive spatial relationships (Welchman et al., 2005). In contrast, VR presentations, through stereoscopic or perspective displays in headmounted devices, provide a rich array of depth cues, allowing users to experience a more immersive and realistic environment (Stone et al., 2021).

Stereopsis is the mind’s ability to perceive threedimensional objects by interpreting the two slightly different images projected onto each retina (Wheatstone, 1838). This process relies on depth cues, particularly binocular disparity, the difference between the images seen by the left and right eyes. Stereoscopy, for example, provides two images taken from slightly different angles to create the illusion of depth, as depicted in Figure 3a(Baños et al., 2008) (Baños et al., 2008). By incorporating binocular disparity as a depth cue, this technique mimics how the right and left eyes receive input from slightly different perspectives, resulting in a strong 3D effect (Boring, 1950; Lee et al., 1986). Figure 3b illustrates how two slightly different images can be used to introduce depth cues and simulate a threedimensional view.<sup>1</sup>

In summary, interactive viewing and depth cues are two key features of VR presentations. These features work synergistically to enhance the virtual reality experience—interactive viewing allows users to dynamically engage with and explore the virtual content, while depth cues create an immersive 3D environment. Together, they offer a more compelling and immersive experience, making VR a powerful medium for presenting and interacting with content (Animesh et al., 2011; Peukert et al., 2019).

## 2.2 Users’ Mental Model

Mental models are cognitive and internal representations of external realities that are built in users’ minds (Norman, 1983; Wilson & Rutherford, 1989). They play a key role in explaining how people reason, process information, and make decisions (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994; Schneider, 2001). According to mental model theory, these models are built based on prior experiences, general knowledge, and current interactions. Mental models help individuals identify critical factors in a task or system (Rouse & Morris, 1986; Wilson & Rutherford, 1989) and guide decision-making to achieve desired outcomes (Jones et al., 2006; Lim et al., 1997). In the current context, this means that mental models help users make sense of the object they see, understand the object’s key features, and make mindful decisions. For example, when shopping online, users form mental images of a product based on the pictures displayed on the website. They also imagine how the product might be used, drawing on descriptions or prior experience with similar items—such as picturing how a camera works or imagining living in a house. These mental images and imagined uses are part of the mental model users create while evaluating a product online. Ultimately, they rely on this model to assess the product and make a purchase decision.

Assessing users’ mental models is challenging due to the diverse manners in which they build and employ these models. In IS research, mental models are commonly assessed based on criteria such as accuracy and completeness, which are termed mental model quality (Balijepally et al., 2015; Cutica & Bucciarelli, 2011; Santhanam & Sein, 1994). Consistent with the literature, in this study, we define mental model quality as the degree to which the representation of one’s evoked sensory experience is composed of many clear images (Bone & Ellen, 1992; Rodríguez-Ardura & Meseguer-Artola, 2019). That is, mental model quality reflects the clarity of the mental model “with which the individual experiences an image” (Bone & Ellen, 1992, p. 96) and the number of details memorized and evoked in a mental model (Bone & Ellen, 1992; Rodríguez-Ardura & Meseguer-Artola, 2019).

According to grounded cognition theories (Barsalou, 2007), two key factors influence the quality of users’ mental models—the amount of information conveyed visually (Paes et al., 2017; Witmer & Singer, 1998) and the level of user control when observing the focal object (Lim et al., 1997). Specifically, a rich amount of visual information allows users to create a more accurate mental model (Barsalou, 2007). For instance, when viewing a picture of a house, if the picture includes detailed information such as its appearance, location, and relative positioning, users can form a clearer mental model of the house (Marr, 2010). At the same time, user control also enhances the quality of mental models (Shi et al., 2020; Staggers & Norcio, 1993). If users can zoom in on the picture and examine the details at their own pace, they can build a more accurate representation of the house in their mind (i.e., higher mental model quality). Moreover, this increased control, along with more naturalistic interactions with the object (Lau & Lee, 2019), allows users to engage with the object in a way that suits their learning preferences, further enhancing the quality of their mental models (Azarby & Rice, 2022; Ke et al., 2016).

Users’ mental model quality directly influences their behavior and performance (Cutica & Bucciarelli, 2008; Norman, 1983; Schneider, 2001), especially in tasks that require mindful decision-making. This is because a wellconstructed mental model enables users to make informed inferences and predictions about objects (Gordon, 1992; Johnson-Laird, 1986; Rouse & Morris, 1986). Building on this idea, we view mindful decisions as key outcomes of high-quality mental models, particularly in the online environment, where mindful decision-making is crucial for achieving optimal results (Thatcher et al., 2018).

## 2.3 Mindful Decision-Making

Mindful decision-making means that one is open to new information and pays attention to details and comparisons during the decision-making process (Langer, 1989). It is associated with positive outcomes such as better alignment between user needs and technology (Sun et al., 2016), more innovative and continuous use of new technologies (Thatcher et al., 2018), and greater confidence, satisfaction, and reduced regret in purchasing decisions (Taylor & Norman, 2019). On the other hand, lacking mindfulness in decision-making can often lead to complacency and over-reliance on IS (Yetgin et al., 2015) and missed opportunities (Sun et al., 2016). In online environments, where decisions are frequently made without physical interactions with products, understanding how online presentation affects mindful decision-making becomes critical.

Prior research has identified four dimensions of mindful decision-making: engagement with the product, noveltyseeking, awareness of local context, and cognizance of alternative products (Sun et al., 2016; Thatcher et al., 2018). They conceptually align with the four dimensions of mindfulness proposed by Langer (1989, 2000). When users are engaged with a product, they actively seek new information about the product, such as carefully reading the product descriptions when shopping online. Users engage in novelty seeking when they compare a product with alternative products to understand its uniqueness. It refers to the openness and ability to compare, contrast, and judge how products’ features are the same or different (Butler & Gray, 2006). Such comparison is the basis for choosing among multiple alternative options (Triantaphyllou, 2000). An example from previous research is that a person mindfully deciding which data analytics software to use would try to understand the differences between Excel, SPSS, and STATA (Sun et al., 2016). In contrast, a person could mindlessly label SPSS as a different version of Excel.

Awareness of local context refers to the degree to which a user thinks about how the product could fit into one’s own needs (Sun et al., 2016). A person who is aware of local context would also notice when situational characteristics change (Butler & Gray, 2006). Therefore, a decision would be made through the context-relevant interpretation of information, i.e., a user would actively process information relevant to the current context to foster a better alignment between the decision and the context (Fiol & O’Conner, 2003; Louis & Sutton, 1991).

The cognizance of alternative products is the degree to which a user is aware of alternative options and their advantages and drawbacks (Sun et al., 2016). This dimension of mindful decision-making emphasizes users’ awareness of the alternatives (e.g., knowing whether five products or ten products are available for purchase). For instance, users who are cognizant of alternative products in data analytics software would know that the product category includes SPSS, STATA, SAS, etc. However, users may not understand the uniqueness of their features, which comes from novelty seeking. Taken together, a mindful decision maker actively seeks and processes information about the product (engagement), compares its features with those of other products (novelty-seeking), pays attention to how this product matches their own needs (awareness of local context), and is aware of multiple alternatives to the product (cognizance of alternative) (Sun & Fang, 2010).

To date, despite the growing stream of mindfulness research, there is no coherent understanding of its antecedents or what may drive users to make a mindful decision (see reviews of mindfulness studies in Sun et al., 2016 and Thatcher et al., 2018). Some researchers believe that certain people are inherently more mindful than others. For instance, research has shown that female users are more likely to make mindful decisions (Battaglia et al., 2020), and those who have a natural disposition toward rationality (Watier & Dubois, 2016) and openness to experience (Goswami et al., 2009) tend to be more mindful. Other researchers believe that mindfulness can be activated or trained. For example, Addas and Pinsonneault (2018) found that task-relevant information can promote mindfulness, even in the form of an email interruption. In this study, we view mindful decision-making as something that can be influenced by users’ mental models of the presented contents (e.g., products), as mental models are critical in evaluating a product, especially in the online context where users cannot touch and feel the actual product.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

One main goal of this study is to unveil the underlying mechanisms through which VR presentation enhances users’ mental models and subsequently improves their mindfulness in decision-making. As previously discussed, the literature suggests that during the mental model building phase, factors such as the level of interaction control and the information conveyed about an object influence the quality of users’ mental models (Coxon et al., 2016; Marr, 2010; Shi et al., 2020). Therefore, in this study, we include these two corresponding cognitive factors that affect users’ mental model quality—the perceived control during the interaction with an object and the users’ acquired information after viewing the object. Figure 4 presents the research model of this study.

## 3.1 VR Presentation and Users’ Mental Models

## 3.1.1 Perceived Control

Consistent with prior work (Chipperfield et al., 2012; Ghani et al., 1991), we define perceived control as the degree to which users believe they have control over the presented information, such as browsing web content or examining online products. In particular, when building mental models, users’ perceived control has two aspects. One is users’ perceived ability to choose information and guide an interaction (Jiang et al., 2010; Lowry et al., 2006). It concerns users’ control over what information is presented. It is a feeling informed directly by the system presentation’s design features and available choices (Trevino & Webster, 1992) and represents the users’ perception of being in charge of the interaction (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). The other one is users’ navigability, which concerns their control of how the information is presented. In the online environment, navigation has been defined as “the decisions and actions that contribute to a person’s ability to find and examine data organized in the computer medium” (Watts-Perotti, 1999, p. 270). It is essential to mental model building: when users can freely navigate the presented content, they are more likely to find and examine the information (such as an online product) and build the mental model at their own pace (Webster & Ahuja, 2006).

Since the feature of depth cues primarily focuses on creating an immersive 3D environment, they are unlikely to enhance users’ perception of control (Kumar & Benbasat, 2004). Therefore, in this study, we do not expect depth cues to enhance users’ perceived control. Instead, we posit that VR presentation enhances users perceived control solely through its interactive viewing. The interactive viewing feature lets users control what is presented when browsing online content. Take a virtual house tour as an example. With interactive viewing, users can choose which room to examine, change viewing angles, and zoom in/out to investigate the details of the room. They can even skip the rooms that they are not interested in (e.g., by swiping or virtually “walking” to another room). As a result, users are likely to have a strong feeling that they are in charge of this browsing process (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000) and will thus perceive a high level of control (Jiang et al., 2010; Lowry et al., 2006).

Additionally, the interactive viewing feature allows users to explore content at their own pace and choose how to navigate through it. For instance, in a virtual house tour, users can determine their own path through the house, deciding the order in which to explore different rooms. Also, within each room, they have full control over their viewing patterns, such as moving from top to bottom or right to left. With the latest headmounted displays, these navigation processes could closely mimic real-life experiences—like simply turning one’s head to change the viewing angle. This natural and intuitive interactive viewing not only enhances the immersive experience but also significantly boosts users’ sense of control (Salisbury et al., 2001; Wells et al., 2011).

In short, when browsing online content, interactive viewing gives users control over both what to view and how to view the online content. Thus, unlike static 2D presentations, which lack this feature, VR presentations enhance users’ sense of control. Based on this, we propose the following hypothesis.

H1: Compared to static 2D presentation, VR presentation (via interactive viewing) enhances users’ perceived control.

## 3.1.2 Acquired Information

In the online context, acquired information refers to the amount of information users obtain from a website (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007). It is a crucial factor in the information processing and knowledge comprehension process (Paas et al., 2003). Since acquired information forms the basis of mental models (Marr, 2010), it is important to assess how VR presentations influence the amount of information users acquire when building these models.

We argue that both features of VR presentation (interactive viewing and depth cues) can increase users acquired information, though in distinct ways. On the one hand, interactive viewing allows users to pay attention to details that may otherwise be easily overlooked. For instance, users can zoom in and out to observe a virtual object in detail. Acquiring this detailed information increases users’ understanding of the target object. In addition, users can choose what information is presented according to their needs (Lowry et al., 2006). Users can allocate their cognitive resources to browsing the contents they are not familiar with by being selective about the information they acquire.

On the other hand, depth cues enhance users’ information acquisition by increasing their sense of immersion. By presenting information and objects with depth cues, users can quickly immerse themselves in the presented context. This is because strong depth cues construct an immersive environment consistent with the experience users encounter in real life (Sheehy et al., 2014; Sun & Perona, 1996). When users immerse themselves in the content browsing process, they will be willing to allocate more cognitive resources to the task at hand (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000) and will actively seek information to better understand subjects of interest (Hannafin & Hooper, 1993). In addition, with the help of stereoscopic devices (e.g., head-mounted display), the immersive environment can direct users’ attention to the target object quickly and effectively (Biocca et al., 2006; LaBerge & Samuels, 1974). For instance, when using a head-mounted display with strong depth cues, the target object would appear near the users. Users could then focus more on examining this object (and thus get more information about it) and are less likely to be distracted by other content that is further away (Biocca et al., 2006). Finally, depth cues themselves present valuable information about the relative locations of components of an object in a three-dimensional space. When depth cues are present, the user is more likely to get information about how the components of an object (e.g., furniture in a house during a virtual house tour) are organized.

In summary, compared to static 2D presentations, we expect VR to increase the amount of information users acquire through both of its features—interactive viewing and depth cues.

H2: Compared to static 2D presentation, VR presentation (via interactive viewing and depth cues) increases users’ acquired information.

## 3.2 Users’ Mental Model

In the context of visual presentation, users’ mental model quality is evaluated in terms of accuracy and completeness after viewing the presented object (e.g., an online product) (Bone & Ellen, 1992; Cutica & Bucciarelli, 2011). In this sense, users build high-quality mental models allowing them to recall many details of the presented object and plausibly imagine the virtual object. The literature suggests that the most effective way to build such a high-quality mental model is to interact with the object (Shi et al., 2020; Staggers & Norcio, 1993) and acquire as much information about it as possible (Coxon et al., 2016).

In VR presentation, users’ interaction relies heavily on their perceived control, as it captures users’ freedom of viewing and interacting with the object. First, when users perceive that they can actively control the viewing of the object presented in front of them, they can be selective in what they view. Specifically, in the mental model building process, users are aware of which parts of the mental model are missing or require further information—they can then change their viewing angles or zoom in/out of the virtual object to fill in the missing information (i.e., increasing the number of details in their mental model) (Cutica & Bucciarelli, 2011; Zwaan et al., 1995).

Second, if users perceive they can control the interaction with the virtual object, they are more likely to build the mental model in a way that they are familiar with, which is more effective (Shi et al., 2020). Specifically, when building a mental model of an object, some people may start from the overall impression and examine specific parts of the object for details, while others may start with a few highlights of the object and then observe it as a whole (Gray, 1990). When users have control over the observation process, they can follow their own style and pace to build their mental model. Without this control, they may miss the information they seek (e.g., being shown details when they want an overall view), which can hinder the development of a high-quality mental model (Sears, 1983).

Moreover, a high level of perceived control, especially in an immersive environment (e.g., VR with a headmounted display), creates a browsing experience that is similar to offline browsing. For example, in online shopping, when users perceive that they can examine the target product as they like (e.g., rotating, zooming in/out, etc.), they can relate and imagine similar experiences they had during offline shopping (Ariely, 2000), which can enhance their mental model quality (Collins & Gentner, 1987). Thus, we expect users’ perceived control to positively influence the quality of the mental model.

## H3: Perceived control positively influences users’ mental model quality.

Mental models are based on information processing (Babin & Burns, 1998; Gavilan et al., 2014). When people engage in mental model building, they are accessing relevant associations, images, and experiences from memory—while at the same time evaluating and analyzing the information presented (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Thus, information, which can take different forms (e.g., text or graphics), is the foundation for mental models (Norman, 1983). The more information that users acquire from the observation and interaction of the object, the more details they will be able to recall for mental model building, and the more likely they will be to build better mental models (Vogt et al., 2021).

In addition, with more acquired information about the presented object, users can develop more plausible imagined scenarios associated with the object. For example, if users acquire information about the stairs in a virtual house tour, such as where they are located and how they look, they will be able to easily imagine themselves walking on the stairs to access the second floor of the house. Such an imagined scenario (i.e., walking to the second floor using the stairs) is more plausible than scenarios that do not supply information about the stairs. Taken together, we expect a positive relationship between users’ acquired information and their mental model quality.

H4: The amount of acquired information positively influences users’ mental model quality.

## 3.3 Mental Model Quality and Mindful Decision-Making

We posit that users’ mental model quality is positively associated with their mindfulness in decision-making. First, a high-quality mental model preserves much spatial information and details about a product (MacInnis & Price, 1987), allowing users to make sense of the product’s novel features, which is an important aspect of mindful decision-making. For example, someone who knows the color and material of a shirt can make a more informed and mindful decision than someone who only knows the color of a shirt. This phenomenon is reversely evident by the high rate of product returns at online clothing stores, where consumers may have access to limited product information (De et al., 2013). That is, low-quality mental models that do not include certain features of a product will result in poor decisions.

Second, when the mental model quality is high, users can put themselves in the local context when evaluating the object. Awareness of location context is one key component of mindful decision-making, as users need to be aware of the local context and foster an alignment between their decisions and the context (Fiol & O’Conner, 2003; Louis & Sutton, 1991). Users accomplish this by building mental models based on new information and previous experiences (Wilson & Rutherford, 1989). For instance, during a virtual house tour, if users can imagine themselves living in the house based on their prior experiences (i.e., building a highquality mental model), they will be better able to assess the property—such as evaluating whether the layout meets their needs—and will make a more mindful decision (e.g., whether to purchase the house).

Third, we argue that higher-quality mental models lead to a higher level of actively making sense of and engaging with the target object (MacInnis & Price, 1987). In the IS domain, using technology mindfully has been associated with more active system use, such as deep structure usage and trying to motivate, as opposed to more automatic system use (Thatcher et al., 2018). For example, devoting effort to processing email messages and rehearsing how to respond (i.e., building mental models of emails and replies) leads to a more mindful state of information processing (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018). In addition, when users have high-quality mental models, they can actively integrate data from existing knowledge structures, such as knowledge about similar products they have previously seen or used (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Walters et al., 2019). This capacity to be cognizant of alternatives is also a crucial part of mindful decisionmaking. Therefore, we expect users’ mental model quality to positively influence mindfulness in decisionmaking.

H5: Users’ mental model quality positively influences their mindful decision-making.

## 4 Method

We conducted two empirical studies in different settings to test our hypotheses. In particular, Study 1 isolates the two features of VR presentation—interactive viewing and depth cues—and examines their distinct effects on the key components of users’ mental models (i.e., perceived control and acquired information). Extending Study 1, in the setting of a virtual house tour, Study 2 utilizes the latest technology of VR presentation (Meta Quest 2) and examines its effect on users’ mental models and mindful decision-making. Using data from two separate experiments in different settings enhances the generalizability of our findings and generates more robust support for our research model (Venkatesh et al., 2013, 2016).

## 4.1 Study 1: Distinct Effects of VR Presentation Features

Since we argue that VR enhances users’ mental model quality through two distinct features (i.e., interactive viewing and depth cues), the goal of Study 1 is to test this inner mechanism and then examine, in particular, whether interactive viewing can indeed boost users’ perceived control and whether both features can increase the amount of information users acquire when building their mental models.

We conducted a lab experiment with a self-developed website called “HardwareOne” that displays six computer hardware components (motherboard, CPU, heat sink, RAM, video card, and hard drive) (Figure 5). The reasons for using computer hardware components as the experimental product are twofold. First, our participants were not familiar with computer hardware (their average prior knowledge of computer hardware was 3.15 out of 7). This limits the amount of prior experience contributing to mental model building and ensures that they must acquire new information from the experiment to complete the experiment task (i.e., an exam). Secondly, computer hardware is a typical 3D object presented online. They also have small components that may introduce occlusion effects, which are needed for presenting depth cues (Cutting & Vishton, 1995). To highlight the manipulation area, based on findings from a pilot study with 90 students, we minimized the amount of textual explanation. Every hardware component was described using only two or three sentences.<sup>2</sup> This helps participants focus on the presented content in the manipulation area.

## 4.1.1 Experimental Design and Procedure

The experiment used a between-subjects design with two treatment groups (interactive viewing and depth cues) and one control group (static 2D). A pilot study was conducted first before the main experiment. Ninety undergraduate students from an undergraduate entrylevel psychology course were recruited for the pilot study. The experimental websites and survey were modified based on feedback and findings from the pilot study.

After the pilot study, we recruited another 91 undergraduate students from the same psychology course offered in the following semester for the main experiment. All participants were in the age range of 18 to 24; 59 were female and 32 were male. ANOVA analyses showed that the three groups were not significantly different from each other in age $( p = 0 . 3 6 6 )$ , gender $( p = 0 . 6 0 9 )$ , or education level $( p = 0 . 3 4 6 )$ , indicating the effectiveness of random assignment. Roscoe (1975) and Sekaran and Bougie (2016) suggested the rule of thumb that a minimum sample size of 30 is required for each treatment. Hair et al. (2010) stipulated five respondents per variable to be analyzed as the lower limit. Thus, the sample size of 91 participants satisfied the requirements for statistical power to test the research model in Study 1.

In the experiment, participants in the interactive viewing group could use a mouse and a keyboard to control the viewing angles of the presented content (i.e., computer hardware components). They could rotate the front side of the hardware<sup>3</sup> and zoom in and out.<sup>4</sup> The participants in the depth cues group, on the other hand, needed to wear a pair of lightweight, high-quality NVIDIA electronic stereo 3D glasses to view the stereo display (with no interaction features enabled). For the control group, the presented contents were shown as static 2D images. Other than manipulating the presentation format, the rest of the experiment settings were the same across the three groups (e.g., computer settings, monitors, mice, and keyboards used). In addition, we ensured that all three versions of the HardwareOne website had equivalent information. That is, although participants in the interactive viewing group could rotate and zoom in/out the image to see it from different viewpoints, those in the depth cues and static 2D groups could also see the same information. This ensured that participants were presented with the same information to prepare for the questions that assessed their acquired information. Table 1 summarizes the implementation of the three groups in the experiment.

The experiment was conducted in multiple sessions, with three participants per session. Upon arrival, the participants in each session were randomly assigned to one of the three groups. Participants were informed that their task was to learn about six computer hardware components on a website in preparation for an exam. They also did a warm-up exercise using stereo 3D to reduce the novelty effect. The warm-up exercise was followed by the main round: participants interacted with the website, with no time limits, to complete the experiment task. When the participants felt they had acquired enough information from the website, they proceeded to the exam session. The exam included nine multiple-choice questions about the six computer hardware components displayed earlier. When participants finished the exam, they were asked about their perceived control when interacting with HarwareOne. Their demographic information and responses to other control variables were also collected. While most participants finished the experiment within 20 minutes, as anticipated, we told them that they had one hour for the experiment. We did not observe any participants who felt time pressure. ## 4.1.4 Summary

The above findings shed light on the distinct effects of the two features of VR presentation on users’ perceived control and acquired information, respectively. As expected, interactive viewing can significantly enhance users’ perceived control. However, neither of the features was observed to increase users’ acquired information, as compared to static 2D presentation. This indicates that the stand-alone VR presentation features (i.e., interactive viewing or depth cues) may not be as effective in enhancing users’ information acquisition as we expected. Therefore, in the next study, we combined the two VR features and examined their integrated effects in influencing users’ mental models and their subsequent mindful decision-making.

## 4.2 Study 2: Integrated Effects of VR Presentation

We conducted a controlled experiment to examine our research model in Study 2. The device used in the experiment for the VR presentation, which integrated both interactive viewing and depth cues, was Meta’s VR device—Meta Quest 2.<sup>6</sup> The task was a virtual house tour. We chose a virtual house tour as the experiment content for two reasons. First, viewing a house, presented either in 2D or 3D, would require users to build a 3D mental model of the house to understand its structure and the relative positions of the rooms. This complemented Study 1, in which a 3D mental model was not emphasized, as the presented computer hardware components were mostly flat. Second, the house tour task can organically utilize the two VR presentation features—viewers need to look around (interactive viewing) and sense the house’s structure with depth cues. This ensured that participants in the treatment group would not rely on only a single feature of VR to complete the experiment task.

## 4.2.1 Experimental Design and Procedure

The experiment used a between-subject design with two groups: one treatment group with a VR presentation and one control group with a static 2D presentation. We conducted one pilot study (n = 33) to assess the validity of the measures and the effectiveness of treatment in the new context. For the main study, 92 participants (46 male, 45 female, and one not disclosed) were recruited from three public universities at both graduate and undergraduate levels. Among them, 48 were in the treatment group (VR), and 44 were in the control group (static 2D). All participants were older than 18 and had an average age of 23.14 (SD = 5.29). The sample size in each group satisfied the statistical power requirement for betweensubject analysis (Cohen, 2013).

The VR treatment consisted of viewing the house in VR mode. The VR mode was constructed by seamlessly integrating high-quality pictures taken by a 3D camera.<sup>7</sup> Through a head-mounted display (i.e., Meta Quest 2), participants could examine a house in a 3D environment by turning their heads and moving around by pulling the controller. Participants in the control group viewed the same house by browsing the static 2D pictures in a web browser. The 2D pictures were taken in each room of the house and showed the same details that participants in the VR mode had access to. This ensured that the information presented to both groups was identical. The two houses used in this experiment (one for the warmup round and one for the main round) were randomly selected from Matterport’s online gallery, which includes both the 2D pictures and the VR mode. ## 4.2.2 Measures

The intention to purchase was captured using one item. Participants were asked: “On a scale of 1 (very unlikely) to 10 (very likely), how likely is it that you would purchase this house?” (Venkatesh et al., 2008). Similar to Study 1, the acquired information was measured by the number of correct answers to nine questions about the house (Blanco et al., 2010; Hong et al., 2004; Jiang & Benbasat, 2007). These nine questions covered three aspects of the house—the overall understanding of the house (e.g., How many bathrooms does this house have?), the specific features of the house (e.g., What is the floor material of the house?), and the relative structure of the house (e.g., Is the kitchen located between two bedrooms?). Perceived control captured the integration of perceived active control (Jiang et al., 2010) and perceived navigability (Salisbury et al., 2001; Wells et al., 2011). Users’ mental model quality was measured using eight items adapted from Lee and Shin (2020) and Walters et al. (2012). Mindful decision-making was constructed as a second-order factor based on four dimensions (novelty seeking, engagement, local context, and cognizance of alternatives), which included 13 items that were adapted from Sun et al. (2016). Agarwal and Prasad’s (1998) PIIT measure, Thatcher et al.’s (2008) self-efficacy measure, Jiang and Benbasat’s (2007) prior knowledge measure (on house viewing and on virtual reality), Marks’s (1973) imagery ability measure (which captures individuals’ differences on the ability to imagine), age, and gender were used as control variables. The descriptive statistics (construct correlations, square root of AVEs, loadings, etc.) of Study 2, presented in Appendix E, suggest satisfactory validity and reliability. We checked the success of our manipulation—whether the VR presentation employed both interactive viewing and depth cues (i.e., the two stand-alone features examined in Study 1). After the participants finished the experiment, we asked them to indicate whether they could interact with the house by changing their viewing angles and positions (0 or 1) and their 3D spatial perceptions (four items adapted from Liu et al., 2019). As Table 3 shows, all participants in the treatment group indicated that they had a higher sense of interactive viewing and significantly higher 3D spatial perception (i.e., perceptions of the depth cues) than the control group. ## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Major Findings

Using two controlled experiments, this research tested how VR presentation affects users’ mental models and mindful decision-making. Table 5 summarizes the results of hypothesis testing. The two studies revealed several interesting findings. First, we found that interactive viewing alone and VR presentation can significantly enhance users’ perceived control. This is consistent with our expectation that by allowing users to interact with their viewing object freely, they will form a strong sense of control.

Second, compared to traditional static 2D presentation, VR presentation can significantly increase users’ acquired information. Nonetheless, in Study 1, we did not observe such an effect when interactive viewing and depth cues were implemented independently. This indicates that either feature alone may not be sufficient to improve information acquisition in online environments. In Study 2, when these two features are integrated into the VR presentation, a significant increase in the acquired information was observed (as compared to static 2D presentation). Taken together, our results show that only when both interactive viewing and depth cues are implemented successfully can VR presentation effectively enhance users’ information acquisition.

Third, according to the mental model theory, we identified and examined two key factors of users mental model quality in the online context—perceived control and acquired information. Both factors significantly and positively impact users’ mental model quality. This indicates that if users feel they have control during the online browsing process and have acquired sufficient information about the target object, they can build a high-quality mental model that accurately reflects the actual object in the real world.

Fourth, we found that high-quality mental models can lead to more mindful decision-making. If users can form a high-quality mental model about the target object, they will be mindful when deciding on this object (e.g., a purchase decision in online shopping). At the same time, we noted that prior knowledge and imagery ability also have significant positive impacts on mindful decisionmaking when content is presented using VR.

In sum, findings from this research suggest that VR presentation is more effective when features related to interactive viewing and depth cues are both provided. Either feature could have its constraints and limitations. In addition, if online content is presented using VR, users are more likely to feel that they have control over the browsing process and will acquire more information about the presented content. All of these things will help them build better mental models, which lead to mindful decisions.

## 5.2 Limitations and Future Directions

Like other empirical studies, this research has its limitations. In Study 1, to highlight the manipulation of the two VR presentation features and to control confounding factors such as price and individual preferences, we used simple computer components as our web content. As a result, the task may not be complicated enough for the stand-alone features to really show their advantages (or disadvantages) over the 2D presentation (i.e., no difference in acquired information was observed). Future studies can revisit these two features’ effects in different settings and explore the potential advantages of presenting the content with stand-alone features.

The use of student subjects limits the generalizability of this research (Compeau et al., 2012). We do not think this limitation is crucial, though, given that students, or more broadly, young people, are typical users of websites. Nevertheless, examining our research model in other populations, such as business decision makers, is necessary. Students tend to be “satisfiers” in decisionmaking. Other types of users, such as online shoppers or business decision makers, are likely to be more motivated to maximize their outcomes (e.g., with higher demands of helpful information), which may change the mechanisms of mental model building and its effect on mindful decision-making.

This research does not study the fit between VR presentation and the presented content, which is a promising area for future research, given that it is important for enhancing task performance and technology utilization (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). As implied in this paper, the VR presentation or its features may not fit or be effective for all contents.

Future research could better distinguish types of content and study how they fit different VR presentations and features. Examining other potential moderating factors beyond task-technology-fit, such as task complexity and product type, may bring insightful findings.

In addition, we found that not all participants could adapt to VR presentations easily during the experiments. Some participants took a much longer time to get used to the VR display. Prior studies have also found that stereo 3D with strong depth cues could lead to visual discomfort (Lambooij et al., 2009) and simulator sickness (Schild et al., 2012). This offers another interesting direction of research—how to present content in traditional 2D without losing the information provided by the depth cues, especially in tasks like viewing a house where depth cues are important (e.g., the position and spatial information).

## 5.3 Research Contributions and Implications

This research offers several contributions to IS research. First, we conceptualized interactive viewing and depth cues as two key VR presentation features and studied their distinct effects on users’ mental models. Prior studies have typically treated VR or 3D presentation as a whole and examined the effect of its presence (e.g., Kumar & Benbasat, 2004; Loup-Escande et al., 2017; Suh & Prophet, 2018). Distinguishing and identifying specific VR features enhances our knowledge of the detailed mechanisms through which VR presentation functions. In addition, this study introduces depth cues, one fundamental feature of VR, to the IS field. With the advance of virtual reality and other 3D technologies, understanding the exact mechanisms of 3D presentation approaches is particularly important for theory.

Second, this research develops a novel research model that examines how VR influences users’ mental model quality through acquired information and perceived control. Though the mental model in information processing has been studied and applied in IS research (e.g., Balijepally et al., 2015), how users would build their mental model when they browse online content or other IS-mediated devices is still an open question. Moreover, prior studies of mental models have typically focused on their influence on users’ attitudes (e.g., Lee & Shin, 2020; Tercia et al., 2020). While mental model building is viewed as a part of information processing and learning (MacInnis & Price, 1987), little research has been done on its mechanics in the online context. This research takes the first step in bridging these two bodies of research (i.e., mental model building and online information processing) by investigating how users form a mental model when information is presented using VR and how mental model quality would affect users’ mindful decision-making in online contexts.

Third, this research investigates the drivers of mindful decision-making, which we believe is a meaningful user behavior in the online environment (Butler & Gray, 2006; Sun et al., 2016; Thatcher et al., 2018). In addition, prior research on mindfulness and mindful decision-making has primarily focused on their consequences. The drivers of mindful decision-making have only recently attracted researchers’ attention (e.g., Dernbecher & Beck, 2017; Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2020). In this study, we add to this literature by revealing the importance of users’ mental model quality in driving mindful decision-making. We believe factors that can increase mindful decisionmaking via IS warrant greater attention.

Moreover, our findings show the alignment of perceived active control and perceived navigability into the same latent factor despite their conceptual distinctions. This convergence can be attributed to the unique characteristics of VR presentation, where controlling what to view often overlaps with how to view it. This shows that VR is different from the traditional web context and 2D presentations, where control and navigation are often clearly separate. This finding suggests a potential avenue for future research to delve deeper into the nuances of user experiences in VR environments, particularly regarding the interplay between control and navigational aspects. Understanding these dynamics more comprehensively could contribute to refining measurement instruments and enhancing our understanding of user interactions within VR presentations.

## 5.4 Practical Implications

With the rising trend of the metaverse and the increasing prevalence of VR-related online applications, understanding how VR affects users’ cognitions (mental models) and behaviors (decision-making) is vital to current and future businesses. Findings from this study offer a few insights to enhance such understanding. First, rendering user interactive viewing is key to an effective VR presentation. A stand-alone presentation feature that only emphasizes the illusion of depth may be less effective than expected. At the same time, with either 3D glasses or VR head-mounted display devices, users will feel constrained when examining the target object if the website does not allow them to interact with their viewing object. Hence, providing interactive viewing alongside depth cues is essential to enhance user experience.

Second, with the established benefits of mindful decision-making (e.g., higher satisfaction, better IT usage, etc.), this study provides guidance to practitioners and web designers on stimulating end users’ mindful decision-making. Specifically, they need to ensure that their presentation media (e.g., a website) can facilitate users’ mental model building. A better mental model, which is driven by users’ sense of control and acquired information, would lead to more mindful decisions.

## 6 Conclusion

VR technologies and presentations have become more ubiquitous in the online world. By examining VR presentation and its two key features—interactive viewing and depth cues—this study reveals the impact of VR presentation on users’ mental models and mindful decision-making in the online environment. In two different settings, this research finds that VR presentation can positively influence users’ perceived control and acquired information, which are the drivers of mental model quality. In addition, users with highquality mental models are more likely to make mindful decisions. Findings from this study contribute to research and practice by revealing the mechanisms of VR presentation and its impacts on users’ cognitions and behaviors. We hope that our work will stimulate future IS research on VR presentation.

## Appendix A: Experiment Procedure for Study 1

The experiment was conducted in multiple sessions, with three participants per session. Upon arrival, the participants in each session were randomly assigned to one of the three groups. The experimental procedure included several steps (Figure A1):

![](/api/attachments/ZZCHUXAH/fulltext/images/2e7028c3874b717d3ec352c940df151b51ef095a84adcca53b9b801d11d6b62e.jpg)  
Figure A1. Study 1’s Experimental Procedure

Step 0: The three participants in each session were greeted and instructed on their task. Participants were informed that their task was to learn about six computer hardware components on a website in preparation for an exam. They also did a warmup exercise using stereo 3D to avoid the novelty effect, even if they were assigned to the other two groups. Since an estimate of 4-10% of people cannot view stereo 3D presentations (CNET),<sup>8</sup> we gave a quick exam including three stereo 3D samples to ensure that the participants assigned to the depth cue group were able to view stereo 3D presentations.

Step 1: The warm-up exercise was followed by the real learning session: participants interacted with the website to browse the six computer hardware components. Participants had plenty of time for the learning task. While most participants finished the experiment within 20 minutes, as anticipated, we told them that they had one hour to do the experiment. We did not observe any participants who felt pressured by time.

Step 2: When the participants felt they had learned enough from the website, they proceeded to the exam session by clicking the Exam button on the website. The exam included nine multiple-choice questions about the computer hardware images displayed earlier. Figure A2 shows two out of nine questions in the exam.

Step 3: When participants finished the exam, they clicked a survey button to finish a post-experiment survey. The survey included measures for their perceptions about using HarwareOne for learning. Incentives in the form of course credits were offered to all participants at the end of the experiment session.

## About the Authors

Heshan Sun, Ph.D. is the Richard Van Horn Professor of IT and Analytics in the Management Information Systems division at the Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma. His research interests include human technology/AI interaction, business analytics, and online crowd behavior. His published and forthcoming papers have appeared in many prestigious academic journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, among others. He is a senior editor at MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and AIS Transactions on HCI.

Siyuan (Seth) Li is the Dorman Family Term Distinguished Associate Professor in Business Analytics at William & Mary. His research focuses on three key areas: online consumer behaviors, information diffusion on online social networks, and human interactions within organizational social networks. Dr. Li has published extensively in top-tier journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems. His work leverages diverse quantitative methods, including big data analysis, social network analysis, surveys, and experiments.

Shih-Lun (Allen) Tseng is an assistant professor of information systems at the Monte Ahuja College of Business, Cleveland State University. His research focuses on design features in gamified information systems. His other research interests include human-computer interaction, e-commerce, and online reviews. His research has appeared in Information Systems Research, Information & Management, Decision Support Systems, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of Interactive Marketing.

Mary A. Peterson is a Professor Emerita of the University of Arizona Psychology Department and Cognitive Science Program. In her research, she uses ERPs, fMRI, and behavioral methods to investigate: object detection with an emphasis on the roles of memory and semantics, iterative feedforward and feedback mechanisms in perception, and unconscious selection of the best-fitting interpretation for visual input. Professor Peterson is a Fellow of AAAS, APA, APS, and the Psychonomic Society. She is an elected member of the International Neuropsychological Symposium and the Society of Experimental Psychologists. She has served on the governing boards of the Vision Sciences Society and the Psychonomic Society and as the chair of the governing board of the Psychonomic Society. She is a founder of the Configural Processing Consortium and served as its president (2016-2019). Dr. Peterson is one of the founding organizers of “Females of Vision, et al” (FoVea, founded in 2014), whose goal is to enhance the success of vision scientists. She has been an active member of the advisory board of Women in Cognitive Science since its inception in 2000.
