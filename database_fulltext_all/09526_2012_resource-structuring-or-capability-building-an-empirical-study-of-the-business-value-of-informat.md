Abstract: This paper examines two ways to create business value of information technology (BVIT): resource structuring and capability building. We develop a research model positing that IT resources and IT capabilities enhance a firm’s performance by providing support to its competitive strategies and core competencies, and the strengths of these supports vary in accord with environmental dynamism. The model is empiri cally tested using data collected from 296 firms in China. It is found that IT resources generate more business effects in stable environments than in dynamic environments, while IT capabilities generate more business effects in dynamic environments than in stable environments. The results suggest that the BVIT creation mechanism in stable environments is primarily resource structuring while the mechanism in dynamic environments is primarily capability building.

Key w ords an d ph rases: business value of IT, capability building, competitive strategy, core competence, environmental dynamism, resource structuring.

In formation techn ol ogy (IT ) h as become in dispen sabl e to modern organizations, and worldwide IT spending grew to a remarkable amount of \$3.7 trillion in 2011 [27]. The business value of information technology (BVIT) has long been identified as one of the key issues for information systems (IS) academics and practitioners [43, 53]. Despite some studies suggesting that IT has little or negative effects on firm performance [25, 41], many have found a positive relationship between IT and firm performance [62]. To date, consensus has been reached that IT does create business value, and BVIT manifests in many different ways [43]. Yet the mechanisms of BVIT creation remains vaguely understood.

When evaluating BVIT, prior research has primarily focused on IT resources and IT capabilities and investigated their effects on organizational performance. Firm performance is found to be enhanced by both IT resources [6, 102] and IT capabilities [9, 11, 66, 74, 88, 104]. Yet it is revealed that IT does not always lead to positive outcomes. For example, Kettinger and Grover [41] evaluated longitudinal changes in performance measures of 30 firms recognized as exemplar IT users and found that half of the firms could not achieve sustainable BVIT. Little is known why IT creates business value in some circumstances but not in others. Motivated by this gap in the literature, we attempt to gain a deeper understanding of BVIT creation by contrasting the effects of IT resources versus IT capabilities in different contexts. Makadok’s [56] work implies that firms can gain advantages by picking appropriate resources in stable environments, while require dynamic capabilities to gain advantages when the environment becomes more unpredictable. The management and IS literature also shows that environmental dynamism leads to substantial uncertainties that require firms to rely on IT capabilities to stay competitive [72, 98, 109]. These observations confirm our surmise that resources and capabilities may have different utility in stable versus dynamic environments. Yet academic research has not explicated the different effects of IT resources and IT capabilities in different environments, and practitioners unaware of these differences could make ill-advised IT investment decisions. Hence, in this study we investigate the impact of IT on business values by examining IT resources and IT capabilities in tandem and in contrast.

Drawing on the resource orchestration research [98, 99], we contend that BVIT creation can be achieved via two mechanisms: resource structuring and capability building. Resource structuring posits that firms create BVIT by being more effective than their rivals at structuring IT resources, while capability building posits that firms create BVIT by being more effective at deploying IT resources. Resource orchestration is a new theoretical framework integrating notions of resource management [98] and asset orchestration [31], which provides a more precise understanding of managers role in structuring resources in the firm’s resource portfolio, bundling resources into capabilities, and leveraging the capabilities to create value for customers [99]. Similar to Makadok’s [56] research on resource picking and capability building, resource orchestration emphasizes the importance of both resources and capabilities to firm success. Yet resource orchestration extends Makadok’s work by pointing out that resource structuring is more than resource picking; rather, it involves (1) acquiring (i.e., the process of purchasing resources from strategic factor markets), (2) accumulating (i.e., the process of developing resources internally), and (3) divesting (i.e., the process of shedding firm-controlled resources) [99]. In this paper, we contend that the concept of resource orchestration can be applied to shed light on BVIT research and propose resource structuring and capability building as two primary mechanisms of BVIT creation.

To date, few IS studies have explicitly differentiated between the two mechanisms of BVIT creation and contrasted their effects in different environments, and the effects of IT resources and IT capabilities require more investigation. In this paper, we seek to answer the following research question:

## RQ: What are the different effects of resource structuring and capability building on BVIT creation in stable versus dynamic environments?

Specifically, we investigate the influences of IT resources and IT capabilities on firm performance by means of providing support to organizational core competencies and strategies and the moderating role of environmental dynamism. Our analysis of survey data from 296 firms is intended to generate theoretical insights that can enrich the understanding of BVIT creation and to provide practical implications that are helpful to firms wanting to reap benefits from IT investments.

## Theoretical Framework

## Resource Structuring and Capability Building

Resource structurin g an d capabil ity buil din g are distin ct mech an isms of BVIT creation because of their different focus [56, 98]. To distinguish between them, the definitions of resource and capability must be clarified. In the resource-based view (RBV) [7, 109, 111], resource and capability are two key constructs employed to explain the sources of competitive advantages. The original RBV broadly defines resources as rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable firm-specific assets that enable a firm to implement a value-creating strategy to generate rents [7]. This encompassing construct of resources include both resources (or assets) and capabilities. Amit and Schoemaker [2] split this general construct into two distinct concepts—resources and capabilities—by defining resources as tradable and nonspecific firm assets and capabilities as nontradable, firm-specific abilities to integrate, deploy, and utilize other resources within the firm. In this sense, resources are the inputs of production processes, while capability refers to the capacity to deploy resources using organization processes [2]. The key characteristic of capability that separates it from resource is its organizational embeddedness, which suggests that capability cannot be easily bought from the external factor market, is embedded within the organization, and must be built or cultivated over time.

IT resources in this paper are defined as commodity-like assets that are widely available and can be purchased from the factor market. IS researchers have identified various IT resources, including physical IT resources, human-related IT resources, and interpersonal IT resources [9, 38, 83, 88, 109, 117]. Human-related IT resources consist of technical and managerial IT resources. Therefore, IT resources include four key components: (1) tangible IT resources comprising physical IT infrastructure such as hardware, software, network, and data; (2) human-related technical IT resources comprising IT professionals who possess technical skills, knowledge, and experiences; (3) human-related managerial IT resources comprising governance structure of the IT function and IT professionals who possess managerial skills; and (4) relational IT resources comprising social capital arising from relationships between IT function and other parties, including internal business functions and external vendors and consultants.

IT resources are conceptualized as a second-order formative construct based on Jarvis et al.’s [34] criteria. First, a firm’s IT infrastructure determines its level of IT resources, but not vice versa. Second, IT infrastructure and technical IT resources are completely different assets, and they cannot replace each other. Third, IT infrastructure and technical IT resources do not need to have covariation, and an increase in IT infrastructure does not necessarily mean that technical IT resources will also increase. Finally, IT infrastructure and technical IT resources have different antecedents—while pricing and slack resources predict IT infrastructure, training and education predict technical IT resources.

According to the framework of resource orchestration [99], IT resource structuring is the process by which firms acquire, accumulate, and divest IT resources. Acquiring refers to purchasing IT resources from strategic factor markets. Purchasing database management application and hiring IT personnel are examples of acquiring IT resources. The price paid for acquiring IT resources affects the cost-benefit ratio of IT resources. Accumulating refers to the internal development of IT resources, such as internally developing IT applications and training employees how to use newly launched enterprise resource planning (ERP) software. Accumulating is necessary because the strategic factor market does not always provide a firm with all its required IT resources. Accumulating IT resources enhances their insulating mechanisms for sustaining BVIT creation. Divesting refers to the shedding of firm-controlled IT resources. Under the context of limited and shrinking IT investment, it is imperative that firms actively evaluate current IT resources and divest less-valued IT resources to generate the slack and flexibility needed to acquire or accumulate IT resources for higher value. Outsourcing IT functions partially or totally can be seen as an example of divesting IT resources. Divesting contributes to BVIT creation when it reduces the tangible or intangible cost of controlling specific IT resources.

Resource structuring is critical to BVIT creation because an IT resource portfolio establishes the upper bounds of a firm’s potential BVIT creation. Heterogeneity in IT resources results from choices made in acquiring, accumulating, and divesting of IT resources. At a certain point in time, the structure of a firm’s IT resources, including the aforementioned tangible, technical, managerial, and relational resources, is the outcome of the firm’s earlier resource structuring activities. Given that resource structuring is concerned with ongoing firm-level activities, it is difficult to measure in a cross-sectional manner. Alternatively, by evaluating a firm’s current IT resources we can gain a reasonably clear understanding about how the firm has structured its IT resources.

Many concepts have been utilized in the IS literature to describe IT-related capabilities. A systematic literature review of leading IS journals reveals the following four concepts. First, IT-related capability is defined as the firmwide ability to acquire, deploy, and leverage IT resources to help a firm achieve a competitive advantage and enhance firm performance [9, 93, 104]. Second, IT-related capability refers to the routines within the IT function to execute IS management and operational processes in a high-quality and sophisticated manner [69, 88, 89, 107]. Third, IT-related capability focuses mainly on the ability of the business function (e.g., new product development) to effectively use IT functionalities [8, 72]. Fourth, IT-related capability is viewed as the ability of IT affecting business processes and satisfying business requirements [38, 95].

Integrating the previous conceptualizations, IT capability in this paper is defined as a firm’s ability to mobilize and deploy IT resources effectively to perform strategic IT planning, develop IS, leverage and use IS, and manage IT function and IT assets. IT capability is not a simplistic one-dimensional concept; rather, it is a complex construct composed of several dimensions. Prior research highlights four important IT capabilities: (1) IT strategic planning capability, (2) IT development capability, (3) IT use capability, and (4) IT management capability. IT strategic planning capability reflects the sophistication of the IT planning process, such as considering the effects of IT on a firm’s business strategies, analyzing deliberately the opportunity of IT-based competitive advantage, selecting the appropriate IS planning methodology, aligning IS strategy with business strategy, and setting proper prioritization of the IT application portfolio [10, 88]. IS development capability pertains to the ability of developing and implementing IS that satisfy business needs in a cost-effective and controlled manner [108]. IS use capability refers to the ability to effectively use IT functionalities, such as understanding why IS are used, knowing what IT functionalities are available, and being familiar with how to use these IT functionalities [72]. IT management capability refers to the ability to manage IT-related issues such as IT asset and cost, IT staff development, and vendor relationships [10, 88]. These capabilities jointly determine a firm’s overall IT capabilities, and none of them should be overlooked. It is important to note that a firm’s IT capabilities are nontradable because they are home grown, highly customized, and impossible for a third party to develop without a deep understanding of specific attributes of the focal firm.

IT capabilities are conceptualized as a second-order formative construct based on Jarvis et al.’s [34] criteria. First, a firm’s different types of specific IT capabilities jointly shape its overall IT capabilities. Second, the four types of specific IT capabilities are distinct. For example, IT strategic planning capabilities and IT development capabilities are completely different and they cannot replace one another. Third, the four types of specific IT capabilities do not need to have covariation. For example, an increase in IT strategic planning capabilities does not necessarily mean that IT development capabilities will increase. Finally, the four types of specific IT capabilities have different antecedents and consequences. For example, CIO (chief information officer) leadership may affect IT strategic planning capabilities while the IT staff’s educational level may affect IT development capabilities.

Under the resource-structuring mechanism, IT resources can help firms gain competitive advantage and improve firm performance significantly when they enable and support the strategic-level organizational goal. Firms have different effectiveness in structuring IT resources that align with their competitive strategies and core competencies. For example, Srivardhana and Pawlowski [102] contend that the ERP system, as an IT resource, can enable business process innovation to create BVIT. In contrast, the capability-building mechanism indicates that firms have different capabilities in using IT resources to realize their strategic goals. Under this mechanism, IT capabilities, rather than IT resources, are the principal reason that firms gain superior performance [95] and sustained competitive advantage [59, 94]. For example, Sambamurthy et al. [93] assert IT capabilities play an important role in increasing agility, which is crucial for innovation and firm performance under turbulent environments.

The resource-structuring and capability-building mechanisms differ from each other in terms of the timing of value creation. The resource-structuring mechanism affects economic profit before the firm acquires, accumulates, and/or divests the selected resource. Similar to Makadok’s [56] arguments, the resource-structuring mechanism affects economic profit before the firm acquires or accumulates good resources whose price is lower than its marginal productivity or divests bad resources whose price is higher than their marginal productivity. In contrast, the capability-building mechanism affects economic profit only after the firm acquires resources whose productivity will be subsequently enhanced by the firm’s capabilities. Therefore, the two mechanisms have their impact at different phases of the IT investment process—while resource structuring influences the decision phase, capability building influences the implementation and deployment phases [56]. For example, the resource-structuring mechanism helps a small accounting firm create profit by acquiring or developing a low-price, lightweight open source spreadsheet software instead of the more complicated Excel; and the capability-building mechanism helps a firm create profit by appropriately reconfiguring and customizing an ERP system to meet its business requirements.

## Environmental Dynamism

Environmental dynamism refers to the rate and uncertainty of change in a firm’s external environment [26]. Firms in dynamic environments must be prepared to respond to unpredicted threats and opportunities to remain competitive. However, the instability of change in market demands, rivals’ competitive actions, and technology development leads to an information deficit that reduces the firm’s ability to manage resources to create value [98]. It is difficult to predict competitors’ actions, technological developments outside the industry, and business opportunities under conditions of high uncertainty. Therefore, in the dynamic environment, firms need to continuously integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external resources to address environmental changes [106]. Sometimes revolutionary changes are needed to achieve new business-IT alignment [110]. As Sirmon et al. articulate, environmental dynamism forces firms to “redesign capabilities and integrate them into new configurations as the firm’s competences rapidly lose their value because of changes in the market and customer needs” [98, p. 286]. Given the unpredictability of dynamic environments, the tasks carried out by firms to respond to environmental changes are knowledge intensive and require IT resources and capabilities to support efficient information flow and decision making [72].

Past research suggests that firms need different IT resources and capabilities in stable and dynamic environments. Sirmon et al. [98] contends that in stable environments firms can gain sustained advantages by developing a fit between their resources and the market, whereas in dynamic environments sustained advantages are difficult to keep and firms need to leverage more advanced capabilities to monitor the market, learn new knowledge, and engage in entrepreneurial actions to respond to the environmental shock more rapidly. Wade and Hulland [109] suggest that although firms can survive in stable environments by leveraging their existing resources and capabilities, as the environment becomes increasingly turbulent they need to emphasize dynamic efficiency by expanding resources and exploring new opportunities that require stronger IT capabilities. Pavlou and El Sawy [72] find that in more turbulent environments, firms’ IT leveraging competence has a stronger relationship with the firms’ dynamic capabilities, which in turn enhances the firms’ competitive advantage in new product development. In summary, prior research suggests that stable environments reward resource structuring focused on exploiting existing IT resources to achieve static efficiency, while dynamic environments favor capability building focused on using IT capabilities to dynamically respond to external changes.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

To un derstan d th e resource structurin g versus capability building mechanisms, we develop a research model (see Figure 1) to compare the effects of IT resources and IT capabilities under different environmental dynamism. Based on the IT value creation model [100], we propose that strategic-level IT effects mediate the effects of IT resources and capabilities on firm performance. Following prior research [90], strategic-level IT effects are depicted from two perspectives: IT support for competitive strategies and IT support for core competencies. We contend that IT resources and IT capabilities generate different levels of effects in stable versus dynamic environments, thus leading to different levels of firm performance.

In previous studies on BVIT, competitive strategy theory and RBV have been applied independently. These two theories provide different perspectives and can complement each other [71, 101]. Integrating these two perspectives, strategic-level IT effects can be conceptualized to two dimensions: IT support for competitive strategies, the extent to which IT supports the development and implementation of competitive strategies, and IT support for core competencies, the extent to which IT supports building and enhancing core competencies [90].

As Figure 1 shows, firm performance, IT support for competitive strategies, IT support for core competencies, IT capabilities, and IT resources are all second-order formative constructs consisting of several first-order reflective constructs. This can be justified using the four decision rules recommended by Jarvis et al. [34]: (1) the causal direction is from the first-order construct to the second-order construct, that is, the former determines the latter; (2) the first-order constructs are not interchangeable with each other; (3) the first-order constructs do not need to be highly correlated with each other; and (4) each first-order construct has its own antecedents and consequences. These justifications can be applied to all five second-order constructs and their subconstructs.

## Strategic-Level IT Effects

Originating from the classical industrial organization theory, competitive strategy theory posits that external factors, such as market structure, play a central role in determining competitive advantage and firm performance [61, 71, 81]. IT support for competitive strategy is defined as the extent to which IS are used to support the planning and implementation of competitive strategies. Porter [81] distinguished three generic competitive strategies: differentiation, low cost, and focus. Focus strategy is not a separate strategy, but describes the scope in which the firm should compete based on cost leadership or differentiation within focused market segment. Miller [64] noted that there are at least two types of differentiation competitive strategies: innovative differentiation strategy and market differentiation strategy. Therefore, we contend that IT support for competitive strategies have three dimensions: IT support for innovation differentiation strategy, IT support for market differentiation strategy, and IT support for low cost strategy [90]. The innovative differentiation dimension refers to the extent of IT use in improving the capability of new product development, shortening innovation cycle, and facilitating business process innovation. The market differentiation dimension refers to the extent to which IT is used to enrich marketing strategies, enhance the capability of sales, and identify unmet requirements. The low cost dimension refers to the extent to which IS are used in decreasing the cost of inventory, purchase, and customer service.

IT support for competitive strategy is conceptualized as a second-order formative construct based on Jarvis et al.’s [34] criteria. First, IT supports for the three different types of strategies jointly determine a firm’s overall IT support for strategies. Second, the three different types of strategies are completely different and their IT supports are also different. Third, IT supports for the three different types of strategies do not need to have covariation—for example, IT support for market differentiation strategy does not necessarily mean that IT will support the low-cost strategy. Finally, IT support for the three different types of strategies have different antecedents (e.g., IT functionalities) and consequences (e.g., strategic outcomes).

IT can enable and support the realization of a firm’s competitive strategy in many different ways. For example, IT can increase the entrance barrier of a specific market, enhance the bargaining power of suppliers and customers, supply the firm with new products and services, and even change the current competition rules [25, 37, 82, 103]. In the contemporary business environment, IT has become an indispensable element of operational and strategic management for most firms. It has been widely accepted that in order to achieve BVIT, IT must be used to enable and support firms competitive strategies [18, 87]. Prior research shows that the business-IT alignment can help firms realize competitive advantage and improve performance significantly [15, 32, 39, 92]. Following Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [88], firm performance is defined as a firm’s performance consisting of the operational dimension and the market dimension. Operational performance refers to a firm’s productivity, profitability, and financial indicators, and market performance is defined as the success of the firm in entering new market and in bringing new products or services to the market. The extant literature suggests that when IT support for competitive strategy is high, both operational and market performances are likely to improve. Therefore,

## Hypothesis 1: IT support for competitive strategies is positively associated with firm performance.

Core competency theory suggests that firms’ internal capabilities that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and nonsubstitutable are a potential source of sustainable competitive advantages [28, 30, 84]. IT support for core competencies is defined as the extent to which IS are used to enhance and develop a firm’s competencies.<sup>1</sup> Following

Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [88], IT support for core competencies has three dimensions: IT support for market-access competencies, IT support for integration competencies, and IT support for functionality-related competencies. IT support for market access competency refers to the extent to which IS are used in facilitating customer inquiries, analyzing customer information, and identifying potential customers. IT support for integration competency refers to the extent of IT use in reengineering business processes, improving process flexibility, and support supply chain integration. IT support for functionality-related competency refers to the extent to which IS increase the speed of critical business processes.

Similar to the rationale that IT resources, IT capabilities, and IT support for competitive strategy are conceptualized as second-order formative constructs, we propose that IT support for core competencies can also be treated as a second-order formative construct that includes the three dimensions.

IT support for core competencies can allow firms to maintain their market position and enhance firm performance. For example, Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [88] find that the variation of IT effects on firm performance is explained by the extent to which IT is used to support and enhance a firm’s core competencies. IT can enhance efficiency and effectiveness of these core competencies, extend their magnitude and scope, increase the cost and difficulty of imitation, and help firms realize their competitive strategies. One well-publicized case of IT support for core competency is that of Wal-Mart synchronizing its inventory management function and IT to create one new IT-enabled core competency call cross-docking Logistics Systems [14, 56, 58]. The IT-enabled competency helps Wal-Mart simplify its purchasing procedure, increase its operation efficiency, reduce its merchandise inventory and stock cost, support its low cost strategy, and enhance its operation and market performance. A distinctive core competence enables the firm to provide more value to customers than its competitors, leading to a competitive advantage [98]. Thus, if IT facilitates the development of core competencies in a firm, it will naturally support the realization of the firm’s competitive strategies and enhance the firm’s performance:

Hypothesis 2a: IT support for core competencies is positively associated with firm performance.

Hypothesis 2b: IT support for core competencies is positively associated with IT support for competitive strategies.

## Information Technology Capabilities

IT capabilities are crucial in creating IT effects. IT capabilities can be understood from two aspects. First, IT capabilities are firmwide organizational capabilities. Most existing definitions limit IT-related capability in the IT or business function, these definitions implicitly posit that the IT or business function should undertake the whole responsibility of BVIT creation. In fact, BVIT creation depends on the participation of all functions and all stakeholders [75, 76, 78]. Second, IT capabilities are embedded in a firm’s IT value creation process by enabling it to be effective in selecting, combining, integrating, deploying, and leveraging IT resources in combination or to cooperate with other organization resources and capabilities [9, 109]. From the perspective of the IS life cycle, IT capabilities cover the whole life cycle of IS, including system planning, analysis, designing, developing, and maintenance and updating [88].

IT capabilities play an important role in enabling IT to provide support for competitive strategies and core competencies. First, IT capabilities can help firms implement the existing competitive strategies or create new competitive strategies [36, 82, 90], and enhance the present core competencies or cultivate new core competencies [13, 88, 90]. Organizations that have strong IT capabilities can select and prioritize IS portfolios, ensure appropriate IT investment patterns, and align IT strategies with business strategies. For example, Chan and Reich [15] suggest that IT capabilities are a critical success factor of IT alignment. Second, IT capabilities can help firms develop or implement the “right” IS to satisfy idiosyncratic business requirements. IT capabilities are particularly important in dynamic environments where organization and technology changes occur quickly. Firms with strong IT capabilities can respond to these changes in a timely manner to avoid IT project failures [45]. Third, in firms with strong IT capabilities, business personnel are trained to understand the IT applications available to them and know how to assimilate IT into their job routines effectively [49]. Appropriate IT use will generate positive effects on enhancing core competencies and implementing the competitive strategies of the firm [100, 109]. Pavlou and El Sawy [72] indicate that the capability of effectively using IT functionalities in new product development can help to build a competitive advantage. Finally, firms with strong IT capabilities manage their IT assets effectively and keep the steady operation of their IT function, which ensures continuous support to their competitive strategies and core competencies [23, 65, 113]. ## Information Technology Resources

IT resources influence how IT supports core competencies and competitive strategies in many ways. First, IT infrastructure is the foundation for IT support for competitive strategies and core competencies. Firms with a flexible IT infrastructure platform can reconfigure their physical IT components to develop new IS in a cost-effective manner [97] and acquire digital options that could be exercised to create new channels for accessing customers, building seamless integration with supply chain partners, improving efficiencies of operations, and offering new digital products or services [93, 112]. Second, technical and managerial IT professionals can solve problems in the process of IT development and IT use to create business effects. Mata et al. [59] find that managerial IT skills contribute to sustained competitive advantages. Third, the relational IT resources can deepen mutual understanding and trust between the IT function and business functions by strengthening the relationship between CIO and other top managers [24] and between IT personnel and business employees [8, 91]. The strengthened relationships can promote information sharing [46, 60] and facilitate alignment between IT strategy and business strategy [15, 40, 54, 92]. The relational IT resources also include positive relationships between the IT function and external players such as software vendors and consultants, which promote knowledge transfer and internalization from external entities to the focal firm [42, 104, 116].

Hypothesis 4a: IT resources are positively associated with IT support for competitive strategies.

Hypothesis 4b: IT resources are positively associated with IT support for core competencies.

IT capabilities are the routines embedded in the processes of development, implementation, and use of IT resources. IT capabilities emerge in the processes of people applying their business and IT knowledge, integrating their knowledge, and interacting with others and coordinating their actions. In other words, IT capabilities depend on people performing their roles in specified organizational structures and processes [74]. IT capability development is essentially the process of organizational learning [4, 67], which transforms widely available IT resources into unique IT capabilities that are embedded in the organization processes. Therefore, IT capabilities cannot independently exist, and they are to a large extent based on the firm’s existing IT resources. More IT resources allow firms to undertake more learning, acquire more experiences, and finally develop stronger IT capabilities:

Hypothesis 4c: IT resources are positively associated with IT capabilities.

## Environmental Dynamism

Environmental dynamism can possibly moderate the influences of IT resources on competitive strategies and core competencies so that IT resources are less influential in dynamic environments. In stable environments, firms can enhance their core competences and competitiveness by adopting a new IT. Yet competitive advantages based on adoption of commodity-like IT resources are unlikely to be sustained in dynamic environments. First, rivals can mimic the strategy by adopting the same or a more advanced IT. Because of inevitable knowledge spillover and information leakage, the secrets of IT innovation can hardly be kept, and it is easy for rivals to imitate at a lower cost or in a more creative manner [21, 74]. Mimetic pressures from the institutional environment also force a firm to imitate others’ IT innovations to maintain legitimacy [49]. In addition, since technology update is fast in dynamic environments, rivals can easily adopt a more advanced IT to dwarf the focal firm’s strategic advantage. Second, IT resources by themselves are rigid in nature. IT resources must be compatible with the focal firm and integrated with other organizational resources to realize synergy that improves competences and competitiveness [68]; otherwise failures will occur [48, 50, 115]. Whereas the compatibility can be maintained for a long time in stable environments, when the environment is turbulent, firms’ personal roles, business processes, and structures need to be frequently adjusted to adapt to environmental changes, and it is difficult for preconfigured IT resources to stay compatible with such adjustments [74, 85]. As a result, IT resources intended to support the old strategies and competencies are unlikely to meet the requirements of new strategies and competencies [105] because environmental dynamism increases the difficulty of mastering the deployment and use of IT resources and routinizing efficient work practices for firms [4]. Hence, environmental dynamism makes it harder to attain the value of existing IT resources:

Hypothesis 5a: The relationship between IT resources and IT support for competitive strategies is negatively moderated by environmental dynamism so that the relationship is weaker in dynamic environments than in stable environments.

Hypothesis 5b: The relationship between IT resources and IT support for core competencies is negatively moderated by environmental dynamism so that the relationship is weaker in dynamic environments than in stable environments.

BVIT emerges mainly from the complementarity and integration between IT and business [93]. In order to realize BVIT, it is necessary for firms to dynamically manipulate their IS and business activities by applying their IT capabilities. IT capabilities, by definition, are flexible, enabling IT capabilities enable firms to respond to changes outside and within the firm [20]. The flexibility of IT capabilities can be viewed as strategic options [93] that give a firm the choice to exploit existing opportunities or explore new directions. In stable environments where firms’ external conditions remain largely unchanged over time, it is unnecessary for firms to cope with changes. Therefore, IT resources suffice to achieve business process efficiency without much reconfiguration, customization, and updating, and IT capabilities have few opportunities to exhibit their effects in terms of supporting core competencies and competitive strategies. In contrast, as the environment gets more dynamic, IT resources can quickly become obsolete or incompatible with new business requirements and no longer create business values. IT capabilities are needed in this situation to reconfigure existing IT resources, acquire new IT resources, or realign business-IT strategies so that the focal firm is agile enough to survive external changes. During this process, the firm’s core competencies and competitive strategies are strengthened with a better fit between IT and business. Pavlou and El Sawy [72, 73] show that the strategic effect of IT capability is more pronounced in higher levels of environmental turbulence. Hence, IT capabilities generate more support to firm’s core competences and competitive strategies in dynamic environments than in stable environments:

Hypothesis 5c: The relationship between IT capabilities and IT support for competitive strategies is positively moderated by environmental dynamism so that the relationship is stronger in dynamic environments than in stable environments.

Hypothesis 5d: The relationship between IT capabilities and IT support for core competencies is positively moderated by environmental dynamism so that the relationship is stronger in dynamic environments than in stable environments.

## Control Variables

To account for differences among firms, two control variables are included in our research model: firm size and industry type. They are selected because of their potential effects on BVIT as suggested by previous research.

Firm size reflects a firm’s past success and may affect current performance [86, 88]. Firm size is viewed as an indicator of leadership in the interfirm relationship, power of pricing and bargaining, and the pervasiveness of operation and management routines, all of which can influence firm performance. In other words, large firms with abundant IT resources and IT capabilities can easily create BVIT.

The potential payoff from deploying and using IT might vary across industries, which is reflected by the role of IT in the industry [104]. For example, prior studies have shown that IT resources and IT capabilities exhibit significant differences between manufacturing and financial industries [63]. Because a cross-industry sample is used in our study, it is necessary to control for the effects of industry on firm performance.

## Methodology

## Construct Operationalization

A survey w as con ducted in Ch in a to test our research model . A questionnaire was developed and pilot tested before the formal data collection. Whenever possible, measurement items were adapted from existing scales in the literature. Some modifications were made to align the scales with the Chinese context. The standard scale develop ment process [33] was followed to create the new measures. An expert panel of three IS professors and five CIOs and senior business managers examined the face validity of the measurement items. All the measures are provided in Appendix A.

## Firm Performance

Firm performance is a second-order formative construct consisting of two first-order reflective constructs: operational performance and market performance [14, 88]. The measurement of each first-order construct was adapted from Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [88] and includes four items. Each item pertains to the extent to which the focal firm exceeded its main competitors in a specific area in the past three years. We expected most of the firms in the sample to be privately held or state owned. It is difficult, based on our past experience, to collect objective accounting data on firms performance. Therefore, we used subjective, self-reported measures of performance. Subjective measures of firm performance have been found to be highly correlated with objective measures and used by many prior IS and strategic management studies (e.g., [14, 88]). Three-year average estimates are likely to minimize the influence of short-term performance variations, but do not require that the respondents have long tenures in the firm. Moreover, the explicit performance comparisons to rivals provide a form of control for differences in performance resulting from industry and strategic group effects [26], which serves this study well since our sample includes firms from more than 10 industries.

## IT Support for Competitive Strategies

IT support for competitive strategies is a second-order formative construct consisting of three first-order reflective constructs: (1) IT support for innovative differentiation strategy, (2) IT support for market differentiation strategy, and (3) IT support for low cost strategy, whose measures were adapted from Rivard et al. [90]. Each first-order construct is measured by three reflective items.

## IT Support for Core Competencies

IT support for core competencies is a second-order formative construct consisting of three first-order reflective constructs: (1) IT support for market-access competencies, (2) IT support for integration competencies, and (3) IT support for functionality-related competencies, whose measures were adapted from Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [88]. Each first-order construct is measured by four reflective items.

## IT Capabilities

IT capabilities<sup>2</sup> are measured as a second-order formative construct that consists of four first-order reflective constructs: (1) IT strategic planning capability [10, 88], (2) IT development capability [108], (3) IT use capability [72], and (4) IT management capability [10, 88]. Each first-order construct is measured by five reflective items.

## IT Resources

We developed a new scale to comprehensively measure IT resources. IT resources comprise four first-order reflective constructs: (1) physical IT infrastructure [10, 97], (2) technical IT resource [21], (3) managerial IT resource [88], and (4) relational IT resource [10, 88]. Each first-order construct is measured by four reflective items.

## Environmental Dynamism

Environmental dynamism is measured using Newkirk and Lederer’s [70] scale that captures the speed of change in products/services and technologies and the difficulty in predicting rival actions and customer requirements.

## Data Collection

Data were collected in China between September 2008 and February 2009. A pilot test was conducted before the formal data collection. In the pilot test, we collaborated with a provincial government agency in charge of local science and technology development. The agency implemented a program to improve firms’ competitiveness through IT investments from 2002 to 2008. The agency released the questionnaire on its Web site and requested CIOs or IS executives of the firms funded by the program to fill out the questionnaire. A total of 55 usable responses were obtained after one month. These 55 responses were used for instrument validation and refinement. Factor loading and Cronbach’s alpha were calculated. Six items were dropped because their factor loadings were lower than 0.5. The Cronbach’s alpha value of all the first-order constructs were greater than 0.70.

For the formal data collection, a sample was drawn from the MBA alumni of business schools at two major Chinese universities. We requested each alumnus to bring the questionnaire to the CIO or IS executive in his or her firm. A total of 1,065 questionnaires were distributed. After three rounds of follow-up reminders, 323 questionnaires were returned. Eleven questionnaires were dropped because they were incomplete or all of the items had the same rating. We used two inclusion criteria to select eligible firms for this study: (1) the organization age should be over three years, and (2) the firm should have implemented at least three information systems. Based on the criteria, 16 more questionnaires were dropped. Finally, 296 questionnaires were kept for data analysis. The effective response rate is 27.79 percent.

The responding firms are located in 4 municipalities, 2 special administrative regions, and 15 provinces, indicating a wide range of geographical and cultural diversity. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the responding firms. As shown in Table 2, the respondents are mostly senior executives or middle-level managers in IT or business functions, and they are considered to be sufficiently knowledgeable about their firms to provide valid answers to the survey questions. Because there are two types of respondents—IT managers and non-IT managers—their abilities to respond to the survey questions of them may differ. t-tests were used to examine the statistical differences of all the measure items. The results indicated that no items have significant differences between IT managers and non-IT managers (p > 0.05).

We assessed nonresponse bias by following Armstrong and Overton [5]. t-tests were used to compare the earliest 74 respondents (25 percent) and the last 74 respondents (25 percent) on two context variables: revenue and number of employees. The results indicated no significant differences between the two groups in revenue (t = –0.925, $p = 0 . 3 3 4 )$ and number of employees (t = –1.445, p = 0.153).

Unlike reflective indicators, high multicollinearity in formative constructs can destabilize the model [22, 77]. The variance inflation factor (VIF) statistic was used to determine if the formative indicators were reliable. The VIF values for all of the formative indicators range from 1.52 to 3.45, which only slightly exceed the recommended 3.3 [77]. In addition, following Loch et al. [52], discriminant validity of the formative constructs was examined. The interitem and item-to-construct matrix (see Appendix D) was developed. In the matrix, each formative construct was represented by a construct score that was the sum of all of its formative items multiplied by their individual PLS weights. To show discriminant validity, the correlation between an item and its construct should be higher than its correlation with other constructs, and the correlation between an item and another item that belongs to the same construct should be higher than its correlation with any other item that belongs to a different construct [52]. The results suggested that all the items met this criterion and provide evidence for discriminant validity of the formative measures.

## Common Method Bias

Since the data were self-reported, common method variance (CMV) is a potential concern [79]. Following Podsakoff et al. [80], we applied procedural remedies, including protecting respondent anonymity, counterbalancing some questions’ order, and retesting items to reduce CMV. In addition, two statistical methods were used to assess the severity of CMV. First, a Harman’s single-factor test on all of the reflective constructs indicated that a single dominant factor was not present and the largest factor accounted for only 27.446 percent of total variance. Second, we performed a partial correlation test using a marker variable to partial out the influence of CMV. Following Lindell and Whitney [51], the second smallest positive correlation among measurement items (r = 0.02) was used as a reasonable proxy for CMV. After controlling for CMV, the adjusted correlations between the principal constructs were slightly lower than the unadjusted correlations and their significance levels did not change, suggesting that CMV did not spuriously inflate the construct relationships [51]. The variance contributed by the marker variable is only 0.01. Therefore, CMV does not seem to be a serious concern for this study. This is consistent with a study [58] showing that CMV does not significantly affect IS studies based on single-source survey data.

## Theoretical Implications

The BVIT is well discussed in the IS literature [43, 62]. What is vaguely understood is the underlying mechanism of BVIT creation and how environmental dynamism influences the choice of BVIT mechanisms. This study makes three important theoretical contributions. First, based on resource orchestration research [98, 99], we propose resource structuring and capability building as two BVIT creation mechanisms. Although the notion of resource orchestration has great appeal to IT management, it has not received much attention from IS researchers. Our research is one of the first attempts to apply resource orchestration in studying BVIT creation. In addition, our conceptualization extends Makadok’s [56] work by replacing resource picking with resource structuring. This extension makes more senses in the IT context because IT resources not only need to be picked/acquired but also need to be accumulated (developed in-house) and divested because of interfacing issues between different systems and quick obsolescence of IT products. Our conceptualization of resource structuring and capability building helps to draw attention to the differential effects of IT resources and IT capabilities. While prior research often treats IT capabilities as a special type of IT resources, we submit that they differ from each other. It is important to set them apart conceptually because they can create BVIT via different mechanisms.

Without differentiating between them, our understanding of BVIT creation will be unnecessarily limited.

Second, we theoretically hypothesize and empirically demonstrate that the effect of IT resources and IT capabilities on BVIT creation is a function of the level of environmental dynamism. This study suggests that while resource structuring is the primary mechanism to BVIT creation in stable environments, capability building is the primary mechanism to BVIT creation in dynamic environments. This finding has important implications for IS research because it offers a deeper understanding of BVIT creation and could reconcile inconsistent findings related to IT effects. For example, based on pooled data, IT resources have no significant effect on IT support for core competencies (Figure 2); however, the multigroup analysis shows that the impact is significant in dynamic environments. Without considering the moderating effect of environmental dynamism, a biased conclusion is likely to be drawn.

Third, although this research is focused on the differences between IT resources and IT capabilities, our findings indicate that IT resources are significantly associated with IT capabilities in both stable and dynamic environments. This suggests that even in dynamic environments where IT resources are not found to be significant, IT resources can still influence firm performance through the mediation of IT capabilities. However, it should be noted that in dynamic environments only having IT resources is not enough, and IT capabilities have to be developed to allow IT resources to contribute to BVIT creation. This research also contributes to IT investment research [114] by clearly delineating the relative importance of IT resources and IT capabilities in different environments, which helps to identify the most critical area that needs investments.

## Managerial Implications

For management and IT practitioners who are concerned about the strategic value of their IT investments, our study presents several intriguing insights. First, the mediating role of strategic-level IT effects highlights that firms should do more than structuring appropriate IT resource portfolio or build strong IT capabilities. They have to clearly understand the effects of IT resources and IT capabilities on firm’s competitive strategies and core competencies. The CIO and other executives should work together and pay more attention to building mechanisms to ensure that IT resources and IT capabilities are channeled toward supporting those critical elements. Second, the moderating role of environmental dynamism reveals that the underlying mechanism of BVIT creation differs in stable and dynamic environments. Firms in stable environments should focus on structuring the right IT resource portfolio that has potential strategic values. Although IT capabilities are also positively associated with IT support for core competencies in a stable environment, resource structuring should be prioritized because capability building takes more time and investments. In contrast, firms in dynamic environments should pay more attention to cultivating IT capabilities because simply structuring the right IT resources will not contribute to increasing firm performance. Third, our findings that IT capabilities are highly dependent on IT resources suggest that they must be synchronized, and firms should acquire, accumulate, and divest (when necessary) sophisticated IT infrastructure and technical, managerial, and relational IT resources.

Given that the global business environment is becoming more turbulent, firms should pay great attention to building their own IT capabilities to enhance their ability to quickly respond the external emergencies. This paper suggests that a two-step approach is advisable for firms to realize BVIT. The first step is to structure an appropriate IT resources portfolio comprising strong IT infrastructure, competent technological and managerial IT skills, and harmonious relations between IT and business functions. The second step is to build IT capabilities that enable the firm to deploy IT resources effectively and efficiently so that these resources can support firm-level competencies and strategies in the process of generating economic rents. Our findings demonstrate that regardless of environmental dynamism, IT resources are needed for firms to create BVIT because they can either directly provide support for the firm’s competitive strategies and core competencies or do so indirectly by enhancing the firm’s IT capabilities.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

While we strived to increase the rigor of this study, limitations are inevitable. First, the data were collected from the same respondents for both the independent and dependent variables. Although CMV is not found to be a major problem, the existing statistical methods have been criticized for not being able to detect CMV. Hence, our data might still be biased by CMV. For example, as Appendix D shows, some of the correlations between first-order constructs are above 0.70, and some of the correlations between first-order and second-order constructs are above 0.90. These unusually high correlations might be related to CMV. Multiple measurement methods and data sources should be used to control potential common method bias in future studies [12].

Second, our research model is tested using cross-sectional data. Since the data represent a snapshot in time, it is difficult to infer causal relationships between constructs and address the issues of how BVIT is created over time. Even though causal relationships between the constructs in our research model are argued based on theory, longitudinal studies should be done to offer stronger empirical evidence for the relationships.

Third, CFA models for the subconstructs show only moderate fit. Although we carefully developed the measurement items, the CFA results indicate that the items could be further purified and improved. One potential problem is that there are too many questions in the questionnaire, and the respondents might get confused by related questions during the process of filling out the questionnaire. Future research should find a way to reduce the amount of questions without losing the validity of the measurements.

Fourth, the overall IT capabilities are proposed to provide support for competitive strategies and core competencies in dynamic environments, and the empirical results also verified the effects. However, various IT capabilities have different effects and importance [1, 57, 104], and there is no one universal IT capability profile that fits all firms. When a firm wants to build IT capabilities, the first question is how to identify the most critical IT capabilities. Future studies should investigate the effects of different IT capability configurations on business strategies and firm performance.

Finally, we employed PLS to test the research model instead using a covariancebased structural equation modeling tool. The benefit of using PLS is that it can accommodate formative measures, whereas the drawback of using PLS is that there are no fit indexes for PLS analyses. Therefore, we cannot assess the overall fit between our data and the research model.

## Conclusions

Draw in g on th e RBV , core competency theory, competitive strategy theory, and extant literature on IT capabilities and BVIT, this study formulates and tests the relationships between IT resources, IT capabilities, strategic-level IT effects, and firm performance under different environmental dynamism. We attempt to investigate the mediating role of strategic-level IT effects and moderating role of environmental dynamism on the effects of IT resources and IT capabilities on firm performance. Survey data based on 296 Chinese firms largely supports the hypothesized relationships in our research model. This research contributes to the BVIT literature by considering the much neglected environmental dynamism, decomposing strategic-level IT effects based on an integrative view of competitive strategy theory and core competency theory, and extending and enriching the extent literature on BVIT. The moderating role of environment dynamism suggests that under stable environments IT resources are the dominant determinant of firm performance, while under dynamic environments the role of IT capabilities becomes dominant. The mediating role of strategic-level IT effects between IT resources and capabilities and firm performance indicates that firms cannot generate BVIT by simply relying on IT resources and firms’ IT capabilities; instead, they need to focus on the effective use of IT to support competitive strategies and core competencies.

## Notes

1. Although capabilities and competencies are often used interchangeably because of their similarities [47, 72, 73, 96], they have conceptual differences. First, competencies have a broader scope than capabilities. Organizational competencies include all firm-specific assets, knowledge, skills, whereas capabilities are embedded in the firm’s structure, technology, processes, and interpersonal relationships [44]. Second, competencies have a higher hierarchy than capabilities [35]. A capability resides in a particular function, whereas a competency refers to a cross-function integration and coordination of capabilities. Finally, capabilities and competencies have different relationships with a firm’s strategy. Capabilities are not always linked to a firm’s strategy, whereas competencies are defined as being closely related to the development, selection, and implementation of a firm’s strategy [19, 44].

2. Note that we focused on IT capabilities in this paper and did not measure comprehensive dynamic capabilities. It deserves further research to contrast the effects of IT resources and dynamic capabilities.
