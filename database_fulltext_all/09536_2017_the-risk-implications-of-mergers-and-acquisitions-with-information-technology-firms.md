# The Risk Implications of Mergers and Acquisitions with Information Technology Firms

YOUNG BONG CHANG AND WOOJE CHO

YOUNG BONG CHANG (ybchang01@skku.edu) is an associate professor at Sungkyunkwan University in South Korea. He received his Ph.D. in management from the University of California at Irvine. His research domain is information technology (IT) economics, with a focus on the analysis of IT startups/investments/valuations and IT-induced competition. He has published in Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and others.

WOOJE CHO (wjcho@uos.ac.kr; corresponding author) is an associate professor at the University of Seoul, South Korea. He earned his Ph.D. at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. Prior to his doctoral studies he was a software engineer at Samsung SDS. His research interests lie in the strategy of IT firms, IT strategy, information security, and software quality.

ABSTRACT: We address the dynamics of post-merger risks for a firm acquiring an information technology (IT) company over a long-term horizon, and examine the impact of mergers and acquisitions (M&A) motives on its post-merger risks after controlling for its self-selection into the specific motives the acquiring firm seeks. We find that a strong run-up in risk occurs before M&A transactions are initiated, but this risk begins to decline over the post-merger period. However, post-merger risks tend to persist for firms seeking M&A transactions with a customer-side motive, whereas this does not occur with a production-side motive. While greater post-merger risks are associated with M&As with a customer-side motive, our results suggest that its association with postmerger risks is moderated by industry dynamism. Overall, our study sheds new light on the post-merger risk by addressing its dynamic nature and uncovering the potential interplay between M&A motives and industry dynamism when IT firms are acquired.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information technology firms, mergers and acquisitions, post-merger risks, return volatility.

In recent decades, an increasing number of firms have undertaken mergers and acquisitions (M&As) as a prominent corporate strategy. While the expansion rate of M&A markets has slowed in recent years due to economic downturns, we have observed that a substantial portion of M&A deals are associated with the acquisition of information technology (IT) firms [39, 76]. For instance, Charles Schwab acquired CyberCorp, an electronics trading technology and brokerage firm to enhance its Internet platform [52]. This enables Charles Schwab to achieve efficiency gains in products/services with more advanced online services by acquiring CyberCorp’s IT assets such as electronic communication networks (ECNs). Google captured a significant portion of the video-streaming market by acquiring YouTube. com at \$1.65 billion. With augmented customer pools to its existing customers from YouTube.com, Google is able to provide more consolidated and integrative services. While M&As are considered a powerful instrument for value creation, they do not come without costs. In particular, when IT firms are acquired as a target, an acquiring firm may encounter difficulties in precisely assessing M&A benefits due to unique attributes embedded in IT firms. For instance, IT firms exhibit unique attributes with respect to intangible assets such as customer databases, practices for business analytics, and so on [69, 77]. Moreover, we expect that the benefits accruing from the target firm’s IT assets are not immediate because it takes time for a firm to fully assimilate IT into its business [14, 43]. This in turn escalates the volatilities in value accruing to acquiring firms in post-merger stages. In our context, an increase in return volatilities reflects an increase in the risks associated with M&As [35, 50, 58].<sup>1</sup>

Given the economic significance of M&As as a major corporate event, a rich body of literature has examined why firms pursue M&As and reported their consequences in the forms of increased efficiencies in scale and scope, market power and new product offerings [41, 47]. However, most studies have estimated their value to acquiring firms as negative or, at best, zero. In an IT context, Tanriverdi and Uysal [93] also show that the stock market reacts negatively to M&A announcements when an acquiring firm is equipped with a higher level of IT capability than its target.

Although these studies have reported the important implications of the motives and outcomes of M&As, they have mainly examined the short-term reaction to M&A announcements in stock markets with less attention being paid to IT attributes associated with specified motives. We address this by examining the acquisition of IT firms with production- and customer-side motives through which unique attributes embedded in IT firms are likely to be captured. In our context, an acquiring firm, with a production-side motive, may not be able to fully exploit acquired assets for operational efficiency due to the heterogeneities in technical architecture and infrastructure for operations. In addition, a number of firms consider the acquisition of IT firms as a means to leverage customer information by integrating customer pools with those of targets [96]. That is, a firm may acquire IT firms with a customer-side motive to reinforce its influence with an enlarged network size given that IT firms are likely to be equipped with assets associated with intangible and network-dominant attributes [9, 28]. However, it is nontrivial to assess how valuable a target firm’s customer information is before an acquiring firm integrates and uses expanded customer pools for market penetration, cross-selling, and so on.

This results in an increase in risks for an acquiring firm in post-merger periods. Given that M&As are sought with different motives, it is important to examine the heterogeneities in post-merger risks in relation to M&A motives (i.e., the customer side, the production side, or both). Moreover, little attention has been paid to possible shifts in post-merger risks attributable to unique aspects of IT over a long-term horizon. Accordingly, our understanding of risk dynamics following the acquisition of IT firms and the conditions under which escalated post-merger risk can be alleviated are still very limited.

The primary objective of our study is to examine the extent to which post-merger risks persist over periods with respect to M&A motives. Then we uncover and examine the moderating role of industry dynamism in the relationship between the M&A motives and an acquirer’s post-merger risks.<sup>2</sup> Overall, we find that a strong run-up in risks occurs before M&As are initiated but that the risks begin to decline over time. Moreover, our results suggest that the risk for an acquiring firm with a customer-side motive is larger and decays slowly compared to the risk with a production-side motive, possibly owing to the higher level of uncertainty embedded in an M&A with a customer-side motive. While greater post M&A risks are associated with customer-side motives than production-side motives in more dynamic environments, we further show that, an increase in post-merger risks is alleviated more effectively when a customer-side motive is sought than when a production-side one is.

Our study builds on earlier research but makes new contributions to the information systems literature. Consistent with prior studies, we show that an M&A is considered a risky endeavor. However, our study enriches and extends the scope of the literature by examining the varying nature of risks related to the acquisitions of IT firms with specified motives. For instance, Tafti [90] addressed the risk aspects of M&As with a focus on integration difficulty for a short time window by examining the U.S. commercial banking industry. While providing important implications regarding the role of IT in an M&A context, unlike our study, the focus of his study is on the examination of an acquiring firm’s IT capability in assessing M&A values. Bharath and Wu [10] examined M&A risks in a long-term horizon, but they did not examine whether post-merger risks could be short- or long-term depending on an acquiring firm’s motive for M&As. Accordingly, the role of the M&A motive in the risk dynamics in their study is unclear. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to address the risk dynamics of firms that acquire IT firms and its implications with a focus on M&A motives. In particular, we uncover the divergence in post-merger risks when M&As are sought with different motives possibly due to the unique aspects of IT, such as intangible, knowledge-like, and network-dominant attributes, acquired from target firms. Moreover, our results provide evidence about the role of industry dynamism in moderating the association between M&A motives and post-merger risks. In sum, we are able to develop rich insights into the dynamics of post-merger risks over a long-term horizon and uncover the potential interplay between appropriately specified M&A motives and industry dynamism.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## Acquisition of IT Firms with Customer- and Production-side Motives

Firms have several motives for pursuing M&As, such as empire building, managerial hubris, market power, and synergy gains [31, 94, 95, 97]. While there are multiple views in the M&A literature, given the scope of our study, we focus on the acquisition of IT firms with a customer- and a production-side motive and its risk implications.

With our focus in mind, note that a firm’s motive for M&As can be categorized by the nature of business activities in a firm’s value chain: production- and customerside activities [12, 72, 74, 79, 86, 98]. In this framework, production-side activities refer to business activities involved in producing products or operating services. Similarly, customer-side activities refer to business activities involved in capturing value from customers. These include marketing, sales, customer service, and customer relationship management.

In our context, a firm may pursue M&As with IT firms to improve its production efficiency by acquiring production-related assets from target firms. In such a case, the acquisition of IT firms allows acquiring firms to pool the target’s IT resources and capabilities in their operations and production. As a result, an acquiring firm can achieve gains in operational efficiencies when a target’s resources are shared or duplicate resources are eliminated [22, 38, 88]. For example, the acquisition of Storwize allows IBM to provide more comprehensive storage services in a fast and efficient manner. Beyond the production-side motive, a firm may acquire IT firms with the customer-side motive such as a customer-base expansion, market penetration, entry into new markets, and a new channel to customers [7, 57, 75]. In particular, thanks to proficiency in the analysis of customer-side activities with accumulated data, IT firms become a more attractive target when M&As are sought with the customer-side motive [92]. For instance, the acquisition of Internet-based firms can provide unique benefits to an acquiring firm since these firms are wellpositioned to collect and analyze customer information, such as their financial profiles and purchasing behaviors, by using clickstream data [54]. With customer information obtained from its targets, an acquiring firm can increase its efficiency in advertising and promotion with a higher retention rate while attracting new customers at a lower cost [19, 53].

In sum, there has been much interest in identifying M&A motives and evaluating its performances. However, limited attention has been paid to the heterogeneous nature of M&A motives on the customer side and production side [1]. As a result, the risk associated with M&A motives [91] is not well-understood. Given the unique attributes of IT especially in the domain associated with the customer-side motive, it is worthwhile to examine the risk profile of acquiring firms when IT firms are acquired. We fill the gap in the literature by examining the dynamics of post-merger risk and its underlying sources with a focus on the M&A motives when IT firms are acquired.

## The Effect of M&A Motives on an Acquiring Firm’s Risk

M&As increase an acquiring firm’s exposure to risk, possibly because of the high degree of irreversibility and uncertainty embedded in M&A deals [70]. Not surprisingly, several studies have shown that a large portion of M&As dilute the shareholder value within the first year of the announcement, and furthermore, the negative performance persists over longer periods [51, 84].

While there are multiple pathways to the risk associated with the acquisition of IT firms, we argue that firms are likely to be exposed to a greater risk when they engage in M&As with the customer-side motive rather than the production-side motive. First, we posit that it is less certain for an acquiring firm to achieve expected benefits when the customer-side, rather than the production-side, motive is sought because the acquiring firm may not have direct control over the acquired resources such as a target’s customer pool. For instance, while Rupert Murdoch’s News Corp. acquired Myspace in 2005 with the hope of capturing Myspace’s 300 million registered users, most users switched to Facebook afterward [82]. On the other hand, when an acquiring firm’s M&A motive is focused on the production side, the firm may be able to maneuver acquired resources from a target into its operation with less uncertainty. This may be the case because the operations that would use acquired assets are likely to be under its internal control. After the acquisition of Perot Systems, for instance, Dell was successfully able to internalize the target’s resources such as production lines and expertise in computer technology services.<sup>3</sup> Given the direct control over a target’s resources deployed for operational gains, we posit that it is relatively easier for the acquiring firm to achieve its production-side objectives compared with an acquisition based on a customer-side motive.

Second, resources acquired through an acquisition with a production-side motive are likely to be tangible ones, whereas those acquired with a customer-side motive are more intangible, such as the target’s customer base and brand. In those cases, a bidding firm may encounter less difficulty in assessing the value of a target’s resources when its primary benefits accrue from the production side rather than the customer side. This is possibly because in the process of due diligence, it is relatively easier for a bidding firm to investigate the extent of commonality and overlap in resources for production processes, such as the property, plant, and equipment, with a target firm [36].<sup>4</sup> For instance, after conducting a thorough due diligence for the acquisition of Compaq, Hewlett-Packard (HP) was confident that it could save \$1.5 billion by eliminating redundant assets from the production side with less uncertainty.<sup>5</sup> However, it was less certain for HP to achieve expected gains accruing from the customer side given its intangible attributes, although HP stressed that the acquisition was intended to increase consumers’ value with a more wideranging purveyor of computers, software, and services [29]. Moreover, given the intangible nature of benefits primarily accrued to an acquiring firm with the customer-side motive, information asymmetry is likely to occur between the two parties regarding the real value accrued from M&As [8]. As a result, it may be less certain whether or not the acquiring firm achieves ex ante expected benefits when M&As are sought with a customer-side motive.

Third, the presence of network effects is likely to provide substantial advantages for a firm with a customer-side motive that attempts to obtain a large installed customer base through acquisitions. This is possibly because the benefits accrued from merged customers may become larger than the sum of benefits from the two separate customer pools when network effects come into play. While attractive for expanding customer pools through an acquisition [3, 85, 96], the presence of network effects makes it more difficult for a bidding firm to estimate ex ante how much additional value could be created by simply combining its customers with acquired customers from a target [42]. That is, it is more difficult to assess the value from M&As with a customer-side motive than those with a production-side motive when network effects dominate. Taken together, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: Compared with M&A deals with a focus on the production-side motive, those with a customer-side motive will be associated with greater risk for acquiring firms.

## The Effect of M&A Motives Under Industry Dynamism on an Acquiring Firm’s Risk

Although the acquisitions of IT firms are considered risky practices, the degree to which post-merger risks increase associated with a specified motive pursued by an acquiring firm could be contingent on industry-wide demand fluctuations. For instance, an acquiring firm with an emphasis on production-side activities may not effectively address external shocks, such as changes in customer preferences and needs. This is because in an M&A with a production-side motive, a firm is likely to place more emphasis on the acquisition of a target’s resources for production efficiency through expanded production capacities and/ or improved technical skills. Of course, in a desirable scenario, we cannot rule out the possibility that a firm can improve its market share and wield market power through acquisitions with a production-side motive by exploiting scale/scope economies [20, 21]. However, with expanded production assets, an acquiring firm may be exposed to a greater risk because it is likely that acquired assets for production/cost efficiency could become superfluous ones when market demand rapidly declines [6, 26]. If the acquiring firm has newly invested in production-related activities, its efficiency gain from expanded production capacities could be compromised with a rapid decline in industry-wide demand [60]. In sum, we posit that a firm’s acquisition with a production-side motive may result in a waste of the firm’s capital with idle resources making its production-side motive less suitable in unstable market conditions.

On the contrary, we posit that in the presence of industry-wide fluctuations in demand, M&As with an emphasis on customer-side motives are considered a better fit than those with production-side motives. This is because firms can respond to changes in demand and secure customers better by developing a customer-oriented strategy [60]. For instance, compared with the acquisition of IT firms with a production-side motive, an acquiring firm can become more adaptive to customer needs and preference through its focus on customer-side activities. As a result, an acquiring firm with a customer-side motive can stabilize and smooth the demand fluctuations it faces by capturing additional demand or increasing the customer retention rate. Moreover, relative to those with a production-side motive, M&As with a customer-side motive may allow an acquiring firm to be better positioned to navigate and monitor customer-related issues in a less stable environment. One might also argue that after absorbing a target’s customers, an acquiring firm may become more vulnerable to undesirable accidents because an enlarged network size can exacerbate a situation. However, through a better understanding of customer relationships with an expanded customer base, acquiring firms with a customer-side motive may attenuate negative word-of-mouth effects better than those with a production-side motive in a dynamic environment [63, 87]. On balance, we posit that a firm is exposed to a greater risk when M&As are pursued with a customer-side motive. However, compared with a production-side motive, the acquisition of IT firms with a customer-side motive is considered a means that fits better in unstable market environments. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 2: In more dynamic environments, an increase in post-merger risks is alleviated more effectively when a customer-side motive is sought rather than a production-side one.

## Data and Econometric Models

Data

Description of Dependent Variables

Our sample for M&A deals with IT firms is obtained from the Securities Data Company’s (SDC) Mergers and Acquisitions Database for the periods from 1996 to 2010. We consider M&A deals in which a target firm’s primary standard industry code (SIC) is matched to 737 or 357 since our focus is on the risk dynamics of firms that acquire IT firms.<sup>6</sup> Specifically, we use three volatility measures as a proxy for the post-merger risks: (1) implied volatility, (2) BHAR (buy-and-hold abnormal return) volatility, and (3) ROA (return on assets) volatility. We obtain implied volatility for individual stocks from the OptionMetrics IVY DB database from 1996 to 2010. Implied volatility is a forward-looking measure derived from an option price by using the Black–Scholes model [40]. It reflects an underlying stock’s price volatility in the future with embedded information about the appetite for holding risk by capturing post-merger risks on the date an M&A is initiated in our context. As a result, it is widely used in the literature to assess the riskiness for individual stocks. Following prior studies, we use implied volatility as a primary measure for post-merger risks [4, 33, 40, 73]. We also drop observations when the necessary data for our analyses are not available. This results in 1,227 M&A deals with implied volatility.

We also complement our approach to implied volatility by employing the standard deviation of BHAR and ROA as an alternative measure of post-merger risks. To estimate the BHAR, we obtain stock return data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). Specifically, we measure an acquiring firm’s long-term returns based on its buy-and-hold strategy, in which a stock is purchased at the market price in the event period and held until specified periods. We also construct benchmark portfolios for M&A firms, based on size and weighted average of non-M&A firms in the sample period, by following prior studies [15, 78]. This leads us to sort all of the returns in the CRSP file into 10 deciles according to size and remove the firms from the benchmark portfolio to contamination bias when they engage in M&A deals [62]. We then sort returns in each decile into 10 deciles based on the book-to-market (BM) ratio for each decile. This eventually gives us average returns of the 100 portfolios for each period. Finally, we compare the buy-and-hold returns for an acquiring firm with those for portfolio benchmarks that take a comparable strategy.<sup>7</sup> That is, BHAR is computed as the difference between the returns of an acquiring firm and those of a benchmark portfolio for the holding period as follows.

$$
B H A R _ {i t} = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T} (1 + r _ {i t}) - \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T} (1 + r _ {b t}),\tag{1}
$$

where T is the holding period, $r _ { i t }$ and $r _ { b t }$ are stock returns of the acquiring firm and benchmark portfolio in month t, respectively.

With the BHAR obtained from the aforementioned procedures, the standard deviation of BHAR for holding period $T$ from the month the M&A transaction is initiated is computed as a measure of post-merger risks. However, we drop observations with fewer than 36 months of trading history before and after the event month, which is required to estimate BHAR volatility around event periods. As we did for the sample with implied volatility, observations are also removed when the necessary data for our analyses are missing. As a result, we are left with 1,230 M&A deals for our analyses with BHAR volatility.

Finally, we retrieve Compustat to obtain ROA, which is defined as the ratio of operating income before depreciation to total assets. Then, we compute its standard deviation as a measure of post-merger risks (see, e.g., [30]). Observations are removed when the necessary data for our analyses are not available. This yields 1,056 M&A deals with ROA volatility.

Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics for the volatility measures employed in our analyses as a proxy for post-merger risks with correlation. The average and standard deviation of implied volatility are 0.568 and 0.262, respectively. The average of BHAR volatility (ROA volatility) is 0.153 (0.092), ranging from 0.016 (0.00003) to 0.961 (2.355). The correlation among variables is also reported in Table 1, and shows that all three variables are highly correlated with each other at reasonable magnitudes from 25.1 percent to 46.7 percent. Not surprisingly, the correlation is positive and statistically significant at the 1 percent level in all cases.

## Description of Independent Variables

Once we identify M&A deals with post-merger risks proxied by three volatility measures, we extract firm-specific factors that are likely to influence an acquiring firm’s risk [34]. These include characteristics of the acquiring firm, such as R&D intensity, capital expenditure intensity, leverage, an acquiring firm’s age, its market share, and the fraction of management ownership from the Compustat and Compustat executive compensation data sets, and M&A experience from SDC Platinum. The R&D and capital expenditure intensity are defined as the ratio of R&D expenditure and capital expenditure to total assets, respectively. Leverage is measured as the ratio of the sum of long-term debt and debt in current liabilities to total assets. An acquiring firm’s age is measured as the difference between the year the M&A transaction is initiated and the firm’s founding year. Market share is defined as an acquiring firm’s sales over total industry sales at the three-digit SIC code [18]. A firm’s M&A experience is defined as its number of M&A transactions with a target in the same industry at the four-digit SIC level within five years before the specific deal. The variable for the fraction of management ownership is measured as the percentage of common stock owned by all directors out of total shares outstanding [23, 25].

To control for the competition effect on a firm’s risk, we construct the Herfindahl– Hirschman index (HHI) as an inverse measure of the degree of competition [50]. This index is specified as $H H I _ { j , t } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n _ { j } } s _ { i j , t } ^ { 2 }$ , where $s _ { i j , t }$ is the market share of firm i in industry j in year t, and $n _ { j }$ is the number of firms in industry j at the three-digit SIC level. We measure industry dynamism to capture the fluctuations of the demand that a firm faces. We do so because unstable industry demand may motivate the firm to resolve the instability through M&As. Following prior studies [37, 55], we measure it by computing the standard deviation of sales in the firm’s primary industry across the preceding five years, and then dividing it by the average industry sales for those years. Finally, we include the measure for economic policy uncertainty (EPU) to control for macro-level uncertainty. The index is constructed by capturing news coverage associated with economy-wide uncertainty such as disagreement among economic forecasters, political uncertainties, tax expirations, and so on [5, 13, 89] .

The descriptive statistics are reported in Table 2. The averages of R&D and capital intensity are 0.068 and 0.095, respectively, with reasonable variations. The average and standard deviations of leverage are 0.144 and 0.178, respectively. On average, an acquiring firm is 34 years old, ranging from 3.2 to 222 years. The other variables, which include market share, M&A experience, industry dynamism, the HHI, the fraction of management ownership and EPU also show large variations. Table 2 presents correlations between the variables used in our analyses as well. In most cases, the correlation between variables is not statistically distinguishable from zero at conventional significance levels. However, our results suggest that firms with higher R&D intensity tend to be young and more levered. In sum, our variables show reasonable variations resulting in high explanatory power in regression analyses without notable multicollinearity among variables.

We are also required to identify deal-specific factors for our analyses. From the SDC database, the type of target is coded one if it is privately held and zero otherwise. Two indicator variables are created to control for the methods of payment in M&A deals [67]. The variable for stock\_deal is coded one for acquisitions financed with stock and zero otherwise. We code the variable for cash\_deal one if the M&A is paid for in cash and zero otherwise. Given that the relatedness between an acquiring firm and a target has an impact on the assessment of post-merger risks, we construct the variable for related\_acq based on the well-known entropy measure [46, 71, 80]. To this end, we first specify the entropy measure that accounts for the number of segments in which a firm operates and the relative importance of each segment as $E n t r o p y _ { i , j , t } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n _ { j } } P _ { i , j , t } \times \ln ( 1 / P _ { i , j , t } )$ , where $P _ { i , j , t }$ is the percentage of firm i’s sales in segment j in period $t ,$ and ln $( 1 / P _ { i , j , t } )$ is the weight for each segment j for firm i in period t. Then we use the difference in the value of the entropy measure between the pre- and post-merger periods. Since an increase in the entropy measure reflects that a firm has undertaken an unrelated M&A, we multiply the difference by –1 to make our interpretation more straightforward.

Our analysis requires the identification of M&A motives pursued by an acquiring firm. These include c\_motive (i.e., the acquisition of IT firms with a customer-side motive), $p _ { - }$ \_motive (i.e., the acquisition of IT firms with a production-side motive), and h\_motive (i.e, the acquisition of IT firms with both customer- and production-side motives). On the basis of M&A deals obtained from the SDC database, we search LexisNexis to retrieve public announcements from primary press releases. We then codify the information in press releases for the M&A announcement as follows. The variable for $c _ { \scriptscriptstyle - }$ motive is coded one if the press releases address only the customer-side issues, with no production-side motive in M&A deals, such as customer base expansion, network effects, market penetration, entry into new markets, or having complementary channels; and zero otherwise. The variable for p\_motive is coded one if the press releases describe only the production-side motive, with no customer-side motive, such as a cost reduction via resource sharing, scale economies, operational efficiency, or product enhancements, product line expansion with no customer-side motive; and zero otherwise. Similarly, the variable for h\_motive is coded one if the press releases address both the customer-side and the production-side motives, and zero otherwise. When the reports from press releases do not convey adequate information for satisfactory classification, we classify the motive of M&A as ambiguous [61]. In most cases, the two authors are in agreement on the coding results (Cohen’s κ = 83.1 percent and 80.8 percent for categorization of the production- and the customer-side motive, respectively). When there are discrepancies between the two authors, they read the full text of the announcement together to resolve the disagreement. Accordingly, all deals are ultimately categorized unanimously.

## Econometric Models

## Time-Series Analyses with Post-Merger Risks

We begin our analysis with a set of unit-root tests to see whether post-merger risks persist over a long-term horizon. We do so because the presence of a unit root implies that an event (M&A in our context) has a permanent impact on risks over a long-term horizon. That is, a change in an acquiring firm’s risk after an M&A deal could be sustained over a long-term horizon until a set of instrumental operations are made to neutralize it. On the other hand, if post-merger risks are found to be stationary (i.e., no unit root), then we infer that an M&A has a transitory impact on the acquiring firm’s risk profile, and, as a result, its risk impacts are likely to attenuate over time.

To formally examine the stationarity of our data, we conduct a set of residualbased augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) tests with specified stochastic processes of risks as follows [49, 65].

$$
\Delta (r i s k _ {i, t}) = \alpha_ {i} (r i s k _ {i, t - 1}) + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p _ {j}} \beta_ {i j} (r i s k _ {i, t - j}) + \varepsilon_ {i, t},\tag{2}
$$

where $r i s k _ { i , t }$ is post-merger risk for firm i measured as implied, BHAR or ROA volatilities. $i = 1 , 2 , . . . , N , t = 1 , 2 , . . . , T ,$ and $p$ is the lag order to reflect the serial correlation $\ ; - 1 < \beta _ { i j } < 1$ for all i and j.

Equation (2) allows us to examine whether risk follows a unit-root process by testing whether $a _ { i }$ equals zero. When we are not able to reject the null hypothesis $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \alpha _ { i } = 0 )$ , in the long run, the stochastic fluctuations of $( r i s k _ { i , t } )$ will be determined by unit-root processes. We test the existence of a unit root under the various model specifications, encompassing the model with an individual intercept, an individual intercept and trend, and no intercept and trend in Equation (2) [49, 65].

## Cross-Sectional Analyses with Post-Merger Risks

Once the stochastic process of risk is confirmed over time periods, we proceed to the next step by investigating the factors influencing post-merger risks for acquiring firms. As discussed earlier, an acquiring firm’s motives to pursue M&As can influence the degree to which it is exposed to a risk following M&A events. Not surprisingly, the decision to acquire with a specific motive is likely to be selfselected. For instance, a firm may undertake the acquisition of IT firms with a customer-side motive to strengthen its market position, whereas others may do it with a production-side motive to improve operational efficiencies or product quality depending on its unique characteristics. To address the endogeneity nature of M&A decisions stemming from a firm’s self-selection into specific motives, we employ the polychotomous choice model developed through the generalization of Heckman’s method to multinomial contexts [24, 27, 32]. This leads us to specify the selection equation with a latent variable and the post-merger risk equation as follows.

$$
\text { Motive } _ {i, j} ^ {*} = \theta_ {j} Z _ {i, j} + \eta_ {i, j} \quad i = 1, 2, \dots N \quad j = 1, 2, \dots M,\tag{3a}
$$

$$
\operatorname{risk} _ {i, j} = \beta X _ {i} + u _ {i, j} \quad i = 1, 2, \dots N \quad j = 1, 2, \dots M,\tag{3b}
$$

where $M o t i v e _ { i , j } ^ { * }$ is a latent variable that represents firm i’s preference of M&A motive j among M alternatives. Z is a vector of exogenous variables affecting the choice of the acquisition motive. X is a vector of exogenous factors determining the postmerger risks.

Similar to the Heckman model with dichotomous choice (see, e.g., [45]), the selection equation in our model enables us to control for selection bias by explicitly modeling the selection process. Since firm i’s M&A motive $j$ is chosen under the condition that the benefit from $M o t i v e _ { i , j } ^ { * }$ is greater than that from max $M o t i v e _ { i , k } ^ { * }$ , we kj model the probability that firm i chooses to undertake the acquisition with motive $j ,$ which is defined as follows:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(\varepsilon_ {i, j} <   0 | Z\right) \text {   where   } \varepsilon_ {i, j} = \max _ {k \neq j} \left(M o t i v e _ {i, k} ^ {*} - M o t i v e _ {i, j} ^ {*}\right).\tag{4}
$$

Following prior studies [59, 68], we assume that the error term $\eta _ { j , t }$ in Equation (3a) is independent and identically Gumbel distributed. Then, our model is specified as the multinomial logit model. This yields:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(\varepsilon_ {i, j} <   0 | Z\right) = \exp \left(\theta_ {j} Z _ {j}\right) / \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {M} \exp \left(\theta_ {k} Z _ {k}\right).\tag{5}
$$

Once the selection process is specified with the multinomial logit model, we can obtain consistent estimates of $\theta _ { j }$ for Equation (3a) by employing the maximum likelihood method [59, 68]. However, we cannot obtain consistent estimates for $\beta$ in Equation (3b) because of the correlation between independent variables $( X _ { i } )$ and error term $( u _ { i , j } )$ . We address this issue by accounting for the correlation between the error term $( \eta _ { i } )$ in the selection equation and the error term $( u _ { i } )$ in the post-merger risk equation, which is captured through the inverse mills ratio (IMR) [24, 27, 59]. Specifically, since the IMR captures the covariance between the two equations, it is explicitly included in estimating the post-merger risk equation. To this end, we specify the selection equation with a set of independent variables that are likely to be associated with a firm’s selection into the engagement of M&A with a specific type of motives. This yields:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Motive } _ {i, j} = \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} S E + \theta_ {2} d y n + \theta_ {3} M S + \theta_ {4} R D \_ i n t + \theta_ {5} C a p \_ i n t \\ + \theta_ {6} I T S e r v i c e s \_ A + \theta_ {7} I T m a n u \_ A + \eta_ {i, j}, \end{array}\tag{6a}
$$

where SE, $d y n ,$ , and MS denote sales efficiency, industry dynamism, and market share, respectively. RD\_int and Cap\_int are the ratio of R&D and capital expenditure to total assets, respectively. ITServices\_A and ITmanu\_A are dummy variables coded one if the primary SIC code of an acquiring firm is listed in SIC 737 and zero otherwise, and coded one if that of an acquiring firm is listed in SIC 357 and zero otherwise, respectively.

With the selection equation in hand, we specify the post-merger risk equation with a set of independent variables that we discussed earlier, such as M&A motives and industry dynamism. We also include several factors as additional controls, such as methods of payment, competition, R&D intensity, capital intensity, leverage, and so on, as additional controls following prior studies [44, 56]. This yields:

$$
\begin{array}{l} r i s k _ {i} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} c \text {\_motive} + \beta_ {2} p \text {\_motive} + \beta_ {3} h \text {\_motive} + \beta_ {4} d y n \\ + \beta_ {5} c \text {\_motive} \times d y n + \beta_ {6} p \text {\_motive} \times d y n + \beta_ {7} h \text {\_motive} \times d y n + c o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {i}, \end{array}\tag{6b}
$$

where the dependent variable in Equation (4) is post-merger risks measured implied, BHAR or ROA volatilities; c\_motive, p\_motive, and h\_motive denote the customerproduction-, and hybrid-side motives, respectively; and dyn is industry dynamism. Controls include competition, stock or cash payment, R&D intensity, leverage, market share, acquirer’s age, target’s industry code, target’s status (private vs. public), related acquisition, the fraction of management ownership, EPU, and yearand industry-specific dummies.

## Post-Merger Risks: Time-Series Evidence

We begin by presenting the risk dynamics following M&A deals over a long-term horizon. To this end, we first document whether a change in risk occurs around the event periods. Figure 1 shows time-based patterns of the acquirers’ risks measured as implied volatilities by M&A motive for 60 months before the M&A events to 60 months afterward.<sup>9</sup> That is, all positive numbers on the x-axes are months after the M&A events; all negative numbers on the x-axes are months prior to the M&A events. The y-axes display the magnitude of risk, measured as implied volatilities. The solid line with × markers in Figure 1 represents the changes in risks over time with all the acquiring firms in our sample. Overall, Figure 1 suggests strong runnersup in risks beginning from one year before to one and a half years after the M&A. It then begins to decline gradually over time. We also present the underlying risk dynamics around M&A events by separating acquiring firms by M&A motives (see the solid, dashed, and round-dashed lines for risks with a customer-side, productionside, and hybrid motive, respectively). We also check the risk dynamics for the market index to rule out the possibility that risk changes around the M&A dates are from market-wide changes. These do not show any systematic patterns and are relatively flat over the time horizon.<sup>10</sup> As a result, we argue that changes in risk around M&A dates are not driven by market-wide shocks.

Although risks prior to M&A initiatives increase in all cases, we see a difference in the level and rate of risk converging to the level it had been before the M&A, depending on the transactional attributes of the M&A deals. With implied volatility as a proxy for post-merger risks, Table 4 shows that when an M&A is pursued with a customer-side motive (or a hybrid motive), the level of post-merger risk is greater than that when an M&A is pursued with a production-side motive. The magnitude of post-merger risk with a customer-side (the hybrid) motive is 0.588 (0.595), but its counterpart with a production-side motive is 0.557 (see Table 4). That is, the difference between the two is 0.031 (0.038), which is statistically significant at conventional levels. Our results with BHAR volatilities also display similar patterns in the magnitude of post-merger risks by the motives of an acquiring firm in M&A deals. However, when ROA volatilities are used as a measure of post-merger risks, we do not find a significant difference in the magnitude of post-merger risks by the M&A motives. In general, our results show that acquiring firms are exposed to a higher level of risk when they initiate M&As with a customer-side motive.

To gain a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of post-merger risks, we examine the persistence of post-merger risks by using the residual-based ADF tests [49, 65]. Overall, without regard for the measures we use, we are able to reject the null hypothesis that post-merger risks follow a unit-root process when all the sample firms are considered. ## Post-Merger Risks: Cross-Sectional Evidence

The analyses above provide us with valuable insights into dynamic patterns of M&A risks over a time horizon under heterogeneous circumstances and suggest that the acquisitions of IT firms are perceived as a risky practice. We enrich our analyses by estimating Equations (6a) and (6b) after accounting for the endogeneity issues arising from the self-selection process with a focus on the interplay between M&A motives and industry dynamism. Then we check the robustness of our results by conducting sensitivity and subsample analyses.

We begin by presenting the results of the selection equation that enables us to alleviate endogeneity concerns stemming from the self-selection issues in undertaking an M&A with specific motives. We show that overall, our model is statistically significant at the 1 percent level (χ<sup>2</sup>= 61,462.7, 1,143.9 and 407.1 for columns [1], [2], and [3], respectively). Moreover, our model shows a high correlation between the predictive M&A motive and the actual motive an acquiring firm had chosen (67.1 percent, 71.9 percent, and 69.3 percent for the customer side, the production side, and the hybrid motive, respectively). This implies that our selection equation appropriately controls for the self-selection issues. The coefficient of sales efficiency and market share is positively associated with the M&As with the customer-side motive at conventional significance levels, whereas it is not with M&As with production- or hybrid-side motives. This is possibly because an efficient firm may acquire IT firms as an instrument to strengthen its market position. Moreover, consistent with our expectation, our results show that firms in a relatively unstable industry are likely to pursue M&As with a customer-side motive (e.g., Panel A of Table 5, 7.754, p < 0.05). However, it is also reported that firms with a higher R&D intensity are more likely to engage in M&As with a production-side motive (see Panel B of Table 5). This is possibly because a firm with an R&D focus is required to maintain its status quo by keeping up with the latest technology through the acquisition of tech-savvy companies. Our estimates for capital expenditure in the selection model with the hybrid motive are positive and partially significant. A firm’s self-selection into the hybrid motive implies that target firms are attractive to the acquirer from both production- and customer sides. Given the attractiveness of target firms from both sides, an M&A with a hybrid motive may require a sufficient amount of financial resources. As a result, acquiring firms with a larger capital expenditure are better positioned in undertaking a relatively larger scale of M&As with a hybrid motive. Our results also suggest that IT manufacturing firms are less (more) likely to pursue M&As with IT firms with a customer-side motive (production-side motive) (see panels A and B of Table 5). Overall, our results from the selection model are statistically significant with the expected signs in the coefficients.

The empirical results for the risk equations measured as three different volatilities with all the interaction terms associated with the M&A motives are presented in columns (1) through (3) of Table 6 (see Equation 6b). We show that the coefficient for the customer-side motive is positive and statistically significant at the conventional significance levels, whereas it is not significant with the equation with ROA volatilities. On the other hand, the coefficient for the production-side motive is negatively significant except in the case of the estimates with implied volatilities. The coefficient for the hybrid motive is not distinguishable from zero in all cases, possibly because of its correlation with either the customer- or production-side motive. This implies that relative to the acquisition of IT firms with an ambiguous motive, acquiring firms with customer-side motives are more likely to be exposed to a greater risk, owing to embedded uncertainties in the acquired customer-related resources.<sup>11</sup> For instance, when a firm pursues an M&A to enter into new markets by acquiring the target’s customer bases, it may be more difficult to assess the value of resources a target firm brings. We also test the difference in the magnitude of the coefficient between the customer- and production-side motives and statistically confirm its difference between the two motives in all three cases at a 1 percent significance level. This supports our H1 that M&A deals with a customer-side motive are associated with greater risks compared to those with a production-side motive. We also show that the coefficient for industry dynamism is positive at a 1 percent level when the post-merger risk is measured with implied volatilities. Specifically, our results suggest that a one standard deviation increase in industry dynamism is associated with an increase in post-merger risks by 0.828 (see column [1] of Table 6). This is possibly because it is relatively difficult to capture synergetic benefits when the market is not stable, increasing the level of risks following M&A.

Next, we examine the moderating role of industry dynamism in post-merger risks when coupled with M&A motives. We show that the coefficient for the interaction term between industry dynamism and a customer-side motive is negative and significant at conventional levels, except for the estimates with ROA volatilities (see Table 6). Given that industry dynamism is a continuous variable, we interpret the coefficient of the interaction as an additional change in post-merger risks when a unit change in industry dynamism is made. This implies that for a firm with industry dynamism 10 percent higher than the mean value, the additional decrease in post-merger risk associated with the customer-side motive is 2.85 percent, whereas that with the production-side motive is not distinguishable from zero. Moreover, the difference between Customer × Dynamism and Production × Dynamism is statically different at the 5 percent level in all cases. Therefore, overall, H2 is supported. On the other hand, the interaction of hybrid with dynamism is not distinguishable from zero.

Our empirical model also controls for additional factors that are likely to influence post-merger risks. These include competition, leverage, methods of payment, R&D intensity, capital expenditure intensity, market share, M&A experience, target’s industry membership, the fraction of management ownership, and EPU. The coefficient for competition is positively associated with post-merger risk in most cases at conventional significance levels.<sup>12</sup> Consistent with prior studies, our results suggest that an acquiring firm’s post-merger risks become greater for an acquisition with stock-based payments [44, 56]. We also find that R&D intensity and an acquiring firm’s market share is positively and negatively associated with post-merger risks, respectively. Moreover, the coefficient of an acquiring firm’s age and M&A experience is negative at conventional significance levels, as expected for all cases. The characteristics of target firms also influence an acquiring firm’s post-merger risks. For instance, our results show that an acquiring firm is exposed to a greater risk when its transaction is made with privately held firms or IT services firms. Consistent with our expectation, the coefficient for the fraction of management ownership is negative and statistically significant at the 1 percent level. Our results also show a positive association between economic policy uncertainty (EPU) and post-merger risks as expected.

Our results suggest that the coefficient for the IMR is statistically significant at conventional levels in most cases. Given that the IMR captures the covariance between the selection and the risk equations, this informs us that self-selection is likely to matter [64]. In such a case, it is worthwhile to compare our results with those from ordinary least squares (OLS) to check whether our empirical model adequately controls for selection bias. Compared with the results from the polychotomous choice model, OLS results are overestimated in assessing the effect of M&A motives (see columns [4]—[6] of Table 6). For instance, the magnitude of the customer-side motive is estimated at 0.260 while its counterpart from OLS is 0.311. A possible explanation for this difference is that our polychotomous choice model controls for the notion that our sample is drawn from self-selected firms that are more likely to benefit from a specific motive, whereas OLS does not.

## Further Analysis

While our empirical model adequately controls for a firm’s self-selection, the model is built on the assumption that we observe a set of variables through which we can identify all the factors that are likely to influence a firm’s M&A decision with a specified motive. However, in practice, it is less likely for researchers to observe all the attributes that influence a firm’s motive to pursue M&As. For instance, a firm may be better positioned in pursuing an M&A with the customer- or production-side motive due to inherited advantages, which are unobservable to researchers. This may compromise the validity of our estimates and produce biased estimates [16]. We conduct a sensitivity analysis to check whether or not our results are seriously affected by unobserved factors. To this end, we construct the odds ratio (Γ), which measures the extent to which firms with the same observed attributes differ in their tendency to pursue M&As with different motives [16]. For instance, if two firms have the same attributes and the odds ratio (Γ) equals 1.5, they differ in their propensity to engage in an M&A with a customer-side motive by a factor of 1.5 (i.e., 50 percent). If there are no unobserved variables, the ratio equals 1. In practice, however, we do not know the true value of Γ. Therefore, we examine how our inference about the effect of M&As with a specific motive is altered by changing the value of the odds ratio (Γ). Following Rosenbaum [81], we conduct the Wilcoxon sign-rank test and our test statistics in Table 7 suggest that our estimates become sensitive due to unobserved variables when Γ= 2 (i.e., firms with the same observed attributes differ in their propensity to pursue M&As with a specified motive by 100 percent due to unobserved attributes). Given that we capture a large portion of variations in the choice of motive through a polychotomous model, we believe that this is a large difference. Taken together, our results from the polychotomous choice model suggests that unobserved heterogeneities stemming from a firm’s self-selection into specific motives are adequately controlled and not being driven by unobserved factors.

While our results generally support our hypotheses, it is possible that our empirical models might not fully capture industry-wide heterogeneity. To this end, we replicate our analysis by splitting the sample into manufacturing and nonmanufacturing firms. This shows that the qualitative nature of our results is unchanged after the sample split (see Table 8). Consistent with our results on the full sample, the coefficient of the customerside motive is positive and statically significant in most cases. However, our results suggest the presence of heterogeneity in the moderating impact of industry dynamism in the relationship between the customer-side motive and post-merger risks across industries. For instance, the coefficient of interaction term between the customer-side motive and industry dynamism is –1.673 for nonmanufacturing firms, while it is –1.001 for manufacturing firms (see columns [2] and [1] of Table 8). In general, post-merger risks respond significantly to increased industry dynamism through the acquisition of IT firms with a customer-side motive in the nonmanufacturing sector. Our rationale for the heterogeneity across industries is that firms in the nonmanufacturing sector are mainly in the service industry where active customer involvement and supports are crucial in service production and delivery [11]. That is, their businesses may require a more serviceoriented strategy associated with customer retention and market timing than firms in the manufacturing sector [54, 63]. This leads us to posit that for nonmanufacturing firms in a less stable market environment, acquisitions with a customer-side motive would be perceived as a less risky practice.

## Conclusion

This study examines the risk implications of M&As around event periods over a long-term horizon. We show a strong run-up in acquiring firms’ risks, beginning one year prior to M&As, but these risks begin to decline over the post-merger periods. Although acquiring firms’ risk increases as a result of M&As, the magnitude of risk is greater when they undertake M&As with a customer-side motive compared to a production-side motive. It is interesting that the post-merger risk is more persistent when M&As are sought with a customer-side motive, whereas it dissipates over time in other cases (i.e., M&As with a production-side motive). After accounting for a firm’s self-selection into specific motives in engaging in the acquisition of IT firms, we further show the interplay between industry dynamism and M&A motives. Specifically, we show that the positive relationship between the customer-side motive and post-merger risks is diluted in less stable market environments. However, this does not hold for the relationship between the production-side motive and post-merger risks. Although the acquisition of IT firms is considered a risky practice, our results with interaction terms suggest that, relative to the productionside motive, the customer-side motive is considered a better fit in an unstable market condition. In addition to implied volatility as a proxy for post-merger risks, our results are also robust to the alternative measures such as BHAR volatility and ROA volatility.

Our study makes significant contributions. We help develop a more comprehensive understanding of the means by which post-merger risks evolve differently over time when IT firms are acquired with different motives due to unique attributes embedded in IT. We found a new significant factor that determines risks of acquisition by compiling the motives of M&A in analyzing post-merger risks. Specifically, our findings offer a new explanation concerning the varying nature of post-merger risks in relation to an acquiring firm’s motive through which IT attributes are likely to be captured. In addition, our study uncovers the potential interplay between industry dynamism and appropriately chosen M&A motives. Our study has valuable implications for the extent to which post-merger risk persists over time and how the magnitude of risk varies by M&A motives when industry-wide demand is not stable.

Our study has important implications for practice. Consistent with prior studies, we show that M&As are a risky strategy in that they increase risks after the M&A deals are initiated. However, we help managers understand the conditions under which they are less likely to be exposed to post-merger risks. For instance, a firm’s M&As with a customer-side emphasis may be more effective in the perspective of post-merger risks when industry-wide demand is not stable. It is also documented that IT is likely to be associated with an improvement in detecting and seizing market opportunities [83]. As a result, when operating in a less stable market, a firm may find a way to expand its customer base through the acquisition of IT firms, with less escalation of post-merger risks by emphasizing customer-side activities. Moreover, in a case where network effects are likely to play a dominant role in acquiring customers, it becomes more critical for firms to shorten time to market. In this sense, our study guides managers in how to preempt markets through M&A strategies with appropriately chosen motives. Furthermore, the long-term nature of M&As and the subsequent shift in risks inform managers that they must consider appropriate planning horizons in their financial decisions with a thorough understanding of the unique attributes of IT.

Although our study provides a clear explanation of risk dynamics in the acquisition of IT firms, it is not without limitations. First, we collected data on M&A motives from press releases, in which larger M&As may have received more attention from the media with more detailed descriptions of the characteristics of transactions. We admit that, similar to other studies, our procedure for coding the variables could be influenced by the information contained in the press releases. For instance, we may have coded an M&A case with the production side, customer side, or both, as zero even though it is actually sought with these intents because the press release did not report the information in detail. Although our work does not deviate from a standard approach, future studies would benefit through the assembly of different data sets to construct a more precise measure. Second, due to a lack of publicly available data, we are unable to specify our empirical models with a set of factors of a target’s characteristics such as intangible and network-dominant attributes, marketing resources, and economic performance. Although our analyses are shown to be robust and have high explanatory power, future studies would benefit from a richer data set. Finally, we limit our attention to one dimension of M&A performance by examining post-merger risks. As firms become more interconnected, however, an increasing number of firms pursue M&As with IT firms to strengthen their position on the customer side as well as the production side. Accordingly, it would be interesting to explore the impact of M&As with IT firms on consumer welfare contingent on an acquiring firm’s motive. We hope that our study motivates future work on this promising issue. Despite these limitations, we believe that our study has valuable implications for the dynamics of post-merger risks and the moderating role of industry dynamism when coupled with a specified motive to acquire IT firms.
