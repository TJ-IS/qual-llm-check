# MEANINGFUL HEALTHCARE SECURITY: DOES MEANINGFUL-USE ATTESTATION IMPROVE INFORMATION SECURITY PERFORMANCE?<sup>1</sup>

Juhee Kwon Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {kwon.juhee@gmail.com}

M. Eric Johnson Owen Graduate School of Management, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN 37203 U.S.A. {m.eric.johnson@owen.vanderbilt.edu}

Certification mechanisms are often employed to assess and signal difficult-to-observe management practices and foster improvement. In the U.S. healthcare sector, a certification mechanism called meaningful-use attestation was recently adopted as part of an effort to encourage electronic health record (EHR) adoption while also focusing healthcare providers on protecting sensitive healthcare data. This new regime motivated us to examine how meaningful-use attestation influences the occurrence of data breaches. Using a propensity score matching technique combined with a difference-in-differences (DID) approach, our study shows that the impact of meaningful-use attestation is contingent on the nature of data breaches and the time frame. Hospitals that attest to having reached Stage 1 meaningful-use standards observe fewer external breaches in the short term, but do not see continued improvement in the following year. On the other hand, attesting hospitals observe short-term increases in accidental internal breaches but eventually see long-term reductions. We do not find any link between malicious internal breaches and attestation. Our findings offer theoretical and practical insights into the effective design of certification mechanisms.

Keywords: Data breaches, electronic healthcare records, healthcare, meaningful-use, security

## Introduction

The U.S. Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act of 2009 stimulated unprecedented growth in adoption of electronic health records (EHR). However, physician experience and other available evidence suggest that the overarching objectives, such as improved patient care and reduced costs, have been illusive (Jha 2010, 2011; Yasnoff et al. 2013). Further, the dramatic rise of EHR adoption has created new challenges: ill-designed and poorly integrated clinical workflow due to a disparate mix of healthcare systems that struggle to share information. Moreover, security management has not kept pace with the digitization of healthcare data. Indeed, the move toward digitization has coincided with a rising number of patients impacted by data breaches (Hoffman and Podgurski 2009; Sittig and Singh 2011). According to (the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS),<sup>2</sup> healthcare breaches continued to increase from 199 in 2010 to 314 in 2014, leading privacy advocates to conclude that digitizing healthcare data simply created a much larger pool of data to steal (Dearen 2012; Eddy 2014; Higgins 2015; Siebert 2012).

The HITECH Act introduced a certification regime for healthcare providers (referred to as meaningful-use attestation) for assessing (difficult-to-observe) effective usage of EHRs beyond simple technology adoption. In other industries, certification schemes have long been viewed as useful tools for promoting voluntary action to improve quality while mitigating moral hazard. For example, the ISO 9000 and ISO 14000 series verify quality processes and environmentally friendly practices through third-party audits. Such certification mechanisms encourage organizations to establish difficult-to-observe practices (Levine and Toffel 2010; Naveh and Erez 2004).

To address the concerns of safe EHR adoption, meaningfuluse attestation incorporated important directives concerning patient data security. Lured with incentive payments linked to a certification process, Stage 1 meaningful-use attestation has been widely achieved, with approximately 95% of Medicare hospitals participating by 2015 (CMS 2015). Representing a widely adopted certification regime, meaningful-use attestation motivated us to examine the effect of certification programs on security management.

In an analogous area of operations management, a number of rigorous empirical studies examined how certification mechanisms affect quality performance (Albuquerque et al. 2007; Berliner and Prakash 2013; Boiral 2007; Corbett et al. 2005; Terlaak and King 2006). These studies showed that disciplined adherence to prescribed certification requirements decreases defect rates and improves quality. Furthermore, the adoption of a certification works as a market signal showing customers that an effective quality improvement program has been implemented. While these factors are also relevant to security management, the security context requires deeper examination.

Certification regimes in operations management aim to help organizations overcome immature quality practices. However, in the case of security management, organizations are often victimized by deliberate attacks from both inside (e.g., employees) and outside (e.g., hackers) as well as by inadvertent breaches arising from ill-established security practices. Thus, in this context of highly uncertain and diverse security risks, we strive to identify how certification mechanisms influence the reduction of inadvertent breaches and defense against deliberate attackers. Identifying the impact of meaningful-use attestation on diverse healthcare breaches provides an opportunity to extend the scope of research on certification programs.

The context of healthcare security provides an ideal research setting with strict breach reporting rules under the meaningful-use regime. Of course, within this context, we need to consider selection bias from both observable and unobservable factors, because attesting and non-attesting hospitals may have differential characteristics. Propensity score matching is widely used to address selection bias from observable factors, while a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis resolves bias from unobservable factors. Thus, to mitigate such biases, we employ propensity score matching combined with a DID approach to resolve self-selection issues from observable factors and to mitigate the impact of unobservable common shocks that affect healthcare providers. Our results show that in the short run, Stage 1 meaningful-use attestation is associated with a reduction in external breaches and the discovery of more accidental internal breaches. The discovery of these accidental breaches appears to drive improvement, as accidental internal breaches significantly decrease over time. However, we do not see any further effect on external breaches following the initial decrease. Finally, we do not find any link between malicious internal breaches and attestation.

This study offers several contributions to the literature. First, previous research efforts attempting to quantify the value of certifications in security management were methodologically limited to self-reported surveys and analytical modeling. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the effectiveness of certification programs on security outcomes using real-world data. Second, the uniqueness of security management, with external and internal failures having diverse root causes, provides a novel context for advanced theory building in certification mechanisms. Finally, our findings provide policy insights on effective security practices by providing empirical evidence that the effects of certification mechanisms are contingent on the nature of data breaches and the time frame.

## Research Background

This section reviews the literature on the impact of certification mechanisms. Little existing research has focused on certification programs in the security context. Thus, we turn to theoretical work in the operations management literature that has addressed analogous quality certifications. Next, we review empirical studies related to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS)<sup>3</sup> mechanisms for meaningful-use attestation.

## Certification Mechanisms

A significant body of research in the operations management literature addresses the effects of certification programs (e.g., ISO 9000/14000 series), employing various theories (e.g., signaling theory, organizational learning theory; see Appendix A). Among these studies, there is a raging debate over the true effects of certification programs—with advocates noting both positive and negative effects.

Proponents of certification programs have argued that certification programs can improve firm performance, providing empirical evidence on the benefits from adopting ISO 9000 or ISO 14000. These studies can be categorized into two streams from the perspective of how certification programs improve operations management practices and corresponding performance. One stream argues that adherence to certification requirements generates organizational learning by developing systematic operating procedures, training, internal inspections, and corrective actions (Benner and Tushman 2002; Gao et al. 2010; Gray et al. 2015). To the extent that greater organizational efforts are exerted to develop and implement such procedures, an organization’s capabilities can improve. Gray et al. (2015) found that initial improvement may be challenging to sustain post certification.

The other stream has employed signaling theory, asserting that a firm’s adoption of a certification program acts as a quality signal in information-asymmetric markets. Information asymmetries between buyers and sellers in markets cause moral hazard, often leading to market failure (Anderson et al. 1999; Gao et al. 2010; Graffin and Ward 2010; Oezpolat et al. 2013). As credible signals of quality and trustworthiness to improve information transparency, third-party certification programs could decrease the likelihood of market failure (Mas-Colell et al. 1995; Tirole 2015). Unlike mandatory regulation that imposes penalties, voluntary certification programs linked to market signaling (e.g., brand image, corporate reputation) can encourage an organization to take action (Arimura et al. 2008; Foster and Gutierrez 2013; Osagie 2008). If government and markets interpret an organization’s certification as an effort to comply with norms and standards related to environmental, social, or security issues, such a signaling effect can motivate organizations to fulfill certification requirements despite the considerable time and effort required. Volition is a critical factor for learning autonomy (Haunschild and Rhee 2004).

On the other hand, critics argue that the processes of attaining certification add to bureaucratic waste, with little impact on actual performance. They further argue that such bureaucracy hinders efficient management practices. Although documented procedures engender a certain measure of rigor, they can provoke the creation of an “iron cage,” making organizations more rigid (Weber 1968). Thus some scholars insist that the implementation of standards, particularly in rapidly changing environments, has hurt organizational efficiency by creating the bureaucratization of organizations (Boiral 2007). Naveh and Erez (2004) showed that certification programs positively affect attention-to-detail in the drive for zero defects but negatively affect the innovation needed to catch up with evolving market demands. Benner and Tushman (2002) argued that implementing a set of managerial practices required by certification programs impedes organizational responsiveness to environmental shifts, although it induces organizational learning to improve existing systems.

Furthermore, institutional and commercial pressures can make the implementation of certification programs loosely associated with real practices. Faced with these pressures, organizational commitment to certification may not arise from a voluntary and proactive approach that initiates self-regulatory activities (although they acquire greater external visibility). In this case, obtaining certification may indicate less-than representative information (Brenner et al. 2004; King and Lenox 2000).

Finally, despite the fact that outcomes vary across organizations, most certification programs use only a pass/fail rating that ignores different outcome levels. The absence of moresophisticated assessments allows some participants to simply meet the minimum requirements. In the end, such certification programs have been criticized as little more than industry marketing campaigns that attract less-than-average performers to exploit certification programs (e.g., greenwashing<sup>4</sup>) (Toffel 2006).

## Meaningful-Use Attestation

As part of the HITECH Act of 2009, CMS introduced a certification regime, meaningful-use attestation, for EHR assimilation into clinical work flows. CMS offered incentive payments to eligible healthcare providers who attest to meaningful use of certified EHRs. Like other certification programs, the attestation requires healthcare providers to establish systematic procedures to address quality and security. The core requirements are a set of best practices that are designed to improve patient care, reduce errors, and lower the overall cost of service (e.g., adoption of a computerized provider order entry system (CPOE) and implementation of drug–drug and drug–allergy interaction checks). One of the core requirements aims to protect electronic health information created or maintained by a certified EHR technology (CMS 2014). Beyond the implementation of EHRs, it mandates that healthcare providers meet certain standards regarding a set of privacy and security provisions along with meaningful EHR usage for patient care. Launched in 2011, the formal attestation of meaningful-use<sup>5</sup> requires healthcare providers to implement policies and procedures to prevent, detect, and correct security violations based on the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)<sup>6</sup> security rules.<sup>7</sup> These rules aim to help healthcare providers avoid common security gaps that could lead to cyber-attack intrusions and data loss. They include four categories for implementing security standards and safeguards: risk analysis, risk management, sanction policy, and information system activity review.

Conducting a risk analysis is the first step in identifying safeguards that comply with and carry out the specifications of HIPAA’s Security Rule. Therefore, in this step, healthcare providers should conduct an accurate and thorough assessment of potential risks and existing vulnerabilities (e.g., patch management, loopholes), and then determine the most appropriate way to achieve compliance, taking into account the security characteristics of the organization and its environment. The risk analysis outcome is a direct input to the risk management process in the next step.

Risk management mandates implementing proper security safeguards for the assessed risks and vulnerabilities. Hospitals first need to conduct risk prioritization and evaluate risk-reducing security measures. The next step requires the actual implementation of security measures (both technical and nontechnical). Both risk analysis and risk management are important to form the foundation upon which a hospital’s security platform is built.

Sanction policies help healthcare providers define clear sanctions for workforce members (i.e., full-time or part-time employees, contractors, medical staff, and volunteers) who fail to comply with security mandates (e.g., first written warning, final warning with/without suspension on careless acts, incomplete training). Although most healthcare providers have security policies and procedures, such policies and procedures are not always well understood by employees. Developing both incentives for good security behavior and disciplinary actions for poor security behavior helps align employee compliance with security policies and procedures.

Finally, information system activity review aims to detect and limit damage to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of a system. Its specification directs organizations to implement procedures to regularly review records of information system activity, such as audit logs, access reports, and security incident tracking reports. Through regular system reviews, healthcare providers can determine whether patient record have been disclosed or used in an inappropriate manner.

CMS requests documentation on these four categories for a desk review, followed by an on-site review<sup>8</sup> (CMS 2014). There is no partial credit (i.e., only pass or fail). A hospital that fails just one element of a meaningful-use audit must return the entire incentive payment for that year and may be subject to future audits in subsequent years. The attestation program directs organizations to perform periodic audits to verify that attesting hospitals keep up with the security provisions.

Like the ISO 9000 series, the mechanism of meaningful-use attestation results in both positive changes (i.e., benefits from the implementation of standard procedures) and challenges (i.e., the bureaucratization of organizations). Meaningful-use attestation differs from other quality certification programs in a few important ways. First, while general certifications focus on reforming work flows and operations inside an organization, the security provisions of meaningful-use attestation address both deliberate attacks from the outside and security incidents from the inside. This range of potential security risks can influence the effectiveness of standard practices required by certification. With rapidly evolving technologies, security risks have also become more uncertain, making it difficult for organizations to develop appropriate security safeguards in a timely manner. Thus, defense strategies against such evolving risks require organizational learning by exploring new surroundings, rather than exploitative learning via experiential refinement within the same trajectory. Given the uncertain nature of security management, identifying the impacts of certification programs across diverse threats allows us to extend existing certification theories.

Second, typical certification mechanisms signal the implementation of difficult-to-observe practices (i.e., operating procedures, training, internal auditing, and corrective actions), thus reducing information asymmetry between customers and firms. Such signals of well-established practices enable certified organizations to generate more demand by building consumer trust. However, meaningful-use attestation was not intended to provide signaling value (i.e., to deter hackers or build trust with patients). Rather the U.S. government developed it to encourage hospitals to adopt technology. Thus, attestation is not something that is widely promoted. A hospital’s attestation is primarily reported to the government to qualify for incentive payments. Monetary incentives from the government have been a key motive for meaningful-use attestation, whereas quality certifications are typically motivated by the prospect of increased demand through signaling effects.

Therefore, building on the learning effect of certification mechanisms, our study empirically examines the impact of a certification mechanism (i.e., meaningful-use attestation) on an organization’s security outcomes, accounting for both external environmental factors and internal organizational factors. Our results enrich the literature on both security management and certifications programs.

## Hypothesis Development

Data breaches vary in source, motive, and intensity, depending on “who” is behind the breach. Breaches can be attributed to outsiders or insiders, with either malicious or nonmalicious intent. According to the data collected from HHS and Privacy Rights Clearinghouse sites (PRC)<sup>9</sup>, approximately 42% of breaches originated from external hackers, 33% occurred due to careless insiders, and the remaining 25% arose from insiders’ malicious intent. Accidental breaches by careless insiders are mostly seen as human errors that can be managed by proper practices, whereas malicious internal and external breaches are treated as criminal actions involving law enforcement (Shinder 2002). Thus, identifying the impact of certification programs across such breach types is critical to short-term corrective actions and long-term defensive strategies (Kwon and Johnson 2014; Tucker et al. 2007; Verizon 2013). As different breach types require different security strategies, we develop our hypotheses based on the key primary sources related to who is responsible for a breach and the intent behind the breach. Understanding the different nature of breaches is important to identify the specific mechanism of meaningful-use attestation affecting the breaches.

## External Breaches

As economic and social activities continue to go online, criminals increasingly exploit the growing sea of data that can be (all too easily) converted to cash through underground websites and chat rooms. As a rich source of personal information, healthcare data have become more attractive to criminals, because they fuel a diverse set of fraudulent activities including fake claims to payers such as Medicare, pharmaceutical black markets, and fraudulently obtained medical care (Miller 2016). The Ponemon Institute (2016) reported that criminal attacks, the leading cause of healthcare breaches, have risen by 125% over the past 5 years.

Criminal attackers have sought to acquire patient data through diverse hacking techniques such as malware to capture keystrokes, stealing electronic devices with confidential data, etc. (Ponemon 2012). While well-known hacking techniques can be prevented by physical and technical safeguards such as encryption, firewall, antivirus, etc., healthcare security systems have not kept pace with the digitization of information (Blumenthal and Glaser 2007; Jha 2010). Industry watchers have noted that the healthcare sector lags in security systems compared to other industries with a long history of cyberattacks (Eddy 2014; Higgins 2015). Indeed, many hospitals have not yet addressed well-known vulnerabilities and weak links.

Given the low-hanging fruit due to the lagged adoption of corresponding security measures, hospitals can learn critical and basic security practices through certification mechanisms. The attestation requirements for risk analysis and risk management help hospitals recognize potential risks and existing vulnerabilities (e.g., out-of-date patches/antivirus, expired encryption keys, loopholes) and adopt proper administrative, physical, and technical safeguards (e.g., information access management, security measures). Along with a sanction policy, regular information system activity reviews help hospitals verify that there have been no intrusions, that they have proper technical safeguards (e.g., firewall), and that they do not have lingering malware, and so on.

However, with the fast pace of technology change, there is increasing pressure to continually scan for potential security threats. Security is a cat-and-mouse game. As attackers continue to update and evolve their techniques to outsmart security measures, the traditional organizational perimeter protected by firewalls and antivirus is eroding, and existing security defenses are coming under increasing pressure. Furthermore, while rapid adoption of advanced healthcare IT has made information more readily available in clinical settings, it has led to increased complexity, breeding unseen security risks. The increasing use of the Internet/mobile working and public cloud services erode the traditional perimeter of a healthcare enterprise. This implies that the risk landscape is becoming less bounded (Kuo 2011).

Recent industry surveys<sup>10</sup> illustrate how the risk landscape has changed. For example, while web-borne malware attacks caused breach incidents for 78% of surveyed healthcare organizations several years ago, denial-of-service attacks have become the most serious threats followed by ransom-ware and malware arising from public cloud services (Ponemon Institute 2011, 2014, 2015, 2016; Ponemon 2012b). Security threats vary over time—even within the same category of breaches (Verizon 2016). Over the past few years, malware attacks have evolved from command and control,<sup>11</sup> memory scrapping,<sup>12</sup> and key logger<sup>13</sup> to backdoor<sup>14</sup> malicious code. Today’s security threats are not the same as yesterday’s threats.

Well-established security policies and systematic procedures remain key controls for combatting known attacks. However, malicious hacking often involves a shift to a different technological trajectory by exploring elements not previously addressed. While such shifting requires prompt action, the bureaucracy of meaningful-use attestation (referred to as an iron cage) may impede organizational responsiveness to the dynamic environment (Boiral 2003). Furthermore, certification mechanisms mostly contribute to exploitative activities that build on well-known knowledge, rather than exploratory activities that search unknown areas such as evolving security threats (Benner and Tushman 2002; Levinthal and March 1993; Naveh and Erez 2004).

Therefore, meaningful-use attestation requiring well-defined and documented security practices can help hospitals quickly address risk factors that are common and well known in other industries but not yet recognized in the healthcare sector. However, such practices become less effective over time as hackers find new ways to circumvent established security measures. Hence, we hypothesize that certification mechanisms negatively affect external breaches in the short term.

H1: Meaningful-use attestation is negatively associated with external breaches in the short term but not the long term.

## Internal Breaches

While most high profile attacks on patient data have originated from the outside, privileged insiders, such as employees, third-party contractors, or temporary workers, have also been responsible for data leakage. Internal breaches are often created innocently through accidental or inadvertent behavior without any intention of harm. Some incidents, however, are driven by an insider’s malicious intent. Privileged insiders can tactfully compromise a target through their knowledge of security countermeasures. Thus while external threats continue to be a real danger, concern about security threats posed by insiders has grown (Hua and Bapna 2013; Watson 2016).

First, accidental breaches (e.g., inadvertent disclosures) are primarily “low-tech” incidents, such as misplaced devices, misdirected e-mail, and mistakes by administrators and programmers (Verizon 2013). Such low-tech incidents are often caused by inadequate training, poor processes, or loose management oversight. Compared with external hacking, accidental breaches have attracted less attention because traditional notions of information security have placed an emphasis on the technical aspects of security. However, the rise of social media, cloud computing, mobility, and big data have provided more ways to transmit patient data and created more opportunities for mistakes. For example, employees working at home could easily disclose locally stored data on stolen or misplaced laptops. Likewise, working in cafes or hotels could expose data on open wireless networks. Medical staff have also inadvertently disclosed patient information on social media by posting patient pictures or comments. These inadvertent security incidents are generally caused by the ambiguity between security-related actions labeled “to do” and “not to do” across daily operations (Watson 2016).

Certification regimes can be effective with regard to inadvertent leakages because they cultivate standardized management processes for detection and prevention of failures (Ittner et al. 2001; Naveh and Erez 2004; Naveh and Marcus 2007; Terziovski et al. 2003). Likewise, meaningful-use attestation initiates the establishment of security procedures and guidelines for detection and prevention of inadvertent internal errors by requiring adherence to HIPAA security provisions (i.e., risk analysis, risk management, sanction policy, and information system activity review) (CMS 2014).

The procedures for risk analysis and risk management help hospitals identify and manage unintentional security errors and omissions across clinical workflows. Awareness of existing and potential weak points enables hospitals to put controls (e.g., security safeguards, policies) in place to watch for similar errors in the future. Next, the requirements of a sanction policy further encourage adherence to established security policies by instituting sanctions or penalties for noncompliance. Along with clear security policies, fear of reprimands for violations can motivate insiders to study security guidelines and to do the right thing. Finally, information system activity review mandates regular audits on information systems that handle patient data, security policies, security-training programs, breach remediation, etc. Through these review activities, hospitals can learn from past errors and uncover vulnerabilities.

These aspects of the four security provisions match with exploitative activities that involve improvements in existing weak links rather than exploratory activities for evolving threats. Many organizations start with exploitative activities to correct current security routines, which are relatively less unpredictable. Exploitative security practices for risk assessment and review activities contribute to identifying and correcting existing inadvertent breaches. Without welldefined security guidelines, negligent security errors and omissions are ignored and unreported. For example, laptops with patient data go missing, but are not tracked as a breach. Indeed, the contents of those laptops are often unknown and even the lost hardware is unaccounted for. Adherence to the security provisions through meaningful-use attestation leads to discovery and possible correction of such incidents. Over time, organizational learning through error correction leads to prevention of inadvertent disclosures. This human learning element is more relevant to inadvertent disclosures where the entire organization must improve.

Thus, meaningful-use attestation can lead to the discovery of more accidental internal breaches, especially in the first few months after attestation. However, improved detection will direct more management attention toward these vulnerabilities and will initiate organizational learning from failure. Thus, hospitals will ultimately improve and experience a decrease in accidental breaches. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2: Meaningful-use attestation is negatively associated with accidental internal breaches in the long term, but positively associated with accidental internal breaches in the short term.

Internal breaches can also originate from malicious insiders. Typically, insiders have greater access than outsiders to sensitive information, a better understanding of internal information processes, more knowledge of high-value targets, and awareness of security weaknesses and safeguards (Verizon 2016). However, most security systems are designed to detect external attacks through a firewall or other perimeter applications. Further, because malicious insiders can exploit their access with a legitimate ID and password (often carrying administrative user rights), they can easily bypass security barriers without being detected, regardless of the number of security layers such as firewalls and security software that are in place. By abusing their knowledge and privileges, malicious insiders represent the largest risk to servers and databases. For example, an employee in a billing department has access to significant patient information needed to justify charges to insurance providers. Using their system credentials they can access and record patient information that can be later sold to identify thieves. Moreover, malicious insiders exhibit a wide spectrum of motivations from the desire for financial gain to retaliation for mistreatment. Controlling malicious internal breaches requires a more holistic view of human psychology and organizational behavior beyond the typical security measures that are the focus of meaningful-use attestation.

However, as noted earlier, meaningful-use attestation focuses on verifying the implementation of appropriate security safeguards to eliminate hazardous security practices. Such capabilities rarely deter elaborate internal attacks. Controlling malicious insiders requires different types of management (e.g., psychological monitoring) beyond traditional security systems. Therefore, although we state the following alternative hypothesis, our main argument supports the null hypothesis:

H3: Meaningful-use attestation is not associated with malicious internal breaches.

## Research Methods

Our empirical analysis seeks to identify changes in data breaches consequent to meaningful-use attestation. Estimating the effect of meaningful-use attestation across diverse healthcare organizations’ breaches is challenging because of possible nonrandom assignment of hospitals to the treatment condition (i.e., meaningful-use attestation), which biases the estimate of the effect of meaningful-use attestation. Identification problems arise because it is possible to observe outcomes of interest among hospitals in either the attesting or non-attesting condition, but not both. For instance, hospitals that have fewer prior breaches may be more likely to attest to meaningful-use status and have better subsequent security performance. If this reverse causality issue is significant, we anticipate that hospitals achieving meaningful-use status would outperform others prior to meaningful-use attestation as well as afterward. Other observable factors (i.e., organizational expense, size, type, location, and adopted healthcare and security systems) can also lead to selection bias. Larger hospitals are more likely to make security investments, attest to meaningful use, and correspondingly have fewer breaches. In this case, breaches may be correlated, but not causally connected, with meaningful-use attestation.

To resolve such issues and establish a causal link between meaningful-use attestation and security performance, we have adopted propensity score matching, enabling us to create a quasi-experimental setting (Imbens and Wooldridge 2009;

Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). However, while propensity score matching can resolve selection bias from observable factors, our research context could still suffer from selection bias arising from unobservable factors that influence both events and outcomes (Heckman et al. 1997). It is possible that hospital-specific unobservable factors jointly affect meaningful-use attestation and security outcomes. Hospitals with a “security culture,”<sup>15</sup> which has been defined as the way in which organizations operate to protect information assets, might be more likely to both insist on strong security and attest to meaningful use. Thus, meaningful-use attestation could be simply correlated with, but not affect, healthcare breaches. The DID method can remove selection bias from such unobserved organizational heterogeneity. By representing the differences in breach occurrences in pre- and postattestation periods, DID allows us to rule out the possibility that security performance is driven by unobservable factors like cultural maturity rather than meaningful-use attestation.

Although the instrumental variable technique is commonly used to correct for selection bias, propensity score matching with DID is better suited for our context due to the fact that meaningful-use attestation is viewed as a decision to adopt a policy (Heckman 1979; Heckman and Robb 1985). Moreover, it is difficult to find an ideal instrumental variable that is highly correlated with meaningful-use attestation but not related to breaches. Prior studies also have demonstrated that propensity score matching in combination with DID analysis is more efficient than an instrumental variable approach requiring strong exclusion restrictions (Blundell and Dias 2000; Girma and Gorg 2007). Therefore, to eliminate selection bias issues caused by observable and unobservable factors, we utilized a DID approach along with propensity score matching.

## Sample

We obtained data from several data sources. Data on hospitals came from the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics™ Database, which has been widely used in previous studies to examine the impact of healthcare information systems (Angst et al. 2014; Angst et al.

2010; Hillestad et al. 2005; Miller and Tucker 2009). The database provides information on over 5,000 hospitals, including the numbers of adopted healthcare and security applications, and organizational characteristics (e.g., bed size, operating expense, organizational type).

The binary indicators for hospitals’ meaningful-use attestation were collected from the CMS website.<sup>16</sup> The CMS website provides a list of hospitals that attested to meaningful use, attestation dates, and the amount of financial incentives paid to those hospitals. The CMS attestation data were matched to the organizational data from the HIMSS database. After eliminating hospitals with missing data, the final sample used in our propensity score analysis includes 2,743 attesting hospitals and 1,396 non-attesting hospitals (for a total of 4,139 hospitals) from 2010 to 2013. Our sample includes only Medicare hospitals that are subject to meaningful-use attestation.<sup>17</sup>

Data breaches were collected from two sources: HHS and PRC. HHS provides a list of post-2009 breaches of unprotected health information under section 13402(e)(4) of the HITECH Act. The California nonprofit organization, PRC, provides a collection of breaches across industries. The postings on both sites contain brief descriptions of each case, including the number of breached records, breach date, type of breach, hospital name, and location. The final sample includes 824 breaches from 2009 to 2014.

## Operationalization of Variables

Our focal variables are meaningful-use attestation and the numbers of subsequent breaches. Data breaches accidentally or maliciously occur at the hands of insiders or outsiders. We specified three primary breach types based on the actors responsible for the breaches and their intent (i.e., outsiders, accidental insiders, and malicious insiders). Based on breach descriptions provided by HHS and PRC (see Appendix B), we categorized all breaches into the three types. This categorization has often been used in the security literature (Hua and Bapna 2013; Kwon and Johnson 2014; Miller and Tucker 2011).

External denotes the number of external breaches arising from outsiders. If a breach description includes “theft,” “stolen,” “hacker,” etc., the breach was categorized as External. AccidentalInternal is the number of accidental breaches, having “loss,” “improper disposal,” “accidental exposure,” etc. in the description, while MaliciousInternal is the number of internal breaches attributed to malicious insiders, including “disgruntled employees,” “temporary workers,” or “contractors” in the context of “stolen” or “identity theft.”

MUAttest is a binary variable that is equal to one if a hospital achieved Stage 1 meaningful-use status and zero otherwise. While meaningful use was enacted in 2009 as part of the HITECH Act, the official attestation period started in 2011, when hospitals began filing for incentive payments. Among our sample of 2,743 attesting hospitals, 664 attested meaningful use in 2011, 1,458 in 2012, and 621 in 2013.

Our model includes an estimate of breach costs incurred by breached hospitals to control for the magnitudes of breaches. To estimate these costs, we focused on two primary direct elements: breach notification costs and fines. Thus, we captured direct breach costs, but not hard-to-estimate indirect costs such as reputational damage. Fines related to breaches were collected from the HHS website. We also estimated breach notification costs, which are mandated by the HITECH Act (these costs can be viewed as part of the regulatory punishment). HHS’s own analysis estimates the cost of breach notification by multiplying the number of affected individuals by the per capita cost of a breach. Per capita estimates used by HHS were developed by the Ponemon Institute, LLC (HHS 2009; Ponemon 2009). Following this approach, we measured hospitals’ breach costs (lnBreachCost) by multiplying the number of affected patients by the per capita cost estimate<sup>18</sup> (Ponemon 2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2015, 2016).

Consistent with prior healthcare research, we employed a comprehensive set of control variables to account for the potentially confounding effects of organizational characteristics. Some of organizational characteristics may vary over time, such as the number of licensed beds (as a measure of hospital size) and operating expenses (as a measure of financial status), and therefore, they must be appropriately controlled (Bardhan and Thouin 2013; Kohli et al. 2012; Menachemi et al. 2006; Miller and Tucker 2011). NofBeds is the number of licensed beds at a particular hospital, reflecting the size of the hospital. lnOexp represents a hospital’s annual spending on operations, such as staffing and property expenses.

Other characteristics that we included are IS planning status, academic status, and location (Appari et al. 2013). ISPlan is dichotomously measured by whether a hospital has an IS plan (Kwon and Johnson 2013). A hospital’s reflects its organizational maturity in the management of healthcare systems. Academic describes the hospital type: one if the hospital is affiliated with a university or other academic institution and zero otherwise. Academic medical centers are likely to face more insider threats, because more diverse individuals, including medical students and academic staff, require access to healthcare systems. Metro indicates whether a hospital is in a metropolitan area (Gaynor et al. 2012). If a hospital is located in a metropolitan area, it could be exposed to more threats from a range of sources, including physical theft of equipment.

We further controlled for the level of hospital digitization, as digitization is related to the probability of breaches. We measured hospital digitization by counting the number of adopted healthcare applications, categorized as clinical and operational applications, administrative applications, EHRs, and security applications. This categorization of healthcare applications has been extensively validated and frequently used by health IT researchers (Bhattacherjee et al. 2007; Burke and Menachemi 2004; Burke et al. 2002; Menachemi et al. 2006; Paré and Sicotte 2001). First, Clinical Operation is measured by the number of adopted clinical and operational applications that support fundamental medical operations, such as operating room scheduling, laboratory, radiology, nursing, telemedicine, transcription, and pharmacy. Hospital Admin denotes the number of administrative applications for improving data processing and sharing, such as patient registration, billing, and payroll. Strategic IT represents strategic applications related to improving critical decisionmaking activities and providing advanced patient care, such as executive information systems, clinical intelligence systems, and patient portals. Security is the total number of security applications, such as antivirus, encryption, firewall, intrusion detection, spam filter, and user authentication (Kwon and Johnson 2014). ## Empirical Analysis and Results

We first assessed the correlations between the variables of our models. Table 2 displays the correlation matrix, along with each variable’s tolerance (Tol) and variance inflation factor (VIF). Although most of the correlations among the variables showed low values, we found elevated correlations among operating expenses, bed size, and some hospital applications. However, multicollinearity diagnostics exhibit tolerances between 0.23 and 0.88, which are above the common cutoff threshold of 0.1 (Hair et al. 2005). The VIFs of all variables are less than five. A usual threshold for VIFs is 10.0, corresponding to a tolerance of 0.1. Therefore, we conclude that multicollinearity is not a significant concern in our models.

Our empirical analysis employs a propensity score matching approach, which constructs a matched pair set of hospitals, one from attesting hospitals (i.e., treatment hospitals) and the other from their peer non-attesting hospitals (i.e., control hospitals) having similar pre-attestation breaches and other observable factors. Subsequently, we conduct a DID estimation on the resulting matched sample to investigate the impact of meaningful-use attestation on subsequent security breaches.

## Estimating Propensity Scores

Our propensity score analysis aims to discern the two groups (those that achieved meaningful use and those that did not) that are similar in organizational covariates, such as bed size, operating expense, academic type, hospital applications, and breaches prior to meaningful-use attestation. We conduct the propensity score analysis using a logit model, because a hospital’s meaningful-use attestation is binary. The logit model generates a hospital’s propensity score by estimating how the hospital’s organizational covariates affect its propensity to achieve meaningful use. The dependent variable, meaningful-use attestation (MUAttest), is a dummy, coded as one at year t if a hospital successfully attested to its meaningful use in that year

## Developing Matched DID Samples

Next, using each hospital’s propensity score (for meaningfuluse attestation), we match the treatment and control hospitals. Matching is used to construct a quasi-control group based on characteristics similar to those of the treatment group (Heckman et al. 1998). Although many matching algorithms are available, we employ the optimal pair matching technique by restricting absolute match differences to 0.003 among hospitals with at least 3 years of consecutive observations. This technique allows us to find each distinct matched pair by minimizing the total absolute difference between the propensity scores in a matched pair (Rosenbaum 2002; Rosenbaum and Rubin 1985).

Given our research purpose of determining the impact of certification mechanisms over time, we separately construct matched DID samples for both short- and long-term analyses. This categorization allows us to identify increasing or decreasing rates over time following meaningful-use attestation. The matched samples include data breaches that occurred over a short term (1–12 months) and over the following term (13–24 months) from (attestation) time t.<sup>19</sup> We employed a 12-month unit of time, because meaningful-use audits for each hospital are conducted annually. If a hospital fails to attest, it can apply for a future audit in the following year. Additionally, HIMSS gathers data from hospitals on an annual basis.

For the short-term analysis, we were able to satisfactorily match 850 treatment hospitals among 2,743 attesting hospitals to equivalent control hospitals, yielding a matched sample of 6,028 observations. In this matched sample, 161 hospitals attested in 2011, 458 in 2012, and 231 in 2013. For the longterm analysis, we limited the sample to pairs whose treatment hospitals attested in 2011 and 2012 to encompass the longterm period (24 months) of post-treatment data. The resulting matched sample for the long-term effect includes 4,424 observations, with 619 treatment hospitals attesting in 2011 and 2012.

Next, we conduct t-tests within the total sample and the matched samples for the short- and long-term effects to ensure the quality of the propensity score matching. The t-tests examine whether our matching is consistent with the underlying assumption that observable covariates are balanced between the treatment and control groups in the pre- and postmatching condition. The results (see Table 4) indicate that none of the covariates used for propensity score matching differ significantly between treatment and control groups, implying statistical balance between them. We further compare the difference between the two groups’ propensity scores to check whether there is substantial overlap in the characteristics of the hospitals that attested to meaningful use and those that did not. For this purpose, we perform a visual analysis of the propensity score distributions through boxplots (Figure 1). In the total sample (before matching), the distributions of the two groups’ propensity scores show a distinct difference. In the matched groups (after matching), they are nearly identical, providing evidence of common support.

## DID Model Specification

We begin a DID analysis by checking the correlations between the variables in the matched samples, and there is no indication of multicollinearity (Appendix C and D). Our DID analysis compares the changes in external, accidental, and malicious internal breaches between the pre- and postattestation periods in the treatment and control groups. The control group comprises hospitals that are very similar to the treatment group hospitals in all respects except for the fact that they had not yet attested to meaningful-use (in contrast to the treatment hospitals that had attested). This approach permits the two groups to retain their baseline levels of unobservable factors between the pre- and post-attestation periods for each outcome (Greene 2011; Wooldridge 2010, 2012). If important unobservable factors differed between treatment and control hospitals, the effect of meaningful-use attestation on the treatment hospitals’ changes in breaches during the post period would have been indistinguishable from those of the control hospitals’ changes. Thus, the changes in breaches between the pre- and post-attestation periods can be plausibly attributed to meaningful-use attestation. where i denotes a hospital, and t denotes a time of each observation. $T R E A T _ { i }$ is a treatment dummy variable (that equals one if a hospital is in the treatment group and zero if a hospital is in the control group), and $A f t e r M U _ { i t }$ is a dummy variable denoting meaningful-use attestation, which takes a value of zero or one for the pre- and post-attestation periods, respectively. Based on a treatment hospital’s attestation date, its (paired) control hospital also has zero or one for $A f t e r M U _ { i t } .$ The main parameter of interest is $\beta _ { 3 } .$ , which captures the changes in breaches for treatment hospitals between the preand post-attestation periods relative to the changes in control hospitals (that do not attest to the meaningful use of EHRs). The pre-attestation period remains the same in the short- and long-term analysis, because a hospital’s post-attestation period depends on its $A f t e r M U _ { i t } , X _ { i t }$ represents a vector of control variables (e.g., hospital information, healthcare systems) with Θ being their corresponding estimated coefficients.

Additionally, in the DID models, early and late adopters are controlled by dummy variables. Recall that meaningful-use attestation commenced in 2011. Thus, we employ two dummy variables for hospital i’s attestation time: MU2012 and MU2013 . If a hospital attested in 2012 (or 2013), MU2012 (or MU2013) is set to one, otherwise zero.

For the DID analysis, our models include fixed effects. Fixed-effect estimation is often superior to other approaches (e.g., pooled OLS or random effects) in applications where policy adoption is determined by pre-adoption attributes that also affect outcomes (Wooldridge 2010, p. 315). In Equation (2), $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ may or may not contain an unobserved effect. The interaction term combining TREAT and $A f t e r M U _ { i t }$ is the policy adoption indicator of interest, and $X _ { i t }$ are control variables that might be correlated with the policy variables (i.e., TREAT<sub>i</sub> and $A f t e r M U _ { i t } )$ . For consistency of fixed effects, we assume $\mathrm { E } ( X _ { i t } ^ { \prime } \left( \varepsilon _ { i t } - { \overline { { \varepsilon } } } _ { i t } \right) ) = 0$ . This assumption implies that each element of $X _ { i t } ,$ particularly the policy variables, may be correlated with $\varepsilon _ { i t } .$ FE requires for consistency that the policy variables be uncorrelated with deviations $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ from the average over the time period. Thus, a policy variable, such as meaningful-use attestation, can be systematically related to the persistent component of the error term, $\varepsilon _ { i t } ^ { \phantom { } }$ as measured by $\varepsilon _ { i t } .$

## Results

For robustness, we estimate a series of alternative models. Models (1) and (2) include fixed effects. Model (1) eliminates the lagged dependent variables and includes only control variables in hospital information and healthcare IT. Model (2) contains all variables. Models (1) and (2) cluster standard errors by hospital to account for heteroscedasticity and nonindependence among observations from hospitals across years. Model (3) employs a logit model to test nonlinear cases. We find that all models yield coefficients on the hypothesized variables with significance levels nearly identical to one another. The overall results are consistent across the models.

Table 5 presents the results for external breaches. Treatment hospitals observe a significant reduction of external breaches in the short term (–0.021 a $p < 0 . 1$ and –0.15 at p < 0.05 in Models (1) and (2)), but no further reductions in the long term. The results from the logistic regression (Model (3)) are consistent with these results (–1.096 at p < 0.01). Adopting the security measures dictated by the meaningful-use requirements leads to stronger defense against external breaches. While this analysis supports H1, our long-term analysis indicates that meaningful-use attestation may not be sufficient to defend against ever-changing external threats over time.

External threats are dynamic and difficult to predict. Thus, it is not surprising that the impact of meaningful-use is most apparent in the year after attestation (helping with well-known vulnerabilities) but shows little additional progress in the subsequent year (involving newer, evolving threats).

Table 6 indicates that the discovery of accidental internal breaches increases in the short term (0.015 at $p < 0 . 1$ and 0.017 at $p < 0 . 0 5$ in Models (1) and (2)). However, they significantly decrease in the long term (–0.018 and –0.017 at $p < 0 . 1$ in Models (1) and (2)). The results from the logistic regression (Model (3)) are consistent with these findings (0.998 at $p < 0 . 0 1$ and –0.565 at $p < 0 . 0 5$ in the short- and long-term). Keep in mind that both detection and prevention influence this outcome. In the short term, meaningful-use attestation procedures seem to aid the systematic discovery of accidental disclosures, often missed before attestation. Over time, hospitals see reductions in accidental breaches. Improved security practices and overall improvement in EHR usage (as indicated by attesting meaningful use) make it more likely that hospitals would more successfully discover (and comply with reporting) accidental breaches and learn how to mitigate the breaches. This result supports H2 by finding the long-term effect on the prevention of accidental breaches. Finally, we observe that meaningful-use attestation does not have any significant short- or long-term effects on malicious internal breaches (see Table 7).

So far, we have separately tested the effect of meaningful-use attestation in the short- and long-term. We further examined overall net changes in breaches in the cumulative period, which combined the short-and long-term periods. Table 8 reports changes in three types of breaches over the cumulative period. The results indicate that meaningful-use attestation leads to an overall reduction of external breaches (–0.026 at $p < 0 . 0 1$ and –0.027 at $p < 0 . 1 \AA$ ). However, for accidental breaches, the net effect of increased discovery and actual reductions cancel, and the net change is insignificant. Prior to meaningful use, many organizations did not carefully account for accidental leakages (e.g., disposal errors, physical loss of IT-related assets). Under meaningful use, hospitals were required to place more attention on accidental losses and report those losses. This explains the short-term increase in discovered and reported accidental breaches. With learning, those losses decreased in the longer term. However, the net effect over the cumulative period (increased discovery and overall reductions) was insignificant.

## Robustness Check

We conduct a robustness check to confirm the results of the DID analysis by incorporating pre-trends of dependent variables (i.e., breaches). Our DID analysis is based on the assumption that meaningful-use attestation is not coincident with other discontinuous changes in hospital characteristics that would affect breaches. Matched hospitals that are of similar size, health IT adoption, and other organizational characteristics are assumed to be on parallel trajectories (in terms of breaches) prior to meaningful-use attestation. In order to confirm this assumption, we conduct an additional test with pre-trends of external, accidental, and malicious internal breaches (Agha 2014). ## Implications and Conclusions

This study has examined the effects of a certification regime on information security. Given the diverse trends and traits of data breaches, we proposed hypotheses concerning the temporal effects of meaningful-use attestation on data breaches. Our empirical models demonstrate that the effect of meaningful-use attestation varies with the nature of a breach, and the effect changes over time.

Our study shows that attesting hospitals observed decreases in external breaches in the year after attestation. However, they did not see any further reductions in the subsequent year. The extent to which the guidance and standards of meaningful use are effective in reducing external breaches is unclear, particularly in light of new technologies and evolving security threats. For compliance assessment, the security practices required by meaningful-use attestation are grounded in wellknown security vulnerabilities. However, the unpredictable and changing nature of external attacks erodes year-over-year improvements achieved. Our findings suggest that continued progress toward preventing external breaches requires ongoing efforts beyond achieving Stage 1 meaningful use.

On the other hand, we found that hospitals discovered and reported more accidental breaches in the year following attestation; however, these reported breaches decreased in the long term. Organizations are often unaware of inadvertent disclosures without standardized and systematic review procedures. Prior to meaningful use, such breaches often went unreported. Through attestation processes, hospitals discover their vulnerabilities and monitor them through periodic audits (i.e., access and incident tracking reports). As noted earlier, whereas external breaches are attributed to a lack of technical solutions (e.g., antivirus, encryption, firewall, intrusion detection, spam/spyware filter, user authentication), accidental breaches typically result from “human factor” errors (Verizon 2013). Given that one of the goals of the meaningful-use program is to encourage the implementation of appropriate security mechanisms, the systematic and standardized approach of meaningful-use attestation enables identification of inadvertent breaches by tracking human errors. This tracking ability ultimately leads to actual data protection over time, because hospitals learn their vulnerable points through ongoing tracking (and more so than from any particular prior breach). Moreover, meaningful-use standards help hospitals integrate EHRs into their workflows more effectively, thus reducing the need to move data into unsecure formats like spreadsheets, which are easily copied onto vulnerable storage devices—leading to a reduction of low-tech incidents.

Unsurprisingly, we did not find an association between meaningful-use attestation and malicious internal breaches. This makes sense because the security provisions required for meaningful-use attestation focus on implementing security safeguards but do not address other issues beyond security. While external and accidental breaches are linked to security issues that can be addressed by meaningful use, malicious insider threats are often more closely associated with organizational and personnel issues. Malicious insiders are motivated by many factors, from greed to personnel actions such as being passed over for a promotion, not getting an expected bonus, or the threat of being fired. Such human resource factors and surrounding social issues beyond security are likely larger drivers of insider attacks. To approach the malicious insider problem, organizations must address psychological as well as diverse organizational and contextual factors.

While the main purpose of our research is to identify the short- and long-term effects of a certification regime on security performance, we further examined the cumulative effect of a certification regime over an extended period combining the short-and long-term periods. Over this extended period, we also observed an association between meaningfuluse attestation and decreased external breaches. However, we found little net impact on the reported accidental breaches over the cumulative period. As noted earlier, meaningful-use attestation led to an initial spike in discovered and reported accidental breaches followed by a subsequent decrease. Thus, the net effect on reported breaches over the cumulative period was negligible. It is important to note that this is not necessarily a disappointing outcome. It shows improved detection of accidental losses over time. Security researchers have long pointed out that organizations often focus on preventing external attacks rather than internal losses (Liu et al. 2009; McFadzean et al. 2007). If organizations focus on external attacks, then they place much of their security investment on protecting the network boundaries from external attackers, and little attention to inadvertent losses. Historically, the healthcare sector has lagged other industries in security technology adoption, and thus meaningful-use attestation has facilitated a quick reduction of external breaches. It has also led healthcare providers to focus on the detection and prevention of accidental disclosures. Finally, we note that we did not observe any association between meaningful use and malicious internal breaches.

Our study makes theoretical and practical contributions to certification and information security. First, our results advance the understanding of the effects of certification mechanisms on organizational performance. Meaningful-use attestation represents a certification regime viewed as a means of solving moral hazard problems in the economics literature. Drawing upon prior research in economics, operations, and information security, we empirically demonstrated that the impact of certification mechanisms related to information security varies with the nature of breaches and the time frame.

As relatively low-hanging fruit, (well-known) external breaches significantly decreased in the short term, but continued progress was dulled—likely because threats evolve over time. This result suggests that the impact of a certification regime has a short life span on the prevention of external attackers, whose techniques are rapidly eclipsed by the next hacker innovations. As effective defenses against such attacks require out-of-the-box solutions, policy makers should strive for policies that include rolling updates to attestation requirements in accordance with the evolving security environment.

On the other hand, a certification regime does affect accidental internal breaches over time by aiding both detection and avoidance: more losses are discovered at first leading to better long-term avoidance. Through certification mechanisms, organizations learn from detection and correction of inadvertent internal errors and eventually achieve the prevention of such inadvertent security incidents. Unlike the prevention of rapidly changing external threats, inadvertent mistakes or negligence are rectified by well-established security practices. Because a lack of awareness by insiders has been a major cause in accidental exposure, the effective assimilation of security policies is critical. Therefore, certification mechanisms for inadvertent security behavior must enhance effective assimilation procedures such as induction training coupled with annual refreshers through e-learning courses.

Overall, meaningful-use attestation helps healthcare providers establish technical and administrative safeguards. However, without continued security investment and rolling updates, static security certification mechanisms will become less effective due to a dynamic security landscape. A certification regime is a useful strategy for laggards, who have fallen behind standard practice. Maintaining top security requires addressing a complex combination of human and organizational factors along with the most recent hardware and software. Thus, policy makers should encourage holistic tactics.

While this study provides theoretical and practical insights on security programs, it has several limitations. First, our sample is limited to hospitals with complete data from the HIMSS database. The HIMSS database is extensive, but the data are self-reported and missing for some periods. Second, our analysis focused on the effects in the first and second year after attestation. However, our results address the core effect of Stage 1 meaningful use, and the analysis is not compromised by the adoption of Stage 2 (that started in 2014). Nonetheless, data covering longer periods and advanced stages would provide more insights and offer opportunities for future research.
