## Abstract

Digital transformation is often described as organizational change that is simultaneously triggered and enabled by digital technologies. As with other types of organizational transformation, overcoming organizational inertia lies at the heart of digital transformation. However, our understanding of the specific processes employed by incumbent firms to overcome organizational inertia in digital transformation is currently limited. In this paper, we draw on the case study of AsiaBank, a large traditional bank in Asia, to explore the microfoundations of how incumbent firms tackle different types of inertia as they embark on a digital transformation journey. We identify four key digital transformation processes—i.e., embracing the consumerization of digital technologies, diffusing and appropriating digital business practices, enabling distributed organizing, and revamping IT architecture—that combine to reduce negative psychology, sociocognitive, sociotechnical, political, and economic inertia in digital transformation. Our findings expand the extant view on the role of agency in overcoming organizational inertia and contribute to the literature at the intersection of digital innovation and transformation.

Keywords: Digital Transformation, IT Transformation, Organizational Inertia, IT Consumerization, Digital Innovation

## 1 Introduction

To respond to shifts in the external environment brought about by the widespread diffusion of digital technologies, incumbent firms are increasingly embarking on digital transformation (Sebastian et al., 2017). Viewed as a profound organizational change process, digital transformation is distinct from ITenabled organizational transformation in its impetus, scope, outcomes, and, most importantly, technology (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Wessel, 2021). Unlike traditional enterprise information technologies, digital technologies are characterized by a modular-layered architecture that affords generative environments that are infrastructural, combinatorial, and servitized in nature (Piccoli et al., 2022; Yoo et al., 2010). As a result, the impetus for digital transformation and the ensuing changes are usually not limited by the firm boundary but span an ecosystem of partners and customers (Hanelt et al., 2015). Similarly, outcomes of digital transformation expand beyond process and practice-level changes and often include shifts in business models and value creation paths (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Vial, 2019).

Despite its distinct nature, digital transformation, like any organizational transformation, implies the need to overcome organizational inertia that slows organizational responses to changing environmental conditions, thus threatening the firm’s long-term survival (Hannan & Freeman, 1984; Rumelt, 1995; Schmid et al., 2017). Prior research has identified the main types of inertia, including negative psychology, sociocognitive, sociotechnical, political, and economic inertia (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Studies also link inertia to key elements of the firm’s deep structure, showing how embeddedness in existing knowledge, values, routines, technology, organizational structure, control systems, and distribution of power generates rigidities that hinder change and increase the likelihood of negative transformation outcomes (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017; Gersick, 1991; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). Indeed, the extant literature provides a solid foundation for understanding the antecedents, moderators, and consequences of organizational inertia (Colombo & Delmastro, 2002; Fairhurst et al., 1995; Gilbert, 2005; Hannan & Freeman, 1984; Haskamp et al., 2021; König et al., 2021; Rumelt, 1995; Schwarz, 2012; Tripsas & Gavetti, 2000). At the same time, indepth empirical examinations of the process for overcoming inertia and the role of agency in this process remain scarce (Mikalef et al., 2018, 2019; Schmid, 2019; Schmid et al., 2017).

Considering the gap in our general understanding of the process for overcoming organizational inertia and the new context of digital transformation, as outlined above, this paper addresses the following research question: How do members of an incumbent firm exercise agency to overcome organizational inertia in digital transformation? We draw upon a case study of digital transformation at AsiaBank (name disguised for confidentiality), a large traditional bank in Asia seeking to adapt to the shifts in customer expectations and competition in its home markets while launching new digital value propositions in growth markets. In analyzing AsiaBank’s transformational journey over a seven-year period, we theorize agency as four core digital transformation processes and explain how each process—and the interaction between the processes— contribute to overcoming different types of organizational inertia.

Our findings extend the literature on digital transformation (e.g., Agarwal et al., 2010; Andal-Ancion et al., 2003; Berman, 2012; Matt et al., 2015; Vial, 2019; Zhu et al., 2006) as well as research on the role of inertia in IT-enabled organizational transformation (e.g., Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017; Besson & Rowe, 2012; Haskamp et al., 2021). In particular, we show how the ubiquitous accessibility of digital technologies and ensuing IT consumerization (Gregory et al., 2018) shape organizational efforts to overcome inertia in digital transformation. Our model also sheds light on the theoretical relationship between IT transformation (Gregory et al., 2015), digital transformation (Vial, 2019; Wessel, 2021), and digital innovation in incumbent firms (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Svahn et al., 2017).

## 2 Conceptual Background

## 2.1 Digital Transformation

There is an ongoing scholarly debate about the unique characteristics and nature of digital transformation (Drechsler et al., 2020; Prügl & Spitzley, 2020; Vial, 2019; Wessel, 2021). The cumulative body of knowledge in this area highlights several key points, which we discuss below.

First, the impetus for digital transformation is not a single top-down organizational decision related to the adoption and implementation of enterprise technology (e.g., the decision to adopt and implement a new ERP system to transform business processes) but a series of distributed organizational decisions and actions aimed at responding to complex societal and industry trends (Drechsler et al., 2020). This unique characteristic of digital transformation is rooted in the consumerization of digital technologies, a process whereby the increasing accessibility, affordability, and availability of digital technologies (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002; Yoo, 2010) change the expectations and practices of consumer-workers and consumer-customers (Gregory et al., 2018). As a result, the decision-making dynamics during digital transformation change from a stable top-down pattern toward a triad of outside-in, bottom-up, and top-down dynamics.

Second, the design and governance of the technologies involved in digital transformation exhibit new properties, including generativity, malleability, dual use by consumers and enterprises, open access, and shared ownership (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Moschella et al., 2004; Tilson et al., 2010). These new properties challenge commonly held assumptions about technologies developed and used in the enterprise context in the past to drive IT-enabled organizational change, including standardization of the application layer, complexity and inflexibility to change, control by the corporate IT function, exclusive access, and proprietary ownership (Gregory et al., 2015; Widjaja & Gregory, 2020). Thus, digital transformation brings about a strategic shift from IT to digital resources (Piccoli et al., 2022).

Third, the scope of organizational change triggered by the widespread diffusion of digital technologies and shaped by digital resources can be profound, extending beyond business processes and involving fundamental changes in the entire value network, including customers, suppliers, and partners, and often also redefining the firm’s role and identity within the reconfigured network (Vial, 2019). While such an expanded scope of organizational change has been observed under certain conditions before (Venkatraman, 1994), a literature review by Gregory Vial highlights that in the context of digital transformation, the broad scope has become the new normal rather than the exception (Vial, 2019).

Fourth, related to outcomes, digital transformation is typically associated with shifts in firm performance achieved through changes in value creation paths (Vial, 2019), a general observation that extends even to less knowledge-intensive and more traditional industrial manufacturing and production contexts (Hanelt et al., 2015). This contrasts with previous ITenabled organizational transformation, which, more often than not, is associated with reengineered business processes that do not necessarily seek to reinvent the existing business model of the focal company (Sarker et al., 2006).

In sum, the unique characteristics of many digital transformation initiatives invite a fresh perspective and empirical research on the process of digital transformation. Central to this process is the core problem of overcoming inertia, which we discuss next.

## 2.2 Inertia and Organizational Transformation

Defined broadly as the inability of an established organization to enact sufficient degrees of internal change in the face of environmental shifts, inertia is a core concept in the literature on organizational transformation (Hannan & Freeman, 1984; König et al., 2021; Miller & Friesen, 1980; Tushman & Romaneli, 1985). Rumelt (1995) describes inertia as the lack of plasticity in an organization, resulting in the strong persistence of existing organizational form and function. He famously points out that the problem of overcoming inertia constitutes the essential question of organizational transformation. Besson and Rowe (2012) build upon this idea to position inertia, stemming from the stickiness of the organization’s deep structure, as the central problem of IS-enabled organizational transformation. In their synthesis of related research, Besson and Rowe (2012) identify five types (or dimensions) of inertia: negative psychology inertia, sociocognitive inertia, sociotechnical inertia, economic inertia, and political inertia (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017).

Negative psychology inertia concerns a natural human tendency to stay in the “comfort zone” and be skeptical, in denial, or fearful of learning (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Although often conceptualized at the individual level (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017), negative psychology inertia also operates at the level of a social collective. Insofar as an organization can be seen as a collection of people and agreements that govern their interactions (Cyert & March, 1963), denial and fear of learning can become systemic. This might happen, for example, when senior leaders openly and repeatedly undermine the importance of learning or demonstrate a fear of it. Because other organizational members look up to senior leaders for guidance and direction, such behaviors reinforce anxiety and resistance to change at lower levels. Silva and Hirschheim (2007) demonstrate how senior leadership’s open skepticism regarding the need to learn and adapt creates major obstacles to the successful implementation of strategic information systems. In the context of discontinuous change, negative psychology inertia at the organizational level often manifests as a tendency to perceive change as a threat rather than an opportunity (Gilbert, 2005, 2006; König et al., 2021).

Sociocognitive inertia describes the organization’s reduced ability to enact internal change because of the stickiness of established norms and values held by organizational members (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Core beliefs and values regarding the organization, its employees, and its environment comprise the foundational layer of the deep structure and are particularly difficult to change (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017). During evolutionary periods, these beliefs ensure the stability and continuity of organizational life, while during revolutionary periods they are a major source of rigidity (Gersick, 1991). Organizational identity, or shared beliefs about what constitutes core attributes and therefore what is legitimate for an organization, has been identified as a particularly salient subset of values and norms in the context of technology-triggered transformation (Tripsas, 2009). A need to change organizational identity has also been singled out as a key characteristic of digital transformation (Tripsas, 2009; Wessel, 2021).

A third type of inertia discussed in the literature is sociotechnical inertia. Described in terms of technological path dependencies and the embeddedness of actors in sociotechnical systems (Besson & Rowe, 2012), as Gilbert explains (2005), this inertia type concerns core resource and routine rigidities. Resource rigidity stems from path dependencies created by the firm’s prior investments in assets and technologies. In this vein, the IS literature identifies inflexibility and complexity of enterprise IT as factors contributing to IT resource rigidity (Schmid et al., 2017). Routine rigidity concerns persistence in patterns of organizational activities reinforced through repeated practices and structural embeddedness (Gilbert, 2005). In today’s organizations, these patterns of activities are highly intertwined with the characteristics of the technologies in use (Orlikowski, 1996). Previous IS research also highlights the interdependent nature of routine and technological resource rigidity within sociotechnical inertia. For example, business process workflows (i.e., routines), when enacted through enterprise software (i.e., resources), can jointly contribute to organizational inertia (Berente et al., 2016; Sarker et al., 2006).

Political inertia, the fourth type of inertia described in the literature on IT-enabled transformation, is associated with vested interests that, according to Besson and Rowe (2012), may hamper the organization’s ability to enact sufficient degrees of internal change in the face of external change. Audzeyeva and Hudson (2017) explain how political inertia is related to the distribution of power within an organization. The authors find that hidden political agendas and interests of managers in the top and middle layers of the hierarchy may have a destructive effect on organizational transformation driven by business intelligence. This suggests that the hierarchy, with its centralized command-and-control structures dominated by managers pursuing their own interests and agendas, may significantly limit actions and decisions for a large number of organizational members (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Such limitations and the ensuing lack of involvement can be particularly detrimental in digital transformation contexts characterized by high uncertainty and complexity of transformation paths. It has been argued that in these contexts, everyone needs to act as a sensor to increase the speed of internal information processing and structural change (Bernstein et al., 2016).

Finally, the literature identifies economic inertia, which stems from agents’ embeddedness in existing paths of value creation (Besson & Rowe, 2012) that, in turn, shape patterns of resource allocation between exploration and exploitation activities (Priem et al., 2013). These paths and patterns are typically very difficult to change because of their direct connection to control systems, another key element of the deep structure (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017). Control systems enable organizations to hire, train, promote, and retain staff in ways that typically reinforce the established models of value creation and capture (Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). Control systems also directly interface with products, markets, technology, and competitive timing, as explained by Audzeyeva and Hudson (2017), and therefore play a critical role in shaping decisions about the firm’s strategic competitive moves. Incremental improvement in existing business models may not require major changes in control systems, but significant business model innovation, with its need to invest in new talent and capabilities coupled with result uncertainty, usually does.

The role of the deep structure in generating organizational inertia, as discussed above, suggests that the main types of inertia will likely be present in digital transformation, as they are in other types of organizational transformation. At the same time, considering the unique characteristics of digital transformation with regard to its focus on digital resources (Piccoli et al., 2022) and the impetus and scope of changes extending beyond the organizational boundary (Vial, 2019), the process for tackling inertia in digital transformation warrants further investigation.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Research Design and Case Selection

Given the lack of substantive theory about the process of overcoming organizational inertia in digital transformation, we opted for an exploratory case research design aimed at building new theory (Gerring, 2007; Sarker et al., 2013; Yin, 2014) through the use of grounded theory techniques (Birks et al., 2013). The research commenced in 2015 when we secured access to AsiaBank, one of the largest banks in Asia, which had embarked on an organization-wide transformation program to respond to the fast-paced changes in consumer behaviors and competition brought about by digital technologies.

In selecting the case of AsiaBank for this study, we followed the extreme-case selection technique (Gerring, 2007), wherein a case is chosen because it exhibits an unusually high manifestation of themes concerning the research question. To this end, our prestudy conversations with AsiaBank’s senior and middle managers as well as on-site observations revealed: (1) significant volume and diversity of organizational activity aimed at developing new technologies and work practices, such as customer journey mapping, and rearchitecting legacy IT systems, and (2) a wide range of initiatives pursuing new value propositions, such as developing “the branch of the future” or creating an online community of SME owners. We viewed these, respectively, as manifestations of exercising agency to overcome organizational inertia and developing new value creation paths—both key elements of digital transformation (Vial, 2019) salient to our research question. These considerations led us to conclude that the AsiaBank case offered a unique theory-building opportunity.

All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and imported into Atlas.ti for further analysis. ## 3.3 Data Analysis

Emergence was the key overarching principle we followed in analyzing the data. It stipulates that new theoretical relationships and frameworks must fit the data under study, conceptually earn their way through relevance, and meaningfully explain the behavior under study (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). To ensure the principle of emergence, we relied on constant comparisons, iterative coding, and theoretical sampling (Birks et al., 2013), as explained below.

The participating researchers played different yet complementary roles in the processes of data collection and analysis. The first author focused on indepth field immersion to ensure rich data collection. He managed the relationship with the case organization and engaged study participants to share their real-life experiences and observations concerning the ongoing digital transformation journey at AsiaBank. The first author also led the coding of the entire data set throughout multiple iterations and proposed initial ideas for the conceptualization and interpretation of findings.

The two other researchers helped develop these ideas by reviewing the coding and giving constant detailed feedback on the emerging analysis and conceptualization. In this process, the second author had also become deeply immersed in the data by closely supporting the coding process and the development of the key findings and the model. The third author played the important role of “devil’s advocate” by challenging the emergent theory proposed by the first two authors over several iterations. In doing so, the third author contributed to the management of bias in the rigorous application of the qualitative approach (Birks et al., 2013).

Throughout the process of data analysis, we made constant comparisons (Glaser, 1978) of different data slices to make sense of the key relationships and gaps. For example, comparing data about the transformation of legacy IT systems drawn from the interviews with IT function leaders and data about active experimentation with the digital banking solution drawn from interviews with the Digital Bank staff helped us conceptualize datafication as one of the mechanisms for revamping IT architecture (see Figure 1). To make the process of constant comparison systematic, we relied on the extensive use of memos, including notes, diagrams, whiteboard drawings, and presentation slides (Glaser, 1978). The memos allowed the multi-author research team to develop a triangulated shared understanding of data, define relations between emerging categories, and spot gaps to guide further data collection.

We employed the theoretical sampling technique (Glaser, 1978; Urquhart, 2013) to conduct Waves 2 and 3 of data collection (see Table 2). Wave 2 aimed to expand upon categories and relationships that emerged through the analysis of Wave 1 data—for example, the three digital business practices of outside-in problem identification, the open resource mobilization, and the evidential tradeoff resolution (see Figure 1). Similarly, Wave 3 sought to develop a more nuanced conceptualization of the relationships between core categories that emerged through the analysis of Wave 2 data, such as the link between diffusing digital business practices, revamping IT architecture, and enabling distributed organizing. In this wave, we also focused more closely on understanding the outcomes of AsiaBank’s transformation. Overall, theoretical sampling thus supported the systematic buildup of our theoretical model and ensured the principle of emergence (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

In our iterative coding process, we followed the Glaserian approach of starting with open coding (i.e., coding the data in every way possible), proceeding to selective coding (i.e., applying the concept-indicator model to develop and focus on key emergent categories), and then moving to theoretical coding (i.e., conceptualizing how the identified concepts relate to each other to construct a theoretical model that explains the phenomenon) (Glaser, 1978). During open coding, we stayed close to the data, focusing on observable events and experiences shared by the informants. This produced the first-order descriptive themes often captured in the exact language of the study participants (e.g., “customer jobs-to-be-done” was the language used widely by our informants in describing the outside-in approach to defining salient business problems). During selective coding, we sought to abstract and combine the descriptive first-order themes to identify core categories related to the phenomenon of digital transformation (e.g., “encouraging employees to use the bank’s digital products as consumers” contributed to “framing digital as opportunity”). To facilitate better presentation of a parsimonious grounded theory, we followed Gioia’s (2013) recommendation and abstracted second-order themes into aggregate dimensions, producing the final data structure shown in Figure 1. In line with the “how” nature of the research question, our selective coding efforts focused on identifying salient processes (e.g., embracing, diffusing, enabling, revamping). “A process is something which occurs over time and involves change” (Glaser, 1978, p. 97), and coding for processes is particularly suitable when one seeks to identify patterns of behavior (Glaser, 1978). In this vein, the process of “enabling distributed organizing,” for example, unfolded over several years and involved changes in organizational structure through boundary spanning and changes in employee roles through greater autonomy (see Figure 1). Finally, during theoretical coding, we sought to explain the relationships among the abstracted concepts (i.e., second-order themes and aggregate dimensions in Figure 1), constructing the theoretical model and case narrative presented in the following section.

## 4 Case Description and Analysis

## 4.1 Case Synopsis

AsiaBank was established in the late 1960s, with the mandate to provide long-term financing to manufacturing industries and to support the development of new industries in its home market. By 2009, the bank had grown to become one of the largest banks in Asia, offering services to both consumer and institutional clients and employing more than 20,000 employees across 18 regional markets. In its home markets, AsiaBank enjoyed an undisputed leadership position in all key business areas and boasted an extensive network of cutting-edge branches and other physical touchpoints. Outside the home markets, the bank’s focus had historically been on wealth, corporate, and SME banking.

Our analysis covers a seven-year period of digital transformation initiatives that involved virtually all organizational units of AsiaBank. The bank’s decision to embark on digital transformation was triggered by changes in customer behaviors and expectations, manifesting first and foremost in low customer satisfaction and increasing pressure from nontraditional competitors such as fintechs and pureplay digital platforms.

In Case Years 1 through 3, the transformation efforts focused on improving customer experience and satisfaction in the home markets while also making the bank’s internal processes more robust and efficient. Initiatives, such as Processes Improvement Events and Customer Journey Design events, sought to develop a set of shared values and methodologies for AsiaBank’s employees to draw upon in redesigning the bank’s offerings to clients.

Starting in Case Year 2, AsiaBank embarked on a massive revamp of its IT infrastructure, commonly referred to as “lean IT transformation.” This effort, which had taken two years to complete, involved scrapping several ongoing “megaprojects” and refocusing IT resources on three key priorities: building a resilient technology infrastructure (“better than the cloud”), ensuring nimbleness of the core banking applications across markets (consistent and flexible platform), and enabling digital innovation to explore new business opportunities (new digital solutions in home markets).

In Case Years 4 through 6, AsiaBank established a new business unit, Digital Bank. The main goal of Digital Bank was to design, develop, and roll out a new digital banking solution for retail customers in Growth Market X (name disguised for confidentiality). The unit was given significant decision-making and resource autonomy and was staffed with the bank’s best internal talent as well as external talent with experience in start-ups, digital technology, and design. In preparation for the launch, the Digital Bank team also had to develop a local partner network to help acquire customers and ensure a seamless banking experience with minimal physical infrastructure. The new solution entered the market at the end of Year 6 and gained significant momentum in terms of customers, transactions, and account balances during the next four months.

In parallel with the launch of Digital Bank, a broader effort to help AsiaBank “embrace the digital world” (one of the three strategic priorities according to the bank’s Year 5 Annual Report) was put in place. In the second half of Year 5, the bank launched its first talent hackathon—an immersive learning experience where the bank’s executive talents were mixed with members of startups to work on business challenges. The teams were given three days to generate initial ideas, develop a prototype, iterate based on several rounds of customer feedback, and—by the end of day three— pitch their solution to the bank’s top executives. In the next six months, the bank held three more hackathonstyle events, engaging, in total, more than 200 vice presidents and senior vice presidents along with 40 startups. Fifty prototypes were created, out of which two were integrated in the new digital banking solution for Growth Market X, while 10 more had secured funding and entered an accelerator program.

In Case Year 7, the final year of the case, several additional digital transformation initiatives were launched. Having scaled its digital banking solution in Growth Market X, the bank decided to replicate this approach in Growth Market Y (name disguised for confidentiality). A new organizational structure akin to Digital Bank was established and staffed partly with key people from the existing Digital Bank and partly with new hires from inside and outside of AsiaBank. Building upon the prior experience in Growth Market X, the new team localized the offering for Growth Market Y and built a network of partners to facilitate the roll-out. Finally, in Year 7, AsiaBank launched the DataFirst initiative. Aimed at helping organizational members to see data as an asset and utilize it productively in their daily work, the initiative provided a set of policies, tools, and educational resources to facilitate data access, discovery, governance, and ownership across the bank.

In the remainder of the case analysis section, we describe the four digital transformation processes we have uncovered (see aggregate dimensions in Figure 1). For each process, we identify subprocesses (second-order themes in Figure 1), provide evidence of their diffusion across AsiaBank, and discuss the mechanisms by which each process contributed to reducing different types of organizational inertia (see Table 3 for a summary).

## 4.2 Embracing Consumerization of Digital Technologies to Reduce Negative Psychology Inertia

Changes in customer behaviors and expectations played a key role in nudging AsiaBank’s managers<sup>1</sup> out of their conform zone and leading to a decision to embark on digital transformation. These changes manifested in: (1) low customer satisfaction—AsiaBank finished last in the annual ranking of regional banks in Case Year 1, while the local taxi drivers joked that the abbreviated bank’s name stood for “extremely slow bank” (paraphrased for confidentiality), and (2) increasing pressure from nontraditional competitors such as fintechs and pure-play digital platforms:

It may not be the banks that are going to take away our business; it’s the non-banks or the newer players that are doing things differently—approaching the customer journey and the customer experience differently. (Managing Director, Consumer Banking)

We found evidence of AsiaBank’s managers purposefully building upon these developments to produce a “burning platform” narrative aimed at extending the sense of urgency to AsiaBank’s workers. In doing so, most of our informants made a concerted effort to frame changes in customer expectations and competition not as a threat, but as an opportunity. The bank’s chief operating officer summed up this sentiment:

Most banks viewed digitalization as a way to shift their existing offerings onto the Internet and mobile platforms. We thought of it as an opportunity to create new business models, enter new markets, and establish new revenue streams.

The bank’s CEO, who joined the bank from a leading global financial institution one year before the start of the case, played a key role in communicating the opportunity framing across the organization. A senior executive in charge of corporate learning and talent development recalled a speech given by the CEO during a senior management retreat held early in AsiaBank’s digital transformation journey:

He called on everyone to embrace the digital world … talk [to their subordinates] abou living in a digital world … The whole event— in terms of the content, the alignment, the time—was really saying this is the new future … That was a big signal to the 250 MDs [managing directors] in the company.

Key messages of the “speech on the beach,” as it had become widely known in the bank, echoed throughout our interviews. The phrase “embrace the digital world” was used by the informants more than 15 times. Many interpreted it as an invitation to draw on their experiences with digital technologies at home to identify opportunities for their application at work. The enjoyment and perceived self-efficacy concerning the former helped reduce the fear of the unknown related to the latter:

In terms of appetite for change … in my current job, I felt that it would not be so easy, but I thought that on a personal basis, it was actually quite easy to do it. I learned a lot about the Internet of Things … and I went to get a Hue [brand by Phillips] personalized lighting system, that could help me with reading etc. (Business Director, Risk Management & Compliance)

To further blur the boundary between the use of digital technologies at work and at home, the bank promoted the use of its new digital products, such as a peer-topeer payment service, by employees as customers:

If we get together with colleagues and we need to split a payment, say in a restaurant, we make sure everybody uses [AsiaBank’s payment service]. (Business Director)

We also found that AsiaBank’s managers took steps to foster learning orientation in order to create long-term conditions for workers to experiment with digital technologies at work. To this end, interventions aimed at fostering new work practices, such as Customer Journey Design events, deliberately avoided setting result-based goals, such as improvements in customer satisfaction or retention. Instead, the goals focused on participation and engagement in the events promoting new practices. The intention was to have workers focus on the challenge at hand to develop new knowledge and competencies without the need to worry about “hitting a target”:

We do not define how the outcome will look like upfront ... It’s very easy to drive an outcome if you start with it—customer hours, turnaround time, etc. Then everybody just goes … zoom … and everybody is very focused … you get very pointy edgy quickly … We were going to measure what you were doing by just logging … the process and asking people … give us qualitatively what … you think your measurements are. (Business Director, Agile Transformation)

By framing digital as opportunity and fostering learning orientation, AsiaBank’s managers helped reduce negative psychology inertia among the bank’s workers. In doing so, the managers sought to leverage the perceived enjoyment and high self-efficacy stemming from the workers’ experiences with digital technologies at home to flatten the learning curve and increase perceived control for the workers incorporating digital technologies into new work practices. We observed clear signs of this effect, for example, during the talent hackathons—AsiaBank’s employees frequently referred to their use of mobile phones in their personal lives, making the challenging task of building an app for the first time more accessible and enjoyable.

## 4.3 Diffusing Digital Business Practices to Reduce Sociocognitive Inertia

Both managers and workers of AsiaBank took active roles in diffusing and appropriating digital business practices, including outside-in problem identification, open resource mobilization, and evidential trade-off resolution, albeit in different capacities. Managers played a key role in designing new practices and creating conditions to allow workers to appropriate these. Workers, on their part, engaged in appropriating and enacting the new practices across AsiaBank’s departments, products, and markets. To structure our discussion, in this section, we describe the three salient digital business practices and detail the role of managers in diffusing these. We discuss how the three practices were appropriated by organizational members at AsiaBank in Section 4.6.

The practice of outside-in problem identification stipulated an approach to identifying salient business problems by focusing on responding to evolving customer needs, as opposed to optimizing the firm’s internal products and capabilities (i.e., the inside-out approach). The concept of “customer job-to-be-done,” a set of actions the customer had to perform to accomplish a critical goal, became a starting point in discussing any new project at AsiaBank. Workers were expected to always start by identifying the target customer and describing the customer’s job-to-be-done and related “pains” before any conversation about a possible solution could take place. Adopting a holistic view of the customer context, often described as a “customer journey,” was also deemed crucial as informants highlighted the importance of understanding the “entire journey,” including segments outside of the bank’s traditional business. As the managing director of Technology and Operations explained:

The job the customer needs to get done is never expressed in financial terms… Making a payment is just a small part of what the customer seeks to accomplish, yet banking has historically been interested in just that. Digital enables you to help your customer fulfill a bigger part of her goal. And that’s how we want to differentiate ourselves.

As the case progressed, we observed the practice of outside-in problem identification diffuse broadly among AsiaBank’s workers. The terms “customer jobs-to-bedone” and “customer journey” figured prominently in the interviews concerning different case events (e.g., Customer Journey Design events, Digital Bank, hackathons, and Data First initiative). Even representatives of the back-office functions, far removed from the end customer, spoke about applying the outside-in approach to better serve the needs of “internal customers.”

The practice of open resource mobilization suggested an approach to mobilizing resources to tackle salient business problems based on fostering and tapping into an open ecosystem of partners as opposed to relying exclusively on proprietary resources or those tightly governed through contractual mechanisms. This practice effectively captured the idea that in order to serve specific customer needs along the entire customer journey, it was increasingly insufficient to rely only on proprietary knowledge and resources controlled by the firm. Mobilizing an ecosystem of partners with an extended set of flexible capabilities offered a better fit for a scenario where the goal was “customer relevance.” Digital Bank, for example, relied heavily on open resource mobilization to build momentum in the vast space of Growth Market X:

We said that we wanted to be very relevant for the customer … But you don’t accomplish this by just building a mobile banking app. You don’t need Digital Bank for that. The reason why we are different is because we’re plugged into [the] ecosystem. Partnerships is what brings Digital Bank to life. (Head of Market Development & Alliances)

Another manifestation of open resource mobilization concerned AsiaBank’s decision to involve startups in the hackathons and later in the development of the most promising prototypes into market-ready products. Consistent with the logic of openness, the bank decided neither to take ownership of the startups that joined its accelerator programs nor to require exclusivity in the market use of the products.

The practice of evidential trade-off resolution sought to resolve key trade-offs arising in problem identification and resource mobilization through a concerted effort to obtain new relevant knowledge, “hard evidence,” by engaging in proactive learning by trial and error. Indeed, our informants talked much about the need to reduce uncertainty by collecting data through experimentation, positioning this approach as an alternative to making decisions based on prior experience and intuition. The head of the Customer Journey Design Program explained:

Experimentation changes the conversation, because when we run an experiment, we want to find out whether it works or not, and the success is defined as whether we have learned something.

Assimilation of the evidential trade-off resolution was by no means a smooth process. On the one hand, we observed instances where experimentation was used to bolster one’s preexisting convictions by “demonstrating why their idea is a great idea, rather than testing out the risky assumptions.” On the other hand, organization members often struggled to see how to apply the new practice in their daily work:

I find the idea of experimenting, of changing your approach based on the outcome of the little steps you take very interesting. … But then I was thinking about how to transfer this to my day-to-day and, to be honest, it’s very difficult. (Business Director, Risk Management & Compliance)

Despite the difficulties, organizational members across AsiaBank were starting to systematically incorporate elements of evidential trade-off resolution into the key decisions they had to make in their daily jobs:

It’s (about) asking questions of yourself along the way and asking, actually running experiments, along the way. (Executive Director, SME Banking)

Our data strongly suggest that AsiaBank’s managers, including the senior leadership team, took purposeful steps to design and diffuse the digital business practices of outside-in problem identification, open resource mobilization, and evidential trade-off resolution among the bank’s workers. To this end, a series of organizational interventions, including process improvement events, Customer Journey Design events, talent hackathons, and the Data First initiative, were carried out. For each intervention, senior managers communicated a clear aspirational goal to mobilize the workers. “‘Making banking joyful’ has been an unbelievable hit because it’s easy to understand,” in the words of an informant recalling the goal of the Customer Journey Design events. Workers were provided with a well-defined behavioral script to follow—for Customer Journey Design events, for example, they had to collect external and internal insights to describe the customer persona, identify critical interactions and emotions, create a future state journey, and improve it through rapid prototyping. The script was typically formalized via detailed manuals and visual aids and reinforced with coaching support. Finally, managers set learning goals (see discussion on fostering learning orientation above) to encourage workers’ engagement and immersion in the new practices.

The managers’ efforts facilitated the broad diffusion of the digital business practices throughout AsiaBank. Working together, outside-in problem identification, open resource mobilization, and evidential trade-off resolution helped reduce sociocognitive inertia by shifting the attention of AsiaBank’s organizational members away from existing products, established capabilities, and prior knowledge:

We wanted people to challenge assumptions behind the way things have always been done, and to get new products to market quicker and develop solutions that are far more customer-centric. Last year, we did 100 customer journeys across the bank, and this year [CEO] has put out the idea of a thousand experiments. It was really to make it impossible to stay where we are, and we wanted people to fundamentally question all of the assumptions that have guided us for so long and, in the last five years, made us very successful. (Chief Innovation Officer)

Focusing on “what the customer needs” through Customer Journey Design events helped dilute the dominant inside-out “banking” identity. This shift was evident in both growth (e.g., Digital Bank) and home (e.g., consumer banking) markets:

We are convinced, most people would happily forget about banking. What they cannot forget about is money. And so, we felt we had to figure out how to embed the four “jobs to be done” concerning money in people’s daily experience in a seamless and invisible way. Our motto for developing the new solution was “less banking and more living.” (Head of Analytics & Innovation, Digital Bank)

We stepped back and said, when you do an unsecured loan, what’s important for the customer? The [internal] decision process is important. The interest rate is important [for the bank]. But [these are] not as important [for the customer]. What matters [for the customer] is whether we are going to give her the money, or not. (Head of Consumer Banking, Home Market B)

Similarly, releasing tight control of resources and opening up to partners led to greater disassociation from established capabilities in both growth and home markets.

We come to provide a process in terms of assessing partners, coming up with the groundwork on the strategy of what sort of partners we should be looking for. Where are our gaps versus the value proposition we are trying to deliver. (Head of Market Development & Alliances at Digital Bank)

I think we’ve learned the hard way that it’s better to build an open system. Even in [Home Market A], where you have a dominant position, we still find that being a “close loop” doesn’t help us. (IT Lead, Corporate Banking)

Finally, as evidential trade-off resolution spread across AsiaBank, it created conditions for launching business initiatives whose business value was uncertain, something that would not have been possible in the past:

if we were to run [this initiative] in a traditional way, like 20 years ago, … it would have never gotten off the ground because no one would have had the courage to try. (Head of SME Banking)

## 4.4 Enabling Distributed Organizing to Reduce Political Inertia

AsiaBank’s managers took purposeful steps to enable distributed organizing by increasing the degree of boundary spanning and autonomy for select organizational members and units within the bank. To foster boundary spanning, or the ability of organizational members to cross internal and external boundaries of the firm, managers created conditions where workers from different units could engage in close unrestricted interactions with each other and actors from outside the bank (see Figure 1). This approach formed the foundation of the innovation strategy at AsiaBank, as explained by the bank’s chief innovation officer:

We build communities inside the bank, and we drive and manage these and get to understand them; we build communities outside the bank, with start-ups, universities, government agencies, and institutional partnerships. We then collide these two [types of] communities in programs which spark innovation, put these sparks of innovation into other programs, and accelerate towards solving problems for the bank.

Manifestations of boundary spanning were evident at Digital Bank, which was staffed partly with talent from outside the banking industry with expertise in startups, design, and technology, and partly with workers from diverse business units across AsiaBank. A middle manager at Digital Bank recalled:

To me it felt a little bit like a social experiment. How different people from different backgrounds [came together] thinking that we could be a startup.

As discussed earlier, members of Digital Bank were also given the mandate to actively scout for and develop external partners in Growth Market X (see discussion of open resource mobilization above). The involvement of external partners further increased boundary spanning, ensuring the inflow and diffusion of new ideas and practices among workers at AsiaBank.

Besides boundary spanning, managers sought to increase worker autonomy or the degree to which organizational members were able to deviate from prescribed routines (see Figure 1). Workers at Digital Bank, for instance, were given significant leeway in figuring out how to best structure their work and interactions with each other. Rules and procedures mandatory for other units of AsiaBank were relaxed and sometimes waived altogether. A middle manager in charge of risk management and infrastructure at Digital Bank explained:

My background is project management ... , when I first joined [Digital Bank], I thought of putting together detailed [project] plans ... I can see now this is not the right environment for detailed Gantt charts. I think the real trick is to find the right balance between getting coordination correct while allowing for a degree of chaos and fast pace.

Multiple other developments in the case data represent examples of AsiaBank’s managers taking action to introduce higher boundary spanning and autonomy across the bank. To maximize boundary spanning in talent hackathons, for example, teams were formed by workers drawn from across AsiaBank along with representatives of external startups. A hackathon participant explained:

There were five of us [i.e., participants coming from the bank] in the team and we came from all over the bank. We worked on a case concerning a business unit that one of us belonged to. … it was stressful. …we were very fortunate with the start-up. … even after we left in the wee hours of the morning, they continued to work.

Similarly, the evolution of Customer Journey Design events at AsiaBank followed the trajectory from a low to high level of distributed organizing. In Case Years 4- 6, the participating teams were comprised of members of the same organizational unit, suggesting a lower degree of boundary spanning. But, in Case Year 7, AsiaBank’s managers launched 10 “iconic journeys”— strategically important journeys crossing multiple business functions—and created cross-functional teams to develop these. The teams were comprised of business, technology, and data experts from across the bank (high boundary spanning) who had significant autonomy from their respective departments and full-time dedication for up to six months to complete the journey.

Increases in boundary spanning and worker autonomy helped weaken the buffering effect of vested interests rooted in organizational hierarchy on change and, therefore, reduce political inertia. By boosting the ease and frequency with which workers were able to venture out of the established organizational silos and by increasing team diversity, boundary spanning effectively lessened the relative weight of local agendas in shaping employees’ actions. The iconic customer journeys mentioned above offered a telling illustration:

If you start with what “sales wants,” “compliance wants,” etc., then you are going to get in-fighting … but if you start with the customer, then compliance, risk management, policy, legal, etc., they’re not going to say no. (Managing Director, Technology & Operations)

Autonomy accomplished a similar effect but in a more direct manner. By increasing the degree to which workers were able to deviate from established rules and decisions of immediate supervisors, autonomy loosened local control mechanisms and enabled greater experimentation with new work practices in the worker’s immediate context. For example, relaxing the formal rules and procedures created conditions for members of Digital Bank to move faster and experiment freely with how to best meet the specific needs of customers in Growth Market X:

There are some rules and policies that we will have to adhere to … to make sure everything we do is secure and safe. At the same time, we are expected to be innovative, creative, and to test the boundaries of the bank. We will try to move things as fast as we can and add a lot of flexibility. (Head of Digital Bank)

## 4.5 Revamping IT Architecture to Reduce Sociocognitive Inertia

Our case data also strongly suggest that AsiaBank’s managers launched organizational interventions to establish processes aimed at revamping the bank’s IT architecture. Having started in Case Year 1, these dynamics unfolded steadily over the course of the case and manifested as two key subprocesses, namely platformization and datafication.

We define platformization as a process whereby the structural arrangement of the firm’s technology resources transitions from tightly coupled to loosely coupled (see Figure 1). Under the tightly coupled arrangement, resources depend on each other and are closely integrated to perform their functions, which are typically predefined. Under the loosely coupled arrangement, resources tend to be modular, relatively independent, resilient to changes in other parts of the overall architecture, and able to be programmatically configured and recombined to perform various often unanticipated functions.

Over the course of the case, AsiaBank’s managers initiated various interventions aimed at fostering loose coupling across all layers of the bank’s technology architecture. At the level of hardware, the bank shifted from a dedicated to shared allocation of computing and storage resources, achieving by Case Year 7 more than 90% virtualization of its infrastructure and running close to 50% of “compute on public cloud.” At the level of the operating system, middleware, and database software, the bank shifted from a single-supplier customized proprietary to open-source standardized solutions (e.g., from Oracle to Maria DB), seeking to assure greater scalability and fault tolerance through automation and component replaceability. Finally, at the level of applications, two API layers—internal and external— were introduced to assure modularity and increase the speed of innovation in the areas of customer acquisition and services. The internal layer enabled AsiaBank’s internal teams to build customer-facing applications (for both internal and external customers) that would integrate with the bank’s core banking solutions in the backend. The external APIs exposed core banking functionality, such as payments, cards, and loans, to potential partners:

[Today] we’ve got a hundred and fifty different types of services we can expose. [Initially] We didn’t know if we’re building the right ones. We didn’t know if we’d use them all, but that was one of these things that we believed was the right thing to do. (Head of Technology, Consumer Banking)

The second process, datafication, captures a change in the IT infrastructure and processes wherein the firm transitions from a piecemeal to a holistic approach in how it collects, stores, analyzes, and visualizes data (see Figure 1). At a more general level, datafication represents a shift away from the sporadic use of data in daily organizational practices, where a few predefined reports and metrics based on restricted samples are employed ad hoc, toward a more proactive and systematic data use, where “all” qualitative and quantitative data are collected and made sense of on an ongoing basis. Initially, in Case Years 1-4, the traditional piecemeal approach to data was evident in the organizational activities at AsiaBank. The bank’s technology infrastructure provided rudimentary data access and reporting capabilities and, therefore, was leveraged in a very limited way to facilitate decisionmaking and action. While running Process Improvement events (Case Years 1-3), for instance, the teams collected limited data through readily available means, such as the “waste walk,” and focused on a single easy-to-collect output metric—number of customer hours. Starting with Case Year 4, the team in charge of Customer Journey Design events made the first step toward a more holistic approach by suggesting collecting detailed data on the process of journey design rather than its outcomes. The fact that most of the customer journeys passed through digital touchpoints made collecting and analyzing these data significantly easier. Later, this first step was reinforced by the creation of data insight teams (Case Year 5) and embedding those into the teams working on “iconic” journeys to help “bring together qualitative and quantitative insights to inform design.” A business director responsible for data insights explained:

[To date] we have accumulated 300 journeys, now that we can see 300, how do we give you a little bit more guidance on how to measure. We used our own team to start crunching that data and started to standardize and give guidance to people. This was a very big piece of what we have done.

Increasing datafication was also clearly visible in the daily activities of Digital Bank (Case Year 4 on). Design of the new digital banking solution for Growth

Market X was, from the very beginning, focused on the ability to collect fast ongoing feedback about how customers were using it. New metrics and technological means to operationalize the metrics were introduced on an ongoing basis to improve data attribution and make the analysis more granular. For example, customers were asked to provide ratings and qualitative feedback concerning all new app features, contributing vast volumes of structured and unstructured data per transaction type per month. To make data more actionable, AsiaBank automated some of the decisions as well as built dashboards that “made the measures visible,” thus helping business decisionmakers in identifying the most relevant data at any given point in time.

We found that the process of revamping IT architecture and its two subprocess of platformization and datafication played a primary role in reducing sociotechnical inertia in digital transformation. As discussed earlier, sociotechnical inertia stems from resource rigidity linked to (1) complexity of and prior investment in existing IT systems and (2) routine rigidity related to established IT-enabled practices and workflows. To this end, platformization makes the firm’s IT architecture more flexible through modular systems design and standardized interfaces interconnecting the modules. This loosely coupled architecture alleviates the resource rigidity of the firm by enabling focused small-scale modifications to the existing IT systems and by making the financial implications of these changes incremental and, thus, more palatable for managers. At AsiaBank, for example, the decoupling of core banking systems and the introduction of API layers allowed for broad experimentation with customer-facing applications such as payments, cards, and loans:

For the core systems, they chug along and do their enterprise waterfall releases, … [while] we go through our 10, 11 sprints and then catch the core systems during the [integration] cycle. (Head of Products at Digital Bank)

Datafication helped overcome sociotechnical inertia by making the limitations of the established IT-enabled practices more visible and explicit. The development of an enterprise-wide capability to collect, store, analyze, and visualize data in a holistic manner helped organizational members see the diminishing efficacy of existing routines in the changing environmental context:

[When I joined] the organization was calcified. It was really hard to change anything because everyone had been there for a long time. We created this really interesting map … a person trying to change a process … Literally the email had gone around, touching about thirty different internal stakeholders, before finally returning with a “No.” Once we showed the map to everyone, there was no discussion this had to be changed (Managing Director, Technology & Operations)

Making the infrastructure for visualizing real-time data (e.g., dashboards) and rapidly testing hypotheses widely available provided impetus for changing the conversation among managers and workers about “what’s possible” and “what works.” This, in turn, decreased the stickiness of the established routines and made the design of new routines easier:

We’re in the early stage of data-driven management. But already today [in setting up the customer onboarding process], we constantly ask: “How many clicks do you get?” “What is the cost per click?” “Where is the drop off?” “How do we increase conversion?” (Managing Director, Consumer Banking)

## 4.6 Appropriating Digital Business Practices to Reduce Economic Inertia

The proactive diffusion of the digital business practices by managers discussed above gradually led to the bottom-up appropriation of the practices by workers. In some instances, such appropriation produced changes in value creation paths. Indeed, our informants actively used the language reflecting digital business practices in explaining their efforts to innovate with value propositions, business activities, and partner networks.

The practice of outside-in problem identification anchored innovation in products and services aimed at rethinking or expanding the bank’s value proposition to customers. We found evidence of this for both retail and business customers:

We realized that when a customer loses her credit card, mailing in a new card in one rather than five days, does not solve the issue. Her real problem is that she lost her purse and, with it, home keys, debit cards, ID etc. We … [developed and introduced]… a different type of approach [service] to tackle these issues. (Head of the Customer Journey Design Program)

I want to plan for my kids’ education. This is what they say in Digital [consumer] Bank. For a corporate [client], it’s the whole supply chain. Where do customers buy from? How to pay? How to get it shipped? How to manufacture, or indeed even how to manufacture, how to repackage, how to deliver, how to sell, where to sell? How to collect the funds, how to do accounting? … [our solution] has to be end to end. (Head of Technology for Institutional Banking)

The practice of open resource mobilization shaped efforts to innovate with business activities and partnership arrangements to redesign the bank’s value networks. Digital Bank, for example, invested heavily in developing a partner network in Growth Market X to drive customer acquisition, facilitate activities requiring physical presence (e.g., fingerprinting of new customers), and extend the range of the value-added services in its offering (e.g., AI-powered virtual assistant):

Just for [Growth Market X] we had spoken to 100 different partners before we arrived at 18 vendors that were mature enough from a technical perspective. … And from a lifestyle partners’ perspective, we now have direct partnerships with about 10 partners.. (Head of Market Development & Alliances, Digital Bank)

Finally, the practice of evidential trade-off resolution dominated the discussion of how to access the outcomes of innovation in value propositions and networks. The ability to leverage digital touchpoints to collect and analyze “hard evidence” beyond the financial metrics made it easier to launch new initiatives and thus enlarged the space for experimentation:

You don’t need to make an immediate ROI [return on investment] on a rigid offering, Initially, the measure of success is how many customers we can onboard and what their average balance and number of transactions are. We start with this success criterion and, once we get there, we can start thinking about making money. (VP, SME Regional Office)

Wide-spread appropriation of evidential trade-off resolution also increased the pace of innovation while reducing the size of each innovation instance. Many teams had adopted an agile approach where new value propositions were rolled out in small increments for the team to collect data and learn from the current instance to improve the next one:

It [our product] may not be the be the best but we put it out there and we learn. I comes back and we pivot very quickly for the second release. You learn again, make some changes and put it out to the customer again. … We used to have one or two releases a year, we are now doing release every six weeks or so. (Managing Director, Consumer Banking)

By bringing about changes in value propositions, networks, and business activities, appropriation of digital business practices by AsiaBank’s workers lessened the bank’s embeddedness in the established value creation paths, thus helping reduce economic inertia. The reduction, however, did not happen uniformly across all relevant case events. Indeed, we uncovered significant variation in the scope and scale of changes in value creation that could not be fully explained by differences in the appropriation of digital business practices.<sup>2</sup> This variation corresponded to the interaction between the processes of appropriation of digital business practices and those of enabling distributed organizing and revamping IT architecture.

The scope of change in value creation paths reflects the degree of newness in a given configuration of business activities, networks, and value propositions as compared to that deployed in the existing (i.e., home) markets. Newness can be assessed in terms of specific business processes, partnership arrangements, products and services, as well as target customer profiles. The digital banking solution for Growth Market X, for example, exhibited high scope of changes insofar as: (1) the Growth Market X offering was fully digital, with virtually no proprietary physical infrastructure involved, (2) significant pieces of functionality, such as the AI-powered virtual assistant, were developed by partners and integrated into the main solution, and (3) the bank had to rely on a network of local partners to support customer acquisition and interactions that required physical contact, such as new customer verification. All of this stood in stark contrast to how AsiaBank went about serving its customers in the home markets.

On the other end of the spectrum lie initiatives with a low scope of change in value creation paths. Indeed, many of the Customer Journey Design events aimed at “making banking joyful” produced only incremental improvements to how customer needs had been met before. A significant effort, for example, was expanded to modifying the user interface of the ATM machines to reduce the percentage of retained cards. This initiative improved overall customer satisfaction and had important operational and financial implications for the bank, but it introduced no significant change in either the existing business processes or the value proposition.

We found that variations in the scope of change were related to the degree of distributed organizing. Our data suggest that appropriation of digital business practices in organizational units and teams with higher intensity of boundary spanning and autonomy was more likely to produce value creation paths with a higher degree of newness. Consider, for example, the evolution of Customer Journey Design events at AsiaBank. Teams comprised of members from the same organizational unit (lower degree of boundary spanning and, thus, lower intensity of distributed organizing) produced new customer journeys that only incrementally deviated from the existing ones—see the ATM user interface example above. In contrast, crossfunctional teams working on the “iconic journeys” (high intensity of boundary spanning) in most instances produced a customer experience that deviated significantly from the status quo. One such journey, for example, sought to improve the customer experience of dealing with a call center by completely “taking the calls out of the call center.”

The scale of change in value creation paths reflects the degree of adoption of the new configurations of business activities and value propositions by customers (external or internal). Our data offered examples of initiatives producing both a high and low scale of change. The launch of the digital banking solution in Growth Market X, for example, was characterized not only by a significant degree of newness, as described above, but also by the very significant adoption of the new offering by customers. Indeed, in the two years after the launch, AsiaBank was able to grow its customer base in the new market from virtually zero to close to two million customers.

Other initiatives failed to gain momentum and achieve a considerable degree of customer adoption. One such example was “Business Class”—a project aimed at creating an online community for small and medium enterprises, where business owners could share pertinent experiences, ask questions, and gain access to expert advice and educational content. The initiative’s value proposition (i.e., peer social network) and its key activities (e.g., production of educational content) expanded well beyond the usual range of services AsiaBank offered to small businesses, signaling high newness and thus a high scope of change. At the same time, Business Class faced significant difficulties in gaining momentum and growing the user base:

We have about 10,000 [members], and about one-third are active in that they write things on the app, while 80% of active users who check the app. [To grow the community] we are going to embark on regionalizing Business Class, so that there will be a community in all six markets that we operate. (Executive Director, SME Banking)

We found that variation in the scale of change in value creation paths was related to the process of revamping IT architecture. To this end, the intensity of platformization and datafication in the areas of Asiabank’s IT architecture immediately supporting a given transformation initiative was positively associated with the scale of customer adoption of the initiative. For example, the new digital banking solution for Growth Market X achieved significant customer adoption while relying on core banking applications deployed in the cloud and linked to the solution at the front-end through a layer of APIs. Indeed, the very decision to expand into Growth Market X with a new retail solution grew, to a large extent, out of the efforts to revamp IT architecture carried out in Case Years 1 through 4:

This was the standard [AsiaBank] platform that we were going to roll out… What this had enabled us to do was [to say] “we want to do digital banking … Digital Bank innovates on top of this core strength.” (Managing Director, Technology & Operations)

Similarly, significant efforts to ramp up datafication supported the launch of Digital Bank in Growth Market X. Managing director for e-business and payments explained:

We get customer[s] to give us ratings, just like Uber … and we can attribute them to fund transfers, payments, etc. We also ask customer[s] to give us reasons in case the rating is unsatisfactory … In [Growth Market X] we are getting pretty good data.

In contrast, the Business Class initiative, which failed to reach a significant scale of customer adoption, as discussed above, relied on 3rd party IT solutions lacking integration with the rest of AsiaBank’s banking platform and provided few data reporting features.

## 4.7 Improving Organizational Performance

As AsiaBank’s managers and workers continued to innovate with business models in the home and growth markets, the bank started to see a tangible improvement in firm performance. In the year following the last year of the case (Case Year 8), AsiaBank reported earning twice as much from each digital customer as from a traditional customer. The distinction between the two customer categories was based on the proportion of interactions a given customer carried out with the bank through digital vs. traditional channels. At the time, the bank served 39% of its customers through digital channels and 61% through traditional channels, and the cost to acquire the former was 43% of that of the latter. A financial newspaper reporting on the bank’s performance observed: “As more users go digital, the previous of 12,9% ROE peak [achieved 4 years prior to the start of the case] could well be exceeded.”

## 5 Discussion of Findings

In this section, we propose a model that explains how digital transformation (DT) processes help overcome key types of organizational inertia in digital transformation (Figure 2). We view DT processes as elements of organizational agency wherein managers and workers create conditions for and engage in behaviors that interact with elements of the firm’s deep structure (Gersick, 1991; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). As a result of these interactions, key organizational rigidities are diminished (Gilbert, 2005) and organizational inertia is in turn reduced (Besson & Rowe, 2012).

In Figure 2 below, the area labeled “Overcoming Organizational Inertia” reflects the core of our theorizing. Inside the area, oval boxes represent a reduction in a particular type of organizational inertia (drawn from the literature) (Besson & Rowe, 2012), whereas rectangular boxes depict DT processes (elicited from the case data). The antecedent condition and outcome variable are also drawn from the literature.

We argue that overcoming negative psychology inertia, which accrues mostly at the individual level, is a starting point and a necessary condition for tackling other types of organizational inertia in digital transformation. The DT process of embracing consumerization of digital technologies plays a key role in this regard (cf. Gregory et al., 2018). Its two subprocesses of framing digital as opportunity and fostering learning orientation build upon the experiences of organizational members with digital technologies in their personal lives (Arrow 1 in Figure 2) (cf. König et al., 2021).

In our data, to help reduce threat perceptions, managers highlighted worker self-efficacy and the enjoyment of digital technologies developed outside of work. Similarly, to encourage learning, managers called on workers to embrace the mostly familiar digital world in their day-today work, while at the same time deemphasizing performance targets for such efforts. Accordingly, we argue that the DT process of embracing consumerization of digital technologies helps reduce negative psychology inertia in digital transformation by channeling the positive experiences of organizational members with consumer digital technologies in their personal lives (Arrow 2 in Figure 2).

The reduction in negative psychology inertia makes possible the DT processes of diffusing digital business practices, enabling distributed organizing, and revamping IT architecture (Arrows 3a, 3b, and 3c in Figure 2). By facilitating the diffusion of digital business practices concerning outside-in problem identification, open resource mobilization, and evidential trade-off resolution, a firm’s managers can reduce the embeddedness of organizational members in the preexisting beliefs and knowledge rooted, respectively, in established products, proven capabilities, and prior experience. This decoupling reduces sociocognitive inertia (Arrow 4a in Figure 2), which in turn makes the appropriation of digital business practices by the firm’s workers possible (Arrow 5a in Figure 2). As such, appropriation gains momentum, and the organization’s general ability to change value creation paths—that is, develop new value propositions and reconfigure core business activities and networks—is enhanced (cf. Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014), thus creating the possibility of overcoming economic inertia (Arrow 6 in Figure 6).

Appropriation of digital business practices is a necessary but not sufficient condition for overcoming economic inertia. Other conditions include a reduction in political and sociotechnical inertia (Arrows 5b and 5c in Figure 2), driven respectively by the DT processes of enabling distributed organizing and revamping IT architecture. The former reduces political inertia by fostering greater boundary spanning and autonomy among workers (Arrow 4b in Figure 2) (cf. Levina & Vaast, 2006). The two subprocesses diminish the relative weight of local political agendas by, respectively, bringing in a greater diversity of actors to bear upon key decisions and actions and weakening direct supervisory control over them. The reduced embeddedness in the local vested interests removes constraints on the appropriation of digital business practices by workers, allowing for greater deviation from established value propositions and networks. This, in turn, increases the scope of change in value creation paths (Arrow 5b in Figure 2).

The process of revamping IT architecture reduces sociotechnical inertia by enabling platformization and datafication (Arrow 4c in Figure 2) (cf. Gregory et al., 2015). Platformization breaks up resource path dependencies by making investment decisions more incremental and embracing the modular ontology of digital resources for the redesign of the enterprise technology architecture (cf. Piccoli et al., 2022; Yoo et al., 2010), while datafication decreases the stickiness of established routines by objectifying their inefficiencies (cf. Grover et al., 2018). The reduction in resource and routine rigidities paves the way for provisioning the necessary material artifacts and processes to support the appropriation of digital business practices by workers. This makes a swifter roll-out and fine-tuning of the new value offering to customers possible, thus increasing the scale of change in value creation paths (Arrow 5c in Figure 2) (cf. Huang et al., 2017).

If and when political and sociotechnical inertia is reduced, appropriation of digital business practices by workers will likely lead to a higher scope and scale of change in value creation paths, effectively reducing economic inertia (Arrow 6 in Figure 2). Our model posits that overcoming economic inertia affects firm performance (Arrow 7 in Figure 2). While transformation outcomes are often associated with failure (Besson & Rowe, 2012), our data shows an improvement in the firm performance at AsiaBank. To this end, we argue that key types of organizational inertia need to be tackled in sequence and that transformation outcomes are contingent on when and how DT processes are initiated and enacted in an organization.

## 6 Implications for Research

The main contribution of our study is a detailed explanation of the process by which organizational agency exercised by managers and workers helps overcome inertia in digital transformation (Berente et al., 2016; Hanelt et al., 2015; Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Hinings et al., 2018; Kane, 2015; Majchrzak et al., 2016; Matt et al., 2015; Vial, 2019; Westerman et al., 2014). We theorize agency through the lens of four digital transformation (DT) processes and describe the mechanisms by which these processes contribute to reducing key organizational rigidities (see Table 3) corresponding to the main types of inertia (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017; Besson & Rowe, 2012). We also suggest a sequence in which DT processes may be enacted (see Figure 2) to increase the likelihood of improving organizational performance as a transformation outcome (Vial, 2019). This sequence assumes that certain types of organizational inertia must be reduced before a given agency intervention (i.e., DT process) is likely to become viable (see Arrows 3a, 3b, 3c, 5, and 6 in Figure 2) or effective (see Arrows 5b and 5c in Figure 2).

The extant literature posits that the new societal-level consumer beliefs about digital technology usher in changes in customer behaviors and competition (Gregory et al., 2018) and therefore serve as a trigger for digital transformation (Vial, 2019). Our research corroborates this claim but goes further to show that the role of digital technology consumerization in this context extends beyond that of a trigger. Indeed, our model suggests that digital technology consumerization becomes a key enabler of overcoming inertia in digital transformation and ultimately improves organizational performance. Early in the transformation journey, digital technology consumerization helps overcome negative psychology inertia by making it possible to leverage positive experiences with digital technologies that the firm’s managers and workers have had outside of work. This stands in contrast to IT-enabled organizational transformation, where such external influence is negligible (e.g., Sarker et al., 2006), necessitating additional organizational interventions to help reduce user resistance because of the lack of perceived selfefficacy (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005).

Further, our model places the diffusion and appropriation of digital business practices by managers and workers at the core of agency interventions aimed at overcoming sociocognitive inertia and, under certain conditions, economic inertia. Here again, digital technology consumerization plays a key enabling role because the three digital business practices draw upon the societallevel consumer beliefs about digital technology (Gregory et al., 2018; Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). In particular, the practice of outside-in problem identification emerges when organizational members extrapolate consumer expectations of low complexity, personalization, and enjoyable experience onto the firm’s value propositions. The practice of open resource mobilization derives from beliefs about common availability and embeddedness. Finally, the practice of evidential trade-off resolution arises when consumer beliefs about low complexity and personalization are fused into efforts to validate new value propositions and networks. Thus, one way to interpret our proposed model is to argue that it is the adoption and assimilation of consumer beliefs about digital technologies at the level of organizational practices that lead to the emergence of new value creation paths.

Zooming into the model depicted in Figure 2, each of the digital transformation processes we identified offers new insights that connect to and extend the extant literature in this domain. First, our model offers novel insights into the relationship between IT transformation—reflected in our model as the process of revamping IT architecture—and digital transformation (Wessel, 2021). Although IT transformation has been extensively studied in the literature (e.g., Gregory et al., 2015) as a stand-alone phenomenon, the role of IT transformation in the broader context of digital transformation (Vial, 2019) remains unexplained (Wessel, 2021). Our model makes a step in this direction. Our findings suggest that IT transformation, manifested as the subprocesses of datafication and platformization, plays a key role in helping overcome sociotechnical inertia in digital transformation (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Rowe et al., 2017). Further, IT transformation serves as a necessary condition for reducing economic inertia. To this end, platformization and datafication enable the scalability of new digital ventures by interacting with digital business practices to facilitate a data-driven search for new sources of customer value and enable fast deployment of new value propositions (Huang et al., 2017).

Second, our findings concerning the process of distributed organizing extend extant research on digital transformation by highlighting the central role of changing organizational structures in overcoming political inertia (Audzeyeva & Hudson, 2017; Gilbert, 2005) and facilitating an increase in the scope of change in value creation paths (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Vial, 2019). Recent research on digital transformation has started to acknowledge that established bureaucratic and hierarchical structures place significant limits on incumbent firms’ ability to increase the scope of change in value creation paths to the extent necessary to keep up with the fast pace of changes in their external environment (Drechsler et al., 2020). To this end, our findings suggest that increasing employee autonomy within hierarchical structures and facilitating boundary spanning across functional silos are critical mechanisms that act as a countervailing force to the typical low speed of decision-making within the formal management structures in incumbent firms (Gilbert, 2005). Increasing the speed of decision-making (e.g., by establishing autonomous teams and outcomes focused on learning) and overcoming the natural tendency of influential managers to retain power by reinforcing the status quo (Besson & Rowe, 2012) are critical for incumbent firms to compete or partner with startups. In our case, this ambition manifested in the CEO’s vision of turning the bank into a “20,000 [employee] startup.” These findings suggest that future research on digital transformation should explore the potential role of new nonhierarchical forms of organizing (e.g., holacracy) in digital transformation.

Finally, our findings regarding the reduction of economic inertia through appropriating digital business practices while increasing the scale and scope of change in value creation paths, extend the literature on embracing digital innovation in incumbent firms (Svahn et al., 2017). We explain that the ability of an incumbent firm to break away from established value creation paths and embed digital technologies into new product development (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014) is influenced by the processes of overcoming inertia during digital transformation. Our model thus sheds new light on the connection between managing competing capability, collaboration, and governance concerns in digital innovation (Svahn et al., 2017) and the role of agency in overcoming inertia during digital transformation in incumbent firms.

## 7 Implications for Practice

The main ramification of our work for managers is that digital transformation efforts in incumbent firms must encompass three interrelated types of initiatives: (1) initiatives aimed at changing the architecture of the firm’s technological platform to make it modular and data-driven, (2) initiatives aimed at changing the organizational structure to increase employee autonomy and horizontal cross-silo collaboration, and (3) initiatives aimed at changing organizational culture and work practices to align these with consumer beliefs about digital technology. While many incumbent firms may declare that they have launched digital transformation, such a holistic three-pronged approach remains an exception. Many firms launch structurally separated digital units with a high local degree of boundary spanning and autonomy yet fail to support that with a systemic effort to diffuse new digital business practices across the organization. Similarly, firms often make significant investments in IT transformation, a process usually led by the CIO, without making corresponding structural or cultural changes, which is the prerogative of business leaders. Our research shows that the firm’s capacity to produce and scale new value offerings and business models to satisfy changing customer demands is contingent on management’s ability to synergistically pursue all three types of action.

## 8 Limitations and Boundary Conditions

The digital transformation initiative we studied was a successful case. This aspect gives our theorizing a somewhat normative shade. We acknowledge this limitation and point out that our model posits one possible pattern of organizational agency that reduces inertia in digital transformation. However, other patterns are possible. As a broader range of empirical examples becomes known, many contingencies will appear related to the model we offer and a more nuanced model is likely to emerge—we offer a foundational step.

Also, reflecting on our work as well the mainstream IS literature in this area, and in line with the recent call by Sarker et al. (2019), we see the need for future researchers to pay close attention not only to the instrumental outcomes of digital transformation (such as economic growth, efficiency, productivity, and innovativeness) but also to its humanistic outcomes (technostress and loss of intellectual property, privacy, human dignity, and well-being). It would be of major concern if IS academics were to uncritically promote the case for digital transformation by corporations, consultancies, and technology vendors (Majchrzak et al., 2016) without being mindful of some of the dystopian potential outcomes that could result from a mad rush toward digital transformation. As scholarship in this and other areas matures, we are hopeful that critiques of digital transformation and commentaries of its “dark side” will begin to appear, thereby providing a more balanced discourse on the topic rather than one that is purely pro-digital transformation. In this light, we might discover that in some circumstances, inertia is not necessarily a barrier to progress but can protect inimitable and potentially valuable aspects of a company that would otherwise be lost because of a hasty move in the wrong direction.

Finally, we would like to point out that not all incumbent organizations operate in industries with a high degree of digital technology consumerization. While its manifestations are very pronounced in industries close to the end consumer, such as banking, retail, and education, other industries such as the chemical and oil and gas industries are further removed from the end consumer, and consumer beliefs about digital technologies are therefore not likely to trigger digital transformation or help address the negative psychology of managers and workers to the same extent. In this vein, a high level of digital technology consumerization of customers and key stakeholders should be seen as a boundary condition to our theorizing.

## About the Authors

Evgeny Kaganer is a professor of operations, information, and technology at IESE Business School, University of Navarra. He received his PhD from Louisiana State University. His research explores how digitalization and artificial intelligence reshape business models and organizations.

Robert Wayne Gregory is an associate professor at Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami. He received his PhD in management information systems from Goethe University Frankfurt. His research interests include digital innovation and strategy, platforms, and technology-driven change.

Suprateek Sarker is the Rolls Royce Commonwealth Commerce Professor at the McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia. He received his PhD from the University of Cincinnati. He is the current president of the Association for Information Systems and he is also serving as the current editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research. He has served as the editor-in-chief of Journal of the Association for Information Systems and a senior editor of MIS Quarterly.
