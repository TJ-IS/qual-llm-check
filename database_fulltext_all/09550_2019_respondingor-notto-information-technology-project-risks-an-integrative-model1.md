# RESPONDING—OR NOT—TO INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY PROJECT RISKS: AN INTEGRATIVE MODEL

Mohammad Moeini University of Sussex Business School, University of Sussex, Brighton BN1 9SL UNITED KINGDOM {m.moeini@sussex.ac.uk}

Suzanne Rivard HEC Montréal, 3000 Chemin-de-la–Côte-Ste-Catherine, Montréal H3T 2V7 CANADA {suzanne.rivard@hec.ca}

This study proposes and tests a model that explains and predicts risk response decisions of information technology project managers (ITPMs), blending the domains of the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and behavioral decision theories, and leveraging information technology project risk management behavioral research. The model posits that a risk response decision is indirectly influenced by perceived risk exposure via overall risk response attitude. The model conceptualizes perceived risk exposure and overall risk response attitude as second-order constructs and examines the dimension-level relationships within each. The model hypothesizes that a risk response decision is also influenced by pressures ITPMs perceive for or against enacting a specific risk response, by a negative synergy effect between overall risk response attitude and perceived pressures, and by their perception of control—or lack thereof—over enacting the risk response.

The model was instantiated for three specific risk responses: having user representatives as team members, appreciating team members’ work in a tangible way during the project, and dedicating much effort to planning. Each model instance was tested in a separate survey (N > 111 per survey, total N = 349). The results support the hypotheses, except for the influence of perceived control, which varied across instantiations of risk responses. Among other antecedents, overall risk response attitude is found to have the strongest effect on risk response decisions. The findings stress the effect of ITPMs’ salient beliefs about specific risk responses on their decision to enact a given response and thus pave the way for designing behavior change interventions.

Keywords: Behavioral research, IT project risk management, risk response decision, risk response attitude, perceived risk exposure, theory of planned behavior, MIMIC

## Introduction

While information technology (IT) projects often hold the promise of substantial benefits, their failure can damage an organization to the point of threatening its very existence (Charette and Romero 2015). As project failure is in part due to the materialization of unaddressed risks, a wealth of research has been conducted on IT project risk management. From a prescriptive stance, some researchers have recommended that IT project managers (ITPMs) identify risk sources (e.g., Keil et al. 1998) and apply heuristics that tie risk responses to risk sources (e.g., Lyytinen et al. 1998). Others have confirmed that applying appropriate risk responses is instrumental in explaining project performance (e.g., Barki et al. 2001). Notwithstanding the value of prescriptions and of confirmatory evidence, studies have revealed that ITPMs at times decide not to enact risk responses (e.g., Taylor 2005). Adopting a behavioral stance, researchers have explored why ITPMs sometimes do not apply formal risk management practices such as risk identification (e.g., de Bakker et al. 2010) or disengage from these practices (e.g., Kutsch et al. 2013). Their findings suggest disbelief in risk estimates, side effects of risk management, negative pressures, and resource consumption entailed by risk management as the main antecedents of such disengagement (e.g., Bannerman 2008; Kutsch et al. 2013).

Although those behavioral studies contribute intriguing insights into what motivates ITPMs’ risk management behaviors, they do not, because of their exploratory nature, theorize or test the relationships between the identified antecedents and the decision of whether or not to enact risk management behavior. Moreover, while those studies focus on the decision to apply formal risk management practices (e.g., performing formal risk response planning), they leave room for examining the decision regarding specific risk responses (e.g., whether to encourage user participation). Given the centrality of risk response decisions for ITPMs’ daily practice and their potential impact on the success of IT projects (Venkatesh et al. 2018), a fine-grained examination of the decision to enact a risk response—or not—would both contribute to the advancement of knowledge and have high practical relevance.

This study proposes and tests an integrative model of ITPMs’ decision of whether to enact specific risk responses that reconceptualizes antecedents identified in behavioral studies and specifies their relationships. An examination of the nature of those antecedents revealed a significant overlap with the constructs of the theory of planned behavior (TPB) (Ajzen 1991, 2011; Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Therefore, TPB served as a model-building canvas. The model was developed by blending TPB with behavioral decision theories—mainly affect heuristic (Slovic et al. 2007) and prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992)—and mobilizing psychology research on attitude (Maio and Haddock 2015). This theoretical anchoring ensured cogent insights for clarifying the antecedents and specifying their relationships.

The proposed model hypothesizes risk response decisions to be indirectly influenced by perceived risk exposure, via ITPMs’ overall attitude toward enacting a risk response. The model also hypothesizes that for ITPMs, the magnitude of loss due to the occurrence of undesired outcomes dimension of risk exposure is more important than its probability of undesired outcomes dimension. The model conceptualizes ITPMs’ overall risk response attitude as having two dimensions related to expected desired effects and expected side effects, and it hypothesizes desired effects to be more important than side effects. Moreover, the model posits that the decision to enact a risk response is influenced by the pressures ITPMs perceive for or against the behavior, and by their perception of control—or lack thereof—over enacting it. Also, it posits a negative synergy effect between attitude and perceived pressure.

The model was instantiated for three specific risk responses: having user representatives as team members, appreciating team members’ work in a tangible way during the project, and dedicating much effort to planning. After contextualizing the constructs for each instance using belief elicitation, a survey of ITPMs was conducted to test the three instantiations (N > 111 per instantiation, total N = 349). The results provide support for all hypotheses, except for the effect of perceived control, which was supported only for “dedicating much effort to planning.”

This study makes important contributions. First, it provides a theoretical anchor to behavioral IT project risk management research by proposing and testing a model that blends relevant theoretical domains. Second, it informs research on riskrelated IT decisions by illuminating the mediating role of ITPMs’ attitude between risk perception and risk-related decision, also by refining the conceptualization of risk perception used in this literature. Third, it contributes to TPB by explicitly accounting for perceived risk exposure, offering a second-order construct conceptualization of attitude, and theorizing the asymmetrical importance of the two dimensions of the construct. The study makes a methodological contribution to TPB research by adopting the MIMIC (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001) approach to address the issue of whether to measure the TPB determinants using reflective items or belief composites.

The following sections review the background of the study, describe the model and the methods, and present the results. The findings and the study’s contributions are then discussed.

## Background

Risk responses are project management activities aimed at dealing with risk sources—that is, “specific factors that present a threat to achieving the expected outcomes of a project” (Bannerman 2008, p. 2119)—and their associated risk events. Risk responses can be enacted (1) to eliminate risk sources before they cause risk events; (2) to mitigate risk exposure by reducing the probability of occurrence of risk events and/or the magnitude of their impact; or (3) to transfer the responsibility for the loss due to risk events to another entity (Charette 1996).

IT project risk responses are traditionally organized under the three categories of external integration (coordination with users), internal integration (coordination within project team), and formal planning (application of various tools, such as PERT and approaches such as incremental development) (Barki et al. 2001; McFarlan 1981; Mignerat and Rivard 2012).

To increase the understanding of risk response enactment, a review of the literature was conducted. Using keywords including “risk” and “project” in addition to “management,” “mitigation,” “strategies,” “response,” and/or “remedies,” the past 20 years of the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket of Eight journals (1996–2016) were searched. Forward and backward searches were also performed when necessary (Webster and Watson 2002), which led to expanding the coverage period to 1978–2016. A pool of papers was created, which fall into three strands: (1) prescriptive research studies how ITPMs should respond to risk; (2) confirmatory research investigates the effectiveness of enacting risk responses; and (3) behavioral research focuses on how ITPMs actually respond to risk. Each strand is briefly reviewed below.

## Prescriptive Research on Risk Response Enactment in IT Projects

Adopting the view that risk management can contribute to IT project success, studies have addressed the question of “what should one do when a specific incident [risk source] is observed?” (Lyytinen et al. 1998, p. 245). The main contribution of this research stream was to propose heuristics lists, which “link recognized risky incidents … to would-beeffective managerial interventions” (Lyytinen et al. 1998, p. 237). A review of the pool of papers identified eight such influential heuristics lists, which are compiled in Table 1.

## Confirmatory Research on the Effectiveness of Enacting Specific Risk Responses

Confirmatory research investigates whether risk management actually contributes to IT project success. Such studies have shown that while risks negatively affect project performance (Wallace et al. 2004), enacting risk responses has several positive effects: reducing the exposure to specific risks (Gemino et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2013), attenuating the effects of technical risk on individual developer’s performance and stress level (Venkatesh et al. 2018), and improving project performance (Barki et al. 2001; Gemino et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2013; Nidumolu 1995; Ropponen and Lyytinen 1997). For example, user–developer partnering is found to effectively reduce user-related risks and positively contribute to project performance (Liu et al. 2013). Likewise, user integration, administrative coordination, and expertise coordination positively affect organizational support risk and significantly increase project performance (Gemino et al. 2008). Overall, enacting appropriate risk responses positively influences project performance (Barki et al. 2001). These results accentuate the importance of risk response prescriptions and advance the prescribed heuristic lists by suggesting which specific risks and risk responses are more influential on project success.

## Behavioral Research on IT Project Risk Management

Behavioral studies suggest that ITPMs sometimes behave differently from prescriptions and decide not to enact risk responses (Taylor 2005) or disengage from risk management altogether (Kutsch et al. 2013). A review of the developed pool of papers yielded 10 studies that allude to the antecedents of ITPMs’ risk management-related decisions. These studies identify various antecedents of such disengagement. A commonly discussed antecedent is risk perception. It is suggested that when ITPMs do not perceive risks, for example because they are not confident in the accuracy of risk estimates (Kutsch and Hall 2009), they do not apply risk management practices (Kutsch et al. 2013). Another antecedent is the overall value or the “cost justifiability” (Kutsch and Hall 2009) of risk management. For example, researchers find that

Active project risk management was not carried out because project managers and other risk actors appeared not to consider project risk management as worthy of pursuit, given the time and cost constraints they were experiencing (Kutsch and Hall 2009, p. 78).

To explain such cost justifiability evaluation, researchers discuss various expected positive and negative consequences of risk management. For example, some ITPMs were found to believe that “risk management must be demonstrably useful” (Kutsch et al. 2013, p. 7), and some ITPMs believed that speaking about risk may create worthless anxiety in project stakeholders (Kutsch and Hall 2005). Pressures from stakeholders were also found to influence ITPMs’ decisions. For instance, some ITPMs believed that “there was an expectation and pressure to conform to the prescribed routine of risk management” (Kutsch et al. 2013, p. 7). The extent of authority—or lack thereof—that led some ITPMs to believe that they were “powerless and had limited authority to act” (Kutsch et al. 2013, p. 9) is also proposed as an antecedent of risk response enactment.

## An Integrative Model of ITPMs’ Risk Response Decision

Although behavioral research has identified several antecedents of ITPMs’ decision to disengage from IT project risk management, this body of research remains exploratory as it does not specify or test the relationships between antecedents and ITPMs’ disengagement. Furthermore, behavioral risk management studies pertain to general risk management practices (e.g., performing formal risk response planning), not on the decision of whether to enact—or not—specific risk responses. Yet, risk response decisions are part of ITPMs daily practice, and confirmatory studies highlight their potential impact on team members’ well-being during a project (Venkatesh et al. 2018) and on overall project success (Barki et al. 2001). Thus, to advance knowledge on IT project risk management, this study proposes and tests a model of the antecedents of this important decision.

The model mobilizes two theoretical domains—the theory of planned behavior (TPB) (Ajzen 1991, 2011; Fishbein and Ajzen 2010) and behavioral decision theories including affect heuristic (Slovic et al. 2007) and prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992)—using a theory blending approach (Oswick et al. 2011). First, the relevance of TBP was revealed by the significant overlap of its constructs and the antecedents of the decision to disengage from IT project risk management identified in the behavioral studies. In TPB, behavioral attitude, perceived pressure, and perceived control predict behavioral intention, which, along with perceived control, influence behavior. Indeed, many antecedents from behavioral studies of risk management can be mapped onto one’s attitude toward engaging in risk management, the pressure perceived from the environment for or against risk management, or the extent to which one feels they have control over the decision. Thus, TPB was considered a foundational theory of choice. In addition, recent psychology research helped refine the TPB conceptualization of attitude (e.g., Maio and Haddock 2015). Second, given the decision making about risk context of this study, further theoretical insights were sought from behavioral decision theories, especially affect heuristic (Slovic et al. 2007) and prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992).

Blending theoretical lenses requires the proximity of the phenomena the theories address and the congruence of their underlying assumptions (Okhuysen and Bonardi 2011). Because in TPB intention refers to a choice decision between behavioral alternatives—for example, performing or not performing an action (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010)—there is indeed congruence between TPB and behavioral decision theories in terms of their phenomenon of interest (i.e., individual-level decisions). In terms of underlying assumptions, TPB uses expectancy value theory to combine beliefs and their evaluations (Fishbein 1963), which is deemed similar to the “subjective expected utility (SEU) model of behavioral decision theory (Edwards 1954)” (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975, p. 30). As such, TPB shares with most behavioral decision theories the assumption that people evaluate the outcomes of a behavior when deciding on whether to perform it.

Drawing from behavioral decision theories helped enrich the TPB-based explanation of ITPMs’ risk response decisions in several ways. First, affect heuristic allowed accounting for and theorizing about perceived risk (Slovic et al. 2007), an antecedent often discussed in behavioral research on IT project risk management. While TPB acknowledges perceived risk as a background factor (see Fishbein and Azjen 2010, p. 25), it does not explicitly theorize about its role. Second, the explanation was further enriched by extending the notion of perceived risk (defined as perceived probability of an undesired outcome) to perceived risk exposure. This allowed accounting for both the probability and the magnitude of loss of an undesired outcome (March and Shapira 1987; Slovic et al. 2007). Third, following behavioral decision theories such as prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992) suggesting that people perceive and evaluate gains and losses separately before forming an overall preference, behavioral attitude was reconceptualized from TPB’s unidimensional construct to one with two dimensions, the expected desired effects and the expected side effects of enacting a risk response, suggesting asymmetrical importance for these two dimensions in constituting an overall attitude. This is in line with psychology research on attitude suggesting that individuals form bivariate attitudes, separating the negative and positive aspects of a behavior, as part of forming an overall bipolar attitude (Cacioppo et al. 1997; Maio and Haddock 2015).

The model is illustrated in Figure 1. Given the centrality of perceived risk exposure and overall risk response attitude in the model and the importance of hypothesizing the relationships between different dimensions of a multidimensional construct (Barki 2008), the first two hypotheses pertain to the relationships within these constructs.

The prescriptive (e.g., Boehm 1991) and confirmatory (e.g., Barki et al. 2001) literatures define risk exposure as the combination of the probability and magnitude of loss of undesired outcomes of an IT project. In this study, perceived risk exposure refers to one’s perception of probability and magnitude of loss. While both the prescriptive and confirmatory literatures assume that the two dimensions of risk exposure are of equal weight, the present model hypothesizes that probability and loss have asymmetrical roles in composing perceived risk exposure, with loss being more important than probability, suggesting an uneven structure.

This view is supported by behavioral decision theories. The evaluability hypothesis suggests that the ease (or difficulty) of evaluating a dimension makes it more (or less) salient in decision making (Hsee 1996). Building on this hypothesis, affect heuristics suggests that while in some contexts (e.g., simple gambles) probabilities are more evaluable, in other contexts (e.g., an airplane crash) losses are more evaluable and thus play a more significant role in forming people’s perceived risk (Slovic et al. 2007). In the present context, because of the difficulty of estimating probabilities of undesirable project outcomes (Barki et al. 1993), and because IT projects’ failures can have severe consequences (Charette and Romero 2015), the potential loss due to risks materializing is more evaluable than their probability. Indeed, IS research on project escalation supports the hypothesis that information provided regarding loss due to undesirable outcomes influences risk perception more than that about the probability of such outcomes (Keil et al. 2000). Likewise, managerial risk-taking research finds that managers are more influenced by the magnitude of loss than its probability (March and Shapira 1987). In formal terms,

## H1 The magnitude of loss (L) due to undesired outcomes is more influential on perceived risk exposure (PRE) than the probability (P) of such outcomes.

Some risk management approaches prescribe identifying the costs and benefits of potential risk responses (e.g., Boehm 1991). Similarly, here ITPMs are considered to hold several beliefs about the desired and side effects of specific risk responses, although often developing them more informally. For example, one might consider simultaneously that increasing user participation could prevent user resistance (Barki and Hartwick 1994) but also cause low project team effectiveness (Heinbokel et al. 1996). This view is supported by TPB, which considers that people may simultaneously hold beliefs of negative and positive valence about specific behaviors, with a belief being an accessible thought that associates a behavior with an outcome (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Similarly, behavioral decision theories suggest that people evaluate the positive or negative outcomes of a decision alternative (Tversky and Kahneman 1992).

Prescriptive approaches also suggest integrating the gathered information about risk responses into a risk-reduction leverage value, defined as the extent to which enacting a risk response mitigates risk exposure compared to its cost (Boehm 1989). Although ITPMs may perform this evaluation informally, they use the information about a risk management practice to assess its cost justifiability (Kutsch and Hall 2009). A theoretical explanation for this can be drawn from the studies of attitude formation in psychology (e.g., Cacioppo et al. 1997; Maio and Haddock 2015) and choice preference construction in behavioral decision theories (Tversky and Kahneman 1992). In psychology, attitude is considered to be formed from salient beliefs of positive or negative valence combined with the evaluations of their importance (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). People might initially form bivariate attitudes separating the negative and positive aspects of a behavior (see Maio and Haddock 2015, p. 39), which leads some to experience attitudinal ambivalence (see Cacioppo et al. 1997, p. 23). Subsequently, they form a summary evaluation in a bipolar fashion, especially when they need one ultimate evaluation to guide their decision because they prefer to avoid decision conflict (Maio and Haddock 2015). Similarly, in discussing the choice preference construction, cumulative prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992) assumes that, although people have separate perceptions of the expected gains and losses of each decision alternative, they combine these perceptions into a preference to guide their decisions. Accordingly, here overall risk response attitude is conceptualized with two dimensions: expected desired effects and expected side effects. In this conceptualization, desired effects positively influence attitude and side effects negatively influence it, as “positive and negative reactions to a stimulus have generally opposing effects on an attitude” (Cacioppo et al. 1997, p. 6).

In calculating the risk-reduction leverage, risk management prescriptions consider an equal role for the costs and benefits of risk responses (Boehm 1989). In contrast, the model suggests that the composition of attitude is asymmetrical, leaning more toward desired than side effects. Such “asymmetry in the effect of positive and negative information” is recognized in the psychology research on attitude (Cacioppo et al. 1997, p. 18) as well as implicitly by TPB, which considers that different positive and negative beliefs make different contributions to attitude (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Which way the asymmetry tends to lean, however, is context-dependent. For example, research finds that “beliefs about negative outcomes were most important in predicting speeding behavior, but beliefs about positive outcomes were most important in predicting the initiation of smoking” (Maio and Haddock 2015, p. 87). This is because attitude formation is based on attention, and people have heightened sensitivity to more accessible attitudes (Maio and Haddock 2015, pp. 56-60), whereas different contexts shape attention in different ways. In particular, cumulative prospect theory suggests that people’s preference construction depends on whether the decision context is a gain or loss (Tversky and Kahneman 1992). In a gain context, people try to protect what they already have and thus avoid taking unnecessary risks. However, in a loss context, people become risk seekers if there is hope for an improvement.

The model suggests that ITPM’s evaluation is usually performed in a gain context as projects aim to achieve certain objectives. Consequently, considering the high failure rate of IT projects (Charette and Romero 2015), ITPMs strive to avoid project failure, and thus are likely to exhibit risk aversion tendencies: their attention is biased toward the desired (mitigating) effects rather than the side effects of risk responses when evaluating them. In formal terms,

## H2 For ITPMs, the expected desired effects (EDE) of risk response enactment are more influential

than the expected side effects (ESE) on their overall risk response attitude (ORRA).

Established models of risk taking suggest that perceived risk exposure has a direct effect on risk-related decisions (e.g., Sitkin and Pablo 1992). In IS, accordingly, studies hypothesize perceived risk exposure to be a direct determinant of ITPMs’ project escalation/de-escalation decisions (e.g., Keil et al. 2000). However, they sometimes find an insignificant effect (e.g., Du et al. 2007). Furthermore, behavioral risk management studies suggest that sometimes risks are ignored, for example, due to issues related to the cost justifiability of risk responses. Combining these insights, this study suggests that perceived risk exposure has an indirect effect on the risk response decisions of ITPMs via the overall attitude construct.

On the one hand, perceived risk exposure is hypothesized to positively impact attitude through two mechanisms: heightening the importance of the believed desired effects and weakening the importance of the believed side effects. First, when ITPMs perceive a high risk exposure, they evaluate the mitigation effects of a relevant response more positively and develop a stronger expectation of desired effects. Second, while ITPMs are concerned about the side effects of risk management activities (Kutsch and Hall 2009), higher levels of perceived risk exposure focus the attention on achieving project success and weaken ITPMs’ evaluation of the importance of the side effects of risk responses, resulting in a weaker expectation of side effects. TPB hints at, without explicitly incorporating, such effect of perceived risk exposure on attitude by suggesting that background factors, including risk perception, can influence the strength of people’s beliefs or the evaluation of those beliefs (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). In the present context, the latter mechanism is hypothesized to come into play.

In turn, attitude is hypothesized to positively influence the decision about risk response. ITPMs are responsible for ensuring project success (PMI 2013), and if they believe that an action is useful (harmful) in this effort, they will most likely decide to enact (avoid) it. Indeed, ITPMs who apply risk management practices are positive about the effectiveness of such practices (Ropponen 1999). Conversely, the unjustified costs associated with managing risks are cited as key reasons for not applying risk management practices (Kutsch et al. 2013; Kutsch and Hall 2009). TPB explains this link by suggesting that people’s decisions reasonably follow their behavioral attitudes (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010) since they want to avoid cognitive dissonance between their beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980, pp. 22-23).

Combining these two arguments, a high level of perceived risk exposure would motivate the enactment of a risk response only if the attitude is positive and strong. Such indirect effect via attitude is explicitly supported by TPB, which suggests that the influence of background factors including risk perception is mediated by the principal determinants of intention, such as behavioral attitude (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010, p. 22). In formal terms,

H3 The influence of an ITPM’s perceived risk exposure (PRE) on the ITPM’s decision to enact a specific risk response in a particular project (RRD) is indirect, via the ITPM’s overall attitude toward enacting that risk response in that project (ORRA).

A sole focus on the effect of perceived risk exposure and attitude on risk response would assume that ITPMs “make decisions in order to maximize efficiency and effectiveness” (Mignerat and Rivard 2012, p. 147). However, the conceptual territory of this study includes cases where ITPMs are subject to various pressures, for example, where they are motivated to follow what significant others expect them to do or what they think successful others would do (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). First, ITPMs are surrounded in their profession by an institutional field, comprising people and organizations (other project managers, professional associations, and certification bodies) who share the objective of successful IT project conduct (Mignerat and Rivard 2012). Because many IT project management practices are institutionalized, ITPMs are often expected to comply with these practices to gain legitimacy (Mignerat and Rivard 2012), which is a key concern (Kutsch et al. 2013). Moreover, many ITPMs go through certification programs in which the common expectation is that risk management “should be done in the way the handbooks prescribe it” (de Bakker et al. 2010, p. 493). More noticeably, ITPMs are exposed to senior management pressures to enact specific risk responses (Lim et al. 2011), such as carrying out detailed up front planning.

Nevertheless, ITPMs may have different levels of motivation to comply with each type of pressure. An ITPM might be motivated to comply with institutional pressures because she/he considers that the enactment of some practices provides functional legitimacy. For example, this applies to the case of project failure when “having used well-recognized project management practices could be invoked as a defense” (Mignerat and Rivard 2012, p. 147). The motivation to comply with top management would be strong for ITPMs who are in an organizational context where a strong power structure is in place (e.g., Drummond 1996; Kutsch and Hall 2009). Indeed, some ITPMs do not adopt formal risk management practices because senior management does not require it (Kutsch and Hall 2009).

Together, as supported by TPB, the perception of an expectation from a salient source of pressure and the motivation to comply with it form perceived pressure. A strong expectation for or against the responses and a high motivation to comply would lead to a strong perceived pressure that would influence a risk response decision. It is noteworthy that while the existence of a strong expectation from a pressure source but a weak motivation to comply is unlikely to influence actual risk response, it is likely to create a decoupling between what ITPMs say and what they actually do. For instance, this would be so in the case of an ITPM who prepares a formal risk management plan according to organizational routines but never actually implements it (Mignerat and Rivard 2012). In formal terms,

H4 The stronger an ITPM’s perceived pressure (PP) for (or against) enacting a specific risk response in a particular project, the stronger the ITPM’s decision to enact (or not to enact) that risk response in that project (RRD).

Recent additions to TPB in IS suggest that attitude and perceived pressure may have a nonlinear, negative synergy relationship or, in other words, a replacing effect (Titah and Barki 2009). When ITPMs do not feel a strong perceived pressure for/against enacting a specific risk response but have a strong attitude toward the risk response, they will act according to their attitude. For example, if an ITPM is not under much pressure to include user representatives in the team but has a strong negative attitude toward doing so, the ITPM is unlikely to plan having user representatives. However, when ITPMs do not have a strong attitude but feel a strong pressure about a risk response, they might reasonably comply with the pressure to fulfill their need for legitimacy. In this vein, regarding the very political environment of the infamous Taurus project, Drummond (1996) concludes that “it is not analysis but politics which determines what risks are run, when, and by whom” (p. 355). In summary, in the presence of a weak perceived pressure, the effect of attitude on risk response is strengthened; and when attitude is weak, perceived pressure will have a stronger effect on the decision:

H5 There is a negative synergy between overall risk response attitude (ORRA) and perceived pressure (PP) in determining an ITPM’s risk response decision (RRD).

Enacting a specific risk response might or might not be under an ITPM’s control. Some facilitating conditions might be required to gain control over performing the behavior but they might not be available. Two such aspects of control are authority and capacity (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). In terms of authority, while risk response enactment might need an uncompromised decision on the part of ITPMs (Bannerman 2008), the requisite authority is sometimes unclear and is not always fully delegated to ITPMs:

Other than the nominal control they have over their own team, they may not have a corporate-wide base of positional power through which they can get resources, issue directives, or enforce their will. As a result, authority, as a power base, is not one that project managers can rely on with any degree of certainty in most organizations (Pinto 2000, p. 86).

In such a situation, to obtain decisional authority, “they are forced to negotiate on a daily basis with a variety of organizational members and external groups” (Pinto 2000, p. 90). Therefore, if authority is required but lacking, ITPMs might experience weak perceived control and feel unable to enact specific risk responses.

In terms of capacity, risk response enactment might require certain resources, skills, and knowledge (Bannerman 2008). Yet, projects often have limited access to resources (PMI 2013), and “almost all resources (financial, human, informational, etc.) must be negotiated and bargained” (Pinto 2000, p. 85). Also, the requisite knowledge and skills of the team members responsible for performing the activities might be lacking. Depending on whether significant resources are required and on the lack or availability of such resources, ITPMs might perceive that they are able or unable to enact specific risk responses. In summary, risk response enactment requires a proper level of authority and capacity. TPB supports this argument by suggesting that people do not plan to do what they believe they cannot do (Ajzen 1991), suggesting perceived control as a direct antecedent to intention. In formal terms,

H6 The stronger an ITPM’s perceived control (PC) over performing a specific risk response in a particular project, the stronger the ITPM’s decision to enact that risk response in that project (RRD).

## Research Methods

The model was empirically enriched and tested using a mixture of qualitative and quantitative methods inspired by Fishbein and Ajzen (2010). First, the focal behavior was specified for three specific risk responses; second, ITPMs’ beliefs about each selected risk response were elicited and used to populate the belief composites of the relevant model instance; and third, the three model instances—one for each specific risk response—were tested in separate surveys.

## Phase 1: Selecting Specific Risk Responses and Instantiating the Model

In Phase 1, one specific risk response from each category of internal integration, formal planning, and external integration (McFarlan 1981; Mignerat and Rivard 2012) was selected. To do this, a web survey (see Appendix A) presented respondents with 19 risk responses drawn from Table 1. Respondents rated each response on three characteristics: importance for risk mitigation, extent of control one has over the enactment of the response, and frequency of enactment in IT projects. A risk response was considered for selection if it averaged medium or high importance, a medium to high extent of control, and a low to medium frequency. Indeed, a high score for frequency of enactment might imply a quasiinstitutionalized risk response, leaving little room for ITPMs agency in decision making (Mignerat and Rivard 2012).

A convenience sample of 60 ITPMs and academics with IT project management expertise were invited to complete a survey. A total o 29 usable responses were received (48.3%). The Euclidean distance between the actual percentage of the ratings that matched the desired values and the ideal percentage (100%–100%–100%) was calculated for each risk response. Within each category, the specific risk response (SRR) with the minimum distance was selected, resulting in having end-user representatives as team members (SRR1), appreciating team members' work in a tangible way during the project (SRR2), and dedicating much effort to project planning (SRR3).

## Phase 2: Eliciting ITPMs’ Beliefs about Each Specific Risk Response

The underlying beliefs of the probability and loss dimensions of perceived risk exposure, the expected desired and side effects dimensions of attitude, perceived pressure, and perceived control were identified, to anchor them in the context of the three SRRs. To do so, a belief-elicitation procedure was used to identify the modal salient beliefs—that is, a few beliefs that are common across a representative subset of target respondents (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010)—about each specific risk response (SRR). This procedure was implemented through interviews with 24 ITPMs (see Appendix B for details).

Content analysis of the interviews revealed that, indeed, the respondents associated enacting each SRR with certain desired and side effects. For example, a salient belief about the desired effects of SRR1 was “preventing end-user resistance” (60.0% of respondents), and a belief about its side effects was “leading to leaking of project’s inside information to end-users” (33.3%). The respondents also identified sources of pressures for/against enacting the SRRs (e.g., upper management was frequently mentioned for all SRRs). Moreover, for each SRR, some salient factors and circumstances that would facilitate or inhibit its enactment were extracted. For example, 27% of the respondents believed that SRR1 would be facilitated by having “the authority to choose the right user representatives.” Moreover, for PRE, using the outcomes mentioned in the EDE beliefs, the probability and loss formative items were created.

## Phase 3: Testing the Three Model Instances

A separate instrument was developed and validated for each SRR (see Appendix C for details). For each proximal antecedent of risk response decision, the unified wholes are reflective and measured with items adapted from Fishbein and Ajzen (2010). The belief composites are formative and were populated with the beliefs elicited in Phase 2.

The formative nature of belief composites is related to two issues. First, the TPB literature is inconclusive on whether belief composites should be modeled as antecedents or as parts of their corresponding constructs (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Here, belief composite items are deemed to have the same attitudinal nature as the unified whole attitude, only at a more granular level. This implies that considering a belief composite as a separate antecedent of its corresponding construct would not only be tautological but would also subsume the variance of the construct and impede the examination of the influence of its determinants. Second, because of the hypothesized effect of PRE on EDE and ESE, having standalone belief composites for these two dimensions would make them endogenous formative constructs, a practice that has been recently debated in the Information Systems field (Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas 2013; Rigdon et al. 2014). The rationale of this debate is that the formative items are sometimes suggested to cover all causes of a construct; thus, no other antecedents could be envisioned.

To address these issues, MIMIC (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001) was used to implement the belief composites and unified wholes simultaneously. This considers the belief composites as the constituents of constructs but not as their causes (for a critical discussion, see Edwards 2011, p. 381). From this point of view, the association between a belief composite and a unified whole (i.e., the MIMIC path) indicates the extent to which the constituting granular elements of the construct are captured. Moreover, the causal paths from the antecedents to the construct explain why a variance in the construct (in its unified whole and in its underlying composite parts) exists. This was implemented in two stages: first, to integrate the variances in the composite and reflective parts, the latent variable scores of EDE and ESE were calculated using MIMIC; second, the scores were used to examine the influence of PRE on these dimensions.

The instrument included control variables, namely risk propensity, experience as an ITPM, number of projects managed, project size, and project management approach (agile versus waterfall versus proprietary).

## Data Collection

The target population consisted of IT managers in charge of an IT project at the time of responding. A focus on the current project alleviated the recall issues in a study of determinants of behavioral intention. A data collection company (Empanel Online) was hired to distribute the surveys. This led to 112, 116, and 121 complete responses respectively for the three SRRs. The characteristics of the ITPMs and the projects are presented in Table 3, which shows a high variation in terms of demographics. Potential biases, including non-response bias and common-method bias, were examined and none posed any issue (see Appendix C for more details).

## Results

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using Smart-PLS 2.0 (Ringle et al. 2005). PLS was deemed an appropriate choice for several reasons: (1) the theorybuilding nature of the study (Gefen et al. 2011); (2) the distribution of the data was only moderately normal (Gefen et al. 2000); (3) belief composites are formative, which are easier to analyze in PLS; (4) the sample size per SRR was fairly small (Gefen et al. 2000). Additional tests of the mediation and interaction hypotheses were performed in SPSS.

## Measurement Model Results

Table 4 summarizes the validity statistics for the constructs. For the reflective constructs, reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity were assessed. All constructs had a composite reliability above 0.7 (Esposito Vinzi et al. 2010) and a Cronbach's alpha above 0.6, which is acceptable for a theorybuilding effort (Nunnally 1967). All constructs exhibited a satisfactory level of convergent validity as the AVE for all constructs was higher than 0.5 (Gefen et al. 2000), and all items had a weight of 0.7 or higher. An exception was R\_PC\_1 in SRR1, which had a weight of 0.629. Given the very low loading of this item on other constructs, it was deemed appropriate to retain it. Finally, all measures had an acceptable level of discriminant validity.

H1: The Stronger Influence of Loss than Probability on Perceived Risk Exposure. The effect of loss on PRE, compared to that of probability, was examined in SPSS using factor scores computed in PLS. For all three SRRs, the loss due to undesired outcomes was a significantly better predictor of PRE than the probability of undesired outcomes (Table 6). This provides support for H1 for all three SRRs.

H2: The Stronger Influence of Expected Desired than Side Effects on Overall Attitude. The effect of EDE on ORRA compared to that of ESE was examined in SPSS. For all three SRRs, the effect of EDE on ORRA was significantly stronger than that of ESE (Table 7). This suggests that for ITPMs, the expected desired effects of a risk response are a more important part of evaluating a particular risk response than are its expected side effects. Therefore, H2 is supported for all three SRRs.

H3: The Indirect Effect of Perceived Risk Exposure on Risk Response Decision via Attitude. Next, the indirect effect of PRE on RRD via ORRA (H3) was examined. For all SRRs, the impact of PRE on EDE was significant and explained a moderate portion of its variance. For SRR1 and SRR3, the effect of PRE on ESE was significant with a small amount of variance explained. For SRR2, the path from PRE to ESE was in the reverse direction as hypothesized and was significant. Moreover, ORRA had a significant path, moderate/ large effect size, and medium predictive relevance for all SRRs.

An initial mediation analysis using bootstrap results in PLS supported this mediation effect for all three SRRs (SRR1: partial, SRR2 and SRR3: full mediation). Nevertheless, such a mediation test is based on Sobel’s test, which uses “a product of two or more regression coefficients” by “assuming a normal sampling distribution of the product” although “the sampling distribution of the product of regression coefficients is not normal” (Hayes 2015, p. 7). To deal with such nonnormal distributions, the approach proposed by Preacher and Hayes (2008) was used. In this approach, instead of indicating a full or partial mediation, the direct and indirect effects of the independent variable on the dependent variable are separately examined. Using the latent variable scores of PRE, ORRA, and RRD, this approach was implemented using the “Process” macro in SPSS (www.processmacro.org). Table 8 summarizes the results. The p-values suggest no significant direct effect. Moreover, all indirect effects are significant at the 5% level as zero falls outside the confidence intervals. Thus, H3 is supported for all three SRRs.

H4: The Effect of Perceived Pressure on Risk Response Decision. PP had a significant path to RRD, small effect size, and small predictive relevance for all SRRs. Therefore, H4 is supported for all three SRRs.

H5: The Negative Synergy between Attitude and Perceived Pressure. The interaction effect between ORRA and PP was investigated in SPSS using factor scores. The negative synergy of overall risk response attitude and perceived pressure was supported for SRR2 and—at 10% level—for SRR1 and SRR3 (Table 9). While including an interaction term does not influence the effect of ORRA on RRD, it renders the influence of PC for SRR1 and SRR2 and the influence of PP for SRR3 insignificant. Accordingly, H5 is supported.

H6: The Effect of Perceived Control on Risk Response Decision. The results for the influence of PC on RRD were mixed, with a nonsignificant effect for SRR1, a significant path but in a reverse direction for SRR2 with a small effect size and predictive relevance, and a significant path in the predicted direction for SRR3, with a small effect size and predictive relevance. Therefore, H6 is supported for SRR3, not supported for SRR1, and in a reverse direction for SRR2.

## Examining the MIMIC Version of the Model

Because we considered the proximal determinants of RRD to have underlying belief composites, the model instances were respecified with MIMIC and reexamined with PLS (Figure 2).

For all three SRRs, all MIMIC paths (between the belief composites and the unified wholes of EDE, ESE, PP, and PC) were significant and in the predicted direction. The mean path was 0.489 (0.434 after including PRE), close to the 0.5 average reported over the past decades of TPB research (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). In terms of the relationships between the constructs, using the MIMIC approach produced results comparable with those of the model presented above. An exception is that using the MIMIC approach renders the PREöESE path for SRR1 and SRR3 nonsignificant (as compared to a significant, small effect in the non-MIMIC model).

## Verifying the Explanatory Power of the Model

To verify the explanatory power of this model, its adjusted R<sup>2</sup> was compared with that of a model based on Sitkin and Weingart (1995), often used in IT risk-related decision-making research (e.g., Keil et al. 2000). The adjusted R<sup>2</sup> for our model was respectively 50.3%, 41.1%, and 48.1% for the three instances, which is greater than that of the model based on Sitkin and Weingart (23.4%, 1.7%, and 17.5%). The results thus indicate that our model indeed explains a larger portion of the RRD variance.

## Discussion

Below we discuss how our model extends behavioral research on IT project risk management and how our study contributes to the broader literature on risk-related decision making in IT, and to the foundational theories leveraged for our model development. After acknowledging the limitations of our study, we offer implications for practice.

## Providing a Theoretical Anchor to the Behavioral Literature

Our study makes two key theoretical contributions to behavioral IT project risk management research. First, it addresses the question of what; that is, it identifies which constructs should be considered to explain the phenomenon, striving for both comprehensiveness and parsimony (Whetten 1989). Indeed, we synthesized the antecedents of riskmanagement-related decisions uncovered by prior studies (e.g., Bannerman 2008; Kutsch et al. 2013) into a parsimonious set of four constructs and offered clear conceptual definitions for each. More precisely, we mapped antecedents such as threat (Bannerman 2008) and awareness (Kutsch and

Hall 2005) of risk—or lack thereof—onto the construct of perceived risk exposure. We reconceptualized elements such as cost justifiability (Kutsch and Hall 2010), unintended consequences of risk responses (Charette 1996), and usefulness of risk management (Taylor et al. 2012) by developing the overall risk response attitude construct. We distilled “handbook prescriptions” (de Bakker et al. 2010), politics (Drummond 1996), and legitimacy issues (Kutsch et al. 2013) into perceived pressure, and we synthesized authority to act (Kutsch et al. 2013) and resource availability (Bannerman 2008) into perceived control. We further specified perceived risk exposure and overall risk response attitude as secondorder constructs, with perceived risk exposure comprising the probability of undesired outcomes and the associated magnitude of loss dimensions, and overall risk response attitude consisting of expected desired and side effects of enacting a risk response.

Second, our study addresses the questions of how and why the constructs are related (Whetten 1989) by blending the theoretical domains (Oswick et al. 2011) of TPB (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010) and behavioral decision making (e.g., Slovic et al. 2007; Tversky and Kahneman 1992), enriched with psychology research on attitude (Maio and Haddock 2015).

Indeed, although extant behavioral studies offer partial explanations for the enactment of risk management practices, none proposed an integrated explanation of the decision of whether or not to enact specific risk responses. Our model offers an explanation wherein an ITPM’s decision is indeed influenced by perceived risk exposure but only through her/ his overall attitude toward enacting a corresponding risk response (H3). The decision is also influenced by the pressures perceived from the environment (H4), the negative synergy between attitude and perceived pressure (H5), and the extent of control the ITPM perceives to have over the enactment of the response (H6). This explanation is refined by hypothesizing the asymmetrical structure of two key constructs.

In doing so, we explained the higher importance of impact than probability on perceived risk exposure (H1), a tendency alluded to in the behavioral literature on IT project management (e.g., Bannerman 2008), by drawing from behavioral decision literature (Slovic et al. 2007). We also proposed a stronger influence of desired effects of enacting a risk response than that of side effects on overall risk response attitude (H2).

To elucidate the mediating effect of attitude, our twodimensional conceptualization enabled us to observe that, although a high level of perceived risk exposure strengthens the evaluations of desired effects of a corresponding risk response, it does not weaken ITPMs’ evaluation of the side effects of the risk response.<sup>2</sup> As such, expected side effects can prevent ITPMs from enacting a risk response despite a strong perception of risk exposure and a strong expectation of desired effects from the risk response. We thus further the finding of qualitative studies that a major reason for avoiding risk management is the cost justifiability issue regarding such actions (Kutsch and Hall 2009).

Moreover, while our findings support the role of perceived pressures on risk response decisions (H4), we found it to have only a small effect size and predictive relevance. This advances our understanding of IT project risk management in two ways. First, we further support the qualitative finding that some ITPMs do not apply risk management because of legitimacy concerns (Kutsch et al. 2013; Kutsch and Hall 2009) and extend it to the context of enacting specific risk responses. Second, we find that, in contrast to the significant role of perceived pressures (e.g., from top management or project management approaches) on applying the formal risk management process (Kutsch et al. 2013), it has only a small effect on enacting specific risk responses, an effect further weakened by an ITPM’s risk response attitude (H5).

Finally, we found perceived control to have a mixed effect on risk response decisions across various specific risk responses, despite our theoretical justifications for its positive influence (H6). The effect was supported for SRR3 with a small effect size and predictive relevance, but the results suggested a small negative effect for SRR2 and no effect for SRR1. We suggest that these findings are due to the ceiling effect (see Fishbein and Ajzen 2010, p. 70). First, Phase 1 of this study selected SRRs that are within the span of control of ITPMs. This increases the mean score of perceived control and diminishes its variance, thus attenuating its impact on risk response decisions: most ITPMs perceived that they had high control over the studied SRRs, with perceived control items having the highest mean (6.4 on a 7-point scale), the lowest variance (SD = 0.7), and the highest kurtosis values (5.14) among all items. Second, a further analysis of the perceived control composite items suggests that the extent to which various resources are essential to have for risk response enactment is positively and significantly associated with the extent to which ITPMs believe that they can obtain these resources (respective R<sup>2</sup> for three SRRs: 0.451, 0.312, 0.409), again leading to inflated perceived control values. Apparently, unlike applying the formal risk management process, which is often deemed to be a formality and might be avoided due to a lack of facilitating factors such as authority (Kutsch and Hall 2009), ITPMs believe that the resources required for enacting the studied risk responses are obtainable.

## Informing Research on Risk-Related IT Decisions

Beyond contributing to the behavioral literature on IT project risk management, we also add to a vast body of research on IT decisions under risk in three key ways.

Revisiting a Key Conceptual Assumption. The prescriptive/ confirmatory studies of IT project risk management and the studies that build on Sitkin and Pablo’s (1992) model of risk taking build on the assumption that managers’ risk awareness will lead them to enact appropriate risk responses. In contrast, by evidencing the mediating effect of attitude on risk response decision, our study calls for more attention to this construct.

First, this assumption is visible in the prescriptive and confirmatory literature on IT project risk management (e.g., Barki et al. 2001; Lyytinen et al. 1998), which portrays ITPMs as having a universal positive attitude toward enacting a risk response. As such, it overlooks that ITPMs take into account expected side effects, which we found to play an important role. Further confirmatory studies can examine how risk response attitude, especially with uneven attention to the desired and side effects of risk responses, is related to project success. Future prescriptive studies could consider the results of confirmatory research to determine how one should account for the unequal influence of the expected desired and side effects of specific risk responses.

Second, this assumption has been inherited from the managerial risk-taking model (Sitkin and Pablo 1992; Sitkin and Weingart 1995) by other studies of risk-related decision making in IT across different contexts such as project initiation and continuation (Du et al. 2007; Keil et al. 2008; Keil et al. 2000), supply chain digitization (Xue et al. 2013), and interorganizational data exchange (Nicolaou and McKnight 2006). This risk-taking model considers a direct path between risk perception and risk taking, which, however, is sometimes found to be insignificant (e.g., Du et al. 2007). We theorize and empirically show that risk response attitude mediates this path. In doing so, we explore theoretical mechanisms through which risk perception has such effect, suggesting that even if managers’ perceived risk exposure is strong, a negative or weak attitude might motivate them not to enact a risk response.

Leveraging the Conceptualization of Perceived Risk Exposure. Our conceptualization of perceived risk exposure contributes to two streams of research. First, risk exposure is a key construct in the confirmatory and prescriptive research on IT project risk management. Confirmatory research could examine whether the observed tendency to give more weight to magnitude of loss than to probability better explains project success than giving these dimensions equal weight, which it currently does. Second, many studies of risk-related decision making in IT define risk perception as unidimensional. Our conceptualization of perceived risk exposure as a secondorder construct, and the finding that the magnitude of loss has more influence on ITPMs’ perception than the probability, could contribute to better capturing the nature and effects of risk-related perceptions.

Examining the Interplay of Managerial Attitudes and Environmental Pressures. Our study also speaks to two interrelated bodies of prescriptive research in the IT project management literature. First, several prescriptive studies of project risk management have dedicated significant attention to institutionalizing risk management practices (see Mignerat and Rivard 2012). While this body of research contributes to ITPMs’ perceived pressure to enact risk responses, our model and findings suggest that ITPMs’ own evaluation of the effects of a risk response is key to the actual enactment of specific risk responses. If ITPMs’ perceived pressure is not aligned with their risk response attitude, they are likely to decouple what they say from what they do; for instance, they may report a plan for a risk response but not substantively implement it (Mignerat and Rivard 2012). To examine such risk response misrepresentation, future studies can extend the selective status reporting stream of research (Keil et al. 2014) to compare the reported and the genuinely intended risk response plans.

Second, the broader project management literature offers approaches (e.g., agile) that can guide the different functions of ITPMs, with one such function being risk response enactment. For example, adopting agile is expected to encourage collaborating with user representatives (Highsmith and Cockburn 2001). However, our survey reveals that ITPMs’ risk responses are sometimes inconsistent with the spirit of agile methods. For example, our self-reported measure of using agile vs. waterfall is negatively associated with SRR1, having user representatives as team members (small effect size, path = -0.284, and R<sup>2</sup> = 0.081). One explanation for this finding is that the adopted methodologies constitute only a weak pressure on ITPMs, which is replaced by their attitude (our H5). Moreover, in reality, agile project management approaches are subject to appropriation, which involves “changing agile methods to align them with the needs of different projects and organizational environments” (Cao et al. 2009, p. 333). Other explanations include the variations between agile approaches in terms of the risk responses they imply and/or ITPMs’ lack of understanding of the spirit of agile methods. Given the limitations of our single-item measure of agile, future studies that better link specific project management approaches and risk responses are warranted.

## Contributing Back to TPB

In lieu of a simple adaptation, our model enriches TPB by mobilizing studies from psychology research on attitude and the behavioral decision theory literature, which leads us to make four contributions back to TPB. First, we explicitly account for perceived risk (exposure) and its indirect effect on intention via overall attitude, a path that has only been alluded to in Fishbein and Ajzen (2010). Second, we define attitude using two dimensions of positive and negative valence. The multidimensionality of attitude has been discussed elsewhere, but the focus has been on distinguishing instrumental from experiential attitudes (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Our separation of attitude into positive and negative valence has allowed us to use them as different theoretical mechanisms to justify the indirect effects of background factors (here risk perception). Third, and relatedly, we theorize an asymmetrical importance for the two dimensions of the overall attitude construct, suggesting how positive and negative expected outcomes are evaluated differently. This is a major update to TPB, taking into account its roots in early behavioral decision theories and advancing it using more recent behavioral decision theories such as prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992). Finally, TPB literature suggests that it is unclear whether the belief composites are antecedents to the overall constructs or a part of them (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). Addressing this issue, we make a methodological contribution by using MIMIC to examine the belief composites as part of the constructs.

## Links to Behavioral Decision Theories

Researchers have called for using more behavioral decision theory insights (e.g., prospect theory) in studying IS phenomena, especially to show how context influences decision making (Goes 2013). Indeed, a keyword search in AIS Senior Scholar Basket of Eight journals plus a forward and backward search (Webster and Watson 2002) revealed only 19 articles alluding to behavioral decision-making notions including decision making biases and prospect theory. Our study adds to this small but growing literature in three key ways.

First, we extend the application of prospect theory to a new IS context. Complementing its application to high-level project management decisions (Lauer 1996; Tiwana et al. 2006), we use it to examine a more operational yet still critical decision of whether or not to enact a specific risk response. In doing so, we follow a core tenet of prospect theory that decisions are reference dependent (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) by using the risk level (when a risk response action is not enacted) as the reference point. We then find support for a core proposition of prospect theory—that losses loom larger than gains—by observing that ITPMs tend to weigh the EDE more heavily than ESE in forming overall risk response attitude. This also means that we find our respondents to be risk averse, being attracted to risk mitigation efforts. To link this finding to that of Lauer’s (1996), who finds ITPMs not to be generally risk averse (they are likely to be risk seekers in a loss context), we note that as our respondents were actual managers of ongoing IT projects, it is likely for them to see IT projects as gain contexts to be protected by risk management in order to achieve their objectives.

Second, by showing that in a risk management context EDE is more important than ESE for ITPMs, we show that biases (e.g., due to problem framing) influence how risk response attitude is formed. This is in line with the propositions of Dinev et al. (2015), who posit heuristics and biases as antecedents to risk/benefit calculus (resembling overall attitude), distinct from other contextual factors. Likewise, we show that loss is more important than probability in composing ITPMs’ perceived risk exposure, further specifying this tendency in the present context. It is noteworthy that with the exception of few studies, such as Hong et al. (2011) in an IT acceptance context, IS studies that explicitly consider biases as antecedents of attitudes and behaviors are scarce and could be furthered.

Third, most studies define biases as errors of judgment, that is, “systematic deviations from a normatively correct answer or standard” (Browne and Parsons 2012, p. 1005). However, we do not make such a value-laden assumption and view heuristic thinking as potentially functional (Gigerenzer 2008). In particular, we do not argue whether EDE having a heavier weight on ORRA than ESE and loss being more important than probability in forming perceived risk exposure are necessarily dysfunctional in this context—perhaps ITPMs need to mind risks and risk responses as such to manage projects more successfully, considering the high failure rate and significant negative impact of failing IT projects. Nonetheless, given the importance of self-awareness in preventing dysfunctional biases in IS (Arnott 2006), we adopt the view that “choice should be presented to others, or to oneself, from a variety of frames simultaneously in order to help determine the most accurate and consistence preferences” (McDermott 2001, p. 26).

## Limitations

Our study has some limitations that future research could address. While our model pertains to risk responses in general, it was actually tested with three instantiations only. Therefore, its generalizability could be enhanced by testing it with other instantiations. Also, we theorized about risk response decisions (i.e., intentions), not their enactment. Although intentions and behaviors are highly correlated (Sheppard et al. 1988), the model could be extended by including actual behavior. As well, the study used a self-reported assessment of method (agile versus waterfall); however, different ITPMs may ascribe different connotations to the term “agile.” Future research could refine our measure (e.g., assess the extent of adoption of agile principles). Moreover, our model assumes an individual-level decision about risk responses. Given the role of other stakeholders (Lim et al. 2011) or the absence of a formal IT project manager in some agile projects, studying this decision at a collective level could illuminate other aspects of risk response decisions. Finally, risk is stressful for ITPMs and developers alike (Venkatesh et al. 2018; Windeler et al. 2017). While we focused on instrumental attitude, future research could study experiential attitude (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010) and examine the anticipated emotions (Loewenstein et al. 2001) associated with enacting risk responses (e.g., fear of project failure).

## Implications for Practice

Notwithstanding its limitations, our study has implications for several IT project management audiences. First, the results should motivate ITPMs to reflect on what shapes and biases their risk response (i.e., the asymmetrical role played by the dimensions of perceived risk exposure and of attitude), as self-awareness may help in making informed decisions. Second, project governing entities (e.g., steering committees) should be aware that exerting pressure on ITPMs to enact risk responses without being ensured of their positive attitude might lead them to formally plan for, but not actually enact, responses. Exerting contradictory forces on ITPMs (e.g., simultaneous pressures for economizing and for risk management) are likely to have a similar effect. To alleviate this, governing bodies could offer explicit support for risk response enactment. Finally, our findings can be mobilized by professional associations that certify ITPMs to recognize risk management as a key knowledge area (e.g., the PMI). Given the critical role of attitude as an antecedent of risk response decisions, our findings should incite the development of training material to foster positive attitude formation. To this end, expertly designed and executed efforts are required (Maio and Haddock 2015) and imply training ITPMs in the domain of project risk management beyond its mechanics, for example, by running in-depth case analyses or simulations to allow experiencing the effects of a decision to enact—or not—a risk response.

## Conclusion

This study aimed at explaining and predicting ITPMs’ decision of whether to enact specific risk responses. To this end, it proposed and tested—with 349 ITPMs—a model integrating IT project risk management behavioral research and blending the theoretical domains of TPB and behavioral decision theories. The results show that risk response decisions are influenced by ITPMs’ perceived risk exposure mediated by their risk response attitude. As proposed, the expected desired effects of enacting a risk response were more influential on an ITPM’s attitude than the expected side effects; also, the magnitude of loss dimension of perceived risk exposure was more influential than its probability. Moreover, the results show that ITPMs’ decisions to enact a risk response are influenced by their perceived pressure, and some are influenced by their perceived control. In turn, perceived pressure and attitude synergistically—yet negatively— influence an ITPM’s decision.

Our study makes several theoretical contributions. First, for behavioral IT project risk management research, it provides a theoretical anchor by blending theoretical domains to offer a set of clearly conceptualized constructs, hypothesizing their interrelationships, and testing the hypotheses. Second, it informs research on risk-related IT decisions by illuminating the mediating role of ITPMs’ attitude between risk perceptions and risk-related decisions, and refining the conceptualization of risk perception used in this literature. Third, it contributes to TPB by accounting explicitly for perceived risk exposure, offering a second-order construct conceptualization of attitude, and theorizing the asymmetrical importance of two dimensions of attitude. The study also makes a methodological contribution to TPB research by addressing the concerns about whether to measure the determinants of intention using reflective items or belief composites. To practitioners, the study offers avenues for better understanding ITPMs decisions in terms of risk response enactment as well as a means of shaping their attitude toward the practice of project risk management.

## Appendix A

## Phase 1: Risk Response Selection Instrument and Results

To select risk responses matching the desired profile, we designed and ran a survey with ITPMs and IT project management academic experts (Table A1). The survey presented the respondents with the specific risk responses (SRRs) from Table 1 and asked them to rate each SRR on the three profile dimensions on a scale of low, medium, and high.

For each SRR, the percentages of the responses that matched the desired value of each dimension of the profile were calculated. For example, for the first SRR (having user representatives), 71% of the ratings matched the ideal profile of low or medium frequency of practice. Then, the Euclidean distance of each risk response from the ideal profile (representing how well the risk response matches the entire profile) was calculated using the formula

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Euclidean Distance} ^ {2} = & (1 0 0 \% - \text {percent of responses rating importance as Med / High}) ^ {2} \\ & + (1 0 0 \% - \text {percent of responses rating control as Med / High}) ^ {2} \\ & + (1 0 0 \% - \text {percent of responses rating frequency as Low / Med}) ^ {2} \end{array}
$$

A one-sample t-test was used to verify whether the calculated distance for each SRR was different from the mean distance of all SRRs. Table A2 presents the results. The mean Euclidean distance from the ideal profile was 0.321 (SD = 0.631) over 19 SRRs. For each risk response category, the SRR with the minimum Euclidean distance was selected for further examination (formatted as bold in the table). As the p-values indicate, the distance for each selected SRR was significantly different from the mean distance over all SRRs.

## Appendix B

## Phase 2: Belief Elicitation Instrument and Detailed Results

This study considers the EDE and ESE dimensions of ORRA, PP, and PC to have underlying belief composites. As the beliefs pertain to the specific risk responses (SRRs) studied, they were created using a belief elicitation procedure (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010), using a semi-structured interview guide. Data analysis intended to identify the modal salient attitudinal, pressure, and control beliefs of ITPMs. While salient beliefs are held by one person, modal salient beliefs are shared by different people, here by at least 20% of the respondents (see Fishbein and Ajzen 2010, p. 103). Responses were analyzed via open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss and Corbin 1990) in NVivo 10. Table B1 presents examples of the interview text and the open, axial (belief statements), and selective codes (theoretical constructs informed by TPB) for SRR1. Data collection and coding continued until saturation was reached. This effort resulted in conducting 15 interviews for SRR1, 16 for SRR2, and 12 for SRR3 with a total of 24 ITPMs. One coder performed the coding, and a second coder verified 10% of the open codes and all axial/selected codes (belief statements). The same PP beliefs were used for all SRRS. The few disagreements—on the wording of the belief statements—were discussed until resolved. Table B2 presents the final belief statements.

## Phases 3: Survey Instrument Development and Validation Details

A survey instrument was created, validated, and tested for each specific SRR.

Item Development: The reflective measures of EDE, ESE, ORRA, PP, PC, and RRD were adapted from a standard TPB questionnaire (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010). The items were worded to capture the ITPMs’ own beliefs and to follow the TPB’s principle of compatibility (i.e., correspond to a specific action, target of action, context, and time). For PRE, reflective items were adapted from the literature (e.g., MacCrimmon and Wehrung 1990 and Keil et al. 2000). The belief composites for EDE, ESE, PP, and PC, and the formative items for the P and L dimensions of PRE were developed through the procedure explained in Phase 2 (Appendix B). Control variables include risk propensity (adapted from Keil et al. 2000; MacCrimmon and Wehrung 1990); experience (a formative index of age, number of years of experience as ITPM, number of projects managed, and project management certification); project size (a formative index of project budget, project duration, the number of team members, and the number of users); and project management approach (agile versus waterfall versus proprietary).

Construct Validation: Three experts (an ITPM and two IT Ph.D. candidates) commented on the face and content validity aspects of the items, and a few adjustments to the items’ wording were made. Three rounds of card sorting for the reflective measures were conducted to ensure item clarity and to perform a preliminary examination of convergent and discriminant validity (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Twelve judges (IT Ph.D. students) participated. The non-agreed-upon items were discussed until an acceptable interrater agreement was achieved (Kappa = 0.848 > 0.65, Moore and Benbasat 1991).

Minimizing Non-response Bias: To minimize non-response bias, several strategies were adopted (Sivo et al. 2006). Efforts were made to make the survey respondent-friendly by making it readable, convenient to answer, and short. A financial incentive was provided by the firm hired for online data collection. Respondents were offered personal feedback on “some aspects of their project management profile” upon survey completion. The required sample size for the main test was estimated and targeted using two approaches. First, the busiest instantiated construct had 7 composite items and 3 reflective ones, totaling 10 items. The rule of thumb of 10 times the number of items in the most crowded construct (Gefen et al. 2000) suggested N > 100. Second, a desired minimum statistical power of 80% (Goodhue et al. 2012) with a confidence interval of 95% and a medium effect size suggested N > 107 (Cohen 1992). Accordingly, a sample of N > 107 useful response for each SRR was then targeted, and a sample of N > 111 per risk response was achieved in data collection

Minimizing Common-Method Bias: Because both dependent and independent variables were measured with the same instrument, several procedural remedies were implemented to minimize common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003; 2012; Sharma et al. 2009). The survey’s introduction motivated respondents to respond accurately. Further statistical tests and remedies were enabled by adding a marker variable that measured intuitive decision making in management (adapted and reduced from Pretz et al. 2014). Also, the effort required to think about the predictor and criterion variables at the same time was increased by putting the marker variable and some demographics questions between them.

Minimizing Undesired Behaviors: Because online panelists might exhibit undesirable response behaviors such as faking their identity, disengaging from questions, speeding, and straight-lining (Gittelman and Trimarchi 2012; Rogers and Richarme 2009), several cross-check were programmed in the survey. These included basic IT project management knowledge questions, attention verification questions, an enforced minimum survey completion speed, and flagging straight-liners. The cross-checks were tested and adjusted several times to ensure their reliability.

Pretest: One ITPM and two experts (a faculty member and a Ph.D. candidate) completed the survey in the presence of the first author and commented on its clarity and form. Using their feedback, the appearance and flow of the web survey were improved.

Pilot Test: A pilot study was conducted to assess the psychometric properties of the constructs (N > 50 per SRR, total N = 152). The same data collection company and panel of IT managers as in the main text were used. Using a unique panelist ID provided by the data collection company ensured that the same respondents would not take part in the main survey. First, the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the reflective measures were examined. The items with less than desirable psychometric properties were dropped, keeping at least three items per construct. Second, the multicollinearity of the composite items was verified. The VIF statistics were above the threshold of 3.3 (Petter et al. 2007) for five items, two of which were slightly above the threshold of 10 (Hair et al. 2009). After a closer examination, these items were kept because they were not tapping into the same aspect of the constructs (Petter et al. 2007). Third, the correlations of the belief composites with their reflective counterparts were verified. For the 15 relevant construct instances (5 MIMIC constructs for each of 3 SRRs), the average correlation was 0.649, which is above the observed average value of 0.5 (Fishbein and Ajzen 2010).

Data Collection: A data collection company—Empanel Online—with a panel of over 30,000 North American IT managers, with high variance of industries and experience was hired to distribute the survey widely. A total of 20,000 panelists were invited to complete the survey. The targeted demographics were verified with screening questions, after which respondents were randomly assigned to one of the three SRRs. Th survey link was inactivated after reaching the response quota of N > 107 per SRR, but those who already had started the survey were allowed to finish it which led to 112, 116, and 121 complete responses (N > 111) respectively for the three SRRs. Out of the 20,000 invited panelists, 3,567 entered the survey. After the initial screening questions, 573 respondents started the main questionnaire. Of these, 349 completed the task (185 of the 573 missed an attention verification question, 31 were identified as speeders or straight-liners, and 8 voluntarily abandoned). This represents an incidence rate (i.e., survey completed/started) of 9.8% (= 349/3,567).

Non-response Bias: Non-response bias was examined by comparing early and late responses (Sivo et al. 2006). Mean difference tests of the principal constructs suggested no significant difference between these two groups; therefore, non-response bias was not a viable threat. A Shapiro-Wilk test of data normality for the reflective items suggested a non-normal distribution (p = 0.000). While no skewness value was found to be higher than the threshold of 2 (Ghiselli et al. 1981), for some of the items kurtosis was above 2, up to 5.1 for one PC item. Since the kurtosis was still below 7, the data was deemed moderately normal (Curran et al. 1996). For descriptive and survey statistics, see Table C1.

Common Method Bias: The influence of this bias was tested. First, a Harman’s single factor test was performed using principal component analysis with no rotation (Podsakoff and Organ 1986). The variance explained by the first factor was respectively 35.5%, 23.4%, and 31.0% for the SRRs. Thus, the first component does not “account for the majority of the covariance among the measures” (Podsakoff et al. 2003, p. 889). Moreover, when significant common method variance is present, “a single factor will emerge from the factor analysis” (Podsakoff et al. 2003, p. 889); however, the second component for each SRR also explains a considerable portion of variance in the data (10.7%, 17.9%, and 13.7% respectively). Second, the highest correlation between constructs was 0.758 (between ORRA and its EDE dimension for SRR1), which is below the threshold of 0.9 (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006). Third, using marker variable items, the procedure suggested by Rönkkö and Ylitalo (2011)—which adapts the procedure of Lindell and Whitney (2001) to PLS—was implemented. Initially, the mean correlation of the measured marker variable items with all other items in the model was examined. This correlation was respectively 0.003, 0.105, and 0.174 for the three SRRs, which is above 0.05 (Rönkkö and Ylitalo 2011) for two SRRs. Therefore, the measured marker variable was included as an antecedent to all the model’s endogenous constructs (Rönkkö and Ylitalo 2011). Because this did not change the significance of any path,

it was deemed unnecessary to partial out the effect of a marker variable or a method factor (Rönkkö and Ylitalo 2011). Together, the results of these three approaches suggest that common method bias does not influence the results.
