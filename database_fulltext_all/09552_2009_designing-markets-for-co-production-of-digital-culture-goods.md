# Designing markets for co-production of digital culture goods<sup>☆</sup>

Karl R. Lang ⁎, Richard D. Shang, Roumen Vragov

Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York (CUNY), New York City, NY 10010, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 19 May 2009

Keywords: Co-creation Co-production Digital culture goods Digital remix Information products Transmutation Transmutability Open culture Personalization Intellectual property rights Content licensing Long tail Market design Experimental economics

## a b s t r a c t

Digital culture products are easily reproduced, easily distributed, and subject to boundless modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, extension and recombination. This sets the stage for the emergence of markets for co-production of cultural content. This paper studies two new market structures using two different licensing arrangements. First, we consider a consumer market that offers products with content access and content transmutation rights allowing consumers to co-create content products. Second, we consider a sourcing market where producers can trade content access and transmutation rights and thus license content for reuse in production processes. We present experimental <sup>fi</sup>ndings that show that under both proposed licensing arrangements total surplus is larger than in the baseline case without tradable transmutation rights. The presence of transmutation rights diffuses monopoly power without hurting producers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that transmutation-based co-production models can serve as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism for producing for the long tail in cultural content goods segments. In addition to our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the paper also contributes a basic experimental framework and market design model that can be readily applied for testing other content licensing arrangements.

## 1. Introduction

The content industry in culture and entertainment represents a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant business sector that produced about 7% of the U.S. GDP and exported roughly \$80b worth of products (in 1999) [42]. Our aim in this paper is to develop and apply the concept of transmutability, a very particular issue at the intersection of technology, economics, and business strategy that, we argue, will play an important role in future production models in the digital content industry.

Transmutability, that is, the technical capability to easily manipulate and modify content products that are encoded as digital data [18], enables producers to more easily and more effectively adopt production models that facilitate the collaboration with other producers or with consumers (users) when creating new content products. For the purpose of this paper, in the context of producing cultural content, co-production (or co-creation) refers speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to the reuse and recombination of previously recorded content components. Hence, co-production markets make licenses available so that others (producers or consumers) can access copyrighted content for reuse in different contexts and for different purposes. We call these extended rights transmutation rights. A speci<sup>fi</sup>c licensing arrangement should de<sup>fi</sup>ne who, when, what, and how a piece of content may be transmuted in the process of creating new content. The agreement also determines whether or not a reuse fee will be charged to the licensee. We will argue in this paper that taking advantage of the inherent transmutability of digital content, digital culture businesses can strategically adopt more open and more collaborative production models and thereby co-produce content with both producers and consumers in order to increase production <sup>fl</sup>exibility and product variety, to support content customization and personalization, to lower production cost, or to enhance customer experience.

Digital culture products – songs, movies, games, multimedia content etc. – are easily reproduced, easily distributed, and subject to endless transmutation, extension and recombination [22]. Once content is represented as digital data, those bits can be copied, changed, and recombined to produce new, derivative works in the form of digital remixes or mashups. In general, the entertainment industry has traditionally been rather protective of its content products. Recently, however, some companies in the digital content space are beginning to recognize that permitting more open access to the digital content they own may mean some loss of control, but at the same time can also drive value by leveraging user-generated creativity and peer innovation [3,4,28,42].

Transmutability of digital content arises from its representation as binary data, and thereby, it enables effective content sharing and reuse for open culture production of new content. According to Hughes and

Lang [18], digital remix of culture products involves three essential activities:

• Sampling: the acquisition of content components from previously made digital works (input).

• Manipulation: the alteration, rearrangement or extension of digital content (transmutation).

• Release: recording the new, derivative work for personal use or public release (output).

In other words, a digital remix product uses <sup>fi</sup>nished culture works such as music and movies as a source for parts and raw materials that are recombined according to the creative choices of the remix engineer. In a typical creative process the artist will combine various content components to create a digital mix that presents a new, cocreated product:

$$
\text { new   product } = f (\text { component } _ {1}, \text { component } _ {2}, \ldots , \text { component } _ {n})
$$

The components may be sounds, images, or some other media content. Individual components themselves may consist of either newly created original content or samples taken from existing works. Samples are often digitally manipulated before they get added to the new product mix. Sampled components may be copyright protected or in the public domain. Access to the sources of copyrighted content materials does require permission from the copyright holders and may involve considerable effort in terms of identifying the copyright owners and negotiating license agreements and fees. Nonetheless, modern digital media technology does enable powerful digital reuse and rework effects in the production of new content and has been increasingly used by artistic creators, and more recently also by consumers, whether legally or not [2,28]. The Cabinet of Dr .Caligari (1920), the German silent <sup>fi</sup>lm classic directed by Robert Wiene, presents an illustrative example beyond the more obvious music domain [16]. In 2005, David Lee Fisher digitally scanned the original <sup>fi</sup>lm and mixed its famously expressionist background sets with newly shot speaking live actors who replaced the original silent acting, and thus created his own version, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (Remix). However, if the silent movie had not been in the public domain already (because of copyright expiration) when the remix project was done, it would have been much more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for Mr. Fisher to get the access and reuse rights to the original content source that he needed to produce the new work.

The main goal of this paper is to examine how the introduction of speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed transmutation rights affects markets for content production. With that in mind, we present an original electronic market design and an exploratory experimental study that compares how two particular content licensing schemes impact market performance in terms of surplus and service levels. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we address the following three questions in our research.

1. Can we design markets that provide incentives to monopolies for sharing content with other producers?

2. What are the speci<sup>fi</sup>c welfare effects of opening content (a) to third-party producers, and (b) to consumers?

3. Which of the two types of transmutation rights (producer-based or consumer-based) is economically more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient?

Based on our experimental research we present three key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. (1) Some pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing content monopolists will open access to their products in exchange for higher consumer prices or the ability to collect reuse license fees. (2) The adoption of speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed markets for trading content transmutation rights increases producer and consumer surplus. In other words, under the right market structure, open licensing arrangements outperform closed licensing arrangements, and both producers and consumers are better off.

(3) While both producer-based and consumer-based transmutation rights create value, our experiments did not indicate whether one is economically more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than the other.

## 2. Related literature

The principles of recombination and reuse are well established in the design sciences and in areas like architecture, engineering, software development, and manufacturing [6,14,15,20]. In the area of cultural content production, however, the idea of sharing and reusing content in order to increase productive or consumptive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency has received much less attention [18,28,42]. The present research draws on two disjoint streams of literature. The <sup>fi</sup>rst concerns research on digital products and the other relates some relevant issues from economic theory.

## 2.1. Digital products

Because digital culture products are a special case of digital products (or information goods), they take on all the qualities that have been recognized as characteristics of digital products, generally. These include high <sup>fi</sup>xed costs of developing the <sup>fi</sup>rst copy, low (or zero) cost of reproduction and distribution, indestructibility, nonrivalry and non-excludability [23,29,30]. But research in information economics to date has not much addressed the economic potential of transmutability. Choi et al [11] do discuss transmutability, but they do so mostly in terms of business risks, arguing that <sup>fi</sup>rms may lose control of their product after it is released commercially. Using the software industry as their main case in point, they argue that <sup>fi</sup>rms often take advantage of transmutability by quickly introducing new product releases in order to degrade the value of older versions and thereby regaining control over their content.

Current experimentation with digital culture products in the marketplace suggests that not only the risks of transmutability need to be considered, but also its considerable potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [42]. In Japan, for example, copyright holders of graphic novels, a highly popular genre known as manga with a multibillion-dollar market, allow other commercial producers to appropriate their creations for the purpose of creating new derivative stories [28]. These doujinshi, as they are called, freely borrow and extend the original characters and storylines in both stylistic form and plot development. Manga is thus creating pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for the content borrower while also increasing the value of and demand for the original source product. In the context of the video game industry, Arakji and Lang analyze modding, a new form of producer–consumer collaboration that is based on user participation in creating new game variants [3]. They <sup>fi</sup>nd, under certain complementarity conditions, that granting transmutation rights to gamers can increase quality and variety of game offerings.

## 2.2. Economic theory

From an economics point of view, there are two particular areas that are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally relevant to the issue of digital remix and coproduction. First, modern economic growth theory considers the recombination of knowledge components as an important factor for growth and development. Second, the economics of intellectual property rights analyzes the impacts of regulation that also affect transmutation activities. Economics research generally theorizes about knowledge in very abstract terms. For the purpose of this paper, we conceptualize cultural content as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c form of knowledge. Applying relevant <sup>fi</sup>ndings from growth theory, content components, a central concept in our research, are thus recon<sup>fi</sup>gured and recombined in the process of producing new content just like knowledge components and ideas are recombined in the creation of new knowledge.

## 2.2.1. Economic growth theory

Classical growth theory derived economic output models from the input factors of capital and labor, and treated technology change and knowledge merely as a residual variable [40]. However, in the modern economy, the factors of production also include the creation of, access to, and use and reuse of information and knowledge as endogenous variables [1]. Knowledge has been variously described as ideas, innovations, know-how, best practices, and creative designs. We extend this conceptualization by asserting that cultural content products are essentially creative designs and, therefore, knowledge-based goods. Hence, we suggest that research about knowledge creation and innovation can be extended to include cultural creativity and development.

In particular, Romer's endogenous growth theory [35] argues that knowledge-based economies can achieve increasing returns with respect to <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments in knowledge creation.<sup>1</sup> Modern growth theories now emphasize aggregate knowledge accumulation speci<sup>fi</sup>cally based on the exploration of new combinations of existing ideas [44]. Romer stipulates that access to knowledge created by individual <sup>fi</sup>rms ultimately bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the entire economy, because some of it, whether voluntarily or not, spills over and accumulates as generally available aggregate knowledge in the public domain [34]. Hence, knowledge cannot be fully controlled. While the <sup>fi</sup>rm's individual production technology can be assumed linear (or having diminishing returns), it does also, as a by-product, necessarily create spillovers that bene<sup>fi</sup>t everyone else. That is, doubling inputs in research investments will double the <sup>fi</sup>rm's output, but also create, at the same time, additional output at other <sup>fi</sup>rms through spillover effects. In other words, the reuse and recombination of existing knowledge has become the driver for innovation and economic growth. In the realm of digital culture, knowledge spillover can occur with incredible speed and effectiveness in terms of shaping new artistic trends and genres based on sharing and reusing ideas, innovative styles of expression, and creative methods.

## 2.2.2. Intellectual property rights economics and regulatory issues

Copyrights, patents, trademarks, and trade secrets have always been important to competitive strategy, but IPR has become an issue that is more central in the information economy than it ever was in the industrial economy [13,30]. This is particularly true for the digital culture industry where copyright questions signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence competitive conditions [31,41]. The goal of copyright regulation is to strike a balance between producer and consumer interests. On the one hand, copyright protection should enable content creators to appropriate enough value from their works to provide strong incentives for them to create new works in the future. But while there is a need to protect content creators by providing them with incentives to produce future works, there is also the constitutional intent to foster social progress, by making useful arts and culture products easily available to society at large, by limiting copyright protection. The implications of copying content for the purpose of sharing among consumers in terms of the various socio-economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and harms have been a thoroughly studied issue in the information economics literature [e.g. 5,10,12,25,29,43].

In this paper, however, we are concerned with a different issue, namely with accessing content for the purpose of remaking it into something new and different rather than copying it just for sharing with other consumers. Copyright law protects content in both situations without differentiating between copying for sharing and sampling for reuse. A number of scholars are calling for reforms in patent and copyright law, which they see as currently too restrictive in terms of allowing effective knowledge sharing and content reuse [42]. In that sense, digital remix approaches are in fact similar to the reuse of artistic ideas, and should arguably not be prohibited by legal restrictions [28]. This does not mean, however, that copyright holders could not, or should not, charge remix producers for reusing some of their content as raw materials. Rather, it has been argued that there should be an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient process in place that provides pro<sup>fi</sup>t-oriented creators better access to sources of cultural materials [26]. Allowing more openness to sharing and reusing cultural content through offering innovative licensing schemes may actually spur creativity and increase the supply of derivative works [27].

The question of Intellectual Property Rights policy has strong advocates on the two opposing sides [13]. The incentives school of thought favors a strong copyright protection scheme and emphasizes economic incentives and the question whether creators can capture suf<sup>fi</sup>cient value from their creations so that they are likely to continue to produce more works in the future. The openness school, on the other hand, advocates weaker copyright regimes that emphasize the stimulation of creativity and innovation. They argue that artistic creators, in particular, are intrinsically motivated to create, even in the absence of strong copyright protection. Overall, the literature on the effects of strong or weak protections remains inconclusive [8,19,24,36]. But in any case, there is currently no transparent mechanism in place that would allow creators to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently source content for the purpose of developing derivative remix works. Bilateral negotiations with uncertain outcomes are presently necessary in order to obtain access and usage rights. This typically incurs very high transaction costs and impedes co-production approaches in the culture industries.

We will next introduce and then test a market-based mechanism for trading access and transmutation rights for cultural content components as a possible solution to overcome the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of accessing source content for the purposes of reuse and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Adopting electronic markets for clearing rights for digital remix production could serve as a basis to create more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient sourcing markets for the co-production of derivative cultural goods.

## 3. Experimental market design for trading transmutation rights

Using experiments with economically motivated human subjects has been accepted as a legitimate research method in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of economics to evaluate alternative market designs [21]. Because there are many possible and plausible market designs that have never been implemented in practice, it is impossible to collect market performance data to evaluate them. In such cases, the methods of experimental economics offer scienti<sup>fi</sup>cally viable alternatives that allow the researcher to study different market designs in the laboratory. Moreover, even in cases where past prices or behavioral data are available, real values and costs are impossible to pin down with any precision or certainty [39]. On the other hand, market performance measures like surplus and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are computable in a well controlled experimental setting. While <sup>fi</sup>nding the best possible market structure for exchanging cultural components is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to do with experiments only, determining optimality is not the goal of our experimental design. Rather, our main goal is to systematically study the effects of introducing more <sup>fl</sup>exible content licensing regimes, combined with appropriate market institutions, on the economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of production and consumption of cultural content goods. In order to accomplish this, we <sup>fi</sup>rst design an experimental environment that simulates the salient characteristic features of the underlying market structure of the industry.

## 3.1. Design of the baseline case

The baseline treatment represents the currently predominant copyright and licensing practice in the cultural goods industry. On the supply side we model producers of content who are essentially licensed monopolies in the content markets. Content owners are considered monopolists because they hold the copyrights over the particular products. They have the exclusive legal right to produce and sell them, and since content products are typically highly differentiated one cannot be substituted for another. All producers incur a relatively high <sup>fi</sup>xed production cost for developing the new product and making the <sup>fi</sup>rst copy of it. Assuming digital reproduction, there is a zero marginal cost for making additional copies and for distributing them to the consumers. There are ten consumers and four producers of digital content in our design model. From a market design perspective, the literature suggests that about <sup>fi</sup>ve sellers and <sup>fi</sup>ve buyers are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to simulate a perfectly competitive market [37]. Since, in our case, we are modeling market arrangements that are less competitive, it is adequate to use only four producers.

## 3.1.1. The products

To put the experimental environment in a context of digital content manipulation, and to make it cognitively easier for the experimental subjects, we chose to use a set of words from the English language to represent the content products. That is, the content products that can be produced and traded in our experimental economy are all represented by words, while the letters that make up the words serve us as content components. Throughout the baseline treatment producers are tasked to make two basic decisions:

(1) which products or words to make (production decision), and (2) how much to charge for each copy sold or licensed (pricing decision).

Likewise, consumers need to decide which products to buy and how much they want to pay for them. As recommended by induced value theory [38], producer subjects are paid according to the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts that they made during the experiment at the end of the lab session. The performance-based reward gives the subjects an effective incentive to participate and make decisions to the best of their abilities in the experiment.

On the demand side we allow different valuations (and thus willingness to pay) across consumers and products. Again as prescribed by induced value theory, we assign heterogeneous values to each buyer for each product. Unlike most theoretical work this modeling choice assumes neither linear demand functions nor homogeneous valuations. As shown in Fig. 1, we introduce three categories of word products that are differentiated by degree of specialization. The products in the <sup>fi</sup>rst category (mainstream) are of general interest and are demanded by all consumers in the economy, although willingness to pay for them varies considerably across individual consumers. The products in the second category (specialized) exhibit some level of specialization and are demanded by a speci<sup>fi</sup>c consumer segment only. Finally, products in the last category (niche) are highly specialized and target only a single consumer in our experiment.

Next, we describe the actual parameters that we used to initialize the experimental environment. As shown in Fig. 1, the economy's total production potential comprises of sixteen digital content products. All products are characterized by a <sup>fi</sup>xed cost of production and zero marginal cost. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c cost schedule with <sup>fi</sup>xed costs ranging between 100 and 490 across the sixteen words, as well as the consumer demand, is presented in Table 1.

There are four content producers in our experimental economy. Each has been granted the exclusive right to produce a different set of four speci<sup>fi</sup>c words from the set given in Table 1. A producer incurs the <sup>fi</sup>xed cost at the moment the <sup>fi</sup>rst copy of a product is produced. Once the <sup>fi</sup>rst copy is made, the producer can make an unlimited number of additional copies and sell as many as consumers are willing to buy. In addition to the four producers, there are ten consumers. Each consumer has a non-zero demand for a different set of <sup>fi</sup>ve speci<sup>fi</sup>c words that are again chosen from those given in Table 1. The sixteen particular product designs that our lab economy can produce are represented by the following set of three-letter words: {“and”, “bad”, “bat”, “bee”, “bet”, “bit”, “boa”, “dad”, “end”, “net”, “one”, “sea”, “sin”, “sit”, “sob”, “ten”}.

The words themselves are designed to fall in three distinct product categories, representing different demand levels. The words “boa”, “end”, and “sit” are bestselling hit products that are wanted by everyone to a certain degree. The induced demand curves associated with these words are downward sloping and depicted in Fig. 2.1–3. They represent the different values that the various consumers place on the three mainstream products.

The words “bit”, “and”, and “net” fall into a second category that represents product genres that appeal only to particular consumer segments. In our economy, they are demanded by a group of 3, 3, and 4 consumers respectively. Their demand curves are displayed in Fig. 2.4.

The remaining ten words are modeled to be long tail, niche products that are of interest to only a very limited number of con sumers (in our case a single consumer). They are shown in Table 2.

The economy's trading institution that we implemented is a version of a continuous double-auction. Producers can submit asks or accept bids. They can lower ask prices, but they are not allowed to increase them. Consumers can submit bids or accept asks. Similarly, they can change bids, but not decrease them. These trading rules are commonly used in experimental economics and are designed to speed up the convergence of market prices to equilibrium levels. They are identical, in particular, to those used by Smith in his classical market experiments [37]. Posted offer markets converge much more slowly to competitive equilibrium. Our trading system automatically matches bids and asks at the same price. This institution allows producers to sell the same product at different prices to different consumers. Notice that this is akin to but not the same as <sup>fi</sup>rst-degree price discrimination, because differences in prices paid may not necessarily be directly related to differences in consumer value.

## 3.1.2. The sellers in the baseline case

A summary of the information <sup>fl</sup>ow and the sequence of the decisions that buyers and sellers need to make in the baseline treatment is depicted as a <sup>fl</sup>owchart in Fig. 3. At the beginning of each round, sellers (producers) receive their production schedule and learn the <sup>fi</sup>xed costs for making each of the four word products for which they are given the rights. Throughout the round they can monitor the market and check the bids that buyers who are interested in their products are posting. Taking into account market signals about consumer demand and the known production cost, producers decide if to produce a word and when to offer it in the market. Given the incomplete market information and the high <sup>fi</sup>xed cost that a decision to develop a new product entails there is considerable uncertainty about the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the different products. Hence, if producers expect that they cannot make money with some of the words they own, they may decide not to make those available to the market.

Once producers choose to offer a product, they need to set an ask price. During the course of a round, they continue to monitor bids and at any point in time they can accept bids from buyers or change ask prices. The pro<sup>fi</sup>t they collect at the end of a round depends on the number of copies sold for each product as well as the price points at which the sales were made. It is calculated by adding up the prices of all sold copies minus all <sup>fi</sup>xed costs incurred in the production process. It is possible that revenue does not recoup the production cost and that the round ends with a loss for the producer (however, in these cases losses were neither carried over to the next round nor converted into cash dollar losses for the subjects). The same process is repeated for each round in the session and the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are totaled at the end of the experiment. The exact payouts to the subjects depend on the exchange rate between experimental dollars and US cash dollars.

3.1.3. The buyers in the baseline treatment

As the <sup>fl</sup>owchart (in Fig. 3) indicates, buyers (consumers) begin each round with receiving their demand schedule, which tells them which words they need to acquire in the present round and what their valuations are. In each round, buyers receive a different set of <sup>fi</sup>ve words with different valuations. Buyers monitor the market for available products and ask prices. They may accept asks or place and change bids for words they need. If a buyer has valuation v for a product and buys it at price p, then her pro<sup>fi</sup>t will be v−p. The same calculation is performed for every transaction and the proceeds are summed over all rounds

## 3.1.4. Hypotheses related to the baseline case

Let us suppose the market structure depicted in Fig. 4, which indicates a market comprising of four producers selling products to ten consumers. Every producer holds the exclusive rights over four speci<sup>fi</sup>c product designs in their IPR portfolio, and thus every producer is a monopoly seller in the market for these products. Table 3 shows the predicted equilibrium prices and quantities for this market environment. The theoretical prediction is based on the concept of static Nash equilibrium with perfect information. That is, given these prices, none of the market participants can unilaterally improve his or her payoff by deviating from them, when all participants know all values and costs in the environment. Notice, that only 10 out of the 16 products are produced in the monopoly equilibrium. This includes all three mainstream products, two of the three specialized products, but only half of the ten long tail products. The other six products are not pro<sup>fi</sup>table and should not get made in this market environment, although there is some demand for them.

Total surplus (TS) is the sum of the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of all market participants. In our case the projected total surplus is 1750. Assuming that the bilateral price negotiation in the niche markets with only a single consumer will result in a transaction price in the middle of the costvalue interval of the pro<sup>fi</sup>table products, we can also calculate the distribution of surplus between buyers and sellers. Total consumer surplus (TCS) is then 1200, and total producer surplus (TPS) is 550. The projected deadweight loss due to monopoly power is 420, which is the total unrealized consumer value due to monopoly power. Because we use a continuous double-auction as the institution of exchange we can expect some deviation from the theoretical equilibrium in the experimental setting. Previous experimental studies [9,32] have shown that the double auction format mitigates the effects of monopoly power somewhat and the resulting transaction prices are on average lower than the theoretical monopoly prices. For the baseline case, we can therefore state some formal hypotheses (summarized in Table 4) based on standard economic theory and previous experimental results, and test them using market performance data collected from our experiment. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we expect (1) that total consumer surplus in our experiment will be higher than the static theoretical prediction (i.e., TCSN1200), (2) that total producer surplus will be lower than the static prediction (TPSb550), and (3) that total surplus will be higher than the static prediction (TSN1750) as reported in [9,32]. We test these three hypotheses formally and report the results in Section 5.

## 3.2. Treatments for co-production and transmutation rights

In this section, we are introducing two different kinds of transmutation rights to the baseline design described above. First we allow transmutation at the consumers' end, and then on the producer side. In either case, transmutation activities resemble the creative process of co-producing a component-based digital remix product. In other words, products can be disassembled into components, and components can be recombined into new products. Please notice that the three words that fall into the mainstream product category – “boa”, “end”, and “sit” – contain all the components necessary – the nine letters a, b, d, e, i, n, o, s, t – to create all other products in both the specialized and the niche category, as Fig. 1 illustrates. We call these three the base words as they can span the entire product space. Since all three are three-letter words, we consider a total of only nine components in our experimental economy. These can be recombined into a total of sixteen marketable words. Only words that have a meaning in English have a market value, other three-letter combinations are considered infeasible products with no value.

Unlike in the baseline case, there are neither theories nor previous empirical results that would allow us to predict equilibrium values for consumer and producer pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in the presence of transmutation rights [7]. Hence, we cannot propose formal research hypotheses for those cases. In the absence of theoretical grounding, we develop an exploratory research design and conduct experiments to study the impact of (1) consumer and (2) producer transmutation rights on market outcome.

## 3.2.1. Co-production market for consumer transmutation rights (CT treatment)

The CT treatment enables the consumers to co-produce words. The basic market structure is the same as shown previously in Fig. 4 for the baseline case, except that consumers now also acquire transmutation rights as part of their user license when they buy a product. Consumers now have complete access to the digital content, which gives them not just the right to play back but also the freedom to alter and remix the content they bought. In the process there are opportunities for making new words from those they already got that are either not offered in the current market or too expensive for them to buy directly. All products in this treatment are transmutable and, assuming the availability of low-cost digital transmutation tools, the direct transmutation costs are zero. However, it is important to note that only those recombinations that yield words a consumer needs accrue value to the consumer. Let's take the product “sit” for example. It is made of the three components “s”, “i”, and “t.” Similarly, the product “bad” comprises the components “b”, “a”, and “d”. Now let's assume a consumer has already bought those two but needs “bat”, too. She has the choice either to buy it in the marketplace or to reuse components from the other two products. In this case, she could disassemble the two words and then recombine component “t” from “sit” with components “b” and “a” from “bad” and make “bat” herself. However, consumers cannot sell words they make themselves to others.

The presence of consumer transmutation introduces some competition among producers because consumers can source components from different producers and co-produce some products through transmutation. In other words, while producers retain copyrights over words, they no longer control individual components, and thus there emerges implicit competition for supplying components for consumer-based transmutation.

In our current design, subjects cannot bid on all possible combinations of words. We allow consumer subjects to bid only on the single words for which the consumer already has been given values. For example, if a consumer has been assigned a value for the word “bed” but has not been assigned a value for the word “bad”, the consumer is allowed to bid for “bed” but not for “bad”. This restricts the number of bids that consumers can submit. This limitation is reasonable because of the simply overwhelming complexity if the experimental subjects were to consider a combinatorial auction with all possible product combinations. Besides that, we must also keep the bidding complexity at the same level across the two treatments, if we want to compare performance between the baseline and the CT treatment.

## 3.2.2. Co-production market for producer transmutation rights (PT treatment)

In the PT treatment producers can co-produce content. They are allowed to license content and sell transmutation rights for their products to other producers in a speci<sup>fi</sup>cally created business-tobusiness (B2B) market, shown in Fig. 5. It has the same trading rules as the previously introduced business-to-consumer (B2C) market. Each producer can reuse the components of the words that they buy from the B2B market to help create their own products. The direct transmutation costs are again zero. There are no consumer transmutation rights available in this treatment.

The buyer and the seller <sup>fl</sup>owcharts in the producer transmutation treatment are shown in Fig. 3. Producers are still monopolies in the B2C market, but they are no longer monopolies in the B2B market. This is because the same basic component might be used in several products offered by different <sup>fi</sup>rms. In a dynamic setting like this, producers in the B2B market always face a strategic tradeoff decision. Suppose that a content provider is considering offering the product “end”. On the one hand, there is pressure to introduce “end” to the market as soon as possible so that they can sell product reuse licenses in the B2B market before competitors may also start supplying components that are embedded in “end”. On the other hand, there is also an incentive to wait until reuse licenses for input components needed to make “end” are available in the B2B at prices that would substantially lower production cost for “end.”

The introduction of producer transmutation rights does not change how producers sell end products to consumers. But, as the screenshot in Appendix A.3 illustrates, producers are now also active in the newly created B2B market where they can sell reuse licenses of their own words to other producers and buy licenses from others so that they can reuse components. For example, the producer in Appendix A.3 bought a license for “end” in the B2B market for \$200, which allowed him to lower the production cost for “bee” from \$270 to \$200 by recombining the component “e” (obtained from “end”) with “b” (from the previously produced “boa”). Recall, that the same producer lost money with “bee” in the previously discussed example without producer transmutation. Switching to a co-production model creates a cost advantage in this case. At the same time, the producer has opened a second revenue stream by selling content reuse licenses for “boa” (one for \$150 and another for \$100) in the B2B market. He is also offering “sob” for \$300, although the bids in B2B market for that word are currently well below that number.

## 4. Experimental procedures

One hundred and twenty-six subjects were recruited randomly from the undergraduate student population at a large university in the US and grouped into nine groups of fourteen participants, four producers and ten consumers each. The subjects were paid a ten dollar show up fee plus a performance-based payout that averaged about thirty-<sup>fi</sup>ve dollars per person and session and ranged from 0 to \$95. The total cost of the experiment was approximately \$6000. Nine sessions lasting about two hours each were held. Each treatment was run three times in three sessions with all different participants. After the volunteering subjects came to the experimental laboratory, they were randomly assigned to the role of a producer or a consumer. They were asked to read a primer and a set of computerized instructions and then participated in a test and a short simulation that were designed to teach the market rules and the use of the trading system that we developed for the experiment. Subjects also participated in a preliminary practice round of ten minutes before the ten rounds of the experimental market were conducted. The length of each round was determined randomly to last anywhere between 5 and 6 min with equal probability. This was done with the intention to remove explosions of activity that typically occur during the last seconds before the market closing. The times resulting from the random draws during the <sup>fi</sup>rst session of the experiment were recorded and used again in all following sessions. Thus, the same number of rounds with the same length was run under all three treatments. Prior to the experiments, a number of pilots were run during the development of the electronic market design and the implementation of the trading system. The software was implemented as a real-time client/server application and programmed in Visual Basic .NET. The server software controls the rounds, manages market information, and records all transactions and activities in the experiments.

## 5.4. Additional findings

Comparing the total surplus <sup>fi</sup>gures in the CT and PT treatments, as shown in Table 5.4, we found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the effect of consumer and producer transmutation rights. However, the total consumer surplus in the former is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger $( p = 0 . 0 3 )$ than that in the latter, and the total producer surplus in the PT treatment is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger $( p = 0 . 0 0 6 )$ than in the CT treatment. This result is interesting because it suggests that the introduction of a market for transmutation rights increases surplus no matter on which side of the production process it is used. That is, we cannot say that co-production would be more or less effective when transmutation rights are offered either to producers or to consumers. Transmutation can be effectively employed at both ends. However, the group that adopts the transmutation right does get the larger share of the surplus increase in both cases and the producers do better in the market with producer-based transmutation rights while the consumers get a larger share in the market with consumer-based transmutation rights.

To further examine market performance in the two experimental settings, we also looked at the number of products acquired by consumers in each treatment. As shown in Table 5.5, we see that the average number of products obtained by consumers in the CT treatment (33.6) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than in the other two treatments (p-valueb0.01). The increase results from the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of consumer transmutation rights which enable consumers to not just buy products off the shelf but also to self-produce some, using transmutation in the home environment. Out of the 33.6 products acquisitions that they made on average, 9.0 were co-created products. Similarly, producer transmutation rights enabled producers to license content in the B2B market for reuse in their production process, which results in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost savings. In turn, some products that were unpro<sup>fi</sup>table before are now getting made and offered to consumers in the B2C market. On average, 10.5 products were produced in the PT setting, which is about 40% more than in the other two treatments. Nearly half of those were co-produced with other producers.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper reports the <sup>fi</sup>rst experimental comparison between two different co-production models for cultural goods. One permits content transmutation on the producer side, while the other affords content transmutation on the consumer side. Our study showed in the laboratory that it is possible to design markets that can provide incentives for monopolies to adopt more open business models and support the production of content that is co-created with other producers or with consumers. Opening content can stimulate creativity and increase product selection. One limitation of our study is that our experimental results are based on running ten round session, which may not be long enough to establish long-term effects that could possibly offset some short-term bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

Of course, other market designs are possible besides the two speci<sup>fi</sup>c settings that we studied in this paper. For example, we did not study a design in which consumer-driven and producer-driven transmutation are both allowed simultaneously. Other future research designs may include the possibility of consumer sharing and the possibility that consumers are allowed to trade products which they co-created. Nonetheless, the two co-production models that were explored in this study do show that it is possible to increase total welfare in terms of both consumer surplus and producer pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by opening content to co-creation. There is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in total surplus between consumer-driven and producer-driven transmutation, but the side that has the transmutation rights fares better.

This suggests that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of transmutation accrue to both producers and consumers no matter at which end transmutation is introduced. In the presence of consumer transmutation producers bene<sup>fi</sup>t by being able to charge higher prices. This suggests that content owners should consider selling content products with open formats (like MP3 in the case of music) and charger higher prices than for closed formats. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that producers should collaborate and open their content for reuse in order to lower production cost, which allows them to pro<sup>fi</sup>tably offer more product variety and also creates a second revenue stream in the reuse license market. However, producer transmutation also increases competition which lowers consumer prices and offsets some of the gains.

While there has been a controversial theoretical debate on the effect of speci<sup>fi</sup>c regulatory frameworks on innovation and production of cultural content [33,17], there has been, to the best of our knowledge, no empirical test of the impact of speci<sup>fi</sup>c licensing arrangements on market performance. This paper helps to close this gap and contributes some initial empirical evidence that open licensing schemes, similar to our transmutation rights or the increasingly popular Creative Commons licenses, can stimulate production of content and increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for producers and create higher consumer surplus. Finally, we contribute an easily replicable experimental environment that can serve as a benchmark for testing different licensing arrangements and market con<sup>fi</sup>gurations in digital content goods.
