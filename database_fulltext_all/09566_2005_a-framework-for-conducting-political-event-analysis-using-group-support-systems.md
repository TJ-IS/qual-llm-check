# A framework for conducting political event analysis using group support systems

Robert W. Blanning<sup>a,</sup>\*, Bruce A. Reinig

<sup>a</sup> Owen Graduate School of Management, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN 37203, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Information and Decision Sciences, San Diego State University, San Diego, CA 92182, USA

Received 30 December 2002; accepted 30 September 2003 Available online 27 November 2003

## Abstract

Corporations seeking to expand their operations internationally often encounter certain political events that they do not encounter domestically. Political events are those events that may occur because of the actions or decisions made by certain political actors having influence in a region. Such events include corruption, currency fluctuations, and civil unrest. However, obtaining information and insight about such events from local knowledgeable persons is often difficult due to authoritarian governments and/or cultural environments that discourage open criticism. We present a framework for using group support systems (GSS) under such conditions. The framework consists of methods that can be employed depending on the number of events (unidimensional or multidimensional) and time periods (static or dynamic) of interest. Each method is illustrated with local Hong Kong business executives addressing the future of Hong Kong as a desirable place to conduct multinational operations.

Keywords: Political risk; Group support systems; Scenarios

## 1. Introduction

Corporations seeking to expand their international operations—by making substantial investments in local corporations, by developing new products for new markets, or by establishing regional manufacturing, distribution, or sales facilities—may encounter substantial risks that often differ from the risks they encounter domestically. These include physical risks (e.g., the kidnapping or assassination of executives), economic risks (e.g., currency fluctuations and discriminatory confiscation of assets), market risks (e.g., rejection by foreign consumers of products that were successful domestically), and technological risks (e.g., lack of a reliable telecommunications infrastructure).

One type of risk that is receiving increased attention is political risk. Political risk has been defined as ‘‘. . . the possibility that political decisions, events, or conditions in a country, including those that might be defined as social, will affect the business environment such that investors will lose money or have a reduced profit margin,’’ ([13], p. 71). Examples are corruption, punitive taxation, and arbitrary rejection of license applications. Political risk may become manifest in the forms mentioned above. For example, kidnapping or assassination of executives can be both politically and economically motivated, and political decisions may affect the development of a telecommunications infrastructure.

Political risks are best defined in terms of political events. Political events result in part from decisions made by political actors—such as government officials (e.g., chief executives, legislators, and regulators). The decisions they make include decisions to expropriate property, to delay consideration of license applications, and to ignore intellectual property violations. These actors may be advised by certain non-government organizations (NGOs)—such as environmental, charitable, social, and religious organizations—who may also be considered political actors. In addition, political actors may include religious leaders, foreign investors, and others whose actions may affect political events. The actors may be identified explicitly, or the events they generate may be the only overt signs of their presence.

The key to analyzing political events is obtaining good information about these events. People closest to the events of interest—such as local business managers, multinational managers specializing in the region, journalists, and academics—often have access to better information than those with less immediate access. For example, Frankel and Schmulker [11] have shown in their study of the 1994 devaluation of the Mexican peso that local investors adjusted their currency holdings before multinational investors did, and consequently they sustained less severe economic damage. They state that, ‘‘. . . either domestic and international investors received two different sets of information, or the local investors were more alert and sensitive to potential warning signals’’ ([11], p. 26). Similarly, Thurow ([19], p. 23) states that, ‘‘The first investors to leave the local market are always the local investors who have the best information. . . . Property developers in Manila have the best information about the rising office vacancy rate in the many new office towers they have helped to build. . . . Outsiders are the last to know.’’ Thus, it is often useful to gather information from knowledgeable people closest to the events being analyzed.

Unfortunately local people, and especially local business executives, are sometimes reluctant to voice their opinions and insights in an open and frank manner. Common reasons for this reluctance are authoritarian governments and/or cultural environments that discourage open criticism, which in turn may result from two characteristics of a nation and its people: power distance and collectivism. Power distance is ‘‘the extent to which the less powerful members of institutions and organizations within a country expect and accept that power is distributed unequally’’ ([12], p. 28). High power distance cultures often resolve political disputes through the use of power rather than debate. Consequently, managers in such cultures may not be accustomed to openly voicing concerns about their local environment. Collectivism describes a ‘‘society in which people from birth onwards are integrated into strong, cohesive ingroups, which throughout people’s lifetime continue to protect them in exchange for unquestioning loyalty’’ ([12], p. 260). Since highly collectivistic cultures stress harmony and consensus, critical viewpoints of a region’s political situation might easily be construed as disharmonious and therefore improper. Notable examples of high power distance, high collectivism countries can be found in Asia (e.g., China, Malaysia, Philippines, Hong Kong, and Taiwan) and Latin America (e.g., Mexico, Brazil, Venezuela, Chile, and Peru). These two regions are also of considerable interest to multinational corporations seeking to invest and expand operations.

We have found group support systems (GSS) to be a useful tool in eliciting the participation of local business executives and other knowledgeable persons to analyze political events. GSS offer three advantages over traditional face-to-face meetings: anonymity, simultaneity, and documentation. Although surveys can allow participants to submit anonymous comments, GSS also allow them to respond to the comments of others without revealing their identities. Under these conditions, participants are likely to experience less evaluation apprehension and pay more attention to the topics being discussed [17]. Also, anonymous participants have been shown to be more critical than identified participants [15]. In analyzing political events, it is important that participants present honest opinions of the events rather than opinions tailored to satisfy political or cultural expectations. The feature of simultaneity (people can participate in parallel) is useful because it makes the process more efficient by allowing participants to share their ideas as they occur rather than waiting for a turn to speak. The feature of documentation (all discussion and voting are recorded) is useful because it facilitates the analysis of information generated during the session.

In the next section, we present a framework for using GSS with a group of local business executives to analyze political events. We consider two characteristics of political events. The first is whether there is only one event of interest (unidimensional) or more than one event (multidimensional). The second is whether the event or events are defined for a single time period (static) or for more than one time period (dynamic). This leads to four possible cases, and we present methods for using GSS in each of these four cases. We describe the methods in Section 2, and we illustrate them in Section 3 by applying them to the business future of Hong Kong. We conclude with a discussion of the integration of one or more of the methods as well as possible extensions of framework into other problem domains.

## 2. Analyzing political events using GSS

We now present a framework for analyzing political events in a way that is relevant to the use of GSS in analyzing these events. The purpose of this framework is to inform managers, academics, and researchers who seek guidance in the use of GSS in political event analysis. This includes persons studying the political risk associated with a particular company, industry, or region. They may be internal to a company and engaged in a politically sensitive operation (e.g., investing in an emerging market) or they may be external to the company, presumably acting as consultants or other advisors.

The framework is based on two characteristics of the problem being addressed. The first is whether analysis of the event or events under consideration are static or dynamic. A static analysis is one which takes place for a single period, whether it is a few months or a few years. A dynamic analysis is one that is assumed to change over time, and typically over a sequence of successive periods.

The second characteristic is whether the analysis is unidimensional or multidimensional. A unidimensional problem is one in which there is a single event or several independent events. In the latter case the group session can be repeated for each event or each event can be addressed separately during one group session. In the multidimensional case there are two or more interdependent events and an important purpose of the analysis is to determine the relationships among the events.

Since the analysis can be static or dynamic and can be unidimensional or multidimensional, we have four possible cases as outlined in Fig. 1. We will describe these cases and the various approaches to them in Section 2 and then illustrate them in the context of political event analysis for the multinational business community in Hong Kong in Section 3.

## 2.1. The static unidimensional case

When there is one event and one period, the problem is to determine the probability (or likelihood) of the event. Although estimates can be obtained directly from online discussions and voting, they can also be derived from more fundamental considerations. These considerations involve the political actors, whether individuals or organizations, who influence the event and who collectively determine the probability that the event will occur. There is an established methodology in political science, called the PRINCE method [8], for doing this, based on three attributes of the political actors. The first is the Issue Position of the actor with regard to the event, which is represented by a positive number if the actor supports the event, a negative number if the actor is in opposition to the event, and zero if the actor is neutral to the event. The second is the Power of the actor, which is represented by a positive number indicating the degree to which the actor can influence the event. The third is the Salience of the actor, which is represented by a positive number indicating the amount of effort that the actor is willing to devote to the issue relative to other issues. These are used to calculate the event probability.

A GSS can be used to (1) identify the political actors, (2) elicit information about their attributes, (3) estimate the event probability, and (4) obtain comments from the participants about possible changes in one of the attributes (i.e., Issue Position). This approach to implementing the PRINCE method, called a Political Actor Analysis [5], proceeds as follows. The facilitator describes the event to the participants and asks them to discuss the actors who might influence the event. The facilitator then uses this discussion to prepare a list of principal actors, perhaps 10 in number. Then the participants vote on the Issue Position, Power, and Salience of each actor.

The facilitator uses these three estimates to prepare a Political Map, in which the Importance (Power  Salience) of each actor is on the horizontal axis and the Issue Position of each actor is on the vertical axis. The Importance and Issue Position measures are averaged across the participants. The facilitator then presents the Political Map to the participants and asks them to discuss the positioning of the actors.

The event probability is estimated by calculating for each actor an Actor Score = Issue Position Importance. The probability is the sum of the positive Actor Scores divided by the sum of the absolute values of all of the Actor Scores.<sup>1</sup> In addition, the comments in the discussion are examined to identify interesting scenarios. A scenario is a recalculation of the event probability assuming a change in one or more of the actors’ Issue Positions. If the assumption is that only a single actor changes its Issue Position, the result is called a Simple Scenario. If several actors change their Issue Positions, presumably because one actor changes and induces other actors to change as well, the result is called a Full Scenario. The deliverables in a Political Actor Analysis are the Political Map, the estimate of the probability of the event, and the probability estimates for the Simple and Full Scenarios.

## 2.2. The static multidimensional case

This is a rich case, with two approaches that one might take. The reason is that scenarios often contain multiple events, and it is important to identify both the events and the relationships among them. The two approaches are Scenario Construction and Cross-Impact Analysis.

In Scenario Construction [3], a scenario is defined as a collection of significant events in a given context (e.g., the financial future of a firm or an economic region). There are three scenarios generated in this process, described as optimistic, pessimistic, and realistic. Participants are asked to discuss and identify possible events for inclusion in the scenarios. This is accomplished by having them brainstorm events using three discussion folders. The discussion folders, in which XXX refers to the organization, industry, or nation about which the scenarios are being constructed, are as follows:

Optimistic: The events you describe here should be desirable for XXX. They need not be utopian, but they should be more favorable than unfavorable.

Pessimistic: The events you describe here should be undesirable for XXX. They need not be disastrous, but they should be more unfavorable than favorable.

Realistic: The events you describe here should be relevant to the future of XXX. They should be the events you believe are most likely to occur. ([3], p. 902)

It is important to note that the discussion uses three folders for generating the events and not just one folder asking for all relevant events. This is done for two reasons. First, it ensures that the process will generate a set of events that contains optimistic and pessimistic events, rather than just one or the other. Second, since Dennis et al. [9] have shown that structuring brainstorming tasks in this manner can significantly increase ideation by as much as 60%, this should increase the total number of events generated by the participants.

The facilitator then uses this discussion to produce an Event List, consisting of perhaps 50 events. The participants are presented with the Event List and asked to vote on (1) the degree to which the event is favorable or unfavorable to the organization (firm, industry, or nation) of interest and (2) the probability that the event will occur. The votes are averaged across the participants for each event. The events are plotted as points in an Event Matrix, in which the horizontal axis represents the event probability and the vertical axis represents the degree to which an event is either favorable or unfavorable. Three scenarios are constructed. The Optimistic Scenario consists of those events that are very favorable and of medium or high probability. The Pessimistic Scenario consists of those events that are very unfavorable and of medium or high probability. The Realistic Scenario consists of those events that are of high probability, whether favorable, neutral, or unfavorable. The neutral events are those that are neither very favorable nor very unfavorable.

The cutoff points for medium and high are selected so that each scenario contains a reasonable number, perhaps ten, of events. This process is made more difficult when multiple events are clustered together and their ratings have large standard deviations and the ratings are not statistically signficantly different. However, our experience suggests that there will be little overlap between the Realistic Scenario and the other two scenarios, primarily because of the large number of high probability events that are neither very favorable nor very unfavorable. Once the events in the three scenarios have been identified, the facilitator constructs a Narrative Summary of each scenario, referring to the events in the scenario. The deliverables of the Scenario Construction approach are the Event Matrix with the three scenarios identified (by encircling each of the three sets of events) and the three Narrative Summaries.

The purpose of the second approach, Cross-Impact Analysis [4], is to identify the relationships, or lack of relationships, among a predefined set of events. The events are defined by the facilitator, or a session designer. They are generally negative events—that is, events that are of concern because of the possible damage they could cause. The resulting event list is small, consisting of perhaps five events. The reason is that there are potentially $N \times ( N - 1 )$ directional relationships among N events, and thus, for example, 20 possible relationships among five events. The facilitator constructs an electronic folder for each of the $N \times ( N - 1 )$ possible relationships asking the participants to comment on the relationship. The participants are then asked to vote on the existence (vote = 1) or nonexistence (vote = 0) of the events, and this information is used to construct measures of the impacts of the events on each other.

These measures, called Impact Coefficients, are calculated as follows. First, the marginal probability of each event is estimated by summing the votes for the event. Second the conditional probability $p ( \beta | \alpha )$ of one event $( \beta )$ given another event (a) is the fraction of cases in which $\beta = 1$ given that $\mathsf { \Omega } \mathsf { z } = 1$ . Similarly, the conditional probability $p ( \beta | \neg \alpha )$ of $\beta$ given that a is false is the fraction of cases in which $\beta = 1$ given that $\scriptstyle { \alpha = 0 }$ . The Impact Coefficient describing the impact of a on $\beta$ is defined by IMP(a, $\beta ) { = } p ( \beta | \alpha ) - p ( \beta | \neg \alpha )$ . We note that - 1 V IMP(a, $\beta ) \leq 1$ and $\mathrm { I M P } ( \alpha , \beta ) = 0$ if and only if a and $\beta$ are independent. We also note that in general IMP(a, $\beta ) \neq \mathrm { \ I M P } ( \beta , \ \alpha )$ , but $\mathrm { I M P } ( \alpha , \ \beta )$ and $\mathrm { I M P } ( \beta , \ \alpha )$ will always have the same sign. For example, if $\mathscr { X }$ has a positive impact on $\beta ,$ then $\beta$ will have a positive impact on $\alpha ,$ but the magnitude need not be the same. Finally, the marginal probability $p ( \beta )$ will lie between the components of IMP(a, $\beta )$ —that is, $p ( \beta )$ will lie between $p ( \beta | \alpha )$ and $p ( \beta | \neg \alpha )$ .

The Impact Coefficients for the $N \times ( N - 1 )$ pairs of events are used to construct an Interdependence Table and an Interdependence Graph relating the events to each other. The Impact Coefficients are partitioned into three categories—high, medium, and low. This is done using the absolute values of the coefficients, and the breakpoints are determined subjectively so that only a moderate number of the Impact Coefficients fall into the high and medium categories. The Interdependence Table consists of a row for each high event pair and each medium event pair containing the names of the events in the pair, the absolute value of the Impact Coefficient, a designation as to whether the pair is considered high or medium, and one or more comments from the participant discussion concerning the impacts. The Interdependence Graph contains a node corresponding to each event and an arc (arrow) joining the nodes that have high (solid line) or medium (dashed line) impact. The deliverables of the Cross-Impact Analysis are the Interdependence Table and the Interdependence Graph. Additional quantitative data, in the form of the marginal and conditional probabilities and the Impact Coefficients, can be included if desired.

## 2.3. The dynamic unidimensional case

The principal approach here is Multiperiod Scenario Development [6] The future of an organization (or a nation) is described in terms of the state of the organization in each of several time periods. We may expect that a potential combinatorial explosion will limit the scenario to two periods and two states, resulting in four possible outcomes. A period may be one or a few years in length, and the states will typically be one desirable state and one undesirable state. Participants are asked to comment on each of these outcomes, and the facilitator uses the comments to prepare a Scenario Table summarizing the arguments for and against the possibilities of the outcomes. Then the participants vote by allocating 100 probability percentage points among the outcomes, and the facilitator uses these estimates to construct a Scenario Tree displaying the state probabilities at each period and the transition probabilities between periods.

For example, if there are two periods and two states, then the participants will enter four probabilities $q _ { i j } , i = 1 , 2$ and j = 1, 2. These are the probabilities that the organization will be in state i at the end of period 1 and in state j at the end of period 2. Then the probability of being in state i at the end of the first period is $p _ { i } { = } q _ { i 1 } { + } q _ { i 2 }$ , and the probability of being in state j at the end of the second period is $q _ { j } = q _ { 1 j } + q _ { 2 j }$ . For example, the probability of being in state 1 at the end of period 1 is denoted as $p _ { 1 } { = } q _ { 1 1 } { + } q _ { 1 2 }$ and the probability of being in state 1 at the end of the period 2 is $q _ { 1 } = q _ { 1 1 } + q _ { 2 1 }$ . The transition probabilities—that is, the probabilities of moving from state i at the end of the first period to state j at the end of the second period—are calculated as follows: $p _ { i j } { = } q _ { i j } / ( q _ { i 1 } { + } q _ { i 2 } )$

The Scenario Tree can then be used to construct a first-order Markov chain [14]. Methods are available to calculate the long run (infinite period) equilibrium probabilities for the states assuming that the transition probabilities are stationary (i.e., do not change). The equilibrium state probabilities, ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * }$ and ${ p _ { 2 } } ^ { * }$ , are calculated as follows: ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * } \mathrm { = } ( 1 - p _ { 2 2 } ) / ( 2 - p _ { 1 1 } - p _ { 2 2 } )$ and $p _ { 2 } { ^ { * } } { = } ( 1 - p _ { 1 1 } ) / ( 2 - p _ { 1 1 } - p _ { 2 2 } )$ . These probabilities exist for all cases except for the one in which $p _ { 1 1 } = p _ { 2 2 } = 1$ . In this case, there are no equilibrium probabilities, and the states remain the same during all future periods.

If one is willing to assume stationarity, then the equilibrium state probabilities can be compared with the state probabilities for the two periods to suggest how stable the future of the organization is likely to be. The deliverables of Multiperiod Scenario Development are the Scenario Table and the Scenario Tree, along with the equilibrium state probabilities if desired.

## 2.4. The dynamic multidimensional case

The principal approach here is Multiperiod Scenario Analysis [7]. The future of the organization is described in terms of two or more states during two or more time periods. However, in this case, information about the events is gathered during the actual time periods—for example, during two or more successive years. If the states are independent of each other (so that no Cross-Impact Analysis is necessary), then the method of the previous section can be applied repeatedly to each of the states. However, when there are several states, scenario development can become quite complex, and it is better to focus on a statistical analysis of the events and their interactions, rather than to construct an underlying stochastic model.

The participants are presented with several, perhaps five, events. They are then asked to consider two attributes of the events. The first is the Importance of the events to the organization in question (e.g., multinational businesses in a particular country), and the second is the likelihood or degree of change, or the Change Index. The events are then plotted on a Political Event Graph, in which the Change Index is on the vertical axis and the Importance is on the horizontal axis. The participants are also asked to comment on the events, and the comments are aggregated to produce several Viewpoints with regard to the events. There are two sets of Viewpoints for each event, one corresponds to the Importance and one corresponds to the Change Index. This process is repeated for two or more time periods.

There are two ways in which the events can be analyzed. The first is to determine, for each event, whether there has been a significant change in its Importance and Change Index. This is accomplished by a conducting a separate MANOVA, which tests the equality of vectors of means (in this case one for Importance and one for the Change Index) for each event. The second is to determine whether there has been a significant change in the collection of the Importance and Change Index for all of the events. This is accomplished by performing a single MAN-OVA for all of the Importance and Change Index measures.

## 3. Applying the framework: Hong Kong’s future after reunification with China

We now illustrate our framework by applying it to the future of Hong Kong in light of Hong Kong’s reunification with the People’s Republic of China.<sup>2</sup>

Hong Kong has been a topic of great interest to business executives and other observers, both inside and outside of Hong Kong [2,10] for two primary reasons. First, Hong Kong has one of the most aggressive, free market economies in the world. The Hong Kong economy benefits from a reliable British legal system, low taxes, free trade, and low levels of government corruption. Hong Kong also has an advantageous location on the South China Sea with a natural deep water port, which is one of the world’s busiest container ports. For these reasons, Hong Kong is one of the world’s largest trading entities and serves as the headquarters for Asian operations of many multinational corporations. Second, on Midnight June 30, 1997, Hong Kong changed from a British Dependent Territory to a Special Administrative Region (SAR) of the People’s Republic of China (PRC). The PRC has promised to honor a ‘‘one country, two systems’’ policy towards Hong Kong’s future. However, the PRC suffers from widespread government corruption and is one of the world’s few remaining communist regimes, which has given rise to concern about political risk in Hong Kong.

Hong Kong business executives are often reluctant to openly criticize issues pertaining to Hong Kong’s future as a SAR of China. In the words of one prominent Hong Kong executive, ‘‘You Westerners don’t understand, [Hong Kong] is like a family company, you don’t have your arguments out in the open’’ ([16], p. 37). One reason for this may be that Hong Kong’s culture, like other Chinese cultures, is different from that of the United States and Western Europe. Namely, Hong Kong is characterized as a high power distance, collectivistic culture whereas the United States and Western Europe can be characterized as low power distance, individualistic cultures [12]. Because of this, GSS may be useful in collecting and analyzing the observations and opinions of Hong Kong business executives with regard to certain political events.

This section proceeds by reporting the results of applying our framework to an analysis of political events pertaining to the future of Hong Kong. Each of the examples presented in this session were conducted with Hong Kong business executives. Almost all were natives of Hong Kong working in the banking, finance, and other service industries.

## 3.1. The static unidimensional case

To illustrate the static, unidimensional case, we conducted a Political Actor Analysis. The event of consideration is the fixed exchange rate between the Hong Kong dollar and the US dollar, often referred to as ‘‘the peg’’. Multinational corporations are interested in the peg because it provides financial stability. The stability of the peg came into question during the Asian Economic Crisis, which began in July 1997. During this time a number of countries in the region, such as Singapore, Korea, and Taiwan, suffered significant currency devaluations, and in August 1998 the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) intervened to support the Hong Kong dollar in the midst of an attack by currency speculators.

In September 1998, 22 Hong Kong business executives participated in a GSS session for the purpose of completing a Political Actor Analysis to determine the probability of the peg remaining over the following year [5]. To begin the session, the facilitator asked the executives to answer the question, ‘‘Who are the actors that have influence in maintaining or removing the Hong Kong dollar peg to the US dollar over the next year?’’ The executives then discussed the question using a GSS discussion tool and identified 11 actors including the International Monetary Fund (IMF), Hong Kong Chief Executive Tung Chee-hwa, foreign investors, Chinese President Jiang Zemin, the HKMA, George Soros, the Hong Kong SAR government (denoted as HKSAR), Alan Greenspan, Japan, the citizens of Hong Kong, and currency speculators.

The participants used a GSS voting tool to estimate the Issue Position, Power, and Salience of each of the 11 actors. Issue Position was estimated using a scale from - 3 (strong opposition to the peg remaining) to + 3 (strong support for the peg remaining), with 0 as neutrality or ambivalence towards the peg. Power was estimated using a scale from 1 (weak power) to 3 (strong power). Salience was estimated using a scale from 1 (weak salience) to 3 (strong salience).

The facilitator then exported the results of the voting (averaged across participants) into an Excel spreadsheet. Excel was used to generate a Political Map, which displayed Issue Position on the vertical axis and Importance (the product of Salience and Power) on the horizontal axis. Participants were then asked, using a GSS discussion tool, to discuss the relative positions of the actors. This discussion revealed two interesting scenarios. First, a number of executives suggested that while Chinese President Jiang Zemin supported the peg (Issue Position = 2.1), his support might change. Thus, the first scenario, a Simple Scenario, was constructed in which Jiang Zemin changed his Issue Position from supportive (2.1) to one that stood in opposition ( - 3.0) to the peg. Second, some participants believed that while Hong Kong Chief Executive Tung Chee-hwa (Issue Position = 2.8), the HKMA (Issue Position = 2.8), and the HKSAR government (Issue Position = 2.6) were all strongly supportive of the peg, that this support was dependent on the support of Jiang Zemin. Thus, the second scenario, a Full Scenario, was constructed in which Jiang Zemin assumed an Issue Position of - 3.0 and Tung Cheehwa, the HKMA, and the HKSAR government all assumed Issue Positions of neutrality (0.0). The Political Map illustrating the voting results and the two scenarios is presented in Fig. 2.

Once the voting results had been tabulated, the event probability was calculated. As discussed previously, this probability is the sum of the positive Actor Scores divided by the sum of the absolute values of all of the Actor Scores. Using the original estimates appearing in Fig. 2, the probability of the peg remaining an additional year was 76%. However, this probability was dropped to 62% when calculated for the Simple Scenario and 10% when calculated with the Full Scenario. Thus, the probability of the peg remaining an additional year was favorable, assuming Jiang Zemin’s Issue Position did not change.

## 3.2. The static multidimensional case

Depending on the goals of the organization conducting the study, the static, multidimensional case may use Scenario Construction or Cross-Impact Analysis. We used Scenario Construction to build scenarios describing the future of Hong Kong following its reunification with the PRC. Prior to the handover, and indeed still today, there has been a great deal of speculation surrounding Hong Kong’s future. Some analysts had suggested that Hong Kong would continue to enjoy economic prosperity (e.g., Ref. [18]), while others had suggested that the PRC would interfere with Hong Kong’s free market policies and make Hong Kong less attractive to multinational firms (e.g., Ref. [1]).

In October 1997, 16 Hong Kong business executives participated in a GSS session to construct Optimistic, Pessimistic, and Realistic scenarios describing possible futures of Hong Kong [3]. To begin the session, the facilitator set up a GSS discussion tool so that the participants could brainstorm potential events to be included in the scenarios. Participants were given three discussion folders in which they could respond. The folders were designated as optimistic, pessimistic, and realistic, as described in Section 2.2 with the phrase ‘‘the business environment of Hong Kong’’ substituted for XXX. Participants were allocated 30 min to brainstorm a list of events, approximately 10 min for each of the three discussion folders. As the participants entered their ideas, the facilitator identified specific events from the discussion and built the Event List that appears as the legend in Fig. 3.

The participants then voted on two attributes of each event. The first attribute was the probability of the event on a scale from 0 = 0% to 10 = 100%. The second attribute was the degree to which the event was favorable to the business environment in Hong Kong on a scale from 0 = Very Unfavorable to 10 = Very Favorable. The results of the voting were plotted in an Event Matrix, which included the assignment of events into specific scenarios (Fig. 3). For example, the Optimistic Scenario consisted of the events that were very favorable and were of medium or high probability. The Pessimistic Scenario consisted of the events that were very unfavorable and were of medium or high probability. The Realistic Scenario consisted of the events that were of high probability, regardless if they were favorable or unfavorable. We conducted a Cross-Impact Analysis to identify and analyze potential interdependencies between several events that were drawing a great deal of attention in the months that followed the handover. The events, by themselves, were thought to have significant negative consequences on the Hong Kong economy and its attractiveness to multinational corporations. However, we were also concerned with relationships between the events and the possibility that the occurrence of one event might change the probability of the occurrence of another event. The events we identified for analysis included (1) China loses most favored nation (MFN) status, (2) corruption becomes widespread in Hong Kong, (3) the peg between the Hong Kong dollar and the US dollar is removed, (4) China intervenes in the Hong Kong economy, and (5) conflict between China and Taiwan escalates.

In December 1997, 20 Hong Kong business executives participated in a GSS session for the purpose of completing a Cross-Impact Analysis to analyze potential interdependencies among the five events [4]. This requires that the participants discuss the twenty<sup>3</sup> possible directional relationships among the events. One approach to this would be to ask them to discuss any interdependencies that may exist. The problem with this is that they might focus on only a few directional relationships and fail to examine each individually. In order to spread the discussion out evenly over the 20 possible relationships, we used a discussion tool that randomly distributed discussion windows, one at a time, to the participants. We then seeded 20 discussion windows with each directional pair in the form of the following question:

Assume that the following event has occurred: {description of one of the five events}. Would it have any impact on the likelihood of this event:

{description of another event}? If so, please state the reasons. ([4], p. 43)

The GSS then distributed these questions at random to the participants and a discussion stream ensued for each of the directional relationships.

Following the discussion, the participants voted (yes or no) as to whether each event would occur in the next 10 years. The cross-impact measures were then calculated in the following manner. First, the marginal probabilities were calculated for each event. The probability of corruption becoming widespread in Hong Kong, for example, was 40% (8/20) and the probability of China losing MFN status was 30% (6/20). Second, the conditional probabilities were calculated for each directional pair of events. The probability that corruption would become widespread in Hong Kong, given that China loses MFN status was 67% (4/6) and the probability that corruption would become widespread in Hong Kong, given that China did not lose MFN status was 29% (4/14). Next, the Impact Coefficient for each directional pair was calculated. For example, the impact of China losing MFN status on widespread corruption in Hong Kong is 0.38 (4/6–4/14). After the Impact Coefficients had been calculated, the Interdependence Graph was constructed (Fig. 4).

The Interdependence Graph in Fig. 4 depicts the relationships that exist among the five events as either high, medium or low impact levels. For a high Impact Coefficient, we set a threshold of an absolute value equal or greater to 0.30. There were three high impact relationships, which are represented in the Interdependence Graph with solid lines. For a medium Impact Coefficient, we set a range from 0.25 to 0.30. There were five medium impact relationships, which are represented in the Interdependence Graph with dashed lines. There were 12 low Impact Coefficients with values less than 0.25 and the corresponding relationships do not appear in the Interdependence Graph. It is interesting to note that of the eight relationships depicted in the Interdependence Graph, three of them have negative Impact Coefficients. That is, the data suggest that the occurrence of one event will decrease the probability of the other event. In most cases, the sign of the Impact Coefficient was consistent with the comments, but in one case it was not. In such cases, we may be less likely to draw firm conclusions from the data.

## 3.3. The dynamic unidimensional case

To illustrate the dynamic, unidimensional case, we conducted Multiperiod Scenario Development. The event of consideration is the state of Hong Kong in terms of its desirability (more or less desirable) to multinational corporations relative to its chief competitor, Singapore. While Hong Kong is widely regarded as the ‘‘Gateway to China,’’ Singapore is also home to a free market economy, maintains a world-class infrastructure and regularly competes with Hong Kong for the title of the world’s busiest container port. Both are frequently chosen as locations for multinational corporations considering expansion to Asia, and the desirability of one over the other changes depending on economic and political conditions. Thus, it is useful to conduct a dynamic analysis that reveals intermediate changes within the time period of interest.

In June 1999, 12 Hong Kong business executives participated in a GSS session for the purpose of completing a Multiperiod Scenario Development to determine the desirability of Hong Kong relative to Singapore over two consecutive 3-year periods ending in 2002 and 2005 [6]. The session began by having the participants discuss the four possible scenarios resulting from two states over two periods. Each of the four scenarios was represented in a discussion window. For example, one discussion window contained the following scenario:

In 2002 Hong Kong will be more desirable than Singapore to multinational corporations as a business city in Asia, and in 2005 Hong Kong will be less desirable than Singapore to multinational corporations as a business city in Asia. ([6], p. 113)

This discussion was later examined to construct a Scenario Table that describes the arguments for each scenario.

Following the discussion, the participants voted on the likelihood of each scenario by allocating 100 percentage points among the scenarios. The voting was averaged across all participants. The mean value allocated to each scenario is the probability of the scenario and is represented as $q _ { i j } ,$ where i = 1 if Hong Kong is more desirable than Singapore at the end of period 1 and j = 1 if Hong Kong is more desirable than Singapore at the end of period 2. Likewise, i = 2 if Hong Kong is less desirable than Singapore at the end of period 1 and j = 2 if Hong Kong is less desirable than Singapore at the end of period 2. These four probabilities were then used to construct the Scenario Tree that appears in Fig. 5.

The Scenario Tree illustrates the importance of the dynamic analysis. For example, the executives were more pessimistic about Hong Kong’s competitiveness with Singapore in the short term or period 1, than in the longer term or period 2. The probability that Hong Kong would be more desirable than Singapore at the end of period 1 is $q _ { 1 1 } + q _ { 2 2 } = 3 0 . 4 \%$ , whereas the probability that Hong Kong would be more desirable than Singapore at the end of period 2 is $q _ { 1 1 } + q _ { 2 1 } =$ 38.4%. The probability that Hong Kong would be less desirable than Singapore at the end of period 2 is $q _ { 1 2 } + q _ { 2 2 } = 6 1 . 6 \%$ . This result is close to the equilibrium probabilities, ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * } = 3 9 . 2 \%$ and $p _ { 2 } { ^ * } = 6 0 . 8 \%$ where ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * }$ is the long-term probability that Hong Kong will be more desirable than Singapore and ${ p _ { 2 } } ^ { * }$ is the long-term probability that Hong Kong will be less desirable than Singapore. This suggests that any fluctuations will stabilize towards the end of the second period.

## 3.4. The dynamic multidimensional case

To illustrate the dynamic, multidimensional case, we conducted a Multiperiod Scenario Analysis. The purpose of this effort was to examine a small set of events that were representative of the political environment in Hong Kong shortly before the handover in an Initial Analysis and then follow up with a Subsequent Analysis 2 years later to see if any significant changes had occurred. For the Initial Analysis, we selected the following events: (1) a change in the use of English in Hong Kong, (2) a change in the tax rate, (3) a change in the independence of judges and courts, (4) special treatment for PRC state-owned enterprises conducting business in Hong Kong, and (5) a change in censorship of political commentary. These five events are representative of the political concerns at the time and analyzed in terms of their likelihood for change as well as their importance to multinational business firms.

For example, the event of censorship was presented as follows: ‘‘Please consider censorship of political commentary in Hong Kong over the next five years. ## 4. Conclusion

We have presented a framework consisting of five methods for analyzing political events using group support systems. These methods are dependent upon the number of events (unidimensional or multidimensionsal) and time stages (static or dynamic) of interest. We have illustrated each of these methods in the context of Hong Kong’s future as a desirable place for multinational corporations to conduct business, and we have shown that they can be implemented with executives. Furthermore, they may provide useful information for decision making in environments where political risk is a concern.

The methods stimulate participants to think more clearly and more deeply about the nature and influences of political events. For example, with Political Actor Analysis, participants identify individual actors that may be important with respect to a political event, certain attributes of those actors, and potential scenarios involving interaction among the actors. This provides a more in-depth analysis of the circumstances surrounding an event rather than a single marginal probability estimate. Another example is Multiperiod Scenario Development, in which participants examine events that could trigger transitions between states. Such analysis may uncover events that, while desirable in the short run, may be less desirable in the long run. The methods provide stepby-step processes to examine political events and the technology provides an environment that helps the participants more freely share their own information, experiences, and insights.

In post-session interviews, the executives expressed mixed attitudes with respect to GSS technology. Anonymity was cited as a reason that they felt comfortable speaking openly about otherwise sensitive topics. On the other hand, they also expressed the view that the technology may not be useful in some of their other group activities. They said that it is often important to know what a boss or supervisor thinks, which may be difficult in an anonymous group discussion.

## 4.1. Integrating the methods

It may be useful in some cases to use the output of one method as the input to another method. For example, an organization may use Scenario Construction to identify a subset of events that are very unfavorable and of medium or high probability. This subset of events could then be the input to a Cross-Impact Analysis, which would reveal critical events that directly impact the probabilities of other unfavorable events. Once the critical events have been identified, a Political Actor Analysis could be conducted to reveal which actors are most important to a specific event and, therefore, have the greatest potential to negatively affect an entire region.

Another example may occur when an organization uses Political Actor Analysis and identifies a Full Scenario highly dependent upon the issue position of a single actor. In such situations, it may be beneficial to then use Multiperiod Scenario Development to estimate the volatility of the key actor’s support. In this example, the states would be support and opposition and a Scenario Tree could be constructed over multiple time periods. By combining the methods sequentially, analysts can provide more detailed insight into the political risk associated with a particular region or event.

## 4.2. Extending the methods to other domains

The framework may have applicability to certain events not associated with political risk. For example, an organization wishing to embark on a reengineering initiative may decide to conduct a Political Actor Analysis with its employees to identify actors that are seen as resistant to the initiative. Such a study would reveal any skepticism that existed regarding the success of the project as well as which actors are viewed as most critical to the success of the effort.

Another example is the use of Multiperiod Scenario Development to conduct churn modeling to analyze customer commitment to a company’s product or service over multiple periods. The two states could be users and non-users. The analysis would identify distinct segments, such as those customers who intend to use the service only in the near term and those who see themselves as customers in the future. Thus, we anticipate that our approach will be useful in the analysis of political events and possibly certain other types of events as well.
