# The role of IT human capability in the knowledge transfer process in IT outsourcing context

Joo Yeon Park, Kun Shin Im \*, Joon S. Kim

School of Business, Yonsei University, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 8 November 2009 Received in revised form 20 September 2010 Accepted 20 December 2010 Available online 13 January 2011

Keywords: Knowledge transfer IT human capability Trust Cooperative learning Human character

## A B S T R A C T

Our research attempted to identify the behavioral process of knowledge transfer by examining the effects of IT human capability, human character, trust, and cooperative learning on it in an IT outsourcing situation. By analysing data collected from vendor and client matched-pair samples of 87 IT outsourcing projects, we found that both the client’s and the vendor’s character influenced trust, trust affects on cooperative learning, and the cooperative learning influence on knowledge transfer. More importantly, it we found that the client’s IT human capability had a direct impact on cooperative learning and knowledge transfer. This indicated that client’s IT human capability was a crucial factor in effective knowledge transfer during IT outsourcing. It also implied that client firms should identify and retain some IT personnel who can apply vendors’ competencies in IT practice and respond effectively to any technological challenges.

## 1. Introduction

IToutsourcing is considered a strategically and economically good choice in enhancing firm performance. In IT an outsourcing context, however, clients without proper knowledge and understanding could encounter poor performance. Thus knowledge asymmetry between the client and vendor tends to favor the vendor, and the client loses control of IT, ceding it to the vendor. Lack of IT expertise in client firms also allows vendors to dominate IT-management. Recently, IT outsourcing research has focused on knowledge transfer between vendors and clients, which posits that effective knowledge transfer is critical in meeting the perceived needs of client firms and in achieving outsourcing success [17]. Clients typically acquire operational, maintenance, and training knowledge of outsourced IT. Willcocks et al. [28] suggested that most organizations do not understand how experiential research on transferring new knowledge can be used to improve an outsourcing situation.

However, few studies have focused on knowledge transfer in IT outsourcing contexts [8]. Furthermore, research that incorporates the perspectives of both clients and vendors is relatively rare, even though outsourcing involves actions from both sides of the relationship [15].

Previous research on IT outsourcing has a focus on outsourcing success that is defined as the extent to which clients achieve financial, managerial, technical, and relational expectations from IT outsourcing or the level of clients’ satisfaction with their vendors. We extended research on IT outsourcing success by considering the behavioral dimensions that are relevant to knowledge transfer from vendors to clients. To do this, a research model was developed based on resource-based and social exchange theory.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Knowledge transfer

By sharing knowledge in IT outsourcing environments, clients and vendors are able to sustain an effective outsourcing relationship over time. Moreover, the effective transfer of technical knowledge can have significant impact on the clients’ IT use and management. The knowledge domain includes implementationand customization knowledge which is both explicit and tacit: it may embody activities associated with configuring and testing applications or ERP modules, installing software and hardware, and training clients in preparing for ongoing operation and maintenance.

There have been several definitions of knowledge transfer; for example, Szulanski [26] takes an exchange approach by defining knowledge transfer as dyadic exchanges of knowledge between a source and a recipient. This definition focuses on the exchange of knowledge among intra-organizational members. Recently, knowledge transfer has been directed toward changes to the recipient and the inter-organizational interfaces. For instance, Argote and Ingram [2] define knowledge transfer as the process through which one unit is affected by the experience of another. Darr and Kurtzberg [7] also argue that knowledge transfer occurs when a contributor shares knowledge that isused by anadopter.Koet al. [15]define knowledge transfer as the communication of knowledge from a source so that it is learned and applied by a recipient. Thus knowledge is transferred when learning takes place and when the recipient understands the implications associated with that knowledge.

We therefore defined knowledge transfer as the extent to which clients acquire, absorb, and utilize knowledge on outsourced IT from vendors.

Based on social exchange theory, the behavioral elements selected in our study were trust, human character, and cooperative learning [20]. From previous resource-based theory studies [5], IT human capability was also adopted as a factor affecting knowledge transfer. Organizational support was used as a control variable in our study.

## 2.2. Resource-based theory

According to resource-based theory, IT related resources can be divided into two categories: IT assets and human capabilities [5]. IT assets facilitate the rapid collection, collation, storage, and dissemination of data, thereby assisting the knowledge creation and diffusion process. However, the human capabilities rather than IT assets were adopted as the factor affecting knowledge transfer [4,10]. Human capabilities refer to skills required to manage resources. IT human capability requires four types of skills: technology management, business functional, interpersonal and management, and technical [6]. IT human capability is costly to imitate and it is a valuable resource of a firm.

Argote and Ingram argued that IT staffs play the most significant role in technology transfer: their ability to absorb knowledge is essential in an outsourcing relationship. O’Dell and Grayson [21] also suggested that the IT human infrastructure could enhance performance in project and knowledge transfer and stressed that many organizations tended to emphasize the development of human infrastructure for effective knowledge transfer and management.

## 2.3. Social exchange theory

Social exchange involves actions that are contingent on rewarding reactions from others. Social exchange theory posits that the purpose of social exchange is to maximize benefits and minimize costs from an exchange. Human character, trust, and cooperative learning were selected as the behavioral factors that we used as influencing good knowledge transfer.

Thus, the role of trust would be more critical in an IT outsourcing situation than in outsourcing for other purposes, because clients rely on vendors for IT control and operation in IT outsourcing. Ba and Pavlou [3] found that trust could diminish information asymmetry between clients and service providers and decrease risk in the relationship. Gefen [9] also agued that trust can advance knowledge transfer and outsourcing performance. We assumed that trust was the expectation that a vendor (or client) could be relied upon to fulfill obligations, behave in predictably, and act fairly even if opportunism could be present [22].

Jarvenpaa et al. found that the human character was an antecedent of trust in a global virtual team environment. In our study, the human attributes included ability, benevolence, and integrity [13].

A cooperative work process among exchange parties is another essential factor in a social exchange relation where a powerdependence relation exists. Social exchange theorists have developed a power-dependence theory which argues that uncertainty resulting from resource scarcity and a lack of knowledge can be reduced through the use of organizational exchange. In the context of knowledge and resources exchange, cooperative learning (an activity where every team member works to maximize performance and learning [12]) can be important in the exchange process. Janz and Prasarnphanich [12] suggested that cooperation and collective approaches were desirable characteristics in knowledge creation and exchange. Hult et al. [11] insisted that cooperative learning facilitated knowledge management. Certain characteristics were essential for cooperative learning: positive interdependence (the degree to which group members feel that they are linked in), promotive interaction (the degree to which each group member identifies his or her strengths and weaknesses in the group and seeks to assist others in developing skills to achieve their goals), and group process (the extent to which the group periodically assesses its works as effective or ineffective).

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Our research model, shown in Fig. 1, was developed as the way in which we could examine the process of knowledge transfer; it incorporated both client and vendor perspectives.

## 3.1. IT human capability and trust

Lacity et al. [16] suggested that clients should have IT human ability to evaluate and monitor vendors’ activity so that they could establish trust with vendors. Having knowledge of system development and business process was also assumed to be important in performing evaluation and effective monitoring of vendors’ performance. Also, clients’ knowledge and experience during the system development process enabled them to develop trust with vendors through evaluation and monitoring of them.

Thus, a project member who had IT human capability might participate in a project and interact with other members more actively than a project member who did not. While knowledgebased trust is facilitated by shared experience between clients and vendors, identification-based trust is developed by mutual understanding and goal sharing between them. Thus trust building is based on both parties knowing and understanding the project and its goals. IT human skills should increase the mutual trust of project members. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H1. Client’s IT human capability is positively associated with trust.

## 3.2. IT human capability and cooperative learning

For efficient IT knowledge transfer in an outsourcing situation, clients and vendors should contribute their ideas and capabilities to be successful. According to the study by Kaiser and Hawk [14], clients should have a strong understanding and knowledge of IT and business process in order to exchange and share information with vendors during the system development phase. They argued that such behavior would result in competitive advantage.

IT personnel with substantial knowledge and skills tend to work cooperatively and interactively. Aladwani [1] demonstrated that a project group they had IT personnel with knowledge and experience performed better in problem solving than a project group without them. Accordingly:

H2. Client’s IT human capability is positively associated with cooperative learning.

## 3.3. IT human capability and knowledge transfer

Clients’ knowledge acquired and transferred from vendors can significantly affect system usage and improve its management. When clients do not receive IT operation and maintenance supports, knowledge becomes more important. Bartlett and

Ghoshal [4] argued that a process converting information into knowledge can be performed by social interaction with IT experts who understand it. Haines and Goodhue [10] indicated that the more capability and knowledge clients had, the more clients involved themselves and participated in the project team, resulting in more effective knowledge transfer. As O’Dell and Grayson argued, IT human capability is much more valuable than IT infrastructure in knowledge transfer. Explicit knowledge enables clients to understand the vendors’ ability and expertise, while implicit knowledge secures the relationship and develops mutual trust. This, lead to the hypothesis:

H3. Client IT human capability is positively associated with knowledge transfer from vendors to clients.

## 3.4. Human character and trust

Trust in a dyadic relationship arising from attributes associated with both the trustee and the trustor. Thus, clients’ and vendors’ attributes are antecedents of trust in a relationship. Jarvenpaa et al. found that team members’ perceptions of other members’ human attributes – ability, benevolence, and integrity – were associated with trust building in a global virtual team relationship. However vendors with a technical specialty often tended to neglect and alienate client team members in a project group, leading to less trust between them.

Also, it has been argued that the degree of trust varies according to the trustee’s experience, personal type, and cultural background. When a trustee’s ability and integrity are perceived to be high, information received is believed to be credible or trustworthy. As a result:

H4. Client’s human character is positively associated with trust.

H5. Vendor’s human character is positively associated with trust.

## 3.5. Trust and cooperative learning

Trust encourages interdependency and increased interaction among project members [25]. Also, it can help in developing a cooperative learning environment through openness while reducing the risk of information and knowledge disclosure. In high trust relationships, people are more willing to engage in social exchange and cooperative interaction. The importance of trust building was reported in [23] in General Motors; it experienced IT failure due to neglecting trust as important to IT staff cooperation.

Many studies show that trust is associated with organizational learning and knowledge acquisition. Accordingly, trust between clients and vendors in an IT outsourcing situation could be an essential factor for developing a cooperative learning environment; thus:

H6. Trust is positively associated with cooperative learning.

## 3.6. Cooperative learning and knowledge transfer

Successful outsourcing can only be achieved by adopting a technology and transferring sufficient knowledge through cooperative learning (an activity in which all team members works to improve performance and learning). Janz and Prasarnphanich pointed out that cooperative learning with members with different knowledge and background is essential for knowledge creation, exchange, and transfer. Moreover, it has been found that the more interaction with partners, the more information, including tacit knowledge transfer occurs [30]; an active cooperative learning process can acquire and transfer knowledge easily and smoothly.

Argote and Ingram stated that a better understanding of socialization was needed by people who could affect knowledge transfer. Foss and Pedersen indicated that knowledge transfer can be affected by the choice of inter-firm mechanisms such as interdependency. Since cooperative learning is a way to expand people’s knowledge and learning ability, it would be associated with knowledge transfer. Accordingly, strong cooperation and teamwork between clients and vendors are desirable characteristics that enhance knowledge transfer.

H7. Cooperative learning is positively associated with knowledge transfer.

The unit of analysis in our study was an IT outsourcing project. The IT outsourcing projects included implementation and customization project of enterprise applications such as ERP, SCM, and CRM. Because of the complexity of these applications, most organizations rely on external consultants for developing and overseeing their implementation. Thus, adopting and customizing a packaged system was at the core of our study. Since knowledge transfer is key in simplifying the maintenance and utilization of such applications.

To incorporate both client’s and vendor’s perspectives, matched-pair surveys were used. The survey was therefore conducted on two client members who were participating or had participated in IT outsourcing project. All 256 member firms of the CIO forum in Korea were contacted in order to collect data. Also, at least one vendor who was participating or had participated in the same project was selected as the respondent for our survey. Data collected from both clients and vendors were averaged. This procedure was intended to reduce potential problems of single respondent and common method bias. Client members were asked about their IT human capability and knowledge transfer from vendors. Data on human character, trust, and cooperative learning were collected from both clients and vendors of the project team.

Because the degree of transferred knowledge in an IT outsourcing project can vary due to the degree of organizational support and project complexity, we added them as control variables in our analysis. Top manager support was also measured in order to determine any causality effects of it on the increase of knowledge transferred. Data on the control variables were collected from client members because the vendors’ organizational support and views on project complexity would not affect knowledge transfe from vendor to clients.

## 4.2. Characteristics of the respondents

Data were collected from a total of 107 outsourcing projects. Twenty were excluded because they had unmatched questionnaires or questionnaires with missing items. Thus 87 project samples and a total of 338 responses – 192 from clients and 146 from vendors – were used. Since only an IT outsourcing project for each of the client firms was used in our analysis, the number of projects was equal to that of the client firms. The client firms were from multiple industries; most were in finance and manufacturing. The client members who responded to the survey had position titles such as manager and director (see Table 2). Table 3 shows that more than 70% of the sample vendor firms were large, having at least 1000 employees. Executives, managers, directors, or employees of vendor firms responded to our survey.

Individual item reliability was assessed by examining the loading (bold values in Table 4) of each item on its construct. The normal guideline of loadings greater than 0.7 was adopted, though items with loading values greater than 0.5 are also suggested as acceptable if the items are important to reflect the construct. As shown in Table 4, an item with a loading value of 0.69 was retained for the hypothesis test. Internal consistency was examined using the composite scale reliability index (CSRI) (similar to Cronbach’s alpha), which should exceed 07. All measures met this criterion, thus they could be considered reliable.

In Table 5, the diagonal and bold values represent the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE): a measure of the convergent validity. All diagonal values exceed 0.5, suggesting that convergent validity was established. Factor loadings also supported this. There are two procedures for assessing discriminant validity. First, the square root of the AVE for each construct should be greater than the off-diagonal correlations. Second, each item within the construct should load highly on the construct that it is intended to measure and the cross-loadings must be lower than the intra-construct item loadings. During assessment of discriminant validity, items not loading highly on their own constructs but loading on others were deleted: the second and fifth items of trust and the third item of organizational support were low and therefore deleted, resulting discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Test of structural model

The hypotheses were tested by assessing the structural model; this involved estimating the path coefficients and the R<sup>2</sup> value. We used the bootstrap resampling method of PLS to determine the significance of the path coefficients. The sample size of 89 exceeded the recommended minimum of 70, which represented 10 times the number of indicators in the most complex construct (i.e., the 7 items of project complexity). Table 6 shows the result of the structural model assessment; over 40% of the variance in trust, cooperative learning, and knowledge transfer were explained. Six of the nine paths exceed the suggested 0.20 minimum value of significance. These results show that the fit of the overall model was acceptable.

As expected, the client’s human character (path coefficient = 0.269, p < 0.01) and the vendor’s human character (path coefficient = 0.506, p < 0.01) both significantly influenced trust, accounting for 42.7% of the variance – supporting Hypotheses 4 and 5. However, there was no significant relationship between client’s IT human capability and trust; thus, Hypothesis 1 was not supported. As hypothesized, cooperative learning was significantly influenced by the client’s IT human capability (path coefficient = 0.250, p < 0.01) and trust (path coefficient = 0.575, p < 0.01), which explain 43.7% of the dependent variables variance; the two paths have effects in the expected direction, and thus Hypotheses 2 and 6 were supported. Finally, knowledge transfer was significantly associated with the client’s IT human capability (path coefficient = 0.356, p < 0.01) and cooperative learning (path coefficient = 0.354, p < 0.01), accounting for 43.9% of the dependent variable’s variance and supporting Hypotheses 3 and 7. ## 6. Discussion and implications

Our empirical results strongly supported six hypotheses (Hypotheses 2 through 7) but contrary to our expectation, Hypothesis 1 was not. Unlike earlier studies, we measured trust by combining responses from both the client’s and the vendor’s perceived trust of each other.

The results of our study contain a number of implications for researchers. First, it confirmed that trust and cooperative learning between clients and vendors are necessary so that clients acquire knowledge from their vendors, demonstrating a need for trustworthy and cooperative relationships between clients and vendors. Second, perceptions of other party’s human character – ability, integrity, and benevolence – had a significant effect on trust, with the vendor’s human character having a stronger effect on trust than the client’s human character. Third, IT personnel in a client firm should have a cooperative relationship with vendors to acquire knowledge from vendors in an outsourcing situation. We employed client and vendor matched-pair data set to incorporate both client and vendor viewpoints, providing better understanding of knowledge transfer in a dyadic relationship.

In addition, our findings can provide lessons for IT managers in both client and vendor firms on facilitating knowledge transfer in IT outsourcing contexts. Relationships between clients and vendors are important and must be boosted to facilitate the flow of knowledge from vendors to clients, suggesting that it is important to create an environment of trust. Failure to select individuals with ability, integrity, and benevolence from both firms can inhibit the flow of knowledge. Finally, client’s IT human capability directly affects cooperative learning and knowledge transfer. Thus, clients should possess and educate IT personnel with capability of business process, technology management, and interpersonal skill to aid in knowledge transfer.

## 7. Conclusions and limitations

We collected data from vendor and client matched-pair samples of 87 IT outsourcing projects. Using PLS estimation method, we found that both clients’ and vendors’ human characters influenced trust and its affect on cooperative learning, which, in turn, affected knowledge transfer. Moreover, client’s IT human capability directly affected cooperative learning and knowledge transfer.

There were, of course, several limitations. First, knowledge transfer should be studied as a long-term process to understand the degree of clients’ knowledge adoption and its use after being transferred. However, our project data was collected at a point of time using a cross-sectional methodology, which may not fully explain the process.

Second, the IT outsourcing project type should have been better controlled. The degree of knowledge transfer could depend on the type of system being outsourced. Even though we used project complexity to act as a control variable to avoid such bias, we could not examine whether the IT outsourced applications projects had different impacts on the knowledge transfer process because of the small sample size.

Third, this study did not consider the different types of knowledge in IT outsourcing: whether knowledge is tacit or explicit could affect the degree of knowledge transfer.

Finally, the way that external knowledge can be absorbed and how it can be later applied were not addressed in our study, nor were the problems accrued by knowledge dependency and asymmetry between clients and vendors.
