## Abstract

A theoretical model of professional identification is developed and empirically examined as a means to understanding information technology (IT) workers’ attachment to the IT profession. Professional identification represents oneness with or belonging to a profession and provides a unique means of investigating and evaluating the IT profession. Results from a survey of 305 IT workers indicate that professional identification is directly impacted by three factors: (1) the individual’s need for professional identification; (2) the individual’s perceived similarity to others in the IT profession; and (3) the individual’s perceptions of the IT profession, signifying the importance of internalization to identification. Professional identification is also indirectly impacted by the public’s perception of the IT profession. Keywords: identification; workforce; IT profession; distinctiveness of profession

## Introduction

The information technology (IT) profession provides an essential resource to organizations worldwide. Within the IT research stream, careful attention has been paid to the relationship between the individual IT worker and their organization. Factors such as job design characteristics, individual differences, career advancement and orientation, and knowledge, skills, and abilities required by the job (Niederman et al., 1999; Ang & Slaughter, 2000) have been a few of the areas receiving attention. Research related to the IT profession has also explored such topics as women in IT and burnout (Moore, 2000; Ahuja, 2002; Pawlowski et al., 2007). Even with this body of literature and given the importance of IT workers to today’s economy, we agree with the observation made by Agarwal & Ferratt (2002) that in this stream of research ‘there is a major gap in theory and practice that needs to be addressed’ (p. 74). A goal of this research study is to explore factors aimed at helping us more thoroughly examine the IT profession. Using identity theory, social identity theory, and selfcategorization theory, and by focusing on the individuals who comprise the IT profession, we hope to better understand behaviors and outcomes noted in practice. It has been found that attitudes related to one’s profession can impact attitudes related to an organization (Vandenberg & Scarpello, 1994). By understanding the IT profession, we hope to offer suggestions for management, including issues, for example, relevant to employee retention.

A primary driver of this stream of research lies in the fact that the IT profession is facing challenges that cannot necessarily be explained by understanding the individual’s relationship with, and attitudes towards, the organization. Challenges such as offshore outsourcing of IT functions and the misconception that there are a limited number of IT jobs in U.S.A. have had an impact on the IT profession specifically (Zweig et al., 2006). It is important to acknowledge that individuals may choose to leave one profession for another – without consideration of the organization. Professional identification is defined as the perception of oneness with or belonging to a profession (Russo, 1998). By studying the professional identification of individuals currently in the profession, potential areas will be highlighted which can assist in understanding attachment to the profession.

Research examining the IT worker has been useful in explaining individual behavior primarily within an organizational context; it has not focused as much on providing the necessary information to understand individual IT worker’s beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors related to the IT profession. As noted by Russo (1998), ‘many types of organizations employee individuals whose attachment and allegiances may be divided between employer and profession’ (pp. 95–96). Values associated with the profession attribute to the individual’s ability to deal with organizational issues such as restructuring and general daily job demands (Russo, 1998). Additionally, previous research in management and organizational behavior has indicated that professional identification is positively related to organizational identification but that the two constructs may operate independently in how they influence outcomes. For example, in a study of auditors focused on both identification with the employing organization and the profession, professional identification did not directly impact organization/profession conflict but contributed only through its impact on organizational identification (Bamber & Iyer, 2002). Therefore, it is important that we understand what impacts the formation of professional identification in hopes that we may foster positive behavior and increased productivity and retention.

## Theories of identification

Research on identification is rich and provides tremendous opportunity for the study of professional identification specifically. Prior research studies including the construct of professional identification have varied on the inclusion of construct and have not been based on comprehensive theoretical frameworks. These models have included the following professions: graduate students (Becker & Carper, 1956a, b), auditors (Bamber & Iyer, 2002), nurses (Apker et al., 2003 as well as Elovainio & Kivimaki, 2001), and the Israeli police force (Moore, 1999). Some of the predictors of professional identification examined include age, autonomy, coworker support, job demands, role ambiguity, rank, experience, and sponsorship. From these studies, a wide variety of both qualitative and quantitative methods were used to test the models.

There are three primary theories used to study identification that fall under two main approaches: (1) the sociological approach (identity theory) and (2) the psychological approach (social identity theory and self-categorization theory). Both approaches view identification as a means to structurally ordering society in relation to the self. The differences between the two approaches relate to (1) the way the self is structured in regard to social identities and (2) the mechanisms under which identification is developed.

The sociological approach is represented by identity theory. Identity theory focuses on the social roles that individuals hold in society (McCall, 1977) and the interaction of these roles with counter-roles. An example of a role/counter-role would be a teacher and student. In general, one would not exist without the other. Symbolic interactionism (Stryker, 1980) is a major component of the sociological approach. The primary mechanism by which identifications are formed is through social interaction between social roles.

The psychological approach is represented by two theories: social identity theory and self-categorization theory. Both theories focus on the individual’s identification with social groups as opposed to specific roles in society. The psychological approach is sometimes referred to as the social identity approach even though it encompasses both social identity theory and self-categorization theory. Each theory is rarely mentioned in the absence of the other (Hogg et al., 1995). Social identity theory defines an individual identity as ‘that part of the individual’s self-concept which derives from his knowledge of his membership of a social group together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership’ (Tajfel, 1978, p. 63). A primary mechanism underlying social identity theory is the individual’s belief that he or she is a member of a group. Identification in this regard is a psychological process and requires no interaction with others in the group.

Self-categorization theory (Turner, 1982) focuses on the ‘knowledge of group membership’ component provided in the original definition of social identity theory. Selfcategorization theory posits that individuals identify with social groups through a process of categorization. Categorization involves a cognitive comparison of the individual to the group and acknowledges the existence of other groups (Turner, 1987).

In order to generate the research model empirically studied here, it was necessary to conceptualize the main components represented by the theories encompassed by these approaches. In doing so, three primary areas emerged: individual factors, social factors, and professionrelated factors. Individual factors relate to those trait-like factors that impact an individual’s identification. Social factors relate primarily to those functions highlighted by identity theory where we focus on the interaction between different roles. Profession-related factors involve those perceptions that the individual has related to his or her place in the profession (internal) and to the profession in general (external). The primary focus of this study is on individual and profession-related factors that influence identification with the IT profession as a whole and not to a specific position within the profession. Social factors are therefore not a component of the final model examined as it represents a different context for the individual.

## Individual factors

General and technical self-efficacy A consistent conceptualization underlying all approaches to the study of identification is the notion that individuals identify in order to maintain a positive self-image (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). One key self-evaluative factor discussed by Judge et al. (2005) is self-efficacy.

Research on self-efficacy generally acknowledges two forms of self-efficacy: general and technical or task specific (Chen et al., 2004). General self-efficacy is defined as the ‘individuals’ perceptions of their ability to perform across a variety of different situations’ ( Judge et al., 1998, p. 170). Technical self-efficacy relates to the individuals’ belief about their abilities to perform in a given situation.

This form of self-efficacy has been examined in relation to the IT profession and is termed technical self-efficacy. Self-efficacy research in the IT discipline often relates to computer user self-efficacy and highlights its importance in determining such factors as use and playfulness (Webster & Martocchio, 1992).

Within the IT industry, technological trends and rapid changes increase the need for individuals to constantly upgrade skills (Ang & Slaughter, 2000; Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002). This need to constantly upgrade skills can be impacted by how workers evaluate themselves in terms of their abilities as members of the profession. As a motivator in the ‘development of the self’ (Erez & Earley, 1993, p. 181), self-efficacy is examined here as a key self-evaluative component. Individuals in professions are typically trained and educated to perform certain tasks with a certain expected level of skill. With the IT profession, this includes the initial undergraduate degree, the earning of certifications, and continual training throughout one’s career to remain current on the latest technology.

One of the driving forces behind identification with a social group is positive self-evaluation. Self-efficacy, as pointed out by Judge et al. (2005), is a form of selfevaluation. Self-efficacy, with the subcomponents of general and technical, is included as an important construct relevant to the IT profession. Individuals tend to choose ‘situations in which they can be competent ( Judge et al., 1998, p. 173). As Stets & Burke state, individuals identify in order to ‘feel competent and effective’ (2000, p. 233). Given that general self-efficacy relates to one’s perceptions of performance in a variety of situations and the choice of IT career can provide the professional the opportunity to serve in a variety of roles (from programmer to analyst) and situations, we state the following.

## H2: Technical self-efficacy will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

General self-efficacy and technical self-efficacy have been shown to be related in previous research. Stanley & Murphy (1997) have shown that an individual’s belief in their abilities represented as general self-efficacy impacts technical self-efficacy. To be consistent with the literature, the relationship between general and technical selfefficacy is included in the model.

Need for identification It is important to note the recognized differences in identification and commitment, as on the surface, the two constructs may appear to be similar. In organizational identification research, Ashforth & Mael (1989) suggest identification is based on factors such as similarity to the organization while commitment is formed by exchange-based relationships. Mael & Tetrick (1992) confirmed that commitment and identification in an organizational context represent two distinct factors. Also, a recent meta-analysis on identification confirmed the distinction between the two constructs in an organizational context as well (Riketta, 2005).

A foundation underlying much of the work on identification research is the idea of need fulfillment. Researchers have argued that individuals have an inherent need to belong (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). In an empirical study of alumni, Kreiner & Ashforth (2004) operationalized the need to identify. The authors found their subjects’ need to identify was significantly related to their level of organizational identification. The greater the subjects’ need to identify, the higher their level of identification. Thus, the concept is useful in explaining how individuals vary in their identification.

The items developed by Kreiner & Ashforth focus on a ‘generalized desire to derive at least a partial selfdefinition’ from the organization (2004, p. 13). Derived directly from this conceptualization, need for professional identification is defined as the individual’s desire to define him or herself in part by membership in a profession, in this case the IT profession. Thus, it is posited that

H4: Need for professional identification will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

## Profession factors – internal

Similarity to others in the profession Self-categorization theory posits that individuals identify with social groups that highlight their similarity to what they perceive as the prototypical member of the group (Chattopadhyay et al., 2004). Interestingly enough, no research studies were found that operationalized and measured this key component of the psychological approach. Similarity to others in the IT profession is defined as the degree to which the individual believes that he or she is similar to the prototypical IT professional (Ashmore et al., 2004). The concept is operationalized here and reflects the degree to which the individual perceives similarity on key factors important to professions: skills and abilities, work ethic, goals, and general attitudes and beliefs. In addition, the construct may include a demographic similarity attribute perception. There has been a focus within research on the IT workforce dedicated to understanding the lack of women and minority group representation (Igbaria & Baroudi, 1995; Igbaria & Wormley, 1995; Trauth, 2002; Woodfield, 2002). According to the latest study by the Information Technology Association of America (2005), the IT workforce still suffers from a lack of representation from key demographic categories. Women comprise approximately 32% of the IT workforce, while African-Americans and Hispanics comprise approximately 9 and 6%, respectively.

From a simple demographic perspective, one could conceptualize that women and racial minority groups would not identify based on perceived similarity to others in the IT profession, if that prototype is represented by the individuals that comprise the majority of the profession (e.g., the white male). Similarities can also exist related to skills, abilities, etc., and individuals may not include demographic factors in the formation of their conceptualization of the profession prototype. It is hypothesized that

H5: Degree of similarity to others in the IT profession will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

Individual perceptions of the profession In the original definition of identification provided by social identity theory, Tajfel (1978) highlights the importance of the evaluation of the group to an individual’s identity. Previous findings in organizational research support the inclusion of an evaluative component as an antecedent to organizational identification (Mael & Ashforth, 1992; Pratt, 1998). Within the model proposed, there are two specific forms of evaluation hypothesized to impact identification with the IT profession. One is considered an internal factor while the other is considered an external factor. The internal factor, individual perceptions of the profession, is hypothesized to impact their identification with the profession. The external factor, which is presented in the next section of the paper, represents how an individual perceives that others view the IT profession and emphasizes the importance of others’ views to professional identification. These two different perspectives have previously been termed private regard (individual perceptions/internal) and public regard (public perceptions/external) (Luhtanen & Crocker, 1992). Prior research has only considered the external perspective; the current study examines both.

Individual perceptions of the social group are defined as ‘one’s judgments of how good one’s social groups are’ (Luhtanen & Crocker, 1992). Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H6: Individual perceptions of the IT profession will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

## Profession factors – external

Public perceptions of the profession As mentioned previously, research has empirically evaluated the impact of the perceptions of others as it relates to organizational identification. One study specific to professional identification by Bamber & Iyer (2002) included a construct called professional image. The justification for the inclusion of this evaluative component is the same as with the individual’s perceptions. More positive perceptions of the group by others will result in higher identification with the group.

The two different perceptions (internal and external) are supported by research on ethnic identity as well. Crocker et al. (1994) found the two views had a small correlation and concluded that for their operationalization, the perceptions of the individual and the perceptions of others as viewed by the individual operate uniquely to impact ethnic identification. Public perceptions of a social group are defined as ‘one’s judgments of how other people evaluate one’s social groups’ (Luhtanen & Crocker, 1992, p. 305). Public perceptions of the IT profession are hypothesized to impact the individual’s level of professional identification.

## H7: Public perceptions of the IT profession will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

Recent research by Ashmore et al. (2004) points to the importance of the relationship between the individual’s perceptions of the group and the public’s perceptions.

The individual’s view of how others feel about a social group will impact how the individual feels about the group. A main theme underlying the notion of identity is the need for a positive self-concept and self-evaluation. When a group is perceived positively by society, the individual’s perception of the group is directly impacted. Thus, it is hypothesized that

H8: How individuals perceive the public views the IT profession will be positively related to individual perceptions of IT.

As discussed, as individuals evaluate a group to which they claim membership, their identification with the group can change. Negative evaluations can cause individuals to alter their identification and perhaps try to disassociate with one group to become a member in a more favorable group. A primary process that impacts this change in identification is the impact of evaluative perceptions on similarity. If individuals perceive the group undesirable, they may attempt to accentuate dissimilarities to the group prototype (Deaux, 1996).

If IT workers positively evaluate the profession or believe that others positively evaluate the profession, then they will have a higher perceived degree of similarity with others in the IT profession.

H9: Individual perceptions of the IT profession will be positively related to similarity to others in the IT profession.

H10: Public perceptions of the IT profession will be positively related to similarity to others in the IT profession.

Distinctiveness of the profession According to selfcategorization theory. when an individual categorizes as a member of a social group, he or she is doing so in comparison to some other group. Social identities are seen as ‘cognitive groupings of oneself and some class of stimuli as the same y in contrast to some other class of stimuli’ (Turner, 1987, p. 44). Self-categorization theory specifically acknowledges the importance of other groups in the formation of social identities.

Only one study focusing on organizational identification research has examined this idea empirically (Mael & Ashforth, 1992). Distinctiveness of the school (organization/group) under study was directly and positively related to the identification of alumni with the school. The concept is extended here, and it is hypothesized that

H11: Distinctiveness of the profession will be positively related to identification with the IT profession.

Uncertainty in the profession Many theoretical papers examining the concept of identification have purported that individuals identify with social groups as a means to reduce uncertainty (Chattopadhyay et al., 2004). Previous research examining uncertainty and identification together has been conducted in experimental settings only (Hogg & Grieve, 1999; Hogg & Mullin, 1999; Hogg & Terry, 2000). The findings from these studies reveal that the level of uncertainty in social groups is related to the individual’s identification as a member of the group.

Individuals are motivated to reduce uncertainty related to their definition of the self (Hogg & Mullin, 1999). As such, any uncertainty related to the membership in the group becomes troublesome for the individual. The greater the degree of uncertainty in the profession, the lower the level of identification with the IT profession.

## H12: Uncertainty in the profession will be negatively related to identification with the IT profession.

## Research design

Data for this study were collected as part of a larger three-year longitudinal study of individuals currently working in the IT profession across several industries: healthcare, IT services/software, transportation, and government. Initially, contact was made with the Chief Information Officer (CIO) of several firms in the south central United States. Each CIO solicited input and participation from his/her IT employees. Five hundred individuals who had participated in the first year’s data collection were sent an email explaining the purpose of the ongoing study and requesting their participation. A link to the web survey was provided in the email. Responses were received from 348 individuals representing an overall response rate of 69.6%. Of these responses, 305 were usable (i.e., they had no missing data). Demographics for the sample are provided in Table 1. The sample comprised 59.5% male respondents with the average age of the participants being 42 (93% of the sample identified themselves as white, providing for a 7% minority representation). A majority of the participants worked full time (96%), had an undergraduate degree or higher (76.8%), and represented a variety of IT positions and industries. The average number of years in the IT profession was 14.4, and the average number of organizations worked for as an IT professional was 2.57.

The next sections describe the process used to assess the measurement and structural models. The analysis follows the two-step modeling approach suggested by Anderson & Gerbing (1988).

## Measurement model assessment

Testing assumptions Prior to conducting an analysis of the data, observations were checked for compliance with assumptions of the statistical tests performed using SPSS and AMOS. No variance inflation factors were above 2, indicating that no problems were faced with multicollinearity. Four observations were removed because they were identified as outliers based on a P-value below 0.001 (Byrne, 2001). All data for the study were collected in the same manner. Potential reasons for the outliers could include interpreting the questions incorrectly or as a result of reverse coding the items, respondents not adjusting his/her responses to the correct scale. The models were also assessed for changes in fit with the removal of the outliers and no significant changes were detected. Normality was also assessed and no violations were found.

The majority of measures used in this study were adapted from previous research. After an exhaustive literature search, it was concluded that no appropriate measures existed for distinctiveness, similarity, and uncertainty so items were created specifically for this study. Distinctiveness had been previously measured in an organizational identification study (Mael & Ashforth, 1992), but the authors noted that the scale was specific to the sample studied and not adaptable to another context.

A complete listing of the items used to measure the constructs and their sources can be found in the Appendix. All items were measured on a 7-point Likerttype scale. Anchors for all items except those used to measure uncertainty were: strongly disagree (1) and strongly agree (7). Uncertainty item anchors were: highly uncertain (1) and highly certain (7).

Item analysis The items developed specifically for this study were first examined for content validity by a group of faculty and Ph.D. students at a large university in southeastern U.S. Revised items were pilot tested with a group of 140 business students. Constructs pilot tested included distinctiveness, uncertainty, similarity, and need for professional identification. Need for professional identification is based on existing and validated work by Kreiner & Ashforth (2004). The construct has not been adapted to another context. Its inclusion in this standard pilot test was necessary to determine whether the translation from an organizational context to a profession context would suffice. Students from different business disciplines were asked to respond to the survey items based on their chosen profession.

Based on the pilot test, items that did not load strongly on the expected factor were removed from the constructs, resulting in 5-item constructs in each case. Distinctiveness, uncertainty, similarity, and need for professional identification had reliabilities of 0.905, 0.923, 0.867, and 0.772 respectively, all above the 0.70 recommended cutoff (Hair et al., 2006).

Professional identification items were adapted directly from organizational identification research (Mael & Ashforth, 1992). In a recent meta-analysis on organizational identification (Riketta, 2005), the Mael and Ashforth scale was found to be the preferred method for measuring organizational identification. General selfefficacy and technical self-efficacy items were adapted from items used in previous research (Sherer et al., 1982;

Riggs et al., 1994). Measures for both public perceptions of IT and individual perceptions of IT were adapted from work by Mael & Ashforth (1992).

Validity Prior to assessing the fit of the path model, it was necessary to evaluate the measures for convergent and discriminant validity. The professional identification construct was examined first. All average variance extracted loadings were above 0.5 (as recommended by Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The standardized residual between PI1 and PI6 was above the recommended cutoff of |2.58| (Joreskog & Sorbom, 1988); therefore, PI1 on the professional identification construct was removed. Table 2 contains the standardized residual covariance matrix. After removing the item, the construct was reevaluated and no additional problems were identified.

General self-efficacy was examined next and GSE1 was removed since its loading was below 0.5; the loadings on the remaining items were well above 0.5. Technical self-efficacy was evaluated and the reverse coded item (TSE2) was also removed. Besides the potential for the item being problematic due to reverse coding, TSE2 measures the individual in relation to others while the remaining two items do not. The analysis of the need for professional identification construct revealed that NPID3 was loading below 0.5, leading to the removal of the item. On the second run, NPID1 and NPID2 loaded below 0.5, so each item was removed one at a time leaving NPID4 and NPID5. Admittedly, it is not ideal to have a construct with fewer than three items, but for this data set, the two-item solution appeared to be the best.

All three of the constructs developed explicitly for this study (similarity, distinctiveness, and uncertainty) had loadings above 0.5 on each item, and no problems were faced in the standardized residual covariance matrix. For both the individual and public perceptions of the profession constructs, the reverse coded items loaded below 0.5 and were removed one at a time, resulting in three items for each construct. It has been noted in previous research examining measurement issues in organizational behavior research, in particular, that reverse coding effects, such as those experienced here, are common (Magazine et al., 1996). The factor loadings on the final measurement model were all above 0.5 with the exception of NPID4 which loaded at 0.475. Since this is the first known adaptation of the NPID construct to another context and the results from the pilot test did not indicate that the construct would be problematic and the factor loadings and reliability were acceptable, we retained this item. Potential differences in results for this factor could be attributed to the use of students as the pilot group or the existence of factors in the sample that are currently not known to the researchers. After analysis, all constructs had acceptable average variance extracted values; see Table 3, as recommended by Fornell & Larcker (1981).

## Discussion

Studying the professional identification of the IT worker provides an additional approach to understanding the IT workforce. In particular, the current research study allows us to examine the IT professional from a new perspective and adds to the professional identification literature in general by examining antecedents from a theoretical perspective. Overall, the research presented here provides a start to understanding components that influence the identification of the IT worker with the IT profession. The following is a discussion of the results, organized and based on the model components presented in Figure 1. First, individual factors are discussed, followed by internal and external profession factors.

## Individual factors

One individual factor, the need for professional identification, was found significant in this study of IT workers. This is the first known adaptation and examination of the construct outside the context of an organization. As addressed, Mael & Tetrick (1992) confirmed that commitment and identification are distinct factors that influence individual behavior. As such, it is important that we understand the antecedents driving identification. The measure for need for identification was derived directly from the conceptualization presented by Kreiner & Ashforth (2004).

For this group of IT professionals, there is a strong connection between the individual’s inherent need to identify and perceived identification with the profession. This trait-like characteristic signifies the importance of social group identification to the self and the positive nature of the relationship. A negative relationship would indicate a desire to distance oneself from the profession in terms of group identification.

Identification research also generally argues that individuals identify with social groups based on a need for a positive self-evaluation. The self-evaluative component of the individual included here was self-efficacy. Both general and technical self-efficacy were not significantly related to professional identification. A potential reason for general self-efficacy not being related to professional identification could be that it is too general. A measure of how well individuals evaluate their abilities across situations may not provide enough of a connection to their place in the profession. The standard deviation for technical self-efficacy was 0.87 and 1.05 for general self-efficacy, thus not much variance exists for this sample of IT professionals. However, one would expect that IT professionals in general would have a high self-efficacy, both generally and technically, because of the tasks they perform on a daily basis. One would also expect minimal variance regarding IT professionals’ selfefficacy. It should be noted that these observations are not drawn directly from the data. Additionally, technical self-efficacy may not have been significant due to the actual wording of the questions and the dropping of the reverse-coded item.

## Internal profession factors

Both of the constructs, similarity to the profession and how individuals perceive the IT profession, were found significantly related to professional identification. The relationship of similarity to identification in this study emphasizes the importance of sharing beliefs and attitudes perceived as prototypical of the profession. Individuals place a level of significance on their perception of this similarity, providing an assessment of how well their self-image is in line with what they see as representative of the group. The process of professional identity development consists of identifying these similarities with others in the profession and dissimilarities with those not in the profession (Ely, 1995; Alvesson et al., 2001). In cases where there is a high level of perceived similarity, individuals may be more satisfied with and have a higher positive evaluation of the profession.

Of the different constructs hypothesized to predict professional identification, the individual’s perception was the strongest. How the individual evaluates the profession represents an important factor potentially related to both making the initial connection with the profession and future judgments on staying in the profession. In this study, IT professionals with a positive evaluation of the profession may have a more positive impression of the profession and thus exhibit higher identification. Since extant research has not considered the impact of the internal factors on professional identification, the significance of both similarity and individual perceptions of the profession as antecedents to PI enhances and contributes to our understanding of PI.

The importance of IPP to PI is also fundamental for practitioners to consider. Managing the worker to maintain a positive view of their profession could result in a higher level of attachment to the profession. Managers should encourage and support participation of their IT employees in professional groups such as VB.net user groups, the Information Technology Association of America (ITAA), or the Association of IT Professionals. Participation in functions that help define the profession could strengthen perceptions of the individual’s place in the profession and thus impact their identity with the profession. It is also often the case that companies select or hand pick employees to participate in a conference as an acknowledgement of their work. Furthermore, management could sponsor or host meetings and functions of these groups to promote strong connection of their IT employees to the profession.

## External profession factors

This study expanded on external profession factors by developing and applying measures for profession distinctiveness and uncertainty along with a previously examined construct: public perceptions of the profession. Based on these findings it appears that the relationship of public perceptions to identification is not straightforward. In the case of the IT professionals examined here, it could be that with previous fluctuations in the profession (such as the dot com bust), individuals who based their ‘membership’ in IT on what others were thinking have already left to seek other career paths.

The inclusion of distinctiveness as a potential antecedent to professional identification is supported by the foundations of the self-categorization theory as well as empirical research examining organizational identification. Since the construct used in the present study was newly developed, the differences in the relationships could be explained by the use of different measures or by there being a more complicated relationship between the constructs. Distinctiveness does not directly impact the individual’s identification with the IT profession.

Uncertainty in the profession was found to be nonsignificant in this study. Previous research on the relationship between uncertainty and identification has been conducted in experimental settings only, and findings indicate that uncertainty did relate to the individual’s identification with the group (Hogg & Grieve, 1999; Hogg & Mullin, 1999; Hogg & Terry, 2000). This is the first known translation of uncertainty from an experimental setting to a field study related to identification research. The results indicate that the amount of uncertainty perceived in the IT profession does not impact the level of identification the individual has with the profession.

The current research study examined several theoretically derived antecedents to professional identification resulting in 41.5% of the variance explained in the dependent variable. To date, only one study has reported an $\dot { R } ^ { 2 }$ value for the professional identification construct. In their study of auditors, Bamber & Iyer (2002) explained 18% of the variance in professional identification.

## Future research

While the results previously discussed explain a significant amount of variance in professional identification compared to the one reported $R ^ { 2 } ,$ , it is important to acknowledge several limitations in this study. As is the case in most studies, it is not possible to generalize the results for the entire population of IT professionals. While the sample was not small, the individuals only represent a portion of those currently working in the profession. The individuals in the study work for organizations located in the U.S. With the continued growth of sourcing IT functions to offshore locations, it would be beneficial to extend this research to include IT professionals working in other geographic locations.

Additionally, a problem with the need for professional identification (NPID) construct cannot be ignored. Despite questions regarding the measure, as described, the significant findings are helpful in understanding the complexities of professional identification. Based on an extensive search, Kreiner & Ashforth (2004) were the only researchers found, who measured need for identification. Further research on this construct needs to continue as it appears to be important in the prediction of identification.

As highlighted in research on the development of professional identification, it is important to note that identification with the profession can change. Reybold & Alamia (2008) found that with the group of faculty studied, characteristics of identity changed with time in the profession. This is an important finding and one that warrants further investigation related to the antecedents identified in the current study. Beech et al. (2008) also underline the dynamic nature of identification in general. The study highlights the comparative nature of individuals in the field of IT. IT is a profession, like others, that is sensitive to economic changes and with these changes come modifications to one’s perceptions of the profession. Therefore, it is important that future research include longitudinal studies of IT professionals to ascertain how their attachment changes over time. Additionally, examining the findings provided here along with key variables from the Reybold & Alamia (2008) study could provide additional insight into the mechanisms driving professional identification and its theoretically derived antecedents.

The results of this study add to the current body of knowledge of professional identification research and to what we know about the IT professional specifically. From a general perspective, it appears that professional identification research can be furthered by including perceived similarity to the group prototype and both individual and public perceptions of the social group examined. While many of the hypotheses related to the external profession factors were not supported in this study, findings do point to a potentially more complex and interesting explanation for the impact of public perceptions on an individual’s identification with the IT profession. Additional research should be conducted to verify these relationships and the importance of the individual’s perceptions as it appears that the impact of public perceptions on professional identification occurs through its impact on the individual. The results of this study highlight the potential importance of the individual’s perceptions to their overall identification with the profession and support the inclusion of the construct in future research on identification with social groups.

Future research into recruitment efforts may also be enhanced by the findings of this study. As we have seen, enrollment in IT-related educational programs has dropped (Panko, 2008). The result is a direct impact on the potential talent pool from which organizations can choose educated and trained IT workers. In the Computing Curricula 2005 report given by the ACM (Shackelford et al., 2006), the authors emphasized the importance of identity for each of the computing disciplines - computer science, information systems, computer engineering, and IT. In a 2007 study by Zhang, examining why students choose to major in IS, the author found that family and professors influenced student intentions to major in IS. Teachers of potential IS majors would encompass a public perception of the IT profession and be able to share that perception with their students. Therefore, this study of IT professional identity could possibly influence the decision of a student to major in IS through the influence of the parents. To counter the drops in student enrollments, another possibility is to encourage more young people to begin identifying with the IT profession during high school and thus ultimately increase student enrollments in IT-related higher education.

## Conclusion

A unique and primary driver of this study was to better understand the individual’s attachment to the IT profession through an examination of professional identification. Findings indicated that individuals in IT identify, based on three primary reasons: need for identification, individual perceptions of the profession, and perceived similarity to others in the profession. ## Table A1 Survey constructs, items, and references

General self-efficacy

Source: Sherer et al. (1982)

GSE1. I give up on things before completing them.

GSE2. If something looks too complicated, I will not even bother to try it.

GSE3. When trying to learn something new, I soon give up if I am not initially successful.

GSE4. I avoid trying to learn new things when they look too different for me.

Technical self-efficacy

Source: Adapted from Riggs et al. (1994

TSE1. I have all the technical skills needed to do well in my field.

TSE2. Most people in my field have better technical skills than I do. (R)

TSE3. I have confidence in my technical skills and abilities.

Need for professional identification

Source: Adapted from Kreiner & Ashforth (2004)

NPID1. I’d like to work in a profession where I would think of its successes and failures as being my successes and failures.

NPID2. An important part of who I am would be missing if I didn’t belong to a profession.

NPID3. Generally, I do not feel a need to identify with the profession I work in. (R)

NPID4. Generally, the more my goals, values, and beliefs overlap with those of my profession, the happier I am.

NPID5. I would rather say ‘we’ than ‘they’ when talking about the profession I work in.

Similarity to others in the profession

Source: Developed for this study

SIM1. In general, I feel that my attitudes and beliefs are similar to the typical IT professional.

SIM2. In general I consider my goals to be similar to the typical IT professional.

SIM3. I consider my work ethic to be similar to that of the typical IT professional.

SIM4. I feel that my skills and abilities are similar to the typical IT professional.

SIM5. I consider myself to be the typical IT professional.

Individual perceptions of profession

Source: Adapted from Mael & Ashforth (1992)

IPP1. I think highly of the IT profession.

IPP2. I consider it prestigious to belong to the IT profession.

IPP3. I consider the IT profession to be one of the best professions.

IPP4. I think the IT profession does not have a good reputation in the business community. (R)

IPP5. I think that people in other professions look down on the IT profession. (R)

Public perceptions of profession

Source: Adapted from Mael & Ashforth (1992)

PPP1. People in general think highly of the IT profession.

PPP2. It is considered prestigious to belong to the IT profession.

PPP3. The IT profession is considered to be one of the best professions.

PPP4. The IT profession does not have a good reputation in the business community. (R)

PPP5. A person seeking to advance should downplay their association with the IT profession. (R)

Distinctiveness of profession

Source: Developed for this study

DIST1. The background necessary to succeed in the IT profession is different than that required in any other profession.

DIST2. The IT profession requires a unique set of skills and abilities compared to other professions.

DIST3. Compared to other professions, the IT profession requires specialized training and education.

DIST4. Overall, the characteristics of the IT profession are different than any other profession.

DIST5. In general, the IT profession is unique when compared to other professions.
