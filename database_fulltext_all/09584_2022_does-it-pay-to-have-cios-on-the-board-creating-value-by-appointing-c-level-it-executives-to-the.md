# Does it Pay to Have CIOs on the Board? Creating Value by Appointing C-Level IT Executives to the Board of Directors

Nikhil R. Bandodkar,<sup>1</sup> Varun Grover<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>South Carolina State University, USA, nbandodk@scsu.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Arkansas, USA, vgrover@walton.uark.edu

## Abstract

In an increasingly digital world, C-level information technology experts (CITEs) such as CIOs and CTOs are playing an increasingly indispensable role in leading IT initiatives. Despite this, recent research shows a lack of oversight by the board of directors over the IT function due to the “IT confidence gap” of directors with no IT expertise. This has resulted in the emergence of a recent trend of appointing CITEs to the board of directors. However, there is little research on whether such appointments result in value creation for the appointing firms. We use perspectives from agency and resource dependence theories to examine two value impacts of CITE director appointments to address this question. We suggest that CITE director appointments create signaling value by enhancing the firm’s ability to signal their intention to institute more effective oversight of the IT function and subsequently accumulate value in the form of share price reaction to appointment announcements. Correspondingly, CITE directors’ human and relational capital application also creates substantive value in the form of firm financial performance over a longer duration. Using an event study and hierarchical linear modeling, our analysis of 334 CITE director appointments largely supports our contention that the human and relational capital of CITE directors and their appointment in firms facing IT-related contextual conditions results in both a positive share price reaction and better firm financial performance over the long term. We conclude that appointing CITE directors with the appropriate capital under certain contextual conditions leads to firm value creation.

Keywords: Board of Directors, Chief Information Officer, IT Corporate Governance, Firm Value, Human Capital, Relational Capital, IT-Intensity, Agency Theory, Event Study

## 1 Introduction

Information technology (IT) has had a transformational impact on businesses, as evidenced by the enormous investments made by firms in IT. According to research by Gartner (2021), technology investments are continuing to rise, generally consuming between 5% and 9% of overall corporate revenue. In 2021, Gartner estimated worldwide IT spending at over USD 4 trillion. IT management and strategic application are of prime importance for firms and can often be the deciding factor between survival and extinction. However, despite the significant role that IT plays in business, most boards of directors (BODs) have mainly remained in the dark regarding IT governance and strategy (Nolan & McFarlan, 2005). A report by PwC’s center for board governance based on a survey of directors from 860 public companies attributes this attitude to what they refer to as the “IT confidence gap.” (PwC, 2012a). Members of the BODs of public firms realize the importance of IT and IT governance to the well-being of the overall enterprise. However, they do not feel confident in their ability to make decisions involving IT investments, IT policy, and IT strategic utilization due to numerous factors such as the dynamism of IT, their lack of IT background, difficulty understanding the new competitive paradigm in the digital era, and the complexity of the IT-related information that they receive.

To fill the IT confidence gap, some firms have started appointing C-level information technology experts (CITEs), such as chief information officers (CIOs) and chief technology officers (CTOs), to their BODs. The percentage of public companies that have appointed IT-focused board members has grown between 2010 and 2019 (Mishra, 2019), with many appointing Clevel information technology expert (CITE) directors in IT domains such as security (Bart & Turel, 2010; Parent & Reich, 2009), big data (Libert, 2013), and value creation through the strategic utilization of IT (Bassellier et al., 2003; Luftman et al., 2010). CITE directors also possess IT relational capital (Hillman & Dalziel, 2003), a network of working relationships built through interactions with other IT experts while performing their job responsibilities.

While there is burgeoning academic research that has confirmed the beneficial value impacts for firms appointing new CIOs to C-level management (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Gerth & Peppard, 2014; Kettinger et al., 2011; Taylor & Vithayathil, 2018), the research on the firm value implications of CITE director appointments to the board remains scant. Thus far, a very narrow stream of research has investigated CITE board appointments under the umbrella of boardlevel IT leadership. Previous research (Yayla & Hu, 2014) has found that having a higher number of CITEs on the boards of firms in high IT-intensity industries resulted in better firm financial performance. In a similar vein, other research has found that a board’s IT competency was positively related to IT governance and financial performance (Turel et al., 2017, 2019; Zhang et al., 2016).

Although these studies make meaningful contributions to addressing our understanding of BOD involvement in IT governance, they also share a few notable limitations. First, most of these studies use an entire board as the unit of measurement to encapsulate IT awareness at the board level. While this approach is traditional and appropriate for investigating board phenomena, it does not highlight the valuable contributions of newly appointed CITE directors. Our study investigates the firm value effects following CITE director appointments using each appointment event as the unit of analysis to tide over this limitation. Second, existing research does not elucidate the characteristics of incoming CITE directors. In doing so, important questions related to the firm value contributions of CITE director appointments remain unanswered, i.e., What capital do CITE directors bring into a firm? and What is the effect of director capital on firm value? We address this gap by elucidating two types of IT-related director capital possessed by CITE directors, i.e., human capital and relational capital (Hillman & Dalziel, 2003). Third, because most studies investigate the phenomena at the board level, there is often limited attention to the CITE appointment event or the context under which CITE directors are appointed. We address this gap by hypothesizing the effect that CITE director appointments have on firm value under two specific IT-related contextual conditions, i.e., IT-related agency conditions and the industry IT-intensity of the firm. Thus, we answer the question: What is the effect of a firm’s contextual conditions on the creation of firm value following CITE director appointments?

A fourth limitation of prior research has been its inability to parse out the different types of value created by appointing CITE directors following each appointment event. The appointment of CITE directors can create long-term substantive value or short-term signaling value for the appointing firm. For instance, numerous CITE director appointment announcements (Caprioni, 2020; P. R. Newswire, 2018) suggest that such appointments give firms the ability to utilize CITE skills for improving firm competitiveness and financial performance in the long term. We refer to this long-term financial performance as substantive value. However, firms might also use CITE board appointments to enhance legitimacy and signal firm quality, producing positive share price reaction effects. For example, firms may get members of prestigious organizations to sit on the board of directors to convey competence, enhance firm image and reputation (Certo, 2003; Filatotchev & Bishop, 2002), or signal recovery from adverse events such as operational IT failures (Benaroch & Chernobai, 2017). Thus, the act of appointing CITEs to the BOD can serve as a signaling mechanism to attract and convey value to shareholders. We call this form of value garnered from signaling the right intentions as signaling value. Our study investigates whether a firm accrues both forms of value following CITE director appointments, i.e., Are there any signaling and substantive firm value outcomes of different CITE director attributes and firm contextual conditions?

Our study makes the following contributions to extant research on board-level IT leadership. First, considering the importance of IT in competitive strategy, CITE board appointments can create shortterm signaling value and substantive value over a longer duration. Evidence from this study sheds light on the type of value accrued by appointing firms. Second, empirical proof of signaling value provides evidence in opposition to the notion that CITE directors add little value to the board and thus do not merit a seat on the board of directors (Loop, 2016; PwC 2012b, p. 29). Evidence from this study highlights that

CITE directors have the necessary stores of relational and human capital to provide value at the board level. Third, our findings on substantive value provide evidence that CITE directors are increasingly involved in determining and monitoring the IT-based strategic actions of the firm. This finding provides evidence that IT governance and management are receiving significant attention at the very top (Haes & Grembergen, 2009).

To aid in the explication of the rest of this study, prevent burgeoning scope, and ensure sampling rigor, we state the following limitations to our study’s scope. First, the objective of this study is to investigate whether CITE director appointments create positive value following the appointment. This endeavor focuses on the nuances of director capital, the appointing firm’s contextual conditions, and how these impact two types of value. It is not our intention to compare the value contributions of CITE directors with non-CITE directors. Second, information technology expertise can be tacitly held by individuals in non-CITE positions. However, identifying individuals with such expertise is limited by the quality of available historical data during this study. Therefore, instead of relying on our subjective assessments to identify individuals with de facto IT expertise, we rely on the historical data to delineate, de jure, individuals who possess IT expertise by limiting this study to individuals that have formally held a CITE position.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first provide a brief overview of the literature/theory and then present our research hypotheses. Following this, we describe our research methodology, data analysis, and results. We conclude with a discussion of our results and the implications of our study.

## 2 Background Literature and Theoretical Overview

## 2.1 Background Literature

A small but essential stream of prior research in the larger IT governance domain investigates the relationship between IT leadership and firm performance. Some of this research looks at the impact that CITEs, such as CIOs and CTOs, have on firm performance through business-IT strategy alignment (Jewer & McKay, 2012; Pang, 2014; Smith et al., 2013) or specific reporting structures (Banker et al., 2011; Bradley et al., 2012). Other studies look at the characteristics, roles, attributes, and abilities of CITEs associated with successful leadership and firm value creation (Carter et al., 2011; Preston et al., 2008; Zimmermann et al., 2016). Previous literature discusses two CITE attributes. The first attribute is

CITEs’ human capital, such as their technical background and expertise in the information technology domain (Chen et al., 2010). There is a suggestion that CITEs’ human capital influences their leadership style and organizational performance (Ghawe & Brohman, 2016; Gupta et al., 2009; Kettinger et al., 2011). A second often-discussed attribute is the ability of the CITE to build relationships or their relational capital. For example, prior research suggests that CITEs must be able to build relationships with other senior members of the organization to create firm value through the utilization of IT (Carter et al., 2011; Gupta et al., 2009).

Significant prior empirical research on IT executives and firm performance is highlighted in Table 1. This literature highlights that previous empirical research on IT leadership and governance has mainly focused on IT executives in C-level positions. While these studies are instructional in highlighting the capital possessed by CITEs, empirical research investigating the value-creating potential of IT executives on the board of directors has remained sparse. However, studies investigating IT expertise on the board have not investigated the value impacts of CITE director appointments. As a result, we do not know whether firms accrue value after appointing CITE directors. Third, very few studies have expounded on the capital possessed by the board IT experts included in their studies.

## 2.2 Theory: Value-Creating Mechanisms

There is general agreement that CITEs utilize their human and relational capital to create substantive value for the firm. However, board members are usually absent from day-to-day administration and management activities (Brown & Grant, 2005; Faleye et al., 2011); therefore, we know little about the value impact of appointing CITEs to the board. The creation of substantive value from CITE appointments falls largely under the aegis of the resource-based view (RBV). According to the RBV, firms use resources and capabilities that are valuable, heterogeneously distributed, and difficult to imitate to procure extra normal firm financial returns and create competitive advantages (Barney, 2001). However, the possession of information technology alone is insufficient to create such advantages; instead, a synergistic arrangement of technological resources and human capabilities is the underlying factor that satisfies all three requirements for creating competitive advantage (Mata et al., 1995). Viewed in this manner, CITE directors bring valuable director capital to the organization in the form of relational capital (e.g., social contacts, influence, and privileged information residing in social networks) and human capital (e.g., technological knowledge, expertise, and experience).

Hillman and Dalziel (2003) highlight two mechanisms through which director capital results in substantive value. The first mechanism linking director capital to substantive value is steeped in agency theory logic and suggests that IT-related human and relational capital enhances a board’s ability to perform its monitoring and control function effectively and creates better alignment between the owner’s and agent’s interests, consequently increasing substantive value. The second mechanism derived from resource dependence theory purports that appointing directors with human and relational capital enhances a firm’s ability to access valuable resources and benefits such as advice and counsel, legitimacy, external communication channels for information, and other preferential access such as commitments and support from elements outside the firm (Hillman & Dalziel, 2003). Access to director capital and synergies between supra-organizational and intra-organizational actors gives the appointing firm a unique opportunity for substantive value creation that may be unavailable to its competitors. We lean on Hillman and Dalziel’s dual mechanisms to explain the link between appointing CITE directors and creating substantive value in our study.

Since board members are generally absent from dayto-day administration and management activities, the appointment of CITE directors to the board may also be an exercise in symbolic management and the creation of signaling value. Prior research on symbolic and impression management (Fiss & Zajac, 2006; Rodrigue et al., 2013; Westphal & Zajac, 1998) has suggested that firms often demonstrate compliance with institutional values and norms with no intention of creating substantive value subsequently by adopting highly salient and visible practices. By doing so, they convey conformity with social and stakeholder expectations while leaving the essential working machinery of the organization unchanged. According to this research, firms are interpretive systems that reduce equivocality for organizational members and relevant constituents by enacting the environment and conveying the enactment through visible and salient symbolic action. Research suggests that the need for enactment through symbolic action increases with environmental uncertainty, a need to align interests with owners (perceived lack of monitoring), coercive pressures due to outside censure, and mimetic and normative pressures caused by the prevailing practices in their industry environment.

The mechanisms underlying symbolic management are rooted in signaling theory. Under conditions of information asymmetry, outside recipients find it challenging to assess two key attributes of the signaler (Stiglitz, 2000). The first attribute refers to the characteristics of the signaling party. To mollify these questions, signalers typically signal information regarding quality by appointing prestigious board members or obtaining certifications by reputed and legitimate certifying agencies. The second attribute relates to concerns about the behavior and behavioral intentions of the signaling party. To address these concerns, firms often report financial practices and appoint expert board members (Hillman et al., 2000). By appointing experts, firms convey the intention to monitor and guide agents with an effective and expertdriven corporate governance body at the helm. Diversity of expertise on the BOD can enhance the board’s monitoring and decision-making abilities and improve firm performance. Therefore, appointing CITE directors can signal the firm’s intention to improve corporate governance of its IT function and improve firm performance.

Actions such as appointing board members can convey competence, enhance the firm’s image and reputation (Certo, 2003; Filatotchev & Bishop, 2002), and create the appearance of improved governance without improving it substantively (Westphal & Graebner, 2010; Westphal & Zajac, 1998). For boundedly rational outside shareholders interested in reducing uncertainty regarding the value of their investments, such actions diminish uncertainty regarding the firm’s practices, particularly as they relate to creating shareholder value, even if a substantial change in the essential working machinery of an organization does not follow the actions. The appointment of CITE directors, even if it is done for symbolic management, can enhance shareholder confidence in the firm resulting in a positive share price reaction. We base our signaling value hypotheses on the rationale that the appointment of CITEs can create a positive share price reaction by reducing shareholder uncertainty (Westphal & Zajac, 1998) about the firm’s ability to create value for them.

## 3 Model and Hypotheses

The research model is described in Figure 1. Below, we develop hypotheses for both substantive and signaling value based on director attributes, contextual conditions, and their interactions.

## 3.1 Director Attributes and Value

Our research includes two director attributes often described in prior governance literature. The first attribute is the presence of “human capital” (Chen et al., 2010; Coleman, 1988; Hillman & Dalziel, 2003) such as knowledge, expertise, and skills in traditional areas of business like accounting, finance, and marketing, as well as related areas such as corporate law and information technology (Ancona & Caldwell, 1988). The expertise (human capital) of area-specific expert directors in their respective traditional business areas can improve board performance by increasing its information processing capability and decreasing information asymmetry between the BOD and top management in the respective functional areas (Coleman, 1988; Hillman & Dalziel, 2003). The human capital (e.g., technological knowledge, expertise, and experience) of CITE directors can diminish the adverse effects of agency conditions in a firm by improving the monitoring and control function of the firm’s BOD. CITE human capital also improves the board’s ability to perform the resource provisioning role, including evaluating strategy (McNulty & Pettigrew, 1999) and providing advice and counsel during strategy formulation (Judge & Zeithaml, 1992; Lorsch & Young, 1990). By using their considerable stocks of IT knowledge, expertise, and experience, CITE directors are more likely to improve board involvement in the strategic decisionmaking process at three levels: approval/disapproval of a strategic decision (usually at the end of the decision process); shaping strategic decisions; and shaping the content, context and conduct of strategy (Banker et al., 2011; Kearns & Lederer, 2003; McNulty & Pettigrew, 1999).

While all CITE directors have IT-related expertise and IT human capital (Applegate & Elam, 1992; Hunter, 2010), we suggest that the human capital of directors that were in a CITE position recently is more instrumental in creating value. In contemporary knowledge economies, the pace of technological change (Joseph et al., 2010), rapid digitalization (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2012), and reorganization (Bresnahan et al., 2000) force CITE’s to rapidly update, upgrade, and overhaul their technical skills and expertise. The human capital possessed by CITEs has a shelf-life, i.e., it is perishable if not upgraded, updated, and overhauled over time. CITE directors that have more recently held a CITE position, therefore, have superior human capital. From a substantive-value creation perspective, CITE directors with recent experience have current and relevant knowledge about the latest information technologies and IT practices that can be beneficial for both, evaluating the oftencomplex technical processes and actions of IT managers as well as providing relevant advice and counsel to manages and the board alike. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1 (substantive): The pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance will be higher for firms appointing CITE directors with more recent CITE experience.

From a signaling-value creation perspective, this means that CITEs that have held a position more recently are more likely to signal up-to-date and more valuable IT human capital than those with historical CITE experience. Therefore, the appointment of CITE directors with recent CITE experience is more likely to reduce shareholder uncertainty about the board’s level of expertise in comprehending IT-related issues, reduce ambiguity about the board’s ability to provide counsel and advice, and reassure shareholders regarding the competence of the board in effectively performing the IT monitoring and control function. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2 (signaling): Share prices will react positively to CITE board appointments in instances where CITEs have recent CITE experience.

The second attribute possessed by directors is access to social networks that can aid in resource acquisition, information and knowledge gathering, and access to problem-solving expertise (Ancona & Caldwell, 1988; Forbes & Milliken, 1999). Hillman and Dalziel (2003) refer to this as relational capital. Directors with relational capital use their network ties to gather strategic information from outside to ascertain the veracity and efficacy of strategic decisions (Carpenter & Westphal, 2001). Relational capital also aids the process of raising financial capital (Mizruchi & Stearns, 1988) and forging alliances and partnerships to enact firm strategy (Haunschild & Beckman, 1998). Thus, a director’s relational capital can benefit the appointing firm by allowing it to gather slack resources and create buffers between itself and the environment.

The relational capital of CITE directors that have previous experience as CITEs or directors in high ITintensity firms is likely to be especially steeped in relationships with other CITEs, CITE directors, and other technology experts. High IT-intensity industry environments have high levels of uncertainty and often undergo rapid technological change (Schmalensee, 2000; Stuart, 2000). To counter the rapid and uncertain technological changes in high IT-intensity industries, CITEs of firms in high IT-intensity industries foster relationship building through a broad range of IT experts, including their own IT employees, contractors, vendors, and partners, as well as boundary-spanning contacts such as other CITEs or industry experts (Rothwell & Dodgson, 1991). Often, the primary objective of CITEs in high IT-intensity firms is to communicate properties of IT vis-à-vis business needs, scan the environment for emerging technologies, network with influential constituents both inside and outside the organization, and utilize relationships to create organizational slack and a buffer between the organization and its environment (Chun & Mooney, 2009; Karahanna & Preston, 2013). Because of the IT-centric focus of the position, CITEs often interact with a broad range of IT practitioners and make IT a consistent mainstay of most relationshipbuilding endeavors in firms in high IT-intensity industries. For this reason, CITEs employed in high ITintensity firms are more likely to possess more valuable IT-specific relational capital. CITEs with boardroom experience in other firms in high ITintensity industries get additional opportunities to foster IT-specific relational capital by building relations with a variety of external constituents such as alliance and partnering firms, software and hardware developers, cutting-edge technology venture firms, IT/IS service providers, licensors, experts in the field of information technology, and potential IT service/product consumers.

By appointing CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital, the appointing firm board can obtain critical IT know-how (Kale et al., 2000), co-create complementary IT skills (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven, 1996), gain access to new markets by making strategic use of IT (Grandori & Soda, 1995), and obtain access and information about innovative IT practices and technologies that others have developed or adopted (Biemans, 1991). Therefore, we expect the appointment of CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital to be beneficial for creating substantive value. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3 (substantive): The pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance will be higher for firms appointing CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital.

From a signaling perspective, directors with ITspecific relational capital have a corresponding cache of information about their individual reputations that their technology-savvy peers can access, allowing shareholders to derive expectations about the appointed director’s future behavior. In previous literature on individual reputation, there is broad acceptance that individual reputations provide a yardstick against which an actor’s capabilities can be evaluated, which is valuable for reducing technical uncertainty, i.e., uncertainty regarding an appointee’s capability or quality (Rindova et al., 2005; Washington & Zajac, 2005). CITE directors who forge their reputations through C-level employment or directorship in high IT-intensity firms and thus have IT-specific relational capital will likely provide a better yardstick to evaluate their capabilities and reduce shareholder technical uncertainty. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4 (signaling): Share prices will react positively to CITE board appointments for those CITEs with IT-specific relational capital.

## 3.2 Contextual Conditions and Value

Recently, there has been more research on the board’s role in monitoring the IT function and involvement in IT-related strategic decision-making. Studies investigating these phenomena have examined the links between contextual conditions such as the IT competitive environment (Turel & Bart, 2014) and the prevalent agency conditions in a firm (Yayla & Hu, 2014), as well as the propensity of its board to be involved in IT governance. We extend the findings of these studies by explicating the effect of CITE director appointments on firm value under the same contextual conditions, i.e., the IT-related agency condition of the firm and the industry IT-intensity of the firm.

A firm has IT-related agency conditions when none of the board members have IT expertise. Prior studies suggest that in the absence of relevant knowledge, expertise, and experience, directors serving on the board exercise lower levels of involvement and monitoring (Carpenter & Westphal, 2001; Huff et al., 2006; Peppard et al., 2011). The board’s lack of IT knowledge and expertise creates an intellectual constraint that exacerbates the information asymmetry between directors and IT management and hampers its ability to effectively monitor and control the IT function. The lack of IT expertise on the board also exacerbates its “IT confidence gap” (Cloyd, 2013).

The addition of a CITE director to the board can reduce agency problems in the following ways. First, a CITE board director can increase the information technology intellectual quotient of the board by serving as an interpreter and translator of information technology concepts and jargon. By increasing board awareness about the role of information technology in achieving business objectives and competitive aspirations, CITE board appointments can decrease the board’s ITconfidence gap and increase board participation in ITrelated issues. Greater board participation and awareness of IT issues can reduce the board’s ITrelated information asymmetry.

Second, under conditions of information asymmetry, boards without IT expertise often rely on executive compensation as a mechanism to align the executive’s goals with those of the principal (e.g., shareholder) (Tosi & Gomez-Mejia, 1989). However, without an accurate understanding of the risks associated with ITdriven outcomes, outcome-based compensation mechanisms may transfer too much or too little risk to the agents. Transferring too much risk to executive compensation can make executives risk-averse (Wiseman & Gomez-Mejia, 1998) and unwilling to engage in innovative, often riskier IT projects and ITdriven strategic action. In contrast, apportioning minimal risk can encourage executives to shirk their duties or invest more in diversifying activities and pet projects that are not in shareholders’ interests (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997). In such instances, the appointment of CITE directors can be instrumental in establishing appropriate levels of IT executive compensation, increasing principal-agent goal alignment, and improving the board’s control function.

Third, the presence of a CITE board director also makes it possible to use an additional control mechanism—that of evaluating agent behavior on programmable tasks. Programmable tasks are tasks for which appropriate task behavior by the agent can be specified in advance (Eisenhardt, 1989). CITE directors can evaluate and monitor IT executive action by specifying appropriate behaviors for critical tasks. These include IT executive behaviors that provide assurances for database integrity, the maintenance of software update schedules and security logs, and the institution of appropriate protocols to detect and address information and network security threats. By providing a reasonable grasp of information technology concepts (Turel & Bart, 2014), the addition of CITE directors can prevent the board from flying blind on IT-related strategic issues (Carter & Lorsch, 2003) and help it effectively perform IT monitoring and control functions (Yayla & Hu, 2014). Therefore, CITE director appointments can create substantive value for the appointing firm. We thus hypothesize:

## H5 (substantive): The pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance will be higher for firms that do not have CITE directors serving on the board a priori

From a signaling perspective, the absence of IT expertise on the board can increase shareholder uncertainty regarding oversight of the IT operations in the firm. IT has become increasingly salient in the competitive strategies of many firms; however, even for those firms that do not use IT strategically but rather in a supporting role, the cost of neglecting the monitoring and control over the IT function can be catastrophic. Therefore, the absence of IT expertise on the board can be a source of significant uncertainty for shareholders. The appointment of CITE directors to boards having IT-related agency conditions can therefore serve as a reassuring signal to shareholders that reduces their ambiguity about the appropriate management of the IT function in the firm. Thus, we hypothesize:

H6 (signaling): Share prices for firms that do not currently have a CITE serving on the board will react positively to CITE board appointments.

The second contextual condition, i.e., IT-intensity in the firm’s industry, refers to the challenges a firm faces because of the level of IT use for competition in the firm’s industry. Industries driven by high information technology use often undergo rapid technological change (Schmalensee, 2000), high frequency of innovation (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997), and unpredictable variation in revenues, market shares, and firm profits (Baldwin & Clark, 2000; Shapiro & Varian, 1999) due to a high rate of entry and exit by competitors, product innovation, imitation (Giarratana, 2004), and obsolescence (Sorensen & Stuart, 2000). Research suggests that in high IT-intensity industries, a firm’s IT strategy leverages, shapes, and positions business strategy and is critical for firm competitiveness and survival (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). In such industries, firms that use IT in a disruptive and transformative fashion redefine industry practices and position themselves more favorably within an industry by accruing sustainable returns (Chatterjee et al., 2001). The industry IT-intensity dictates how critical it is to manage and strategically use a firm’s IT functions and assets. It also provides opportunities for differential positive performance payoffs from the heterogeneous use of the IT function and assets.

However, in highly dynamic and uncertain environments, acquiring information, knowledge, and resources is also a key imperative of firms (Stuart, 2000). Firms constantly strive to reduce the uncertainty associated with their environments by acquiring critical information, knowledge, and resources from their environment (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) to create buffers that protect them from the adverse impacts of a changing environment. The characteristics of a firm’s industry can pose unique challenges for its BODs. For example, BODs of firms in high IT-intensity industries face significant information processing challenges because of the complexity and dynamism of the ITrelated information they must process as part of their monitoring and control duties. Likewise, IT strategy formulation and IT-related resource acquisition are critical for competition and survival in high ITintensity industries. In order to be effective, BODs of firms in such industries must also be able to perform their IT resource provisioning role. The appointment of CITE directors, with their accompanying assortment of IT-related human and relational capital, benefits firm management, and strategic use of the IT function is more likely to create substantive value in high ITintensity industries because of the higher ceiling for IT-driven value creation, compared to low IT-intensity industries. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H7 (substantive): The pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance will be higher for firms that appoint CITE directors in high ITintensity industries.

The industry IT-intensity also indicates the extent of uncertainty and ambiguity residing in the firm’s competitive environment. The appointment of CITE directors in such industries signals the availability of human and relational capital that can alleviate the informational and resource uncertainty faced by the appointing firm. The resultant access to information and resources reassures shareholders and enhances their confidence in the IT-related value-creation capacity of the appointing firm. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H8 (signaling): Share prices for firms in high ITintensity industries will react positively to CITE board appointments.

## 3.3 Interacting Effect of Director Attributes and Industry IT-Intensity on Value

High IT-intensity industries are characterized by rapid technological change (environmental dynamism) and hypercompetition (K. Kim et al., 2016; Stuart, 2000). Environmental dynamism and hypercompetition increase the complexity and equivocality of the available information that board directors have for decision-making. In such environments, the addition of directors with recent CITE experience can improve the board’s ability to perform its monitoring/control and resource provisioning roles because such directors possess more state-of-the-art IT expertise and knowledge. From an agency theory perspective, the addition of CITEs with recent experience reduces information asymmetry and improves the monitoring efforts of the board (Gillan & Starks, 2003), leading to less opportunistic and self-interested behavior by agents. CITE directors with recent experience also enhance the board’s ability to process complex IT information, and strengthen the control mechanisms employed by the board (Bart & Turel, 2010; Hillman & Dalziel, 2003). As a result, firms that appoint such directors are less likely to employ mismatched governance structures and compensation strategies and more likely to improve the effective management and strategic use of the IT function (Baysinger & Butler, 1985). Because the alternative of mismatched governance and control structures can adversely affect firm performance (Cai et al., 2015), especially for firms in high IT-intensity industries, the appointment of CITE directors with recent experience can result in substantive value. From a resource provisioning perspective, the addition of such directors can enhance the board’s ability to perform its strategy evaluation and counseling functions, further increasing the potential for differential value creation using the firm’s IT function. Thus, for firms in high IT-intensity industries, the appointment of CITE directors with more recent CITE experience leads to better substantive value. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H9 (substantive): The relationship between the recency of the director’s CITE experience and pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance is moderated by the industry ITintensity of the appointing firm such that it will be strongly positive for firms in high IT-intensity industries.

The appointment of CITE directors with recent CITE experience can reduce shareholder uncertainty about the availability of IT expertise through providing the most up-to-date IT knowledge at the board level; it can also reduce the corresponding equivocality regarding the performance of the IT monitoring, control, and resource provisioning functions at a high level. At the same time, the addition of directors with recent experience can reduce shareholder uncertainty regarding the availability of information processing resources to evaluate complex and critical IT-related decisions and provide astute advice and counsel in contemporary digital economies for firms in high ITintensity industries. The multiplicative effect of reducing such uncertainties will likely result in a positive share price reaction when CITE directors with recent experience are appointed to firms in high ITintensity industries. Thus, we hypothesize:

H10 (signaling): The increase in share price following the appointment of a director with recent CITE experience will be higher for firms in high ITintensity industries than for appointments in other industries.

In high IT-intensity industries, IT creates environmental dynamism and hypercompetition, resulting in higher environmental uncertainty. Therefore, the value of directors who can reduce this uncertainty increases in high IT-intensity industries (Hillman et al., 2000). The appointment of CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital can benefit firms in high IT-intensity industries in two ways. First, the director’s IT-specific relational capital may be helpful for obtaining critical know-how (Kale et al., 2000) and acquiring privileged internal information about innovative practices and technologies that others have developed or adopted (Biemans, 1991). Such information can reduce uncertainty about the causeand-effect relationship of future technological actions. Second, the director’s IT-specific relational capital can be beneficial for attracting early adopters and partners of the firm’s technology-driven products and services. Such actions can effectuate the achievement of intended outcomes, ensure the heterogeneity of competitive actions, and enhance firm performance. Additionally, CITE directors can also gain access to additional perspectives from their boundary-spanning expert peers by using their IT-specific relational channels. This information and knowledge can reduce the complexity of board decision-making and improve the board’s resource provisioning function, increasing the likelihood of effective management and strategic use of its IT function. Because the ceiling for deriving value from the appointed CITE director’s relational capital is much higher in high IT-intensity firms, we hypothesize:

H11 (substantive): The relationship between the CITE director’s IT-specific relational capital and pre- to post-appointment change in firm performance will be moderated by the industry IT-intensity of the appointing firm such that it will be strongly positive for firms in high ITintensity industries.

Firms in high IT-intensity industries often use IT offensively to gain a competitive advantage. Using IT offensively (Nolan & McFarlan, 2005) often involves IT projects that are ambitious and risky with high resource requirements. Under such circumstances, firms must not only ensure the reliability of their dayto-day IT function but also avoid surprises emanating from internal sources such as legacy systems or external sources such as IT-related legal intellectual property disputes or information technology-based competitive threats. In high IT-intensity industries, these threats represent significant sources of uncertainty for shareholders. The appointment of CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital can allay shareholder concerns regarding the availability of expert guidance and oversight during both routine and ambitious IT projects and, via the CITE directors’ relational networks, can also fulfill shareholder demands for boundary-spanning experts capable of handling legal or competitive surprises. Thus, perceptions of lowered uncertainty and risk will likely manifest as a positive share price reaction following the appointment of CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital. We therefore hypothesize:

H12 (signaling): The increase in share price following the appointment of a director with IT-specific relational capital will be higher for firms in high IT-intensity industries than for firms in other industries.

## 4 Methodology

To test our hypotheses on substantive value creation through CITE director appointments, we used hierarchical linear modeling to test a repeated measures growth model to estimate the effect of our predictors on substantive value growth. The signaling value effect of CITE director appointments was measured using event study methodology. This methodology is commonly used in accounting, finance, and management studies to measure the impact of corporate control changes (McWilliams & Siegel, 1997), to evaluate the impact of information technology investments on the market value of the firm (Im et al., 2001), and to examine shareholder reaction to announcements of newly created CIO positions (Chatterjee et al., 2001).

## 4.1 Sample of Firms and Announcements

The sample for this study consists of all publicly traded US firms that appointed a board director with prior or concurrent experience as chief information officer (CIO) or chief technology officer (CTO) between the years 2004 and 2019, inclusive. CIOs and CTOs are included in our sample because they are often the most senior IT executives in an organization and have an educational and professional background in IT (Drechsler et al., 2019). In contrast, emerging positions such as the chief digital officer often do not have functional IT responsibility (Singh & Hess, 2017) and may report to other roles such as CIOs/CTOs, chief marketing officers, or chief innovation officers (Berman et al., 2020). We did not include chief data officers in this study because they primarily focus on the limited organizational capability of big data (Lee et al., 2014). We included only publicly traded US firms because our measures rely on share price data and data from proxy statements, 10K filings, and annual reports that are uniformly reported and publicly available. Information on director appointments and appointment dates was obtained from the BoardEx database. We retained appointments with ancillary data for independent and control variables in Mergent Online, COMPUSTAT, or Thomson Reuters databases in our study, and used sampling criteria established in previous research for our event study (MacKinlay, 1997; McWilliams & Siegel, 1997). We excluded CITE appointment announcements that were confounded by other informational announcements regarding, for example, dividends, earnings, and mergers/acquisition; capital events such as stock splits and structural changes; legal suits brought by or against the firm; executive changes; joint ventures; and multiple director appointments if these announcements occurred at least 10 days before or after the event date. We discarded announcements involving board turnover in a three-month period before the CITE appointment announcement to eliminate preanticipated CITE director appointment announcements. Descriptive statistics on our sample firms are provided in Appendix A and a detailed listing of the firms is in Appendix B.

## 4.2 Measures

## 4.2.1 Substantive Value

Substantive value is measured as the sum of normalized return on equity (ROE), return on assets (ROA), and return on sales (ROS) (Staw & Epstein, 2000; Wang, 2010). Substantive value was measured at three instances. The first instance (t + 0) was during the year immediately preceding the CITE director appointment. We used the appointment date instead of the announcement date to establish the periods. The second instance was one year immediately following the appointment date (t + 1), and the third was two years after (t + 2). We used the sum of normalized ROA, ROE, and ROS at each instance as our measure. The use of accounting measures gives us two advantages (Thanos & Papadakis, 2012). First, accounting-based measures of performance measure realized performance as reported in the financial statements of firms. Second, the utilization of multiple accounting measures provides a more well-rounded view of organizational performance. For example, high values for our measure based on the normalized sum of ROA, ROE, and ROS would indicate a firm that is both highly profitable and efficient.

## 4.2.2 Signaling Value: Average Cumulative Abnormal Returns (ACAR)

The signaling value effect of CITE board appointments was measured using an event study. Using an event study to measure the signaling value of CITE appointments is appropriate because it is a tool that assesses the specific financial impact of specific changes in corporate governance and policy changes. Announcements of CITE appointments serve to convey value to shareholders, which provokes the set of hypothesized responses by shareholders. This response may occur as immediately as the day of the announcement. The event study method makes it possible to determine whether and how investors responded to the firm’s signal of appointing a CITE director by measuring the abnormal price reaction. This method can be used to obtain specific insights that may be relevant to the firm’s efforts in stakeholder management (Janney & Folta, 2003; Jones & Murrell, 2001).

To measure the effect of CITE director appointment announcements on the announcing firms’ share prices, we used an event window of two days, which included the day of the announcement and the day before the announcement day (Im et al., 2001; McWilliams & Siegel, 1997). A two-day event window ensured that the share price reaction of an announcement was captured effectively. For instance, if an announcement occurred after the close of trading, it would have been reported in the media the following day, at which point any immediate reaction to this new information would be reflected in the share price on the day the announcement appears in print. In contrast, if the announcement was made before the close of trading for the day, the stock market reaction would have occurred on the same day; however, the news of the announcement would have only appeared in print the following day. Therefore, the day on which the announcement appeared in print (day 0) and the previous day (day -1) were both included in the event window (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Im et al., 2001), giving us an event window of two days.

We estimated the firm’s expected returns using the Fama and French three-factor asset pricing model, which includes the market risk factor and two additional risk factors, i.e., size risk and book-tomarket value risk. The three-factor Fama and French model has significantly greater explanatory power than the one-factor capital asset pricing model (Gaunt, 2004). A 200-day estimation window (Im et al., 2001; Santos et al., 1993) with a gap of 50 days between the estimation and event windows was used. Finally, we assessed the statistical significance of the event on the market value of the sample firms using average cumulative abnormal return (ACAR) and the corresponding Patell Z-statistic (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Han et al., 2012). More detail on the event study method is provided in Appendix C.

## 4.3 Independent Variables

An overview of the major constructs used in this study is provided in Table 2.

IT-Intensity of the industry: The industry IT-intensity of a firm was calculated using the ratio of IT capital expenditure to the total expenditure of that industry (Kim & Brynjolfsson, 2009). We used IT capital spending data published in the Tangible Wealth Survey by the US Bureau of Economic Analysis to gather our data. The survey summarizes capital spending in several information technology categories for each major industry in the United States. Information technology categories in the survey include capital expenditures for hardware (servers, personal computers, direct access storage devices, terminals, tape drives, other storage devices, databases, and integrated systems), software (prepackaged, customdesigned, and in-house software development), and communication equipment. To construct the measure, we calculated the ratio of IT capital expenditure over the total expenditure in each industry for every reported year. Using these ratios, we then created tertiles to create three groups. The group with the highest ratio of IT capital expenditures (top tertile) was categorized as a high IT-intensity industry and coded as 3. In contrast, the group with the lowest ratio of IT capital expenditures (bottom tertile) was categorized as a low IT-intensity industry and coded as 1. A table with our categorization is provided in Appendix D.

IT-Related Agency condition: An appointing firm has IT-related agency conditions when none of the board members have previous experience as a CIO or CTO.<sup>1</sup> IT-related agency condition is a binary variable that was coded as 1 when the appointing firm’s board had no other directors with previous CITE experience. The IT-related agency condition variable was coded as 0 when the appointing firm’s board had at least one other CITE director at the time of announcement. Data for board composition and the director’s historical professional experience was collected from the BoardEx database.

Director capital: The recency of a director’s CITE experience is a binary variable that was coded as recent, or 1, if the appointed director concurrently held a CITE position or held a CITE position in the two years <sup>2</sup> prior to being appointed as a director. The recency of the CITE experience variable was otherwise coded as 0. A director’s IT-specific relational capital is also a binary variable that was coded as 1 if the appointed director was a CITE and had previous boardroom or CITE experience in a high IT-intensity industry firm and 0 otherwise.

## 4.4 Control Variables

Advertising, R&D, and SG&A spending can have a positive and consistent influence on future firm performance (Chauvin & Hirschey, 1993; Ho et al., 2005; Lévesque et al., 2012) and were included as control variables. A negative relationship between board size and firm performance has been noted (Ferris et al., 2003; Mak & Li, 2001); thus, board size, measured as the numerical count of board members, was included as a control. Firm size, operationalized as the natural logarithm of the number of employees in a firm, was included as a control related to firm performance (Bharadwaj et al., 1999; Gabrielsson & Huse, 2002). Previous board governance literature suggests that the presence of board-level IT committees (Price & Lankton, 2017), board independence (Hillman & Dalziel, 2003), and percentage of shares held by institutional shareholders (Mehran, 1995) significantly impacts the efficacy of board action in overseeing firm performance and were therefore included as controls.

The appointment of outside directors can be positively related to firm financial performance (Ezzamel & Watson, 1993). We included a binary variable named director independence to record whether the appointed CITE director was an independent (1) or nonindependent (0) director. We included ACAR as a control variable in the substantive value analysis to control the possible correlations between our dependent variables, i.e., ACAR and substantive value. Previous research has suggested that CIOs operate more strategically, whereas CTOs operate in a more operational capacity (Beatty et al., 2005). To control for any systematic value effects arising from the CITE director’s previous experience, we included a dummycoded variable called CIO or CTO, which was coded as 1 for CIOs and 0 for CTOs.

## 5 Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Substantive Value Creation

Our sample consisted of 282 US firms that appointed CITE directors to the board between 2004 and 2017 (inclusive). Since our dependent variable was measured over the two years following CITE appointment, we only included appointing firms where the director was retained for at least two years after appointment. We used hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) to analyze the repeated-measures mixed-effect model using a maximum likelihood (ML) estimation with substantive value as the dependent variable measured over three time periods.<sup>3</sup> We conducted the analysis using HLM 7.0. One advantage of this approach is the ability to examine the influence of higher-level variables (e.g., Level 2 IVs) on lowerlevel variables either through direct effects on intercepts or through cross-level moderation of slopes (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). We conducted robustness checks before conducting the HLM analysis, as suggested by Raudenbush and Bryk (2002) to ensure that our model met the data assumptions for HLM analysis and demonstrated an acceptable fit for our data. HLM robustness checks and model fit statistics are described in Appendix E.

## 5.2 Result of Analysis

Using HLM, we modeled substantive value change over time. We examined the effects of covariates (i.e., recency of CITE experience, presence of IT-specific relational capital, agency condition, and industry ITintensity) on group differences in the rate of growth or decline of substantive value. Several Level 1 and Level 2 independent variables (IVs) were included in the analysis. All variables were grand mean-centered to prevent multicollinearity. Level 1 IVs included year, percent block holders, annual advertising expenditure, annual R&D expenditure, board size, and firm size. Level 2 IVs included director independence, cumulative abnormal returns, presence of IT committee, and board independence. The last two variables, i.e., presence of IT committee and board independence, were aggregated to Level 2 variables from their original Level 1 measurement because of very low within-group variance (ICC2 > 0.98) and a high level of agreement $( \mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { w g } } > 0 . 9 5 )$ for both variables (LeBreton et al., 2005; LeBreton & Senter, 2008; Tabachnik & Fidell, 2007). Other Level

2 IVs include recency of CITE experience, presence of IT-specific relational capital, agency condition, and industry IT-intensity. Interactions between Level 1 and Level 2 variables were also included in subsequent models to test our main hypotheses.

Since our study used HLM, it was essential to determine a null (or baseline) model for the dependent variable to determine how much variance resided within and between groups. Furthermore, the null model (Model 1) serves as a baseline for further analyses. To establish the null model, we ran an intercept-only mixed model analysis. This analysis indicates that 97.5% of the total variance resided between firms and only 2.5% of the total variance resided within firms, further supporting a mixed model approach. All Level 1 (except year) and Level 2 IVs were centered around the overall mean. The year variable was set such that a year value of zero equated to firm performance before the appointment for all appointing firms. Following Model 1, the predictors were entered into the model in a blockwise hierarchical fashion to determine the incremental prediction of substantive value after adding each predictor group (cf. ## 6 Discussion of Results

A summary of the support for the hypotheses in this study is shown in Table 8. Overall, we found support for both signaling value and substantive value following CITE director appointments and found that value creation is contingent on the director’s capital and the contextual conditions of the firm. Our hypotheses are undergirded by logic from resourcebased view/resource provisioning theory, agency theory, and theories on symbolic management and signaling theory. The result of our analysis is largely commensurate with the logic of these theories. We note possible discrepancies and deviations from our reference theories, as evidenced by the lack of support for some of our hypotheses in the following paragraphs.

## 6.1 Director Human Capital and Value

Does the capital of an incoming director have any bearing on firm value? We found some evidence that it does. Incoming directors have human capital, measured in this study as the recency of their CITE experience, and IT-specific relational capital, measured in this study as whether or not a CITE director had previous experience as an executive or board member in a high IT-intensity firm. We hypothesized that directors with recent CITE experience have current knowledge about IT and IT issues; ergo, they have superior human capital capable of creating more substantive value.

We found statistically significant and steeper growth in a firm’s substantive value over the two years following the appointment of such directors (H1). The corresponding growth in substantive value for directors with historical CITE experience was also statistically significant but much slower over the two years. This result highlights the importance of appointing CITE directors who keep up with the everchanging technologies and competitive paradigms of digital IT (King, 2012).

The share price reaction to the appointment of directors with recent CITE experience tells an entirely different story. In contrast to our hypothesis (H2), there was no statistically positive share price reaction to the appointment of directors with recent CITE experience, indicating an absence of signaling value from such appointment announcements. This result was unexpected, and we posit the following explanations. First, a director with recent CITE experience by our measure is one that is concurrently working as a CITE or has only been recently removed (less than two years ago) from CITE responsibilities. As a result, one possible explanation for the lack of signaling value is that recent CITEs may have not had adequate time to establish a cache of legitimacy and reputation to reassure shareholders with technical uncertainty. This reasoning is in line with the logic underlying theories on symbolic action and impression management (Fiss & Zajac, 2006; Rodrigue et al., 2013), which values the cultivation of symbolic markers such as the director’s cache of reputation and legitimacy. It is likely that the directorial credentials of recent CITEs are nascent and therefore may not be well known, certified, or accredited, resulting in a weak response to such appointments by shareholders. Also, according to RBV/resource provisioning theories, the reaction to CITE director appointments may be moderated by the normative acceptance of IT as a value creator in the appointing firm’s industry. Investors in specific industries (e.g., high IT-intensity industries) are more likely to realize the importance of IT oversight and its related potential for value creation in their industries and place a premium value on the appointment of CITE directors with more current technical knowledge in such industries. Support for the latter hypotheses involving the recency of CITE experience and ITintensity of the appointing firm’s industry corroborates this explanation.

## 6.2 IT-Specific Relational Capital and Value

We found statistical support for our hypotheses relating to the presence of IT-specific relational capital and substantive (H3) and signaling value (H4). The share price reaction was positive for firms appointing directors with IT-specific relational capital but not for appointments without such relational capital. This supports our hypothesis that IT-specific relational capital is a valued commodity among directorial appointments and possesses significant signaling value. However, since we posited that CITE directors engage more extensively with the IT function and therefore possess more IT-specific access to resources, knowledge, and information when appointed to boardrooms in firms in high IT-intensity industries, we conducted a post hoc analysis to distill this key distinction. In our post hoc analysis, we analyzed the share price reaction to the appointment of non-CITE directors that possessed prior boardroom experience in high IT-intensity firms. We compiled data on appointment announcements of non-CITE directors by the same firms over the same period that had prior boardroom experience in high IT-intensity firms.<sup>4</sup> We found a total of 84 announcements that met our criteria. The share price reaction to the appointment of non-CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital was positive but statistically insignificant (ACAR = 0.073%, Z-statistic = 0.65, p > 0.25, n.s.).

A CITE’s IT expertise and knowledge facilitate knowledge transfer and relationship-building with other IT experts with similar backgrounds (McPherson et al., 2001), making the IT-specific relational capital possessed by CITE directors unique and different from that of non-CITE directors. Because of their IT knowledge, the interpretation of IT functional area issues, situations, and solutions that flow through these IT-tinged relational channels are more likely to be sagaciously processed by directors with CITE experience than those without CITE experience. The result of our primary and post hoc analysis highlights the following key take-away points. First, the primary event study analysis indicates that shareholders are cognizant of the IT-specific relational capital possessed by CITE directors with experience in high IT-intensity firms. They also recognize that those in low and medium IT-intensity firms may not possess superior IT-specific relational capital. Second, shareholders value this unique relational capital as a legitimate signal of the technical capabilities of the CITE director, further assuaging their technical uncertainty. Third, based on the post hoc analysis, we indicate that the technical uncertainty of shareholders is not placated through the appointment of non-CITE directors with experience in high IT-intensity environments. Therefore, there is value in combining IT expertise with IT-specific relationship building, and this IT-specific relational capital accrued by CITEs in high IT-intensity industries is more valuable to shareholders. CITEs are also likely to possess numerous years of formal technical education preceding their ascendancy to the CITE position (Mazzola et al., 2017). In line with the logic of the RBV, the IT-specific relational capital of CITEs combined with their technical education, expertise, and leadership, creates an inimitable resource that enables the accrual of signaling and substantive value.

## 6.3 IT-Based Agency Conditions and Value

Share price reaction to the appointment of CITE directors in firms that did not already have CITEs on their board was significantly positive (H6). Firms that already had a CITE on their board did not demonstrate a positive share price reaction to new CITE director appointments, indicating that shareholders view CITE director appointments as more valuable when such expertise is completely missing from incumbent boards. Subsequent appointments of CITE directors did not appear to have a similar positive reaction on the shareholders of the firms in our sample. We proposed that CITE appointments reduced shareholder ambiguity regarding the quality of IT monitoring and control as a primary explanation for the positive valuation, and the result of our data analysis essentially confirms our explanation.

The appointment of CITE directors in firms with ITbased agency conditions improved substantive value (H5). Overall, the substantive value grew over the two years following appointment for firms with IT-related agency conditions, suggesting that adding CITE expertise to boards that did not already have CITE directors decreased the board’s “IT-confidence gap” and improved the firm performance. We also conducted post hoc analyses to study interaction effects between agency conditions and director capital (Agency x Recency of CITE experience, Agency x ITspecific relational capital) and between agency and industry IT-intensity (Agency x IT-intensity) but found no significant interaction effects. We conclude that the IT-related agency condition mitigating the effect of appointing CITE directors is broadly consistent across industries and levels of director capital. This finding supports the agency theory (Eisenhardt, 1989; Hillman & Dalziel, 2003; Yayla & Hu, 2008) rationale undergirding our hypotheses.

## 6.4 IT-Intensity of Industry and Value

Surprisingly, we did not find statistically significant results for our hypotheses relating the industry ITintensity of the appointing firm and substantive (H7) and signaling (H8) value. Here, we argued that the ability of a CITE director to provide IT expertise, guidance, and IT-related resources would result in value creation in high IT-intensity industry environments, as these environments would value access to such inimitable resources. This assertion has precedence in prior research on IT governance (Turel et al., 2017; Turel & Bart, 2014; Yayla & Hu, 2014), relying on RBV and resource provisioning logic. While this result is surprising, we note the following two points. First, prior literature on IT governance has analyzed the firm-value effects of boards with CITE directors who have also been continuing and often long-standing members on the board.

In contrast, our study analyzes new CITE director appointments. In doing so, we forgo the possibility that CITE directors’ activation of resource provisioning might have an initial gestation period or rely on motivational factors not considered in this study (Dalziel et al., 2011). This gestation period could explain the nonsignificance of hypothesized substantive value growth following CITE director appointments in the high IT-intensity industry environments observed in our study. Along this line of thought, more theorizing is needed to understand the factors that motivate CITE directors to activate their resource provisioning roles. Second, it should be noted that both hypotheses analyzed the value effect of CITE appointments in our industry categories without paying any cognizance to the attributes of the appointed CITE director. Subsequent moderation analysis found significant differential value effects in the presence of specific director attributes in different industry categories, particularly for signaling value. This indicates that the nuanced signaling of director attributes might be a critical factor in accruing signaling value from CITE director appointments, which is an assessment within the purview of signaling theory (Connelly et al., 2011). However, more research is required to elucidate other director attributes that might influence substantive and signaling value.

## 6.5 Value Effect of Director Human Capital in High IT-Intensity Industries

In high IT-intensity industries, we found that the appointment of CITE directors with concurrent or more recent experience in CIO or CTO positions was more instrumental for creating substantive value than the appointment of CITE directors with historical experience (H9). High IT-intensity firms that appointed CITE directors with recent or concurrent experience displayed higher substantive value growth than firms in moderate and low IT-intensity industries. This result indicates that the state-of-the-art IT knowledge possessed by directors enables them to perform their monitoring, control, and resource provisioning roles more effectively. Also, the value impacts of performing such roles more effectively at the board level have significantly large payoffs in substantive value in high IT-intensity industries.

We found a significant positive share price reaction to appointment announcements of directors with recent CITE experience in high IT-intensity industries. In contrast, the appointment of directors with historical CITE experience produced a more minor and statistically insignificant positive share price reaction (H10). Share price reaction to appointments in low and moderate IT-intensity industries was not significant. Our results suggest that the appointment of CITE directors with more recent technological skills and knowledge reduces shareholder uncertainty regarding the availability of information processing resources at the board level to reduce decisional complexity and improve the understanding of complex technological concepts and the business implications of new information technologies. Additionally, such appointments also increase shareholder confidence in the board’s ability to perform IT governance and resource provisioning activities. This result adds intricacy to our findings for H2, where overall, we found no share price reaction to the appointment of CITE directors with recent CITE experience. Instead, we conclude that the recency of a director’s CITE experience, thus their more current technical knowledge and skills, are quite valuable in high IT-intensity industries in terms of assuaging shareholder uncertainty.

## 6.6 Value Effect of Director’s IT-Specific Relational Capital in High IT-Intensity Industries

The results of the analysis of the interaction between relational capital and IT-intensity (H11) were not statistically significant in terms of creating substantive value, suggesting that substantive value change following such appointments is similar across all three industry categories. Lack of support for H11 indicates that the IT-specific relational capital of an appointed director results in broadly similar substantive value increases across industry categories. One possible explanation for this homogeneity in substantive value is that the IT-specific relational capital of CITE directors is being utilized to address operational IT issues in low and moderate IT-intensity firms and strategic issues in high IT-intensity firms. As a result, input by appointed CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital positively affects substantive value but for slightly different reasons across our three industry categories.

Share price reaction to appointment announcements of CITE directors with IT-specific relational capital was found to be statistically significant (H12). However, similar announcements in low and moderate ITintensity firms produced a statistically nonsignificant share price reaction. This result supports our hypothesis that CITE directors with access to social resources are highly valued in high IT-intensity industries, as such access reduces shareholder concerns about the availability of expert guidance. The appointments may also be instrumental in allaying shareholder fears about potentially being blindsided by IT-related legal or technological issues by making available a network of external relationships that can provide expertise if such eventualities arise.

## 6.7 CITE Director Appointments, Symbolic Management, and IT Governance

We tie our results back to the literature on signaling and symbolic management (Fiss & Zajac, 2006; Westphal & Zajac, 1998). Often, in instances where board appointments are used for symbolic management of shareholder expectations, a decoupling effect between signaling value and shareholder value exists, wherein announcements have a significant effect on creating signaling value but do not result in any substantive actions or subsequent substantive value (Fiss & Zajac, 2006). We find that one instance of such decoupling, i.e., the appointment of CITEs with IT-specific relational capital, had a moderating effect on signaling value in high IT-intensity firms (H12), but there was no such moderating effect on substantive value (H11). This decoupling suggests that while the appointment of CITEs with IT-specific relational capital did not create significantly more substantive value in firms in high IT-intensity industries, the appointed CITEs still had the necessary capital for the symbolic management of shareholders of firms in high IT-intensity industries.

Except for this one result, it should be noted that most appointments that created signaling value also created substantive value indicating that CITE directorappointing firms in our sample “walked the talk.” Therefore, it appears that simply acquiescing to norms and shareholder expectations and adding CITE directors to the board is not enough. Instead, most boards in our sample followed through with substantive action and represented a net positive to the IT-governance endeavors of the appointing firms, as demonstrated by the substantive value following CITE director appointments. However, the complexity of the IT function should make symbolic management the de jure form of shareholder management. Under conditions of complexity, “investors are intendedly but boundedly rational information processors who are interested in reducing uncertainty and therefore value socially legitimate indications that agency problems are being addressed” (Westphal & Zajac, 1998, p. 131). Nevertheless, the repercussions of failed IT operations and projects can be embarrassing and financially devastating, making reliance on symbolic management alone very risky. Consequently, as far as CITE director appointments are concerned, there is only slight decoupling between signaling and substantive value in our study. Still, more research is needed to investigate phenomena associated with symbolic management and decoupling involving the appointment of salient actors in IT governance and management positions.

## 7 Study Implications

Our study highlights director capital and found evidence of signaling value (positive share price reaction) following CITE director appointments in instances where CITEs had IT-specific relational capital and in instances where firms faced IT-based agency conditions. Additionally, signaling value was more remarkable when firms appointed directors with recent CITE experience (human capital) and ITspecific relational capital in high IT-intensity industries. This study also found positive substantive value following the appointment of directors with higher human and IT-specific relational capital and in instances where firms faced IT-based agency conditions. This finding directly answers the question: What is the effect of a firm’s contextual conditions on the creation of firm value following CITE director appointments? These results are encouraging, as they provide evidence that CITE board appointments lead to tangible long-term performance gains and that CITE director capital is valued by shareholders. Our results also answer the question—What is the effect of director capital on firm value?—by revealing that firms appointing CITE directors tend to accrue both shortterm signaling value and long-term substantive value when they appoint CITE directors with a particular type of director capital.

Prior research has often highlighted the relationshipbuilding roles of CITEs (Carter et al., 2011) as highly influential for effective management of the IS function in effective and mature firms (Grover et al., 1993). The positive share price reaction to IT-specific relational capital suggests that focusing on relationship building has been beneficial for CITEs planning to make the jump to directorial positions. The positive share price reaction to the appointment of CITE directors with ITspecific relational capital suggests that shareholders highly value it. While a portion of this reaction might be due to familiarity and the effect of name recognition in social circles, subsequent positive substantive value following such appointments provides evidence that access to IT-related social resources may indeed have had a significant part in the share price reaction to such appointments. This provides preliminary evidence that CITEs making the jump to the boardroom possess the social capital necessary to enjoy a seat on the board of directors.

We found that both signaling and substantive value were positive in CITE appointments where firms faced IT-related agency conditions, implying that monitoring and controlling the IT function and bridging the “ITconfidence gap” are potent motivations for making such appointments. Admittedly, shareholders are concerned about the governance of the IT function and view the presence of CITE directors as a positive step toward addressing these concerns. Following these CITE director appointments, the substantive value was positive, implying that directorial oversight and the possible institution of governance mechanisms result in positive payoffs irrespective of whether firms primarily use IT for operational functions or competitive strategy. Positive signaling and substantive value suggest that considerations regarding the organization’s IT function and strategy are no longer viewed as secondary to business strategy but rather enjoy attention, at least at a level concomitant with business strategy.

Our study found differing levels of impact for the recency of CITE experience (human capital) in the boardroom on firm substantive value. In doing so, we found answers to our question: Are there any signaling and substantive firm value outcomes of different CITE director attributes and firm contextual conditions? We found that directors with recent CITE experience are associated with more substantive value growth in high IT-intensity firms. The impact of IT-specific relational capital did not display any differences in substantive value creation across different industry IT-intensity levels. One implication of this finding is to propose a pathway toward directorship for CITEs that want to make the jump to the boardroom. CITEs who are currently in a CIO or CTO position and aspire to have the most impact in their boardroom position should first seek positions in firms in high IT-intensity industries because their current IT knowledge and skills can be readily applied as a CITE director in such organizations, resulting in more profitable outcomes. CITE directors can then parlay the positive outcomes into practical demonstrations of merit, build ITspecific relational capital, and pursue future CITE directorial opportunities. Admittedly, this assertion is untested and requires further research to ascertain its veracity. Taken together, the results of this study shed more light on director capital and the contextual conditions that enable the creation of two forms of firm value.

Finally, we scratch the surface on whether CITE director appointments reduce the IT-confidence gap. The support for our substantive value hypotheses provides evidence that director capital under certain contextual conditions can alleviate the IT confidence gap of non-IT board members. Support for our signaling value hypotheses suggests some beneficial outcomes from CITE director appointments in reducing the IT confidence gap of shareholders, mainly related to their perceived uncertainty regarding the management of the IT function in the firm. Our study highlights how firms can manage shareholder expectations by appointing CITE directors on the board by demonstrating the signaling value created from such appointments. ## 8 Future Research

Our study also presents exciting avenues for future research. For instance, this study is restricted to C-level IT executives appointed to the board of directors. However, with the ubiquity of digitalization today, it is possible and even likely that IT expertise could reside in other areas like venture capital, social media, service operations—and executives/owners in these areas could bring corresponding value to the board. Future research can take a broader view of IT expertise on the board of directors, focusing on different types of expertise. Also, we used tangible and measurable outcomes in our study to determine value creation following CITE board appointments. However, previous research indicates other possible value outcomes from such appointments, such as conveying social responsibility (Huang, 2010), enhancing corporate image (Fahlenbrach et al., 2010), reputation (Deutsch & Ross, 2003), and legitimacy (Kor & Misangyi, 2008), and better acquisition performance (McDonald et al., 2008). The range of value outcomes highlighted above includes tangible and intangible value, a value that manifests quickly or over a longer duration, and a value that becomes apparent only upon expanding the scope of the study beyond a single firm unit. More research is required to emphasize and investigate these factors and illuminate the full range of value outcomes arising from CITE director appointments. Researchers might also find it fruitful to contrast the firm-value outcomes of boards appointing CITE directors versus boards appointing non-CITE directors. Such research could provide evidence that might establish the increasing salience of appointing directors with digital technology and IT expertise on the board of directors.

Our study finds a positive relationship between CITE director appointments and firm substantive value outcomes, which we ascribed to possible mechanisms such as control, monitoring, and resource provisioning. Future research could unravel the organizational black box to determine how these mechanisms work in an organizational context. For instance, prior research suggests that boards of directors often use outcomebased compensation to align agent behavior with the firm’s goals (Yayla & Hu, 2014), which can transfer significant risk to the agent, resulting in delinquency or lower risk-taking behavior and impeding the achievement of desired outcomes (Baiman, 1990). As a remedy, both monitoring and outcome-based contracts may be used in tandem to effectively align agent behavior (Tosi et al., 1997). However, the cost of monitoring increases as IT complexity increases (Nelson et al., 1996); therefore, more research is needed to understand how firms balance the paradoxes of different mechanisms to effectively govern their IT function.

Finally, our study took a traditional positivistic research approach by focusing on a limited number of quantifiable director characteristics. We ascribe a reduction in the board’s “IT-confidence gap” to the presence of CITE directors. However, other “soft elements” such as group dynamics of the board, boardroom culture, style of meetings, and style of debate (van den Berghe & Levrau, 2004) might be equally influential in mitigating the IT-confidence gap. We encourage more research on the behavioral facets of CITE directors, particularly the sequence of soft elements discussed above and other individual directorial traits that enable a CITE director to effectively bridge the IT-confidence gap.

## 9 Limitations

The firms considered in our sample are mostly large USbased public firms. Directorial involvement and the way directors engage with executives, and upper management may be different in small firms. Additionally, there is a possibility that the emphasis on directorial roles and responsibilities—whether it be monitoring, control, stewardship, or resource provisioning—may also be different in smaller firms. Thus, we advise caution in interpreting and generalizing some of our results. Second, we took several steps to isolate CITE director appointment announcements from other contemporaneous announcements and the anticipation of the announcement by the market. However, the results may be driven by other contemporaneous events not covered in press announcements, resulting in a more subdued share price reaction to firm announcements. Third, we considered the strategic role of IT at an industry level and did not precisely measure it for each firm. This is a limitation, as the extent to which each firm uses IT for strategic purposes may differ for firms in the same industry. Consequently, there may be a higher or lower emphasis on CITE directors performing their various roles and responsibilities, resulting in a higher or lower substantive value than that demonstrated in our study. Finally, because of data sampling limitations, the isolation and extraction of the IT credentials of non-CITEs are outside the scope of the present study. However, this in no way precludes the possibility that non-CITE board directors might possess tech-savviness and IT expertise. Future studies might find it fruitful to include such IT experts in their analyses.

## 10 Conclusion

The relationship between corporate governance and firm value is an essential stream of academic research. Whereas firm value can manifest in many ways, our study centers around whether appointing IT executives such as chief information officers and chief technology officers as board directors results in signaling and substantive value. This study found evidence of signaling and substantive value creation through the appointment of CITE directors with IT-related human (recency of expertise) and relational capital. The CITE director’s expertise was significantly more valuable in industries characterized by high IT use. Similarly, firms facing elevated agency conditions also benefited from such appointments by decreasing their board’s IT-confidence gap. Overall, the findings of our study highlight that under certain conditions appointing

CITE directors with IT-centric director capital can create both signaling and substantive value for appointing firms. By being attentive to firm contextual conditions and the capital that CITE directors bring to the table, firms can place boards with a composition that reflects the IT expertise needed to improve their IT-related governance, strategic decision-making, and effectiveness in monitoring and controlling agents in increasingly IT-reliant organizations.

## Appendix E: HLM Robustness Checks and Model Fit

To ensure the robustness of our HLM results and adherence to the underlying assumptions of HLM analysis a number of diagnostic tests were conducted (Hox et al., 2010; Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). To test the assumption that the variance of Level 1 residual errors is the same in all groups we computed a one-way analysis of variance of standardized Level 1 residuals by group (each firm appointing a CITE director). A significant F-value in this test suggests that the between-group variance of the Level 1 residual error is different (Tabachnik & Fidell, 2007). This test was not significant $( F = 1 . 0 1 8 , p = 0 . 4 2 6 ,$ , 281 numerator df, 564 denominator df) indicating that the residual errors is the same in all groups. To test the assumption that both Level 1 and Level 2 residual errors are normally distributed, the standardized residuals for each level were plotted against the normal scores (QQ plot). Residuals that are normally distributed show a straight diagonal (45 degree) line. Both Level 1 and Level 2 residuals met this condition. The Kolmorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were also nonsignificant, indicating that the distribution was normal (Figures E1 and E2).

## Propensity Score Matching

As a robustness check, we also conducted a regression analysis of the difference in differences (DiD) by comparing the change in average cumulative abnormal returns between our sample firms and a set of counterfactual control firms (Table 9). In order to create our set of counterfactual control firms, we used the propensity score estimation method (Rosenbaum, 2010; Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983) using logistic regression to match our sample firms with control firms. Our procedure for propensity score matching is described below.

In order to create our set of counterfactual control firms, we used the propensity score estimation method (Rosenbaum, 2010; Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983) using logistic regression to match our sample firms with control firms. We first created a list of publicly traded American firms that were not in our original sample. These firms, shortlisted for inclusion into our control firms list, were obtained from the BoardEx database. The aim of propensity score matching (PSM) is to form a matched set of sample and control firms. In order to make the matched set as similar as possible, we selected a number of baseline characteristics upon which propensity scores for our sample and control firms were estimated (Austin, 2011; Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). The baseline characteristics included firm size, board size, percent block holders, R&D expenditures, advertising expenditures, and the firm’s annual financial performance (return on assets) and have been used for PSM in prior research (Corbett et al., 2005; Ding et al., 2018). Control firms that had data for the baseline variables in Mergent Online, COMPUSTAT, or Thomson Reuters Company Financials databases were retained. Since the study encompasses multiple years, the data for control firms was collected for several years. Therefore, each row in our control firms list is actually a firm-year entry. A total of 1342 control firmyears (referred to as control firms henceforth) were included in this preliminary sample.

The propensity scores were estimated using the MatchIt (Ho et al., 2011) package in R using logistic regression. Once the propensity scores were calculated, we used the optimal matching with replacement procedure (Austin, 2011; Rosenbaum, 2010) to match one control firm that had a similar propensity score to a sample firm. A number of criteria were applied while matching. First, a control firm was never matched to two sample firms in a single year. Second, the matched firms both had to be in the same industry and therefore had to have the same first two digits for their NAICS code. Additionally, the control firm was matched to a sample firm on the basis of baseline data from the same year as the data for the sample firm. Third, firms were matched depending upon the nearness of their propensity scores, a metric called caliper (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983, 1985), and measured as the absolute difference between the propensity scores of the sample and control firms. A caliper value less than 0.25 times the standard deviation of the propensity scores has been recommended in previous research (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1985). We used a caliper value of 0.0625 (the std. dev. of the propensity scores for our sample firms is approximately 0.25) as the threshold for matching firms. We were able to match 303 out of a total of 334 sample firms using this matching procedure. Descriptive statistics on baseline characteristics for our sample and control firms are shown in table F4. The back-to-back histogram in Figure F1 shows the distributional similarity between the propensity scores of our sample and control firms.

## About the Authors

Nikhil R. Bandodkar is an assistant professor of information systems in the College of Business at South Carolina State University. Nikhil’s research interest is in the area of IT-related organizational value creation—particularly through the agencies of technology adoption, IT-centered governance mechanisms, and acquisitive innovation. His work has appeared in the Communications of the Association for Information Systems and the proceedings of the Americas Conference on Information Systems.

Varun Grover is the George & Boyce Billingsley Endowed Chair and Distinguished Professor of IS at the Walton School of Business, University of Arkansas. He has published extensively in the information systems field, with over 300 refereed journal publications, with ten recent articles ranking him among the top four researchers globally. Dr. Grover has an h-index of 98 which is ranked in the top 5 in the field, and over 46,000 citations in Google Scholar. Thompson Reuters recognized him as one of 100 Highly Cited Scholars globally in all Business disciplines. He is a senior editor for MISQ Executive, editor of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems Section on Path Breaking Research, and has served as a senior editor for MIS Quarterly (2 terms), the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (2 terms), and the Database for Advances in Information Systems. Dr. Grover’s current work focuses on the impacts of digitalization on individuals and organizations. He is the recipient of numerous awards from USC, Clemson, University of Arkansas, AIS, Academy of Management, DSI, the OR Society, Anbar, PriceWaterhouse, among others for his research and teaching. He is an AIS Fellow and holds the prestigious AIS LEO award for lifetime achievement.
