# User personality and resistance to mandatory information systems in organizations: a theoretical model and empirical test of dispositional resistance to change

## Abstract

This research is driven by the assumption made in several user resistance studies that employees are generally resistant to change. It investigates the extent to which employees’ resistance to IT-induced change is caused by individuals’ predisposition to resist change. We develop a model of user resistance that assumes the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of dispositional resistance to change on perceptual resistance to change, perceived ease of use, and usefulness, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence user resistance behavior. Using an empirical study of 106 HR employees forced to use a new human resources information system, the analysis reveals that 17.0–22.1 percent of the variance in perceived ease of use, usefulness, and perceptual resistance to change can be explained by the dispositional inclination to change initiatives. The four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change – routine seeking, emotional reaction, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity – have an even stronger effect than other common individual variables, such as age, gender, or working experiences. We conclude that dispositional resistance to change is an example of an individual difference that is instrumental in explaining a large proportion of the variance in beliefs about and user resistance to mandatory IS in organizations, which has implications for theory, practice, and future research.

This phenomenon is even more surprisingly when the new system was designed and implemented according to the best available guidelines and principles, yet employees are still resistant.

In general, research and practice recognize this sort of negative employee reactions to mandatory IS implementation (Lapointe and Rivard, 2005) as problematic for organizations (Luftman et al., 2009). Nonetheless, only few studies have pried open the ‘black box’ of user resistance (for an overview see Laumer and Eckhardt, 2012). There is evidence that user resistance behaviors vary widely (e.g., sabotage, non-usage, non-compliance, workarounds, etc.; Dickson and Simmons, 1970; Ferneley and Sobreperez, 2006) and have highly diverse causes (Cenfetelli, 2004; Hirschheim and Newman, 1988; Klaus et al., 2010). These studies share the assumption that user resistance is based on a given natural human proclivity to resist change (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007), which would explain why employees resist even an almost perfect system. However, prior user resistance studies do not explicitly theorize and test whether the natural tendency to resist changes is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enabler of user resistance in the context of mandatory IS implementations in organizations.

This research aims to extend user resistance literature by analyzing the impact of the presumed general natural and predisposed tendency to resist change in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c case of implementing mandatory IS in organizations, and whether user resistance can be explained independently of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c system characteristics. Our study intends to show the impact of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait ‘dispositional resistance to change’ (Oreg, 2003, e.g. used in IS research by Maier et al., 2012, 2013; Polites and Karahanna, 2012) as a variable representing the natural tendency of individuals to resist change in general on perceptions of mandatory IS implementations in organizations. We discuss importance of this trait in predicting these perceptions compared with other factors re<sup>fl</sup>ecting individual differences (Agarwal and Prasad, 1999).

The model is tested empirically in a study during the implementation of a new human resources information system that employees are mandated to use. We subsequently develop our user resistance model and then present and discuss the results.

## Theoretical background and research model development

Research on user resistance to IS in organizations (e.g., Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007) and organizational change in general (e.g., Oreg, 2003, 2006) are the underlying basis of our research model. We focus on the implementation of an IS which employees have no choice other than to use (Gallivan, 2001; Brown et al., 2002). For example, when an enterprise resource planning system is implemented, employees have to use the new system because not using it would prevent the organization from running their business smoothly. Our objective is to observe the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of dispositional resistance to change on employees’ resistance to these mandatory IS in organizations.

User resistance to mandatory information systems in organizations Since the introduction of the technology acceptance model (TAM; Davis 1989), a tremendous body of research has focused on various extensions, modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, replications, and competing models of technology acceptance (Williams et al., 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Some approaches also focus on user resistance, arguing that user resistance is more than simply the opposite of acceptance (Cenfetelli, 2004, Laumer and Eckhardt, 2012). In general, user resistance can be divided into user resistance behavior (the active or passive, overt or covert negative behavioral response of employees) (Kim and Kankanhalli, 2009), resistance to change (an inhibiting perception about the change to the status quo) (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007), and dispositional resistance to change (a natural human proclivity to oppose change in general) (Oreg, 2003) (see Table 1 for an overview). This differentiation is in line with Markus (1983), who contrasts the dispositional perspective on user resistance (which she calls the person-oriented view) with other explanations of resistance that focus on the contextual environment like perceptions of the new IS or the change induced by the new IS.

In order to develop an understanding of how perceptual and behavioral user resistance is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by user personality in terms of dispositional resistance to change, we consider variables capturing individuals’ perceptions of the change situation, since an individual’s perceptions of a new IS mediate the effects that all other variables in the external environment may have (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Agarwal and Prasad, 1999). Consequently, the two perception variables of the TAM (Davis, 1989) mediate the impact of dispositional resistance to change on user resistance behavior. The <sup>fi</sup>rst variable, perceived ease of use, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the degree to which the prospective user expects the target system to be free of effort’ (Davis et al., 1992: 985) and further speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) as ‘the degree of ease associated with the use of the system’ (Venkatesh et al., 2003: 450). The second variable, perceived usefulness, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the prospective user’s subjective probability that using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c application system will increase his or her job performance within an organizational context’ (Davis et al., 1992: 985) and further speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the UTAUT as ‘the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance’ (Venkatesh et al., 2003: 447). Both TAM and UTAUT suggest that perceived ease of use and usefulness are two salient cognitive determinants of IT usage behavior because any new IS is supposed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t employees in their work without costing them a lot of effort. As employees will resist systems that do not provide any bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or which are not free of effort, we assume that:

Hypothesis 1: The lower an employee’s perceived ease of use, the higher her user resistance behavior.

Hypothesis 2: The lower an employee’s perceived usefulness, the higher her user resistance behavior.

Moreover, we consider employees’ perception of the change from the old to the new system modeled as resistance to change as an inhibiting perception in<sup>fl</sup>uencing user resistance behavior. Whereas ease of use and usefulness are based on the characteristics of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS, resistance to change is a perception of the change induced by the implementation of a new IS (Oreg, 2006; Polites and Karahanna, 2012). The importance of resistance to change as an employee’s reaction to an IT-induced change has been demonstrated to the extent that users tend to resist using a technology based on their perceptions of the change to the status quo induced by a new IS (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007). We theorize that user resistance behavior is a function of the simultaneous examination of enabling in terms of perceived ease of use and usefulness (Davis et al., 1992) and an inhibiting perception in terms of resistance to change (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007) by employees (Cenfetelli, 2004). If employees are threatened by the change to the status quo, user resistance behaviors will occur (Kim and Kankanhalli, 2009), so we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3: The higher an employee’s resistance to change, the higher her user resistance behavior.

As discussed in the introduction, prior research into user resistance in the context of mandatory IS implementations in organizations assumes that ‘if change is of a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high magnitude, given natural human proclivity to oppose change, many users will tend to resist the technology’ (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007: 728); however, prior studies have not explicitly tested whether the natural tendency to resist change is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enabler of perceptual and behavioral user resistance; although, the importance of user personality has been demonstrated in several change scenarios (see Oreg et al., 2011 for an overview). Consequently, we will theorize thi assumed natural tendency in the following section based on research on dispositional resistance to change (Oreg, 2003).

## Dispositional resistance to change

Researchers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of personality psychology, the science of the individual person, have sought a theory that explains why individuals are the way they are and what guides their exploration of the unknown, such as organizational change projects (McAdams, 1996). In this context, personality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment’ (Allport, 1937), and dispositional traits represent ‘endogenous, stable, hierarchically structured basic dispositions governed by biological factors’ (Romero et al., 2009). Personality research provides evidence for both the heritability and longitudinal consistency of personality traits and for the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of traits scores in the prediction of behavior aggregated across many different situations (Ajzen, 1985; McAdams, 1996; McCrae and Costa, 1987). Hence, personality research focuses on assumed natural individual tendencies in and the resulting perceptions of and behaviors in change situations such as the implementation of new IS in organizations.

According to the framework for studying persons (McAdams, 1996), dispositional resistance to change (Oreg, 2003) is an example of personality traits that focus speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on the assumed ‘natural human proclivity to oppose change (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007: 728) and that affect an individual’s personal concerns, as do ease of use and useful ness of an IS or resistance to IT-induced change. Dispositional resistance to change is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘an individual’s dispositional inclination to resist changes’ (Oreg, 2003: 680). The basic assumption is that individuals with the strongest resistance character trait will be the least disposed to initiate changes for themselves and will develop more negative beliefs and attitudes toward changes with which they are confronted. The core argument emerges from an analysis of the resistance to change literature, which reveals distinct manifestations of dispositional resistance to change (Oreg, 2003): reluctance to lose control, cognitive rigidity, lack of psychological resilience, intolerance to the adjustment period involved in change, preference for low levels of stimulation and novelty, and reluctance to give up old habits.

Although, these traits are related to how people react to change, they have not been conceptualized to assess the dispositional inclination to resist change (Oreg, 2003). Consequently, Oreg (2003) proposes four dimensions, which are conceptualized to ‘measure an individual’s dispositional inclination to resist changes’ (Oreg 2003: 680). These dimensions are routine seeking, emotional reaction, short-term focus, and cognitive rigidity. Routine seeking ‘involves the extent to which one enjoys and seeks out stable and routine environments (Oreg et al., 2008: 936) in general and re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual’s ‘reluctance to give up old habits’ and a predisposed ‘preference for low levels of stimulation and novelty’ (Oreg, 2003: 680) in the context of any change initiative (Oreg, 2003). ‘Reluctance to lose control’ and a ‘lack of psychological resilience’ are captured in emotional reaction as a dimension of dispositional resistance to change, which ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which individuals feel stressed and uncomfortable in response to imposed change’ (Oreg et al., 2008: 936). Short-term focus ‘involves the degree to which individuals are preoccupied with the short-term inconveniences versus the potential long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the change (Oreg et al., 2008: 936) and with it an individual’s ‘intolerance for [the] adjustment period involved in change’ as well as ‘reluctance to lose control’ (Oreg et al., 2008: 936) . Finally, cognitive rigidity ‘represents a form of stubbornness and an unwillingness to consider alternative ideas and perspectives (Oreg et al., 2008: 936).

The proposed conceptualization of dispositional resistance to change has been shown to predict speci<sup>fi</sup>c change-related behaviors above and beyond other related personality characteristics (Oreg, 2003), such as the Five-Factor Model (McCrae and Costa, 1987), tolerance for ambiguity (Budner, 1962), risk aversion (Slovic, 1972), or sensation seeking (Zuckerman and Link, 1968). The validity of the proposed measurement model has been demonstrated by several studies in various contexts and cultural areas (Oreg et al., 2008). In psychology research dispositional resistance to change has been focused using the four distinct dimensions of the concept and in empirical studies the correlations of each dimension with several other constructs have been evaluated (Oreg, 2003). In other studies an overall dispositional resistance to change construct has been used (Oreg, 2006).

In the following, the effect of dispositional resistance to change will be theorized in the context of mandatory IS implementation in organizations.

Dispositional resistance to change and perceived ease of use Regarding employees’ expectations toward a new IS in an organization, we theorize that an employee’s perceived ease of use is determined by her degree of dispositional resistance to change. For example in terms of routine seeking, if one is reluctant to give up old habits and prefers working with stable routines, one would perceive the effort necessary to learn to work in a changed environment as rather negative (Polites and Karahanna, 2013). Concerning emotional reaction and short-term focus, if employees are predisposed to be stressed by or uncomfortable with an imposed change or perceive the short-term inconveniences rather than the long term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a change, they are likely to evaluate the efforts required to implement the change as more negative (Oreg, 2003). In organizational change projects like mandatory IS implementations, the efforts required to incorporate a new way of working into an existing working routine are short-term inconveniences, which occur at the beginning of a change project (Oreg, 2006). With regard to cognitive rigidity, if employees are predisposed to be unwilling to consider alternatives and prefer the status quo per se, they will perceive any change to the status quo as rather negative, act to retain their old way of working, and perceive any effort required to change their way of working as negative (Oreg, 2003). In the context of imposed change in organizations, these aspects of dispositional resistance to change are strongly related to overall work performance and, hence, the effort associated with incorporating the change into individual working routines (Oreg, 2003).

Hence, for mandatory IS implementations, we theorize that employees with high levels of dispositional resistance to change (routine seeking, emotional reaction, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity) will generally perceive the newly implemented IS to be rather hard to use because they expect to have to make great efforts to learn the new system and work with it easily. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis for dispositional resistance to change in the context of IS implementation is:

Hypothesis 4: The higher an employee’s dispositional resistance to change in terms of a) routine seeking, b) emotional reaction, c) short-term focus and d) cognitive rigidity, the lower her perception of the IS’s ease of use.

Dispositional resistance to change and perceived usefulness Besides the assumed impact of perceived ease of use we further theorize that employees with a high dispositional resistance to change have lower expectations of the performance and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a new IS. For example, since organizational changes involve a substantial change in employees’ routines, it is theorized that employees predisposed to enjoy and seek out stable and routine environments also perceive a change as more threatening (Oreg, 2003, 2006). Hence, since they are more prone to seek routine, they do not expect change to lead to higher performance. In terms of their emotional reaction, if they are predisposed to be stressed and uncomfortable, they will have low expectations of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a changing environment (Oreg, 2003, 2006). In a changing environment, employees predisposed to be reluctant to lose control and who prefer to focus on short-term inconveniences will have lower expectations that they will work more effectively in the new environment. With regard to cognitive rigidity, employees who are less able to perceive the potential performance improvement resulting from the alternative work<sup>fl</sup>ow made possible by the new system are expected to perceive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a new system as more negative (Oreg, 2006). In his pioneer work on dispositional resistance to change Oreg (2003) reveals that when employees of<sup>fi</sup>ces are relocated and they are more preoccupied with the short-term inconveniences than with the potential longterm bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the change, they are more likely to show negative reactions to the imposed change. In his study involving voluntary usage of a course website generator, Oreg (2003) shows that dispositional resistance to change in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

We therefore expect that when implementing mandatory IS, employees with higher levels of dispositional resistance to change in terms of routine seeking, emotional reaction, shortterm focus and cognitive rigidity will perceive the performance and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the newly implemented IS rather negatively. Hence, our second hypothesis regarding dispositional resistance to change is:

Hypothesis 5: The higher an employee’s dispositional resistance to change in terms of a) routine seeking, b) emotional reaction, c) short-term focus and d) cognitive rigidity, the lower her perception of the IS's usefulness.

Dispositional resistance to change and resistance to change The importance of dispositional resistance to change has been demonstrated clearly in the context of imposed changes (Oreg, 2003), because such changes can dramatically restructure employees’ working environments and working routines. Oreg (2006) <sup>fi</sup>nds that employees predisposed to prefer routine environments express higher resistance to changes to their environment. Moreover, with regard to emotional reactions, those individuals who are predisposed to feel stressed by changes to their environment or who are highly reluctant to lose control express higher resistance to change (Oreg, 2003). Individuals who are predisposed to focus on the short-term inconveniences of an organizational change rather than the long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and who are intolerant to the adjustment period involved with the change individuals will express higher perceptual resistance to change. Moreover, Oreg (2006) reveals that cognitive rigidity is an important predictor of employees’ perceptual resistance to imposed changes in organizations. His study of two organizational units planning to merge shows that employees less willing to welcome alternative ways of working express their resistance to the change with cognitive and affective reactions. He concludes that dispositional resistance to change is an important predictor of individuals’ affective and cognitive resistance to the merger (Oreg, 2006). Polites and Karahanna (2012) also highlight the connection between dispositional resistance to change and perceptual resistance to change in the context of voluntary technology usage.

Consequently, we also theorize that the extent of dispositional resistance to change in terms of routine seeking, emotional reaction, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity has an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the affective and cognitive resistance to the change induced by a mandatory IS. Consequently, our <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis for dispositional resistance to change is:

Hypothesis 6: The higher an employee’s dispositional resistance to change in terms of a) routine seeking, b) emotional reaction, c) short-term focus and d) cognitive rigidity the higher her resistance to change.

## Individual differences and user resistance

Dispositional resistance to change is an example of individual difference, which are discussed by several scholars in the context of technology acceptance (for example, Zmud, 1979; Agarwal and Prasad, 1999). In the context of imposed change, age, gender, and tenure are considered important individual characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uencing employees’ perceptions of an organizational change such as the implementation of a new mandatory IS (see Oreg et al., 2011 for an overview). For example, Oreg (2003) considers tenure in the context of voluntary usage of a course website generator, showing that tenure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the usage of the new technology, and age and gender are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the context of of<sup>fi</sup>ce relocation, showing that especially cognitive evaluation is related to change recipients’ age. Moreover, Oreg (2006) reveals that age correlates with several perceptions of change. In general, age, gender, and experience are considered the most relevant examples of individual differences in the context of technology usage (Venkatesh et al., 2003). We follow the lead of such prior research in the context of mandatory IS implementations in organizations and propose age, gender, and working experience as relevant variables modeling individual characteristics. We use these variables to observe the impact of dispositional resistance to change on perceived ease of use and usefulness as well as resistance to change and to estimate its extent. This approach is in line with a call for research to identify and analyze individual differences that are instrumental in explaining a large proportion of variance in individual perceptions of information technology (Agarwal and Prasad, 1999).

## Research methodology

How recruiting is managed within organizations has changed dramatically over the years (Weitzel et al., 2009). As recent research shows that the Internet has changed how HR processes are managed (Lee, 2007; Lang et al., 2011; Laumer et al., 2010; Eckhardt et al., 2014; Laumer et al., 2015), we chose an e-recruiting system as our IS implementation example to investigate the impact of dispositional resistance to change on technology beliefs and user resistance.

## Target information system implementation and sample characteristics

The observed organization is an automotive supplier with over 50,000 employees at more than 150 locations in more than 50 countries worldwide that generated over €7 billion total revenues in 2009. The project observed is the implementation of a new IS aimed at supporting the company’s recruiting process and used by 150 employees. The system is similar to a holistic e-recruiting system proposed by Lee (2007) designed to transform the paper-based managed recruiting process of the organization to an IT-enabled one. The new system is mandatory, which means that every recruiter has to use the new system to access open positions, applications, and to manage her working routines. It is hardly possible for HR employees to perform their work tasks without using the system. The project began in January 2010 and the system was completely implemented by July 2010. To obtain a broader sense of user resistance, the empirical survey was conducted in the pre-implementation phase from mid-May to the end of June 2010. The study was executed after the training period and while users had access to a test system, which means that all affected employees had begun to perceive the new system as either positive or negative. In total, 106 of 150 recruiters participated in our study (70.7% response rate). Table 2 presents demographic information about the research participants.

## Dispositional resistance to change as a dimension set

As our research focuses on dispositional resistance to change, it is important to decide whether dispositional resistance to change will be modeled as a uni-dimensional construct, a dimension set, or a multidimensional construct (Polites et al., 2012). Polites et al. (2012) provide guidelines for IS research to determine the speci<sup>fi</sup>c structure of a construct. On the basis of their guidelines, one can argue that dispositional resistance to change is a <sup>fi</sup>rst-order formative, second-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective multidimensional construct. However, the use of multidimensional constructs is sometimes criticized as too ambiguous, not reliable enough, and for having too low explanatory power (Edwards, 2001). Edwards (2001) points out that it is unclear how the variation in aggregate constructs results from their dimensions and a multidimensional construct can explain less variance in an outcome variable than its individual dimensions. Furthermore, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine with precision how multiple dimensions relate to outcome variables (Johnes, 1998; Paunonen et al., 1999). Hence, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop a theory explaining the relationship from such constructs to speci<sup>fi</sup>c outcomes because different explanations may apply to different dimensions (Edwards 2001).

Polites et al. (2012) posit that these challenges when developing theoretical arguments can be addressed by using dimension sets. When operationalizing the model each dimension is directly related to other constructs in the nomological net (Edwards, 2001; Polites et al., 2012) and theories are formed about collections of individual dimensions as a group (Polites et al., 2012). This is especially the case when a study focuses on complex concepts. In such a case, it is generally best to create a measurement model with maximum critical conceptual distinction (MacKenzie et al., 2005). However, if such a construct is not central to the research, it is acceptable to use a multidimensional or uni-dimensional construct.

Therefore, we follow the guidelines by Polites et al. (2012) and model dispositional resistance to change as a dimension set. As we are interested in the effect of each dimension of dispositional resistance to change on perceptual user resistance, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness we have already theorized for the collections of each dimension of dispositional resistance to change (see the section ‘Theoretical background and research model development’) and we now operationalize the model such that each dimension is directly related to the other constructs in the model. With this approach we follow the tradition of psychology research (Oreg, 2003) and the guidelines provided by Polites et al. (2012).

We acknowledge that there are trade-offs in choosing the best model. In this context, it is important to consider the underlying theory and prior research in the subject domain. Therefore, the decision about the best modeling approach is a complex one, such that the decision for a model approach should be made based both on the underlying theory and the prior research tradition. Given the underlying theory, four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change are proposed (Oreg, 2003); however, prior research followed different traditions to model dispositional resistance to change. On the one side, in studies focusing on theoretical arguments to explain the impact of dispositional resistance to change on different individual cognitions and behaviors the distinct dimensions are used (Oreg, 2003). On the other side, when using dispositional resistance to change as part of broader theoretical models focusing on different theoretical arguments and using dispositional resistance to change as a control variable aggregated con structs are used (Oreg, 2006, Maier et al., 2012, 2013, Polites and Karahanna, 2012). As our theorizing focuses explicitly on dispositional resistance to change and its impact on user resistance to mandatory IS implementations we model in line with prior research (Oreg, 2003) dispositional resistance to change as a dimension set. However, we also acknowledge the methodology of studies not focusing on the dimensions of dispositional resistance to change and that in other studies dispositional resistance to change might not be used as a dimension set because of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical focus of these studies we also provide the results for a multidimensional and uni-dimensional con struct (see Appendix C).

In each model, dispositional resistance to change is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor for perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of the IS, as well as the resistance to change.

## Content validity

In setting up our questionnaire, we referenced methods of measurement used in previous empirical research (see the section ‘Research methodology’). The items (Table A1, Appendix) were tested with students studying IT at a German university and re<sup>fi</sup>ned based on interviews with HR professionals to ensure content validity (see Laumer et al., 2010 and Maier et al., 2011 for details of the pre-test). As advised by project management, we removed three items (one each from routine seeking, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity) that irritated students and employees.

## Indicator reliability

Indicator reliability shows the proportion of the variance of a single indicator that derives from the relevant latent variables. All loadings should be greater than 0.7 (Chin and Newsted, 1999). As one can see in Table 3, all loadings except one were above this recommended threshold. We kept the one below the threshold in the model because it was just small amount below the recommend threshold, as Carmines and Zeller (1979) suggest that indicators above 0.6 can also be included in the measurement model for relatively new scales. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of all loadings at P≤0.001 is high and was calculated using the bootstrap method with 5,000 samples (Henseler et al., 2009). However, two items (one each for SF and CR) indicate a loading below the recommended level of 0.4 and were removed from the <sup>fi</sup>nal model. The scale used is similar to already translated and evaluated one for the German context (Oreg et al., 2008). Although, removing items limits our results, also Oreg et al. (2008), Stewart et al. (2009), and Polites and Karahanna (2012) indicate that scales vary across cultures and research settings, so removing items does not necessarily reduce their explanation power.

## Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity can be evaluated by cross-loadings and the inter-variable correlation (Campbell and Fiske, 1959). The indicator loadings are higher for the corresponding constructs than for any other construct. In addition, the inter-variable correlations must be smaller than the root of the correspond ing AVE. As this is also the case (Table 3), the discriminant validity of the latent variables is high (Fornell and Larcker, 1981, Hulland, 1999).

## Structural model

We were able to determine the explanatory power of our structural model using the squared multiple correlations $( R ^ { 2 } )$ and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels of the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients (Chin, 1998). The $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { \check { R } { ' } s }$ for perceived usefulness are 0.232 and 0.170 if only the dimensions of dispositional resistance to change are considered. For perceived ease of use, we observed an $\mathbf { \Delta } ^ { \circ } R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.305 (0.210 without age, gender, and experience) and for resistance to change 0.223 (0.221 only for dispositional resistance to change). The $R ^ { 2 }$ of user resistance behavior is 0.436 (as Figure 2 illustrates). The t-values were evaluated to analyze the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in the research model. We conducted signi<sup>fi</sup>cance tests using the bootstrap routine with 5,000 samples. Figure 2 shows all calculated path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels.

## Common method bias

As with all self-reported data, there is a possibility for common method bias (CMB) resulting from multiple sources such as consistency and social desirability (Podsakoff et $a l . ,$ 2003; Podsakoff and Organ, 1986). To test for CMB, we introduced a common method factor in the PLS model (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Liang et al., 2007). The factor included all the principal constructs’ indicators and calculated each indicator’s variances, explained substantively by the principal construct and by the method. The results demonstrate that the average substantively explained variance of indicators is 0.763, while the average method-based variance is 0.012. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance is approximately 61:1 and is therefore even better than the level proposed by Liang et al. (2007). In addition, even fewer method factor loadings are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant compared to the study of Liang et al. (2007). In this context we acknowledge the critique Chin et al.’s (2012) critique of this approach. Given the insigni<sup>fi</sup>cance and small magnitude of method variance, the conducted method is unlikely to be a serious concern, however, given the critique by Chin et al. (2012) it is possible that our approach might still be affected by CMB.

## Limitations

While the results revealed by our study are interesting, they are also limited to some extent. The study reveals the effect of dispositional resistance to change for only one particular system (mandatory IS) in one particular department (HR) in one cultural setting. In addition, we did not test any environmental variables within the research model. System complexity, characteristics of the previous system, and the nature of the task may all play important roles in resistance, but because we investigated only one system these were not controlled for in our proposed model. Moreover, our study captures just a single point of time. This may limited the generalizability of the results.

## Discussion and implications

The results of our research show that the personality trait of an individual’s dispositional resistance to change is an important predictor of how the individual perceives and reacts to a mandatory IS implementation in organizations. In total, the four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change, age, gender, and experience explain 23.2 percent of the $R ^ { 2 }$ of perceived ease of use, 30.5 percent of the $R ^ { 2 }$ of the perceived usefulness, and 23.3 percent of the $R ^ { 2 }$ of resistance to change, whereby dispositional resistance to change can be revealed as the factor with the highest effect size. These results from a mandatory IS implementation indicate that perceptions driv ing user resistance behavior can indeed be explained by individuals’ assumed natural tendency to perceive change negatively rather than positively.

This observed variance in the perception variables is good compared with other studies investigating the effect of personality traits on individual beliefs or behavior. Junglas et al. (2008) explain 11% of privacy concerns with the Five-Factor Model of Personality (McCrae and Costa, 1987), which contains <sup>fi</sup>ve different personality traits, and Devaraj et al. (2008) explain 9– 18% of perceived usefulness using the Five-Factor Model. In addition, in the social science settings, where personality variables are the focus of many studies, an R<sup>2</sup> value in the range 10–20% is considered acceptable (Gaur and Gaur, 2006: 109). In our model, we explained 17.0–22.1% of the variance in perceived ease of use, usefulness, and resistance to change, respectively, using only one personality trait developed to measure an individual’s character with regard to resistance.

Compared with other studies investigating individual differences and belief variables, the variance is also good, explaining an equal level of variance in perceived ease of use as Agarwal and Prasad (1999) found for tenure, level of education, experience, participation in training and role with regard to technology. The results of our study show that the effect of dispositional resistance to change is even stronger than these individual differences variables.

In this section, we discuss the implications for theory and practice as well as opportunities for future research based on these results.

## Implications for theory

Recent personality research has emphasized the relationship of personality and individual’s resistance to organizational change (Oreg, 2003). At the same time, IS research scholars have proposed that future research move beyond TAM (Hirschheim, 2007) and investigate user resistance in greater detail (Lapointe and Rivard, 2005). Furthermore, IS research has called for a deeper investigation of personality and IS research phenomena (Devaraj et al., 2008; Eckhardt et al., 2015), for an integration of user resistance and personality research (Venkatesh, 2006), and for research identifying those individual differences that are instrumental in explaining a large proportion of variance in individual perceptions of information technology (Agarwal and Prasad, 1999). Our approach in this paper responds to all of these mandates by <sup>fi</sup>nding that dispositional resistance to change has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct effect on how a new mandatory IS is perceived, employees’ resistance to change, and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indirect effect the resulting user resistance behavior.

Prior research provides evidence that resistance to change in a particular situation and the evaluation of the change situation impacts behavioral responses to the new IS (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007). Regarding dispositional resistance to change, the results of this paper highlight the importance of individual differences and personality in how IT-induced organizational change initiatives are perceived. The results provide the foundation for an alternative explanation of predictors of technology perceptions or resistance to change by individuals in the context of mandatory IS implementations. In addition to the situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology or change characteristics, an individual’s personality is an important predictor of the perceptions of imposed technologies in an organization. Hence, our approach extends the understanding of individuals’ technology beliefs as part of an investigation of the acceptance of or resistance to IT-induced change in organizations. It shows that beyond the situational beliefs about the technology or the change, understanding an individual’s dispositional resistance to change is also important in understanding user resistance as it in<sup>fl</sup>uences both enablers and inhibitors of a mandatory IS implementation. In addition, it illustrates that in future attempts to understand and control for the impact of an individual’s characteristics, future studies should consider dispositional resistance to change as an individual-oriented variable. Our study shows that in addition to situational and context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables, which focus mainly on possible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, personality is important in a particular change situation.

Focusing on the four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change, the results indicate that routine seeking, emotional reactions, short term focus, and cognitive rigidity are important predictors of an individual’s perception of mandatory IS in organizations, however, their in<sup>fl</sup>uences differ. According to the analyses of the effect sizes (Table B1) emotional reaction is the dimension of dispositional resistance to change which has the strongest effect on all considered perceptions of our research model. Hence, employees who generally experience change as stressful are likely to perceive mandatory IS rather negatively. Although, we can observe signi<sup>fi</sup>cant path coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients, the short-term focus dimension shows the smallest effect size (see Table B1). Hence, the predisposed focus on short-term inconveniences is not as important in the context of imposed IS implementations.

In addition, we can provide evidence for a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of cognitive rigidity. This result is in contradiction to a pioneer study by Oreg (2003) who found that cognitive rigidity is not relevant in the context of imposed change. However, in a 2006 study, Oreg reveals that cognitive rigidity as part of the dispositional resistance to change scale is an important predictor of the resistance to a merger of two organizational units. Moreover, Oreg (2003) and Polites and Karahanna (2012) observe an effect of cognitive rigidity in the context of voluntary technology usage. Our result contradicts Oreg’s (2003) study and we conclude that an employee’s predisposed unwillingness to consider alternative ideas is relevant in the context of mandatory IS implementations. Such employees do not expect or consider positive individual impacts, are unwilling to think about the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an alternative way to perform their tasks, and prefer to prolong the status quo. Hence, such employees perceive efforts to implement the change and the change itself rather nega tively. In contrast to an of<sup>fi</sup>ce relocation, which is a major change at one particular point of time (see Oreg 2003), mandatory IS implementations are a daily source of stress as employees have to use these systems regularly and are confronted with the new way of working every day (Gallivan, 2003; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). Hence, if an employee is predisposed to prolong the status quo and is unwillingly to consider the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of this new way of working, cognitive rigidity is an important factor predicting the perceptions of an imposed organizational change, inducing changes with daily implications such as the implementation of a new IS.

Compared with others studies using the concept of dispositional resistance to change, we are able to show that dispositional resistance to change is an important factor in the context of mandatory IS implementations in organizations. Other studies in the context of voluntary usage have shown that dispositional resistance to change is an important moderating variable for intention – behavior, past behavior – belief, and the belief – belief relations (Maier et al., 2012). Moreover, Polites and Karahanna (2012) investigating the usage of GoogleDocs observed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct impact of a second-order dispositional resistance to change construct on inertia, but not on perceptual beliefs such as ease of use and relative advantage. In addition, Nov and Ye (2008) reveals that a mean-calculated dispositional resistance to change construct has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the perceived ease of use of a digital library system. In an organizational context, Maier et al. (2013) calculate dispositional resistance to change as a mean value, which is used as a control variable using bivariate correlations to control for personality impacts on the variables used in their study. Hence, although prior research studies struggle to evaluate dispositional resistance to change modeled as either a second-order or a mean-calculated construct as an important predictor of different belief variable, our study extents these insights, explicitly analyzing the four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change and observing a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant impact on perceived ease of use and usefulness as well as resistance to change. Hence, breaking down dispositional resistance to change into its four dimensions and analyzing them explicitly reveals a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which could not be observed by other studies using a different conceptualization of dispositional resistance to change. Moreover, our posthoc tests using different conceptualizations of dispositional resistance to change reveal similar results (see Appendix C). It is especially important for future research studies that include dispositional resistance to change as a control factor to note that a uni-dimensional construct using only four items reveals signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and resistance to change (see Appendix C). Therefore, future research might use the four tested items to control for the impact of user personality in user resistance research models.

Moreover, while personality traits are recognized as individual characteristics, research studies have focused very seldom on them in the context of inhibiting and enabling perceptions of mandatory IS by individuals in organizations (Devaraj et al., 2008). Our study shows that the four dimensions of dispositional resistance to change are important predictors of the evaluation of technologies by individuals and are better predictors of an individual’s perceptions of technologies than other common variables such as age, gender, or work experience. Hence, dispositional resistance to change is an example of an individual difference that is instrumental in explaining a large proportion of variance in beliefs, positioning our results as a direct response to Agarwal and Prasad’s (1999) call for research on these factors. Future studies of user resistance might consider dispositional resistance to change broken down into its components as a control variable in models to more accurately weight the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of effects caused by the individual or individual differences and to avoid overweighting characteristics of the situation.

## Implications for practice

Our results also have implications for practice. The results show that the perception of the technology is not driven solely by the characteristics of the technology in question and that the perception of the change is not driven only by the characteristics of the change. Rather, such perceptions are also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by one’s level of dispositional resistance to change. On the one hand, these results indicate that even if systems are designed according to the most appropriate guidelines and principles and even if a change process is designed as perfectly as possible, such systems or change processes may be perceived negatively because the perceptions are driven by an individual’s predisposition. From a design and management perspective, design or management failures may not be the only cause of resistance to change initiatives. On the other hand, if negative perceptions are also caused by individual personality and overcoming the resistance resulting from these negative perceptions may not be possible solely by <sup>fi</sup>ne tuning designable characteristics of the system or change process, this study reinforces the importance of managers focusing not only on system and change design, but also on their employees’ attitudes toward change.

Although, practitioners may be inclined to hire people with a low level of dispositional resistance to change if they want employees who perceive management initiatives more positively, they should also consider that an employee with a high level of dispositional resistance to change who becomes convinced of an IT-induced change initiative may also end up a long-term supporter of the project because she does not change her opinions easily. The dimension of cognitive rigidity indicates that such employees do not often change their minds. Hence, there are positive and negative effects of recruiting and having employees with a high degree of dispositional resistance to change.

A second implication is that the results indicate that employees with a high level of dispositional resistance to change exhibit negative perceived ease of use. Such employees may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from training that focuses on the ease of using new IS, and should be selected for training programs designed to help them overcome their natural inclinations and change from the status quo to the desired new modus operandi. The results also indicate that extra effort may be required to convince certain personality types about the usefulness of a given technology. Designing training speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for the target group with a high level of dispositional resistance to change may yield a greater acceptance of technologies and reduce user resistance. As routine seeking is one important predictor for perceived usefulness, these trainings should explicitly focus on the usefulness of the new routines and how employees can establish new stable environments without great effort.

The design of organizational change management initiatives involving new technology and the design of sales campaigns related to IT innovation may also be affected by the results. The implication of this study is that a ‘one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all’ approach is not the whole answer in terms of technologyrelated change management policies or initiatives. Individual dispositional resistance to change affects the equation, and so management awareness of various personality types and how they view technologies or the induced change can lead to better designs, better change management strategies and less resistance behavior overall.

## Future research

We recommend that future research analyze whether contextual or situational variables moderate the impact of dispositional resistance to change on beliefs and attitudes about IS in organizations. One possibility is to test whether change management measures such as training, communication, or participation moderate the impact of dispositional resistance to change on perceived ease of use, usefulness, or resistance to change. This might be the only way for managers to in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavior as ‘the internal psychological process is not amenable to direct manipulation’ (Agarwal and Prasad, 1999: 381).

Future research could also examine the adoption patterns of early adopters versus late adopters, for example how individuals with a high level of dispositional resistance to change modify their attitudes and beliefs. One could argue that, on the one hand, it is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to convince employees with a high level of dispositional resistance to change to perceive a change initiative positively. On the other hand, as mentioned above, when such employees are convinced, they will not change their opinion easily and may be long-term supporters of a particular project. Thus, one might hypothesize that employees with a high level of dispositional resistance to change perceive new ideas negatively at the beginning of a system lifecycle and try to keep old habits at the end, but may be major supporters during the other phases of a system lifecycle (Maier et al., 2015). Investigating such hypotheses could help us understand the impact of dispositional resistance to change on the perceptions of individuals during the lifecycle of an IT innovation, beyond the individual’s initial contact with an IT innovation, as was the focus of this study.

## Conclusion

In general, this study reveals that dispositional resistance to change impacts employees’ perceptions of mandatory IS implementations in organizations. The results indicate that employees with a high level of dispositional resistance change perceive technologies and the corresponding change in organizations more negatively than those with a low level. This personality trait is a better predictor of technology or change perceptions than age, gender, or work experience and is therefore an example of an individual difference that is instrumental in explaining a large proportion of the variance in beliefs. Our results help explain why an almost perfect system can still be resisted by employees, implying that is not always only the system that causes user resistance behaviors but rather in part also the predisposition of the users themselves.

## Effect size

In general, effect size is a quantitative measure to determine the strength of a phenomenon (Kelley and Preacher 2012). Cohen’s ${ \check { f } } ^ { 2 }$ is one possible measure of effect size in structural equation modeling. The general formula for $f ^ { 2 }$ can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows whereas $R ^ { 2 } { } _ { \mathrm { i n c l } }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of determination $( R ^ { 2 } )$ of a variable including the impact of the phenomena of interest and $R _ { \mathrm { \ e x c l } } ^ { 2 }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of determination $( R ^ { 2 } )$ of the same variable excluding the impact of the phenomena of interest (Cohen 1992). As suggested by Cohen (1988) $\mathsf { a } f ^ { 2 }$ of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as small, medium, and large effect sizes.

Formula 1: effect size

$$
f ^ {2} = \frac {R _ {i n c l} ^ {2} - R _ {e x c l} ^ {2}}{1 - R _ {i n c l} ^ {2}}
$$

Using the formula we have calculated the effect size of dispositional resistance to change compared to other individual differences such as age, gender and experience. We <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated the model using all predictors of perceived useful ness, perceived ease of use, and resistance to change. Than we exclude the dispositional resistance to change variables.

On the basis of the two $R ^ { 2 }$ values for each variable we calculated the effect size using the above formula. We can observe a medium effect of dispositional resistance to change, and small or non-existing effect for the other control variables. Hence, we can conclude that dispositional resistance to change has a stronger effect on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and resistance to change than age, gender, and experience.

Besides this general comparison, we also controlled for the effect size of each dispositional resistance to change dimension. These results are illustrated by Table B1 and reveal that emotional reaction has a stronger effect on the outcome variables compared with all other variables used. Hence, it is the dimension of dispositional resistance to change with the strongest effect on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and resistance to change.
