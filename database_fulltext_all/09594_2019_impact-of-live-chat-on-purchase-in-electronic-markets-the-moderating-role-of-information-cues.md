# Impact of Live Chat on Purchase in Electronic Markets: The Moderating Role of Information Cues

Abstract. Live chat tools have emerged as a channel for fostering synchronous communication between sellers and buyers. The role of live chat in the e-commerce environment, however, is largely underexplored. Using granular data from Alibaba, we examine the effect of live chat on consumers’ purchase decisions. After controlling for the selection process that customers with high purchase intention are more likely to initiate live chat in the first place, we find that live chat can increase purchase probability of tablets by 15.99%. We also investigate how the effect of live chat is moderated by existing information cues: product sales volume and seller feedback score. The substitutional or complementary patterns allow us to understand how live chat tools change the ways consumers evaluate product quality and seller credibility. We observe a substitutional effect of live chat on seller feedback score such that sellers with low feedback score benefit more from live chat conversations than sellers with high score. On the contrary, products with high past sales volume sell better after live chat, indicating a reinforcement effect. Such findings deepen our understanding of the difference between various information cues in electronic markets. As one of the first systematic studies to investigate live chat, our paper contributes to web trust conceptual frameworks with empirical analyses and sheds light on practical decisions faced by e-vendors and platform designers.

## 1. Introduction

The lack of trust in the computer-mediated environment has long been regarded as the biggest challenge facing e-vendors<sup>1</sup> (Gefen et al. 2003, Pavlou and Gefen 2004, Kim et al. 2009). Both cognition-based factors and emotion-based factors can affect trust. Cognitively, the spatial distance between sellers and buyers leads to uncertainty about product quality and seller credibility. When consumers purchase online, they cannot experience the product or service before making a payment, or have their questions answered in a timely fashion. Further, without face-to-face interactions with the seller, consumers do not know whether sellers are honestly disclosing the true quality of products and whether they genuinely care about the customers welfare (Park et al. 2005, Komiak and Benbasat 2006). This leads to higher perceived risk and lower purchase intentions. Emotionally, shopping online is less enjoyable because of the lack of human interaction. In brick-and-mortar stores, the salesperson can identify the customers’ needs and provide tailored recommendations (Perrault and Brousseau 1989). Such adaptive assistance is likely to create pleasure, facilitate trust between the transacting parties, and encourage purchasing (Park et al. 2005). In online shopping, consumers face a static set of information cues, and the lack of interaction reduces purchase intentions (Park and Lennon 2006).

Live chat has the potential to combat the abovementioned issues in computer-mediated marketplaces because it provides a synchronous conversation that reduces information asymmetry and fosters a strong customer relationship. Live chat tools allow e-vendors to communicate with customers in a real-time fashion. When the live chat function is embedded in an online store, a customer can initiate a conversation with a human web assistant by clicking the “chat” button on the web page. Live chat tools have shown their effectiveness at improving customer satisfaction and boosting purchase probability. According to a survey conducted by Forrester Research (Strothkamp et al. 2010), “Around 44% of online consumers say that having questions answered by a live person while in the middle of an online purchase is one of the most important features a website can offer.” AT&T claimed to have achieved the highest satisfaction when their chat function is used (J.D. Power 2013). Abt Electronics experienced conversion rates as high as 20% after introducing live chat (Wagner 2010). Wells Fargo claimed to see both high satisfaction and a double-digit increase in conversion rates with the use of live chat tools (Strothkamp et al. 2010). Despite such industry reports and anecdotes, no empirical examination has been conducted to quantify how live chat affects the decision to purchase. Systematic studies are needed to address econometric challenges and accurately quantify the causal effects of live chat. This work aims to bridge this gap. Thus, the first goal of our research is to quantify the effect of live chat on the decision to purchase.

A comprehensive understanding of live chat requires an investigation of its interaction with existing information cues in online marketplaces. Specifically, we focus on two information cues: seller feedback score and product past sales volume. Seller feedback score has been studied extensively in the reputation system as a predominant mechanism that facilitates trust between online sellers and buyers, making it possible for strangers to transact online (Ba and Pavlou 2002). Reputation systems document feedback after transactions, serving as a type of electronic word of mouth (eWOM) (Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002). Seller feedback scores are the sums of all numerical ratings of products sold by the same seller. Consumers use this measure to gauge sellers’ credibility. Throughout the paper, we use reputation and feedback scores interchangeably.

Although reputation systems drive sellers to behave in a trustworthy manner (Walden 2000), they can also generate market inefficiency when sellers enter the market sequentially. Because of buyers dependency on reputation measures, new sellers initial cost of establishing reputation is high. This potentially deters high-quality sellers to enter the market (Ba and Pavlou 2002). Feedback scores have also been criticized for inducing opportunistic behavior. When an online marketplace makes the entire feedback history available to buyers, sellers, after establishing an initial reputation for honesty, may cheat occasionally (Bakos and Dellarocas 2011). Moreover, a seller may gain positive feedback from selling cheap products and use that reputation to attract buyers who intend to buy expensive products (Su et al. 2006). This renders the difficulty for customers to effectively make purchase decisions based on reputation, and tools that can substitute for reputation are highly desired. Thus, our second goal is to examine the substitutional or complementary patterns between live chat and seller feed back scores.

Another information cue is product past sales volume. E-vendors usually document past sales volume on product pages to assist with consumers’ decision making (Cheung et al. 2014). A higher past sales volume is believed to indicate higher quality because it reflects the collective evaluation of the crowd (Ye and Fang 2013). According to information cascade theory (Duan et al. 2009), consumers likely form their decisions based on others’ choices, making online adoption a preferential process of attachment to a product, whereby the “rich get richer” (Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992). The presence of live chat may influence such a preferential attachment process because it provides an additional channel for signals from sellers to buyers. Thus, our third goal is to evaluate the substitutional or complementary patterns between live chat and past sales volume.

To achieve these research goals, we use large-scale granular data from Alibaba, one of the largest online marketplaces in the world. The data are from March 2013 to June 2013 and focus on a homogeneous market of Apple and Samsung tablets. The data provide an ideal means to address our research goals. First, Alibaba has a reputation system similar to that of eBay, where feedback is collected after each transaction. The feedback is aggregated into reputational measures, including feedback scores, and made publicly consumable. More importantly, Alibaba displays the last month’s sales volume for every product. Second, Alibaba has developed its own live chat tool, Trade Manager, for sellers and buyers to communicate instantly. This tool is readily installed on the web page and is downloadable as a software application for free Third, the data are obtained from the Alibaba server, with consumers’ browsing, live chatting, and purchasing histories. Such granularity allows us to recover the sequence of users’ behavior and unpack the impact of live chat.

After controlling for potential endogeneity issues with respect to consumers’ chatting decisions, product price premiums, and the information cues, we obtain a set of findings. We find that (1) live chat has a significantly positive impact on purchase. On average, a live chat conversation will increase the purchase probability by 15.99%. However, this number will be overestimated if not controlling for the endogeneity; (2) live chat has a substitution effect in regard to seller-level feedback scores such that sellers with low feedback scores benefit more from live chat than do sellers with high feedback scores; and (3) live chat has a reinforcement effect on past sales volume A product with a high sales volume is more likely to be purchased after a seller–buyer communication as compared with a product with a low sales volume.

Through our extended analyses and robustness studies, we show that the positive effect of live chat in boosting rates of purchases results from its ability to effectively signal benevolence and strengthen the seller–buyer relationship.

Our findings contribute to the interdisciplinary subject of trust in online marketplaces. First, our work is among the first empirical studies to take into consideration the endogeneity problem with feedback scores and past sales volume figures. Prior studies with secondary data mainly treat feedback scores or sales volumes as exogenous, potentially overestimating their effects (Ye and Fang 2013, Tadelis 2016). Second, we take into consideration the endogeneity of the consumer’s decision to use live chat. We develop novel instrumental variables (IVs) leveraging the clickstream data and the differential time costs between weekdays and weekends. Such IV constructions can be widely applied to other studies, given the increasing availability of clickstream data. Third, our results contribute to the literature on information systems and consumer behavior theories in the marketing literature, as we examine a new channel that potentially alters consumers’ perception of existing information cues that have been widely studied in reputation systems and information cascade literature. Finally, the findings of our analyses can inform decisions about platform design and seller effort allocation.

The manuscript is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review past works. In Section 3, we lay out theoretical background and develop our hypotheses. We describe our data in Section 4. In Section 5, we present our model. We discuss the results in Section 6 and show the extended analyses in Section 7. We show the robustness checks in Section 8 and conclude our paper in Section 9.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section we review the conceptual framework of trust models in order to map the informational artifacts we study onto previous discussions. Although it is difficult to accurately draw a one-to-one mapping from empirical study to the theoretical framework, this discussion helps us show the contributions of our study and how it aligns with past work. We will review several related theories and empirical findings in order to establish the nature and interrelationships of three phenomena: the seller-level variable of reputation; the product-level variable of past sales volume; and our key variable of interest, live chat conversations.

## 2.1. Web Trust Model

Ajzen and Fishbein (1975) proposed the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) to define and describe the relationships among beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. McKnight et al. (2002), applying TRA and integrating perceptions from multiple disciplines, proposed a TRA-based Web Trust Model to conceptualize the trust between an online customer and an e-vendor. In this integrative model, trust is defined as a composite concept that includes disposition to trust, institution-based trust, trusting beliefs, and trusting intentions. Disposition to trust refers to people’s general tendency to depend on others, and institution-based trust is the perceived trustworthiness of an online platform. These two factors are shown to be antecedents to trusting beliefs and trusting intentions. Trusting beliefs imply specific consumer perceptions of an e-vendor’s attributes, including competence, benevolence, and integrity. They are causally related to trusting intentions—that is, the willingness to depend on the e-vendor—and eventually, to trusting-related behaviors. Trusting beliefs as defined in the TRA-based Web Trust Model are similar to the “perceptions of the other” defined by Kee and Knox (1970) and “cognitive perceptions of trustworthiness” discussed by Mayer et al. (1995). In the trust-based consumer decision-making model proposed in Kim et al. (2008), antecedents of consumerperceived risk are categorized into cognition-based, affect-based, experience-based, and personality-oriented factors. In Kim’s framework, e-vendors’ reputation and buyers’ feedback are considered affect-based antecedents of perceived risk.

Our study follows the Web Trust Model in specifying well-delineated trusting beliefs: competence, benevolence, and integrity (Bhattacherjee 2002). Competence is related to the trustee’s capability to deliver the product on time and in quality condition, benevolence is about how much the trustee cares about the truster’s interests, and integrity reflects the honesty of the trustee. Integrity and benevolence are considered ethical traits, with be nevolence driven by altruism (Mayer et al. 1995) and integrity possibly driven by utilitarian considerations. The perception of seller competence, on the other hand, is often considered together with perceived risk as a cognitive antecedent to trust-related behaviors by consumers. In the Web Trust Model, trusting beliefs have received the most attention from information system researchers. This is because trusting beliefs can be affected by the design of web vendor systems. By introducing reputation systems and displaying past sales volumes, e-vendors provide venues for customers to acquire the information they need to evaluate the e-vendors and cultivate trust. Another reason for the focus on trusting beliefs is the proliferation of online marketplaces in which many e-vendors compete in the same market, within which customers hold a unified disposition to trust and an institution-based trust. Understanding the process of building trusting beliefs is critical both for e-vendors to improve their conversion rates and for platform owners to leverage electronic tools like live chat to stay attractive to sellers and buyers.

## 2.2. Reputation Systems

Online reputation systems are considered the best technological means for building trust in electronic markets (Dellarocas 2003). In an eBay-like reputation system, consumers leave a feedback rating after each transaction. The feedback can be positive (+1), neutral (0), or negative (–1), and the sum of the feedback ratings is the net feedback score, one of the most popular components in a seller’s feedback profile. Empirical evidence has found that most feedback ratings are positive because leaving a negative feedback is costly for consumers; thus, the net feedback score is highly correlated with the number of all ratings (Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002). The findings regarding the relationship between feedback scores and price, however, are inconclusive. Dewan and Hsu (2001) find that a higher feedback score is related to a higher price in the product category of postage stamps. A similar positive relationship is found between feedback scores and price in the product category of dolls (McDonald and Slawson 2002). Studies by Kauffman and Wood (2000) and Lucking-Reiley et al. (2007) found an insignificant relationship between feedback scores and price in the product category of coins on eBay. Finally, with respect to sales, feedback scores seem to have a positive effect on subsequent purchases (Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002), but such a link is weak (Cheung et al. 2014).

## 2.3 Information Cascade

Past sales volume is another piece of information that can affect customers’ purchasing behavior, according to information cascade theory. Information cascade refers to the phenomenon that, when consumers are uncertain about product quality, they follow the adoption decision of preceding individuals (Duan et al. 2009). According to information cascade theory, past sales volume drives more subsequent sales, resulting in a preferential attachment process. Ye and Fang (2013) investigate the role of past sales volume on new purchases, using data from eBay and Alibaba, and confirm the predictions of information cascade theory. They also used eye-tracking systems in a laboratory experiment to show that consumers spent more time looking at the area of the page where past sales volume is displayed and that they are more likely to purchase when past sales volume is high. Similarly, empirical evidence from Alibaba shows that historical sales volume has a positive impact on subsequent sales (Li et al. 2010). Liu (2006) finds that box office revenue comes mainly from the volume of word of mouth (WOM) rather than the valence. Similar results were also discovered in experimental settings. Huang and Chen (2006) controlled for historical sales volume at different levels and found a positive effect of past sales volume on future sales. Zhu and Zhang (2010) confirmed the role of review volume on product sales and further examined the moderating roles of product popularity and user experience. Park et al. (2007) examined the moderating role of involvement in how reviews affect purchase. Our work contributes to this stream of literature by investigating the substitutional or complementary patterns between past sales and live chat in terms of the impact on purchase decisions.

A limited number of papers have compared the impact of seller reputation and product past sales volume. Cheung et al. (2014) compared the effectiveness of information cues from transaction data versus cues from reviews and concluded that “actions speak louder than words”—that is, many people buying a product has more effect on conversion rates than many people writing a review for the product. Another study, which uses Alibaba data, showed that historical sales volume has a significantly positive effect, while feedback score has a weak influence on subsequent sales (Peng and Song 2014). Our work contributes to this stream of research because we not only confirm the role of feedback scores and past sales volume in affecting purchase decisions, but also de lineate their interaction patterns with live chat.

## 2.4. Live Chat as a Website Function

Behavioral information systems (IS) research looks at the role of online agents in facilitating trust in electronic markets. Aberg and Shahmehri (<sup>˚</sup> 2000) developed a live chat prototype system and performed a usability study with a field trial. They showed that participants in their study were enthusiastic about the live chat feature and enjoyed the human touch in their shopping experience. In an empirical study matching survey data to a conversation database, live chat was found to improve newcomer adjustment in the online banking industry (Kohler et al.¨ 2011). Qiu and Benbasat (2005) compared text-based live assistance with a system using text-to-speech voice and three-dimensional avatars in order to determine the best user interface design for live chat. Basso et al. (2001) compared an online store with no real-time interpersonal communication with other stores offering different forms of real-time communication. These studies all show the effectiveness of live assistance in building customer relationships and boosting sales. In addition to live chat using human agents, computer-based recommendation agents have been studied. Research has shown that communications, even with computer-based avatars that are purely technological artifacts, help consumers develop positive product attitudes (Holzwarth et al. 2006, Komiak and Benbasat 2006). Further, Kohler et al. (¨ 2011) examined the agent–customer interaction content and found a differential effect of functional content versus social content. In online labor markets, Hong et al. (2018) showed with granular data that live chat can serve roles of information provision and private information signaling. To the best of our knowledge, however, no prior work has investigated how the effects of live chat interact with the effects of other well-studied information cues such as feedback scores and past sales volumes. Our work contributes to this stream of studies by examining the substitutional or complementary patterns between live chat and existing information cues.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation 3.1. Live Chat

Live chat can be utilized to address a lack of consumer trust, through two primary mechanisms. First, the communication provides the e-vendor an opportunity to build an interpersonal relationship with customers (Maes 1994, Qiu and Benbasat 2005, Kohler et al.¨ 2011). By patiently listening to the customers’ concerns and responding in a professional and dedicated way, sellers signal to buyers their benevolence, a key factor of trusting beliefs. Second, live chat effectively reduces the perceived risk of purchase by mitigating information asymmetry through information provision (Hong et al. 2018). The real-time question and answer allow customers to acquire product information that is not displayed on the website, making consumers more confident that the product will meet their needs. This lowers perceived risk and the need for other product quality signals.

## 3.2. Reputation (Feedback Scores)

A couple of factors explain why feedback scores have been found to be a weak signal of product quality, leading to a weak relationship with purchase (Ye and Fang 2013). Sellers’ reputation, as reflected in their feedback scores, is an aggregate measure derived from their past sales of all products (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006). Since buyers rarely leave negative ratings because of the high cost of doing so (Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002), this measure in effect serves as a proxy for the sellers’ overall volume of transactions on the marketplace. Thus, feedback scores are usually considered a measure of sellers’ trustworthiness that encompasses honesty, competence, and benevolence. A seller usually offers a portfolio of products with varying levels of risk. A customer may see that the seller has a high feedback score and equate this with a high reputation. However, this reputation may have been built through the sale of completely different products from the one the customer intends to purchase. This problem becomes worse if the customer intends to buy high-risk products. For example, high feedback score accumulated primarily through selling iPad cases does not provide sufficient evidence that the seller is competent and honest as a vendor of tablets.

Nevertheless, the seller’s feedback score provides a good signal of the seller’s benevolence. Benevolent sellers are ones who act in the customers’ interest and are likely to provide high-quality presale and postsale services. These sellers honor customers’ trust and genuinely care about their welfare. Since benevolence is a trait inherent to the seller, a seller who is considered benevolent when selling iPad cases is likely to behave the same way when selling iPads. If anything, the seller will behave more benevolently when selling iPads because the profit margin is likely higher. Therefore, when customers shop for relatively high-risk products, feedback scores are a better signal for sellers benevolence rather than for honesty or competence.

## 3.3. Past Sales Volume

Unlike the seller feedback score, which aggregates data at the seller level, past sales volume reflects data at the level of a distinct product. Throughout this paper, a distinct product is considered a specific model sold by a certain seller. The same model sold by different sellers is considered a different product According to information cascade theory, past sales volume conveys the strongest signal about the quality of a product (Cheung et al. 2014, Peng and Song 2014). Since any previously reported dishonest behavior or unsatisfactory delivery would have stopped a seller from achieving a high sales volume (Cabral and Hortacsu 2010), a consumer is likely to believe that the seller is honest in product information disclosure and competent in delivering this product as promised if the product has a high sales volume.<sup>2</sup> Past studies show that people tend to update their trusting beliefs after their first-hand experience or observing secondhand WOM from third parties (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004). Therefore, the product quality signal from past sales volume is unlikely to be substituted by a live chat conversation because the seller himself has little incentive to reveal the low quality of his product, if there is any.

## 3.4. Hypothesis Development

Seller–buyer interaction has long been studied in marketing literature (Sheth 1976). Research has shown that salespeople can increase the probability of a consumer purchase by establishing trust and satisfaction through a face-to-face conversation (Crosby and Stephens 1987, Crosby et al. 1990, Anderson and Zahaf 2007). The benefit of face-to-face seller–buyer communication is threefold. First, when a salesperson provides tailored recommendations and adaptive assistance, customers obtain additional information that they do not get from seeing and touching the product. The additional information reduces perceived risk and eases customers concerns about product quality. Second, customers may receive signals from the salesperson that indicate his regard for the customer’s interests, which facilitates trusting beliefs. These signals are conveyed through body language, emotional cues, and the comprehensiveness of their responses. Third, the pleasure resulting from seller–buyer interpersonal communication enhances trusting intentions. However, these aspects of face-to-face interaction have become difficult to reproduce in a computer-mediated environment, creating challenges for sellers (Dewan and Hsu 2001, Ba and Pavlou 2002). The technology of live chat provides real-time human-to-human contact, making it the best substitute for a face-toface conversation between sales agents and buyers (Komiak et al. 2004). According to the Web Trust Model (McKnight et al. 2002), a digital interaction between sellers and buyers enhances both trusting beliefs and trusting intentions, leading to an increase in purchase intentions. Based on the above discussions, we hypothesize that live chat can boost rates of purchase.

## Hypothesis 1. Live chat boosts rates of purchase.

The remaining hypotheses hinge on the difference between the seller-level reputation score and the product-level past sales volume.<sup>3</sup> Comer et al. (1999) emphasized the multifaceted nature of consumer trust in e-vendors. They posit that trust has multiple referents that include the salesperson, product, and company. Since a reputable company may sell defective products, consumers evaluate information cues at both the seller and product level. Based on our early discussion, we would like to propose that feedback scores provide a better representation of seller characteristics like benevolence, while past sales volume has the relative strength to signal a seller’s honesty and competence to deliver the specific product on time and in quality condition. Below we develop Hypotheses 2 and 3. Hypothesis 2 is concerned with live chat’s potential substitution effect on information cues and Hypothesis 3 with the reinforcement effect.

Live chat provides the seller with the opportunity to express benevolence, making it a natural substitute for feedback scores. Because of the differential expectations that consumers hold for sellers with varying feedback scores, consumers may ask different questions to high-reputation versus low-reputation sellers. Livechat questions addressed to high-reputation sellers whom customers already perceive as benevolent are likely to be related to concerns that cannot be addressed through high feedback scores; these questions are by nature more difficult to answer than questions received by low-reputation sellers. Lowreputation sellers are likely to receive questions that give them an opportunity to offset their low feedback score by demonstrating benevolence: warm greetings, patience, emotion, and comprehensive responses. Thus, we expect low-reputation sellers’ marginal gain from live chat conversations to be higher than that of the high-reputation sellers. As such, we propose that there is a substitution pattern between live chat and reputation.

## Hypothesis 2a. Live chat has a substitution effect on seller feedback scores in driving sales.

Live chat can also reduce information asymmetry, lowering the perceived risk of a product. Perceived risk affects the need for product quality signal (Kim et al. 2009). For example, the perceived risk of buying an Apple tablet with a newly adopted retina display is high. Therefore, it is relatively more important for a seller to provide a strong quality signal (e.g., past sales volume) for this model. A live chat conversation can potentially mitigate such risks because the sales agent can provide additional product specifications like the power consumption difference between a traditional screen and a retina display. If live chat mainly plays the role of perceived risk reduction through information provision, we expect to see a lower dependency on product quality signals, the product past sales volume specifically, after a live chat conversation.

Hypothesis 2b. Live chat has a substitution effect on prod uct past sales volumes in driving sales.

If the benefits of live chat in information provision and benevolence signaling are both strong, we expect that both Hypotheses 2a and 2b hold. If one force is strong while the other is weak, we still predict the strong force to cause substitution. However, the weak force will lead to reinforcement, as we detail below Our next two hypotheses concern reinforcement ef fects based on risk aversion, which translates into a convex utility function in customers’ decision-making process. When risk-averse people are faced with decisions involving multiple factors, the factor with a low evaluation level has a high weight. For example, a customer may prefer a product with a past sales volume of 5 from a seller with reputation of 10 to a product with past sales volume of 1 from a seller with reputation of 14. In such a case, the low-level information cue becomes the hurdle preventing people from making purchases if, we propose, it does not get effectively substituted by live chat.<sup>4</sup>

The next hypothesis proposes that information provision may not be the main role of live chat. In the case of tablet sales, both manufacturer information and user-generated review videos are publicly available and easily accessible. Some websites even provide model-to-model comparisons for competing brands, including Apple and Samsung. Following this line of argument, live chat conversations are unlikely to reduce perceived risk through information provision. If live chat mainly serves the role of benevolence signaling but not quality assurance, then high-sales-volume products will benefit more from a live chat than low-sales-volume products. This is because a high-sales-volume product already signals its quality through the sales volume and only needs help from a live chat conversation to complete the last mile (i.e., benevolence signaling) before consumers make a purchase decision. Since live chat does not effectively make up for low sales volume through information provision, a product that has sold poorly will still struggle to win trust from the consumer. As a result, live chat reinforces the signal of past sales volume.

Hypothesis 3a. Live chat has a reinforcement effect on product sales volume in driving sales.

Alternatively, if we entertain the idea that the main functionality of live chat is information provision rather than signaling sellers’ benevolence, the reinforcement effects of live chat will take effect on sellers’ feedback scores. In this case, a seller with a low reputation can hardly make it up through live chat conversation and will have a hard time convincing consumers to purchase. However, a seller with a high reputation can more easily convert a consumer to purchase than his counterpart, because in this case the bottleneck in consumers’ purchase decision is about product quality, a type of concern that can be alleviated through live chat. As such, we propose the last hypothesis regarding the feedback score.

Hypothesis 3b. Live chat has a reinforcement effect on seller feedback scores in driving sales.

Theoretically, it is possible that live chat neither signals benevolence nor reduces information asymmetry. In that case, no substitution or reinforcement effect will be discovered. We do not specifically hypothesize that condition because of its low chance of occurrence and minimal economic impact.

## 4. Data

## 4.1. Research Context

We obtained our granular data from Alibaba, the largest online marketplace in China. Alibaba Group was founded in 1999. It was reported to have 446 million registered users by August 2017 and 10 million sellers by March 2016. It was considered a transformational technology that mobilized small business owners who previously could barely use the Internet to grow their business by transacting online. During the fiscal year of 2016, Alibaba Group had an annual revenue of about 15.69 billion U.S. dollars (Alibaba Group 2016, Smith 2017). Alibaba sells an enormous variety of products. Our data focus on the tablet markets. The online tablet market flourishes in China because Apple used to price its tablets higher in China than in Hong Kong or in many other areas and countries. In addition, Apple used to release its new tablets at different times in different countries, and China is usually among the last countries to see new Apple products on the market. The price difference and time gap stimulated the secondary market, which operates largely in online marketplaces, such as Alibaba. Just like eBay, Alibaba is a typical two-sided market in which sellers and customers both need to register to sell or buy. Each seller owns an online shop with a distinct link, and products are displayed in each online shop for consumers to browse and purchase Different sellers can sell the same product (e.g., the same iPad model) and price them differently.

Alibaba provides information on product pages to assist consumers’ decision making. We focus on the two most popular information cues: (1) the lastmonth sales volume for a specific product and (2) the feedback score for a specific seller. For ease of exposition, we use “sales volume” to denote “lastmonth sales volume” in the remainder of the manuscript. As mentioned earlier, we use “reputation” to refer to feedback score as it is considered an important reputational measure in the literature (Cabral and Hortacsu 2010). The calculation of product past sales volume is, intuitively, the number of the previous-month transactions of this model sold by this seller. Feedback score is calculated as the sum of a seller’s past ratings. Alibaba has an automatic mutual reviewing system that guarantees a rating after each transaction. Both the seller and the buyer can voluntarily give each other a feedback score within 15 days of a transaction. If only one party gives positive feedback and the other party remains silent, positive feedback will be given at the end of the 15- day period to the other party by the system. Each numerical rating takes three values: +1 for a positive rating, 0 for a neutral rating, and –1 for a negative rating. ## 4.2. Data Acquisition

We obtain our data from the Alibaba server. Our data contain users’ daily visit history to pages selling Apple and Samsung tablets from any seller, from March 1, 2013, to June 6, 2013. The daily visit history is aggregated at a user-product-day level. For example, we know that user i has visited product j on day t. These data do not, however, provide the sequence of pages browsed by individuals within a day, nor information about how users jump into a specific product page (e.g., from a price sorting tool or a referral). We also have these users’ live chat session data with tablet sellers from March 1, 2013, to June 6, 2013. The chat session data contain the timestamps for each live chat conversation and the number of messages sent and received by both parties in each conversation. We do not have the live chat content. Further, we have product sales data and review data, between January 1, 2013, and June 6, 2013, which can be used to calculate the last-month sales volume at the time of any live chat and its lags. In the sales data, the listed product price and the negotiated transactional price are both available in each transaction record. At the seller level, we have the weekly feedback score reports for all of the sellers involved in our observation. In addition, we have full clickstream data for involved users in March 2013. These full clickstream data are not limited to Apple and Samsung tablets. They include users’ product page requests and corresponding timestamps for all product categories, including computers, cosmetics, clothes, food, cell phones, etc. It is noted that we have the full clickstream data for one month but other data for over three months. We segment the time horizon into the presearch stage (March 1, 2013–March 31, 2013), search stage April 1, 2013–May 31, 2013), and postsearch stage (June 1, 2013–June 6, 2013). We select as our target customers who started searching for Apple or Samsung tablets in the search stage but not in the presearch stage or postsearch stage. We do not include users who started searching for Apple or Samsung tablets in March because we want to avoid left truncation. Left truncation occurs if a customer visited a product page and chatted with the seller prior to March 1, 2013, and returned to visit the product page again during our observational period. Since we do not have chatting data prior to March, we would incorrectly see this customer as one who has not conducted a live chat. Focusing on users who did not search for tablets but other products in March largely avoids such truncation bias because a user’s product search is usually a continuous process; users who started searching after April are very unlikely to have had a previous live chat about the same product before March. In the meantime, this data strategy allows us to obtain some user characteristics from the clickstream data to construct IVs that are not related to Apple or Samsung tablet searches. We do not include users who started searching for Apple or Samsung tablets after June 1 because we want to reduce potential right truncation. By leaving a minimum window of 6 days to observe users’ chatting and purchasing behavior after they browse the product page, we avoid missing the purchase record for browses made in the last few days of our observation. It is notable that the window for observing users’ live chatting or purchasing behavior varies according to users’ first browsing date. If a user browses a product on April 1, 2013, we have a 2-month-and-6-day window to observe his or her subsequent behavior. If a user browses a product on May 31, 2013, we have a 6-day window to observe his or her chatting and purchasing behavior. The choice of a 6-day window as the minimum is based on our conversation with data analysts from Alibaba, and the minimal window is expanded in a robustness check.

Our analyses are conducted at an individual– product level. For each product visited by an individual, we look at whether this individual eventually made a purchase of this product. Unlike some predictive clickstream analyses (Moe 2003), we are not interested in the exact point in time or in an action sequence that leads to a purchase from the seller. This is mainly due to the nature of our research goals to understand the impact of live chat and the limitation of the data.<sup>5</sup>

## 4.4. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation

4.4.1. Overview. The biggest challenge in determining the causal relationship between live chat and purchase is the endogeneity issue. Our analyses include four potentially endogenous variables. First, a buyer’s decision to initiate a live chat conversation is likely to be endogenous. Because Alibaba implements a reactive live chat strategy instead of a proactive one, sellers usually cannot talk to potential buyers unless the buyers request a live chat. A buyer’s decision to initiate a live chat can result from perceived risk factors not observed by the researchers. Omitted variables, such as consumers’ satisfaction level with a previous purchase, can result in endogeneity. Specifically, a customer’s dissatisfaction with a prior transaction may affect his or her live chat request decision and purchase decision at the same time. The endogeneity may also arise from simultaneity, whereby buyers initiate a live chat to check the estimated delivery time after forming their purchasing intentions. Second, price premium is likely to be an endogenous variable (Villas-Boas and Winer 1999) Premium is likely to be endogenous because it is, by design, endogenously set by the seller. Unlike official channels (e.g., the Apple store) that comply with a uniform price, the sellers on Alibaba set varying prices for the same model. The price premium is potentially correlated with marketing activities, such as promotions, that are not observed by researchers. These unobservable factors are correlated with customers’ purchase decisions, leading to endogeneity Third, past sales volume and seller feedback score are also likely endogenous variables. This is because both variables are likely to be correlated with seller or product characteristics that are not observable to the researchers but can potentially influence customers purchase decisions. The unobservables may include third-party warranties or high-quality product pic tures (McKnight et al. 2002).

To handle the potential endogeneity issues, we construct four sets of IVs and use a conditional mixedprocess (CMP) model to utilize these IVs in a system of five simultaneously estimated equations (Roodman 2009). The IVs allow us to more accurately estimate parameters in the purchase decision by satisfying the exclusion restrictions<sup>9</sup>: (1) correlation with the endogenous variable and (2) conditional independence from the error term (Rivers and Vuong 1988). First, we devised two instrumental variables for the chatting decision: (a) whether a customer’s first visit to a prod uct page was on a weekend and (b) the shopper’s intensity in browsing other electronic products prior to their tablet search—that is, products that are neither tablets nor Apple/Samsung products. Second, we use the average price of similar models in the competing brand as an instrument for the price premium. Third, we use the first difference of product sales volume as an IV for past sales volume. Finally, for sellers’ feedback scores, we construct a set of IVs to indicate whether the lagged feedback score is at the boundary of a feedback category (i.e., heart, diamond, silver crown, and gold crown). The feedback category is discrete and mapped from the continuous feedback score. The mapping policy is exogenously imposed by the platform, providing identifying sources. A detailed discussion of the IVs is presented in the following sections.

4.4.2. Chat. The independent variable $C h a t _ { i j }$ is likely to be endogenous for reasons listed in the previous section. To handle the endogeneity, we construct two instrumental variables, $I V w e e k e n \dot { d } _ { i j }$ and IVintensity<sub>i</sub>. $I V w e e k e n d _ { i j }$ is a binary variable that takes the value of 1 if customer i’s first visit to product j’s page was on a weekend. We assume that whether people start their search on a weekend is not directly related to their purchase probability. Given the high penetration rate and convenience of digital devices,<sup>10</sup> it is unlikely that people will wait until weekends to conduct product searches (Xu et al. 2016). However, live chat conversation is more likely to happen over the weekend because it is time consuming. Indeed, in Online Appendix A.1 where the ratios of chatted individual– product pairs over all records are presented, we see that people are more likely to request live chat over the weekends. IVweekend varies at an individual– product level, just like the endogenous variable $C h a t _ { i j }$ A similar instrument has been adopted in healthcare research (Hauck and Zhao 2011).

IVintensity , on the other hand, is user i’s intensity of search for electronic products that are neither tablets nor from Apple/Samsung prior to the Apple/ Samsung tablet search. The rationale is that a user’s search intensity for electronic products reflects his or her capability to acquire knowledge about electronic products through web browsing (Moe 2003). As will be supported in our model estimation, search intensity regarding other electronic products is negatively correlated with the decision to initiate live chat. This is because a higher capability of searching for electronic products through web browsing reduces the need for live chat (Gu et al. 2012). Such a search intensity shall not correlate with consumers purchase decision in the tablet market conditional on browsing.<sup>11</sup> The conditional independence is established with the 1-month time gap, the difference between searching and buying processes (Moe 2003), and the inability for other electronic products to substitute for tablets. We use the number of unique product pages that are neither Apple/Samsung cell phones nor tablets browsed by user i in the presearch period as the IV for decisions to chat. IVintensity varies at an individual level. Since users on average browse only 4.47 tablet product pages, an individuallevel IV provides enough variation to serve its identifying purpose.<sup>12</sup> In Section 8.4, we further corroborate our findings using the average time and clicks made in the presearch period on nonelectronic product pages as alternative IVs. The estimation results show almost identical patterns as in our main analysis.

4.4.3. Price Premium. The price premium is likely to be endogenous for the reasons that we described in Section 4.4.1. We construct a Hausman-type IV for price, $I V p r e m i u m _ { i j } ,$ as the average price for similar models in the competing brand (Hausman 1996, Lurkin et al. 2017). We define “similar” models as tablets that are of the same screen size (small if the screen is less than 8 inches, and large if the screen is 8 inches or greater) and of the same wireless connection type (Wi-Fi or 3G). The competing brand for Apple is Samsung and vice versa. The rationale is that competition drives the correlation between similar models of different brands such that the price of a big-screen Apple tablet with Wi-Fi is correlated with the price of a bigscreen Samsung tablet with Wi-Fi. The price of the Samsung tablet, however, does not have channels other than the competition to impose an impact on a user’s decision to purchase the big-screen Apple tablet with Wi-Fi. This IV varies at both a model level and an in dividual level. It varies at a model level because models with at least one different feature (e.g., size, wireless connection type, or brand) correspond to different sets of competing-brand models. Note that model is a higher-level category than product because the same model sold by different sellers is considered a different product. This IV also varies at an individual level because each individual browses products at different points in time. That is, the timelevel variation is absorbed into the individual-level variation.

4.4.4. Last-Month Sales Volume. Although most stud ies that use secondary data to understand online reputation systems have treated past sales volume as exogenous, it is likely to be endogenous (Tadelis 2016). As described in Section 4.4.1, unobservable factors that affect past sales, such as high-quality product pictures, may also affect new shoppers’ purchase decision. The IV that we construct for the past sales volume is $I V s a l e s _ { i j } ,$ the first difference of product j’s monthly sales volumes when individual i browses product j. Suppose the date when i visited j was t; $\bar { S } a l e s _ { i j }$ is j’s sales volume during the month prior to t. We also construct the lagged sales volume $\mathbf { \dot { \boldsymbol { S } } } a l e s _ { i j } ^ { \prime }$ for the second month before t. The difference between these two will be our instrument, $\begin{array} { r } { I V s a l e s _ { i j } = S a l e s _ { i j } - } \end{array}$ $S a l e s _ { i j } ^ { ' }$ . The first difference cancels out unobservable product fixed attributes like product picture quality, and accounts for any exogenous shock that may have happened the preceding month $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , promotions).

Such an exogenous shock influences the past sales volume but not current focal users’ purchase decisions directly if sales is stationary. The instrument variable we construct is similar to the first-difference variables used as instruments for equations in levels in a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator (Blundell and Bond 2000). We discuss in details in Online Appendix A.2 about how we adapt the GMM estimator to our context (Arellano and Bover 1995). We also present the result of a Harris–Tzavalis unit-root test to show the stationarity of past sales volume, an assumption for the validity of this IV.

4.4.5. Feedback Score. The feedback score is likely endogenous because it might be correlated with unobservable seller characteristics—for example, a seller’s loyalty program—that can drive both the feedback scores and focal customers’ purchase decisions. We leverage a special platform-imposed design to devise the instrument for feedback scores. To lower consumers’ cognitive burden in comprehending the numeric feedback score, Alibaba developed its graphic cues for different levels of feedback scores. There are four reputation categories: heart, diamond, silver crown, and gold crown. Within each reputation category, there are five subcategories, as depicted in Figure 3.

Sellers who have a five-heart reputation may expend effort to earn a one-diamond reputation because consumers usually perceive “diamonds” to be much higher than “hearts.” Therefore, a past feedback score level that will upgrade the seller to the next reputation category is likely to predict a higher feedback score in the next period. At the same time, being at the boundary feedback score level in the last period should not have an impact on consumer purchase in the following period for reasons other than the one listed above. Such an idea is similar to that of a regression discontinuity, where the cutoff values are equivalent to the boundaries of different reputation categories. We first include a set of dummies, the lagged reputation category dummies $( I V r e p 1 _ { i j } \ : - \ : I V r e p 4 _ { i j } )$ , to account for the general time trend of feedback score increase. The four lagged reputation category dummies correspond to different lagged reputation levels. For example, $I V r e p 1 _ { i j } = 1 \mathrm { i f } 4 \leq \overline { { R e p u t a t i o n _ { i j } } } < 2 5 1$ , where Reputation is the lagged feedback score. Similarly, $I V r e p 2 _ { i j } = 1$ if $2 5 1 \leq \overline { { R e p u t a t i o n _ { i j } } } < 1 0 0 0 1$ . We further construct a set of dummy variables as instruments for feedback scores: the lagged reputation boundary dummies $( I V r e p b o u n d 1 _ { i j } - I V r e p b o u n d 4 _ { i j } )$ . The lagged reputation boundary dummies take the value of 1 if the lagged feedback score is in the highest reputation subcategory. For example, in the reputation category of hearts, there are five subcategories. The highest subcategory is five hearts, which correspond to a feedback score between 150 and 251 (see Figure 3 for details). We then have that $I V r e p b o u n d  { \mathrm { 1 } } _ { i j } = 1  { \mathrm { ~ i f } }$ $1 5 0 < \overline { { R e p u t a t i o n _ { i j } } } < 2 5 1$ . The two sets of lagged dum mies will predict the feedback score in the current period, with IVrep predicting the general trend and IVrepbound predicting the boundary effect.

## 5. Model

We specify a model with a binary outcome $( P u r c h a s e _ { i j } ) ,$ a binary endogenous independent variable $( C h a t _ { i j } ) .$ and three continuous endogenous independent variables $( P r e m i u m _ { i j } , S a l e s _ { i j } ,$ , and $R e p u t a t i o n _ { i j } )$ . To account for endogeneity, we use a multiequation system approach. A multiequation system is used when estimating multiple equations with linkages in their error processes. Zellner’s (1962) seemingly uncorrelated regression equations (SURE) model is a special case of the multiequation system, in which each individual equation can be estimated consistently with ordinary least squares (OLS). The SURE model is also used to account for endogeneity with the endogenous determinant of one equation specified as the dependent variable of another equation (Costa-Font and Gil 2005). Roodman (2009) further developed the SURE estimator using a maximum-likelihood approach to allow each individual equation to have either a continuous or a binary outcome. Roodman’s model is named a conditional mixed-process (CMP) model and has been widely used in economics studies (Ferreira et al. 2011, Roodman and Morduch 2014) to account for endogeneity. Our main analysis treats chatting as a dichotomous decision. In this section, we go beyond the binary variable to investigate the number of messages in each conversation. Such scrutiny allows us to gauge the motivations for initiating live chat conversations. We define the log-transformed number of messages sent<sup>18</sup> by the seller as $C h a t M s g _ { i j } .$ . This variable is only applicable when a live chat conversation has been conducted. We restrict this extended analysis to 6,329 individual–product pairs that correspond to a live chat. Two models were estimated to descriptively understand the correlation<sup>19</sup> between purchase, information cues, and the number of messages in a chat conversation. The chatting model specifies message counts $( C h a t M s g _ { i j } )$ as the dependent variable and examines how sales and reputation measures correlate with message counts. The purchase model disentangles the relationship between purchase decisions and the message counts in a live chat. Before introducing the result of the models, we examine the heatmap where the x-axis denotes reputation and the y-axis denotes sales volume. Comparing the two heatmaps, we immediately see that more messages are exchanged in cases where the seller’s reputation is high but the product sales volume is low. While this model-free evidence did not consider other variables, it indicates that, for a seller with a strong reputation, more messages are not necessarily good.

## 8.1. Past Purchasing History

Our main analysis focuses on the trust between sellers and buyers. Hence, we did not include the institutionbased trust measure—past purchase count—which can potentially affect customers’ chatting and purchasing behavior. A higher past purchase count indicates that consumers have had positive shopping experiences (Bhattacherjee 2001), which usually translate into high institutional trust in the platform (Gefen et al. 2003). Experienced buyers with high past purchase counts will likely rely less on live chat because they already have a sense of security from past interactions on the platform (Zucker 1986). We split our sample into three segments: low past purchase count, medium past purchase count, and high pas purchase count. The cutoff points for the split are the 33rd and 67th percentile of the past purchase count. We rerun our model and present the results in Table 7. Live chat has significantly higher impact on con sumers with 86 past purchases as the coefficient for $C h a t _ { i j }$ is 2.894 in this group but 0.986 and 0.982, respectively, for the other two groups with more past purchases. The substitution and reinforcement patterns stay qualitatively the same as in our main model. The only difference is that the coefficient of sales is insignificant with high and low levels of past purchase experience, potentially because of customer segment heterogeneity. Overall, we can see that live chat has the potential to increase sales through trust building, given that institution-based trust and trusting beliefs are of the same nature.

## 8.2. Brand Effect

We leverage the difference between Apple and Samsung tablets to add support to our earlier argument that live chat better mitigates perceived risk from transacting with sellers perceived as less reputable. This reduces the need for a consumer to validate the benevolence of an Apple seller because it is unlikely that the customer will request postsale service from the Alibaba seller (Epstein 2015). As a result, the effect of live chat will reduce. By separately estimating the model for Apple and Samsung tablets, we show in Table 8 that Samsung tablets benefit more from a live chat as compared with Apple products. ## 8.3. Negative/Neutral Ratings

Studies have shown that negative ratings have a greater impact than do positive ratings (Ba and Pavlou 2002). If live chat can be used to substitute for reputation, it should be able to help sellers who suffer from historical negative or neutral ratings. Accordingly, sellers with more negative or neutral ratings are expected to benefit more from live chat. We split data based on the ratios of negative or neutral ratings, and run our main model for every subset of the data. The cutoff values are found through the 33rd and 67th percentile of negative/neutral ratings ratios. We find in Table 9 that sellers with high negative or neutral rating ratios benefit more from a live chat than do sellers with low ratios. This supports the postulation that live chat boosts purchase through its ability to make up for negative or neutral ratings.

## 8.4. Nonelectronics Clickstream IVs for Chat

In our main analysis, one IV for chat is the search intensity for other electronic products (IVintensity ). In this robustness check, we provide two alternative IVs that collectively capture users’ browsing behavior toward nonelectronic products in the presearch period. Specifically, we use IVavgtime to denote the discretized average time a user stays on nonelectronic product pages, and IVavgclick to denote the discretized average number of clicks made in nonelectronic product pages. For both variables, we discretize them into four bins. If users spend more time or make more clicks on product pages, they likely have better ability to search for information through web browsing. However, such search trait is irrelevant to their tablet purchase because of the product category and time difference. The estimation results stay unchanged as in our main model, with the marginal effect of live chat estimated to be 16.58%. We report the full estimation results with the alternative IVs in Online Appendix A.4.

## 8.5. Alternative Measures and Models

To further examine the robustness of our results, we alternate the ways we construct our variables. First, we reconstruct time-sensitive measures (e.g., reputation, sales volume) using customers’ most recent visit before the purchase, if there is one. This is different from our measures constructed using the date of customers’ first visit. Since most visits are conducted on the same day, only 12% of user–product pairs correspond to a time shift in constructing the variables. On average, the construction timing has been delayed for 1 $\breve { \mathrm { d a y . } } ^ { 2 1 }$ The results are reported in Online Appendix C.1, and our main findings stay robust.

Next, we alter the construction of price premium. In the main analysis, price premium is calculated as the price of the focal product minus the average price of the same model. The reference price, however, may not be the average price of the same model but, rather, the minimum price of the same model. We calculate this alternative measure of price premium and rerun our model. The results are reported in Online Appendix C.2, and our conclusions still hold.

In addition, we test an alternative construction of chatting decisions. In the main analysis, the dummy variable $C h a t _ { i j }$ takes the value of 1 if individual i talked to seller j as long as it happened before the purchase. In this robustness check, we constructed $C h a t _ { i j }$ based only on recent live chat conversations, because a live chat conducted 1 month ago may have little influence on the customer’s current purchase decision. We constructed three alternative versions of $C h a t _ { i j }$ based on users’ use of live chat within 1 week, 2 weeks, and 3 weeks, respectively, of the most recent product page visit. As shown in Online Appendix $\mathsf { C } . \bar { 3 } ,$ our results are not sensitive to the variable construction alternation. We notice that the impact of live chat exhibits a decreasing trend as we impose stricter restrictions for its construction. This shows that the live chat sessions conducted over 3 weeks ago still impact consumer purchase decisions, just at a lesser magnitude.

## 9. Conclusions

## 9.1. Theoretical Implications

Our analysis builds on and extends several theoretical frameworks about online purchase decisions under uncertainty (Gefen et al. 2003, McKnight et al. 2002, Pavlou et al. 2007, Kim et al. 2008). Although multiple works have discussed the impact of shopper seller interaction on trust or perceived risk, the forms of interaction studied have been limited to website design, recommendation agents, or other technological artifacts related to web quality and usability. Live chat, as a humanbased communication medium, has certain properties that other media do not exhibit. First, the synchronous nature of live chat makes it an especially effective tool for generating trust between buyers and sellers. We confirm past predictions with empirical data that human interaction results in better sales performance (McKnight et al. 2002, Gefen et al. 2003). Second, we find that seller–customer communication is moderated by existing information cues (McKnight et al. 2002). Our findings, that live chat is positively moderated by sales volume and negatively moderated by reputation, deepen the understanding of reputation systems and information cascade theory. Third, we find that consumers are more likely to initiate a live chat conversation when the perceived risk of purchase is high, and a lengthy conversation is conducted when the product has a low sales volume and the seller has a strong reputation. These findings contribute to the consumer behavior literature (Kim et al. 2008).

## 9.2. Managerial Implications

Our results have implications for the online marketplace. Through the positive effects of live chat, we can conclude that the marketplace can facilitate more transactions through live chat tools. Platforms should develop and invest in technology that promotes interactions with potential buyers in order to alleviate information asymmetry and to strengthen customer relationship. The substitution and reinforcement effects of live chat on existing information cues imply that there may be opportunities for platforms to charge sellers for use of live chat. Although this study looks at reactive live chat, whereby the seller can communicate with the buyer only if a request is initiated by the buyer, there is also increasing interest in proactive live chat utilities, in which the sellers can approach the customer. Because live chat has a substitution effect for lowreputation sellers, these sellers may be willing to pay a fee to proactively connect with potential buyers. When the seller detects that a consumer is browsing its popular product, the seller has more incentive to pay to initiate a live chat conversation, reinforcing its existing sales performance.

Sellers can also benefit from our analysis. Our findings indicate that sellers who lack a high feedback score can optimize their use of the live chat feature to establish benevolence. This can be done by being patient and helpful in the live chat conversation. Similarly, when selling popular products with high past sales volume, sellers should show the positive eWOM from previous buyers to reinforce past performance. We also find that live chat is more effective for increasing sales of products that carry a higher perceived risk. Apple products benefit less than Samsung products because of Apple’s better warranty program. We suggest that, when selling Samsung products, the seller should spend more time and effort to promote their product warranty and postsale service. In addition, given the high cost of hiring live chat agents, we can recommend specific areas of focus to optimize training. Our findings show that consumers may carry out lengthy conversations with high-reputation sellers about products that do not yet have a high sales volume. Such conversations imply concerns that are hard to address by live chat, in which case live chat is economically unattractive. Sellers can optimize their use of live chat by providing agents with a detailed Q&A guide geared toward such potentially hard-to-convert customers. Last but not least, our findings have implications for the unsolved problem of establishing sellers’ early reputation. Focusing on a flagship product helps sellers to accumulate sales volumes and earn their initial trust from the market. The critical quality signal from sales volume is not well substituted by live chat.

## 9.3. Limitations and Concluding Remarks

Our work is not without limitations. Because of our inability to access the live chat content, we could not conduct a thorough analysis to understand how different live chat content completes the tasks of sub stitution and reinforcement of existing information cues. Our context is restricted to Alibaba, where live chat is a default function. In other e-commerce settings, sellers may have the option to install such a function and choose between proactive and reactive live chat systems. Studying live chat in more diverse contexts may lead to deeper understanding of how to optimize the use of technology-based interpersonal interaction between sellers and buyers. We are also restricted as to the nature of secondary data, where no exogenous shock happened to prevent some sellers or buyers from using live chat. Finally, despite our multistage data strategy to minimize the potential bias from truncation, we cannot rule out the possibility of left- and right-truncation given the length of our data.

With that said, our study is one of the first empirical investigations of the impact of live chat on online purchase decisions. Given the increasing popularity of live chat tools, our work is urgent and relevant. We not only directly examine the return of live chat in terms of boosting purchases but also look at how live chat is moderated by existing information cues. We discover that live chat substitutes for feedback score and reinforces past sales volume. These findings underscore the difference between these two widely used information cues, and uncover the essence of live chat. While live chat can be used as a way to reduce information asymmetry and build trust between two strangers in an online platform, it can hardly replace quality signals sent from third parties— namely, other buyers. This insight complements existing studies and enhances our understanding of the trust building process in e-commerce contexts. We believe that live chat is a critical element that has been overlooked in the marketing matrix of e-commerce. We look forward to more studies that wil allow e-commerce platforms and sellers to understand live chat and put it into the best use.
