# Predicting Web Site Audience Demographics Using Content and Design Cues

The size and dynamism of the Web pose challenges for all its stakeholders, which include producers or consumers of content as well as advertisers who want to place advertisements next to relevant content. A critical piece of information for producers/publishers of content as well as advertisers is the demographics of the consumers who are likely to visit a given web site. In this article we explore predictive models that attempt to deduce the demographics of the audience of a web site using cues embedded in the design or the content of its homepage. We find that it is possible to efectively predict diferent types of demographics of consumers of web sites on the basis of the suggested approach. Through a statistical analysis we observe that several design elements and the content difer significantly among web sites dominated by consumers of diferent demographic classes. We also suggest the use of an ensemble classifier that combines the content and design cues with the goal of further improving the prediction performance.

Key words : machine learning, predictive analytics, text classification, audience demographics

## 1. Introduction

The Web is a powerful medium for connecting the producers of information with the consumers of information. In addition to the primary content, information is displayed on web pages through advertisements. For advertisers who wish to place their advertisements (e.g., banner ads) on a web site, a profile of likely consumers of the web site is valuable. For example, a computer manufacturer interested in placing online advertisements may have diferent types of advertisements for diferent demographics (e.g., male versus female). If the manufacturer (or an advertising exchange on its behalf) could predict the demographics of the likely audience of various web sites, it would be able to match demographic-specific advertisements to the appropriate sites.

Several studies [41, 67, 64, 37] have highlighted consumer demographics as one of the important factors that contribute to the attitude toward advertisements. However, given the large size and dynamic nature of the Web, any such process of matching advertisements to web sites must scale to millions of sites. To seek efective advertisement venues, some advertisers use methods such as ad networks or ad exchanges. Both ad networks and ad exchanges are intermediate platforms that help an advertiser and a publisher to connect with each other without significant search costs. They adopt various methods [61, 6, 47] for matching selected advertisements with users of Internet content. One popular method is behavioral targeting, which seeks to profile users on the basis of their behavior. Behavioral targeting uses data on users (e.g., web pages they visit, the amount of time they view each web page, demographics) that is collected and forwarded by publishers for ad targeting. For that reason, such techniques require a lot of costly data (with potential privacy concerns) from publishers, users, or advertisers.

A mechanism to predict audience demographics can be helpful for content publishers as well. For authors and publishers of web sites, it is important to know the profile of likely consumers (e.g., high-income males) of the information they produce. This knowledge of the likely consumers would allow producers to better design and market their web sites. While authors may already have the target audience in mind when they design web sites, it is still important and useful to predict whether the designed web sites will actually be visited (mainly) by the targeted audience.

Previous research work attempted to estimate the profile of likely consumers of web sites by aggregating and using data such as the click stream and user registration information. However, as noted earlier, such data is costly to gather and evokes privacy concerns. In addition, it is unlikely to be directly accessible to a third party (such as advertisers) that wants to estimate the profile of likely consumers of the web sites. Therefore the approaches that use such data cannot be applied to the web sites for which users’ behavioral and demographic data are not available. Moreover, the data collected using cookies and user registrations is plagued by incomplete and inaccurate information. Hence, in the current study, we explore an automated approach that estimates the demographics of the audience of web sites based solely on the design elements and/or the content of the sites that are locally available on the homepage of each web site. If advertisers (or content publishers) can successfully predict the demographics of the audience of web sites on the basis of only the local cues, they will be free from the aforementioned concerns related to the click stream (cookie) and user registration information. In addition, the automated approach that uses only the publicly accessible local cues would also fix the scalability issue of the previous methods. Hence there are several advantages of using just the design elements and/or the content to predict the demographics of the audience of the web site. However, a key unanswered question is whether efective predictive models for this purpose can indeed be created with such limited information. Through a systematic exploration of the suggested automated approach to predicting the demographics of the audience of web sites, we provide various insights. Firstly, we present empirical evidence for our conjecture that there are demographic-related cues in both the design and the content elements of homepages of web sites. In particular, we find that some of the design variables difer significantly between homepages of web sites belonging to diferent demographic classes (e.g., college educated versus non-college educated). Also, the content similarity between homepages of web sites belonging to the same demographic classes is significantly higher than the content similarity between homepages of web sites belonging to diferent demographic classes. Next, we exploit the design and content diferential between demographic classes to build a model that predicts the demographics of the audience of a web site.

We note that some previous studies [26, 15, 62, 46, 71] explored the diferences in the design and content of web sites that cater to diferent demographic groups. These studies primarily focused on manually intensive analysis and interpretation (such as hermeneutics) that cannot scale to a large number of web sites. Unlike these studies, the focus of the current work is on an automated approach for predicting the demographics of the audience of web sites. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that has shown that the demographics of the audience of a web site can be efectively predicted solely on the basis of either design elements or content of the web site.

To validate the suggested approach, we identified sample web site URLs, each of which is labeled as belonging to one of the demographic classes (e.g., male/female). We identified sample web sites for four diferent demographic dimensions (gender, age, salary, and education). From each web site, we programmatically crawled the homepage and derived variables of interest from it. Using the computed variables across web sites, we performed empirical analyses to understand the demographic cues contained within variables that are local to the homepage of the web site. We then subsequently built demographic classifiers using modeling techniques such as naive Bayes, support vector machines (SVMs), and logistic regression. Because of the local and computationally tractable nature of information (i.e., variables computed from the homepage of web sites) used in the proposed approach, it can be expected to scale well to the large size of the Web.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the research background based on a review of related studies. And we explain the data that we use for emprical analysis and building (and testing) our predictive models in Section 3. We then describe the details of our experiments in Section 4. In Section 4.1, we address the selection of design variables of interest and describe their measurement. This is followed by a description of how content is represented for our analysis and modeling. In Section 4.2, we describe the classification schemes that we use for building predictive models. In Section 5, we present our experiment results and related findings. The results of the empirical analyses using design and content variables are presented in Section 5.1. We then describe the prediction performance of the design- and content-based classifiers in Section 5.2. In Section 5.3, we describe and test an ensemble approach that linearly combines predictions from both design- and content-based classifiers. Finally, we draw conclusions from our results and describe future works in Section 6.

## 2. Research Background

The most commonly used consumer profile variables are those associated with the demographics of the consumers. Demographics are well-established variables for market segmentation [68].

Marketing campaigns and advertisements are often positioned for a certain demographic. The role of demographics such as gender is well known in terms of diferential response to advertisements [26, 5, 17]; hence knowing the demographics of the likely audience of a web site is valuable for advertisers. To obtain demographic profiles of their consumers, web sites require consumers to register. The registration process may include questions on demographics such as the age or gender of the consumer. The web site can subsequently track the browsing activity of the registered consumers using cookies or IP address information [10, 19, 61]. The Web usage or click stream data along with the demographics of the consumers may be used to identify the typical audience of a given site. However, in addition to privacy concerns, this process is plagued by a number of potential problems, such as (1) registration is necessary to collect the data, (2) registration data may be inaccurate, (3) registered consumers may browse anonymously, (4) cookies used for tracking may be deleted by the consumers, and (5) cookies need to be matched to people since one person may use multiple computers and multiple people may use one computer. Moreover, a Web usage approach will not be able to provide the profile of the likely audience of a new web site. Several studies have attempted to find patterns in Web usage or click stream data that include the sequence of web pages visited by a user in one or more sessions to profile the user. The focus of such studies is to profile a given user in terms of understanding the user’s topical interests for personalization/recommendations [36, 12] or finding the user’s demographics [27] and even to uniquely identify the user [70]. Unlike these studies, the unit of analysis of our work is a web site and not a person. In other words, we are interested in finding the demographics of the likely audience of a given web site and not the profile of a given person. Also, our approach does not use click stream or other Web usage data for predictions. The approach that we suggest estimates the demographics of the audience of a web site using design- and content-based variables that are automatically extracted from information that is locally available on the homepage of the web site. Hence our approach does not encounter the problems described above in collecting Web usage data and can be a low-burden complement to the more intrusive registration/cookie-based techniques to predict the demographics of the audience of web sites.

Several studies explored the relationship between demographics and web site design [62, 46] and between demographics and language [13]. For example, in an empirical study of the impact of gender and culture on web site design preferences, Simon [62] found that the preferences of males and females difer with regard to web site design elements such as the color or layout of web sites. Similarly, Moss et al. [46] suggest that males and females exhibit diferent preferences with regard to web site elements such as the navigational structure, the language used, and visual aspects.

Our approach relies on local design variables and content representations that are computationally simple to extract and quantify from a web site. We use them to build models to predict the demographics of the audience of web sites. In addition, we also perform statistical analysis to identify the demographic cues captured by the design and content variables. The design variables we are interested in are (a) word count, (b) links-to-words ratio, (c) numbers-to-words ratio, (d) links to social networking sites, and (e) readability of the web page. These variables were chosen because all of them have been used in previous studies on web site design and Web/text mining [50, 48, 45, 23, 55, 38, 2, 51, 54, 52]. More generally, text and links on web pages have been used for prediction tasks such as identifying competitors [53] and detecting fake web sites [1]. Each of the design variables we have identified can be easily computed for arbitrary web sites, thus allowing our approach to scale to a large number of web sites. We represent web site content in the space of a vocabulary of terms where each term represents a dimension. We associate a weight with each dimension for a given site, thus representing the site with a vector of term weights. A term weight-based bag-of-words representation is popular in the text mining literature for capturing the underlying themes in text. There are also studies that test the relationship between demographics (of authors or readers) and the content of documents and web sites. For example, using a large set of documents from the British National Corpus, Argamon et al. [3] found statistical diferences in the writing styles of male and female authors. Newman et al. [48] also examined gender diferences in language use by analyzing a database of more than 14, 000 text files, and found that women used more words related to psychological and social processes, while men referred more to object properties and impersonal topics. We emphasize that, unlike these studies, we are not attempting to predict the demographics of the author but rather are attempting to predict the demographics of the audience of a web site. An interpretive work by Zahedi et al. [71] explored the masculinity and femininity of web documents by focusing on the inherent gender-related quality of web documents and how it may be identified through the content of the documents. Zahedi et al. [71] used a hermeneutic approach to analyze the text of web documents to associate various feminine and masculine attitudes to web documents in an attempt to identify their predominant androgyny. While being insightful, the approach they suggest is manual and time-consuming. Such an approach cannot be expected to scale to millions of web sites. However, their work suggests that textual signals within web sites may indicate the inherent gender bias toward the sites. Furthermore, it raises the possibility of the appearance of attitudes related to other demographics (e.g., age) in text as well. This is an important theoretical argument for us to attempt an automated design to identify gender and other demographic bias from the text of web sites.

## 3. Data

For our analysis, we use the data provided by Quantcast. Quantcast is one of leading Web analytics companies and provides its services to a large number of web sites. Given its leadership in online audience measurement [59], it provides a valuable gold standard for measuring the performance of alternative techniques such as ours. Quantcast leverages cookie data from several million participating web sites to provide “people-based demographics” [58] of the web site audience. One of the key innovations claimed by Quantcast is its technology that maps cookies to people [58]. Quantcast is clearly aware of the limitations of cookies (e.g., people-cookie mismatch caused by cookie deletion and use of multiple machines) and addresses them by adjusting the direct measurement inputs from cookies using its proprietary estimation/modeling technique. Its technique considers several factors that impact translation from cookies to people, including the “time period of cookie under analysis,” “typical visit frequency of visitors,” “percentage of usage from work or public machines,” and “type of sites,” from which the company can estimate the degree of the bias when it translates unique cookie counts to people [57]. In addition to cookies, Quantcast collects other data from multiple complementary sources (such as the Web usage data from panels as well as the anonymous identifier information provided by many of its quantified publisher partners) and triangulate the data to more accurately estimate visitors and their characteristics (e.g., demographics) for each web site [57]. While such technology should (at least partially) solve one of the problems that we listed earlier (people-cookie mismatch), the other problems with using Web usage data can be expected to persist. Quantcast’s method still involves issues of high cost, privacy concerns, and limited scalability because of its dependence on the data from cookies, panels, etc., which demand additional eforts in acquisition and triangulation. In addition, the number of web sites from which Quantcast collects the data for prediction is limited to the publishers/partners that share data with the company [58]. However, the availability of Quantcast data provides us with an opportunity to explore and validate our models against painstakingly gathered ground truth. The sophistication and breadth of Quantcast’s methods also provides additional validity to the use of Quantcast’s data as a gold standard from the industry.

Several other studies have used data from Quantcast to acquire the demographic labels of web sites to understand various phenomena on the Web. For example, Culotta et al. [14] used Quantcast data to study whether the demographics of visitors to each web site can be predicted on the basis of the Twitter followers of the site. Using data from Quantcast, Hannak et al. [24] tested whether using the search history of an individual that is demographically biased is efective for successful personalization of a Web search. Although the goals of the studies (including ours) difer, they are all able to run experiments based on the reliable demographic labels of web sites from Quantcast.

Given this role of Quantcast data as a gold standard, by definition, our approach cannot be shown to outperform Quantcast’s method. However, it allows us to show that our methods, which are based on local cues (low cost and devoid of privacy concerns), can achieve competitive performance as compared with an industry-leading data collection process (high cost and full of privacy of concerns) and be (at least) a good complement to Quantcast’s method by addressing its major shortcomings, such as scalability. Hence we use Quantcast’s demographic data to build and test our predictive models, which rely purely on design elements or web page content as inputs. Building a predictive model using limited Quantcast data is a one time cost that can be amortized over a large number of web sites on which the predictive model may be applied.

From Quantcast we collected data for four diferent demographic dimensions: gender (male/ female), income (\$0-60,000/>\$60,000), age (3-34 years/>35 years), and education (no college education/college education or graduate school). Hence we have demographic classes (e.g., male/female) corresponding to each demographic dimension. We consistently divided each demographic dimension into two classes so that we can compare the performances of predictive models across diferent demographic dimensions by maintaining the same class priors (50%) for all dimensions. Thresholds for dividing each dimension into two classes were also carefully decided by our choosing the dividing point provided by Quantcast that is closest to the median value of the US population for each dimension. For each demographic class (e.g., no college education) we obtain the URLs of the top 120 web sites<sup>1</sup> by composition. Composition is measured as the percentage of the target demographic class among all visitors to the web site. We restrict the web sites to only those that have a minimum reach of 1 million US users. Hence we have web sites that represent a clear bias toward the target demographic class, and the substantial reach of these web sites assures us of the quality of the measured bias (i.e., composition). In summary, we have close to 240 web sites for each demographic dimension, with half of those web sites having a bias toward each demographic class within the dimension. We filter out adult-content web sites and attempt to download the homepage of each of the web sites using a Web crawler. For each demographic dimension (e.g., gender), the downloaded homepages along with their associated demographic classes (e.g., male or female) form a data set. Hence we have four diferent data sets corresponding to four diferent demographic dimensions that we use for our experiments. Each data set is randomly split into a training set (66%) and a held-out testing set (34%). We build the predictive model using the training set web sites and test the model on the testing set web sites. No information from the testing set web sites is used to build the model, and hence the testing set provides a holdout sample for evaluation. A brief overview of the data collection and modeling procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

## 4. Design of the Proposed Method

## 4.1. Design Variables and Content Representation

Palmer [50] used word count as an important measure to evaluate web site design characteristics and quality. Newman et al. [48] considered word count and the proportion of numbers within a document (i.e., numbers-to-words ratio) as variables to explain gender diferences in language use. The corpus they analyzed was not from the Web but from general writings of numerous people who participated in their study. Also, their conclusions were about the gender of the authors and not the gender of the audience of those writings. However, it would be interesting to see whether these variables can still be of use to capture the demographic characteristics of a web site audience. The relationship between numerical information and gender has also been explored in the literature. Jackson et al. [30] suggest that males attend more to numerical information than females. Educational literature has also emphasized diferences in the “attitude” of the genders toward mathematics in general [28, 21, 29, 11, 43, 49]. We expect that this diference in attitude between males and females may be, in part, reflected in their information-seeking behavior on the Web. We compute the number of words appearing on the homepage of a given web site and use that as the word count of the site. We count only alphanumeric words and ignore numbers. Similarly, we count the number of numbers appearing on the homepage and compute the numbers-to-words ratio for the web site.

The links-to-words ratio is another interesting variable that may be used to explain the difering design preferences of diferent demographic audiences. For example, several studies suggest that diferent age groups have diferent navigational preferences on web sites. Meyer et al. [45] found that during Web browsing, older users tend to wander, while younger users find the information they are looking for relatively quickly. Grahame et al. [23] suggested that older users are more hampered than younger users when faced with a large number of links on a web page. In addition to those studies, the links-to-words ratio, in general, has been widely used in various studies to diferentiate between content and navigation pages. In other words, a visitor’s vocabulary knowledge, quantitative inclinations, and information-seeking behavior may afect his or her preference for web sites (in terms of the information volume, mix, structure, and complexity). We compute all of the variables for a web site on the basis of the homepage of the web site.

Pingdom [55] showed that the popular social networking sites have more female users than male users. In addition, in her study of the influence of gender and psychological factors on the use of social networking sites, Barker [4] reported that there is the relationship between the use of social networking sites and an individual’s perception of his/her social status. Similar claims are made in other studies [20, 39]. Furthermore, Barker [4] also suggests that while males use social networking sites mainly for learning, females use social networking sites for communication with peer-group members, which is a (more) common motivation for use of social networking sites. Hence we feel that it would be interesting to test potential efects of (links to) social networking sites on attracting the diferent gender and socioeconomic groups to the sites. For this purpose we computed the number of links to social networking sites as another design variable.

Finally, we also considered the readability of web site content as one of the design variables. Logan and Johnston [38] and AL-Khataybeh and Al-Masri [2], in their study of gender diferences in reading ability, consistently claim that females have better reading comprehension than males. In addition, as discussed earlier, there are also some other studies [7, 25, 60] that report the efect of socioeconomic factors on vocabulary development and/or information-processing capability. Hence it would be interesting to see whether the readability of web site content is a valuable predictor of the demographics of the audience of a web site. To measure the readability, we use the Flesch-Kincaid readability index [34]. The index uses the ratio of the number of total syllables, words, and sentences to calculate the Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES). The values of this index range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating it being easier to comprehend text. The FRES is calculated as follows:

$$
F R E S = 2 0 6. 8 3 5 - 1. 0 1 5 (\frac {\mathrm{totalwords}}{\mathrm{totals sentences}}) - 8 4. 6 (\frac {\mathrm{toversyllables}}{\mathrm{toverswords}}).\tag{1}
$$

Table 1 summarizes the design variables that we use. We represent the web site content by extracting the terms appearing on its homepage and computing the weights of each term using a standard term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) representation. Each web site is thus represented as a vector of term weights. We describe this representation in greater detail in

Section 4.2.

While our local variables and representations can be easily extended to other web pages of a web site, we currently restrict our computation to the homepage. We note that the homepage is typically the most popular page (in terms of inlink counts or PageRank) of a web site and hence a good candidate for extracting the design and content cues [65, 51]. Moreover, it is interesting to explore if the demographics of the audience of a web site can be efectively predicted solely on the basis of the design and content cues on its main page.

## 4.2. Page Demographics Classifier

Each of our data sets consists of downloaded pages that fall into two classes (e.g, male or female). A random portion of each data set (training set) is used for building a page demographic classifier that can discriminate between the two classes of the corresponding demographic dimension. Hence we build and test four diferent classifiers corresponding to the four diferent demographic dimensions (gender, age, income, education).

As introduced earlier, we study the efect of two diferent types of cues, design elements and content, for the prediction of audience demographics. To study the design elements, each web site is represented by a set of coordinates that corresponds to the selected design variables. As described earlier, the profile of each web site is composed of the following five design variables: word count, links-to-words ratio, numbers-to-words ratio, links to social networking sites, and readability of a web site (see Table 1). For each downloaded homepage, the values of the five design variables are computed to build a profile vector for the corresponding web site. Given those values, a training set web site t is represented as a vector $\mathbf { w } _ { t } = ( w _ { w c } , w _ { l t o w } , w _ { n t o w } , w _ { s n } , w _ { r d } )$ , where wc represents word count, ltow represents links-to-words ratio, ntow represents numbers-to-words ratio, sn represents links to social networking sites, and rd represents readability.

Similarly, for the content, each downloaded homepage is represented by a set of coordinates that corresponds to vocabulary terms. These vocabulary terms are strictly obtained from the training examples. In particular, we first obtain all of the words (except stop words such as and, or, and the) appearing in all of the training set pages. We reduce each word to its morphologically invariant stem such that words such as technology and technologies reduce to the same term. We then count the document frequencies of each term. The document frequency, $d f _ { i }$ , of term i is the number of training set pages in which i appears. We filter the terms that appear in more than 90% of training set pages since such terms are unlikely to be helpful in discriminating between demographic classes. We also filter terms that appear in less than 1% of the training set pages since they are likely to be very specific (e.g., proper nouns) or misspellings and hence may not be helpful for a general classification model.<sup>2</sup> After these steps, several thousand terms appear in the vocabulary for each of our data sets. Hence the classification problem remains a high-dimensional one. Given a vocabulary V of n terms, a training set web site t is represented as a vector $\mathbf { w } _ { t } = ( w _ { t 1 } , w _ { t 2 } , \ldots , w _ { t n } )$ in ${ \mathcal { R } } ^ { n }$ space. The weight of each term i $( w _ { t i } )$ for a training set site t is based on a standard TF-IDF representation [40] of the homepage of the web site. Using the profile vectors based on design elements and content generated from training web sites, we build separate design-based and content-based classifiers for each demographic dimension. We then evaluate their performance for the prediction of the audience demographics using the testing web sites. To build the predictive models, we use three diferent classification schemes: naive Bayes, SVMs, and logistic regression, all of which are popular classification methods in text classification (naive Bayes, SVMs) and/or empirical research (logistic regression) [16, 22, 31, 35, 42].

4.2.1. Bayesian Approach The naive Bayes classifier is a popular text classification technique [35, 42, 16]. It assumes that the distribution of terms appearing in a document is independent given the class of the document. Hence, using the Bayes rule, we can estimate the probability of a testing web site (also represented as a term vector ${ \bf w } _ { h } )$ belonging to a class (e.g., female) through an equation such as

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (c l a s s = f e m a l e | \mathbf {w} _ {h}) = \frac {\prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p (w _ {h i} | c l a s s = f e m a l e) \cdot \operatorname* {P r} (c l a s s = f e m a l e)}{p (\mathbf {w} _ {h})},\tag{2}
$$

where the prior probabilities such as $\operatorname* { P r } ( c l a s s = f e m a l e )$ are based on the proportion of training set web sites from the given class and $p ( \mathbf { w } _ { h } )$ is a normalization factor that will appear as a denominator for both classes (therefore it doesn’t need to be computed). In other words, the training of the naive Bayes classifier essentially involves estimating the distributions of terms weights (or weights of design variables) given the class $( \mathrm { e . g . } , p ( w _ { h i } \vert c l a s s = f e m a l e ) )$ using the training set web sites (homepages). Instead of using a parametric method where we would need to assume a certain type of distribution (say, normal) for the term weights, we use a non-parametric kernel density method whose implementation is available in the Weka machine learning software suite [33, 69]. As suggested earlier, we train the classifier using the training set web sites and test the trained classifier on held-out testing set web sites. We perform the training-testing process 10 times for each demographic dimension, randomly splitting the corresponding data set between training and testing pages each time. This allows us to compute the statistical stability of the results.

4.2.2. Support Vector Machine The SVM approach frames the classification problems as a quadratic programming optimization problem [66]. SVMs are especially well suited for highdimensional contexts such as text classification [32, 31]. We follow the training-testing process described in Section 4.2.1. Each of the training and testing web sites is represented as vectors in the n-dimensional space, where n is the number of terms in the vocabulary (or the number of design variables). Using the training web sites, the SVM classifier finds a hyperplane that maximizes the separation between the given classes (e.g., male/female). The “learned” hyperplane is then used to classify web sites in the testing data. More specifically, the hyperplane is of the form:

$$
y (\mathbf {w} _ {t}) = \beta_ {0} + \beta^ {T} \mathbf {w} _ {t} = 0,\tag{3}
$$

where $\mathbf { w } _ { t }$ is a vector representation of web page $t ,$ and $\beta _ { 0 }$ and $\beta$ are the parameters that determine the position and orientation of the hyperplane in the ${ \mathcal { R } } ^ { n }$ space. The training set with $p$ pages can be represented as $\left\{ { \bigl ( } \mathbf { w _ { 1 } } , c _ { 1 } { \bigr ) } , { \bigl ( } \mathbf { w _ { 2 } } , c _ { 2 } { \bigr ) } , \ldots , { \bigl ( } \mathbf { w } _ { p } , c _ { p } { \bigr ) } \right\}$ , where $\mathbf { w } _ { t }$ is a vector representation of page t that belongs to class $c _ { t } \in \{ - 1 , 1 \}$ (the two possible classes such as male/female can be coded as $- 1 / 1 )$ . The SVM algorithm finds a hyperplane that maximizes the margin or separation between the two classes. The margin maximization problem can be written as [8]

$$
\arg \max _ {\beta , \beta_ {0}} [ \frac {1}{\parallel \beta \parallel} \min _ {t} \{c _ {i} (\beta_ {0} + \beta^ {T} \mathbf {w} _ {t}) \} ].\tag{4}
$$

The above problem can be rewritten as a quadratic programming problem, and the application of Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions reveals that the solution to the margin maximization problem is dependent only on the points/vectors (in our case these vectors represent training web sites) closest to the hyperplane. These closest vectors are called support vectors and they greatly reduce the complexity of the problem. A popular sequential minimal optimization algorithm for solving the above optimization problem was suggested by Platt [56]. Once the optimal hyperplane has been learned, vector representation of a testing site, $\mathbf { w } _ { t } ,$ is applied to Eq. (3). The output $y ( \mathbf { w } _ { t } )$ is positive when the page lies on one side of the hyperplane (female) and negative when it lies on the other (male). Hence the sign of the output can be used for classifying the testing pages. We use the SVM implementation available in Weka [69] for our analysis.

4.2.3. Logistic Regression We also use logistic regression where the dependent variable has binary class membership (e.g., male/female) and the independent variables are the weights of the various terms in the vocabulary (or the weights of design variables). We also chose logistic regression because of its linear nature (which provides a more transparent structure) and its popularity in empirical research.

Logistic regression is a generalized linear model that relates predictor variables to a categorical outcome variable using a logit transformation [44]. The set of predictor variables may be continuous, discrete, or a mixture of the two. In our study, the design-based predictor variables are weights of the design variables, which are both continuous and discrete. The content-based predictor variables are the weights of the various terms in the vocabulary, which are continuous. The outcome variable, $\hat { Y }$ , of the model produced by logistic regression is the probability of having one outcome (e.g., male) or another (e.g., female) based on a nonlinear function of the best linear combination of predictors with two outcomes [63]:

$$
\hat {Y} _ {i} = \frac {e ^ {u}}{1 + e ^ {u}},\tag{5}
$$

where $\hat { Y }$ is the estimated probability that the ith case (i.e., ith web site; $i = 1 , \ldots , n )$ is in one of the categories (or demographic classes) and u is the normal linear regression equation:

$$
u = A + B _ {1} X _ {1} + B _ {2} X _ {2} + \dots + B _ {k} X _ {k},\tag{6}
$$

where A is a constant and $B _ { j }$ is a coeficient for the predictor $X _ { j } ( j = 1 , 2 , \ldots , k )$ the equations, the following logit equation is derived:

$$
\ln (\frac {\hat {Y}}{1 - \hat {Y}}) = A + \sum B _ {j} X _ {i} j.\tag{7}
$$

Once the coeficients of the linear regression equation in Eq. (7) have been estimated on the basis of the maximum likelihood estimation, Eq. (5) combined with the estimated coeficients is used to predict the demographic class of a web site i based on the estimated probability. We use the implementation of logistic regression available in Weka [69] for our analysis.

5.1.2. Content Variables In addition to design, we expect the content to difer across web sites of interest between diferent demographic classes. If there are significant demographic cues in the content of web sites, then such cues should manifest themselves in the distribution of words appearing on the web sites dominated by diferent demographic classes. To test this argument empirically, we measured the pairwise similarity between the homepage content of web sites within a demographic class and across diferent demographic classes (within a demographic dimension). For example, for the demographic dimension of gender, we compute the pairwise similarities between web sites from the same class (e.g., female-female and male-male) as well as between web sites from diferent classes (e.g., male-female). We calculate the similarity scores for all possible pairs and average them. Hence we have three average pairwise similarity scores, two of them are for web sites of the same class (e.g., male-male) and one is for web sites from diferent classes. If there is a demographic cue in content, then the average pairwise similarity between web sites from diferent demographic classes must be significantly lower than the pairwise similarity between web sites from the same class. To calculate the similarity, we build a profile of each web site (using the homepage of the web site) using a standard TF-IDF representation (see Section 4.2) and use the cosine similarity measure [40]. Table 3 shows the average pairwise similarity scores for diferent demographic dimensions and provides the standard errors to measure the stability and the significance of the results. From these results, we observe that the average pairwise similarity of web site content within a demographic class is significantly higher than the average pairwise similarity between web site content from diferent classes. We observe the same pattern consistently over all demographic dimensions. The results suggest that the audience from the same demographic group (e.g., male) tends to share a common bias in their preferences for web site content. Such a bias can be exploited in a predictive model for estimating the demographics of the audience of web sites.

5.2.4. Estimating Composition The classification models described above allow us to predict the demographic class of a given web site. However, not all web sites that belong to a given demographic class are equal. For example, some of the “female” web sites identified are the web sites that have 55%–60% female audience, while others are the sites that have 70% or more female audience. In some scenarios it is important to estimate the exact demographic composition (e.g., percentage of females) instead of a discrete classification. The estimated demographic compositions of a web site can provide more granular information to advertisers so that they can deploy their online campaigns more strategically. Hence we now build models that estimate the actual composition (e.g., percentage of the audience that is male) for a given web site instead of just a binary class (e.g., male/female). In other words, we are now interested in a problem that requires regression models with a continuous dependent variable instead of a binary class variable. The independent variables are the same content variables with the TF-IDF term weights that we used for the content-based modeling. On the basis of the results in Section 5.2.2, we found the SVM to be a strong performer for the classification problem. We therefore chose an SVM-based regression scheme [18] to build our demographic composition estimation model. We also tested other popular regression models such as M5P (a type of decision tree for continuous response variable) and linear regression for comparison. Through this additional testing, we found that this SVMbased regression scheme is indeed the best performing prediction model. The implementation of SVM regression that we use is available as part of the Weka machine learning software [69]. The page (homepage of a web site) representation as well as the testing and training data remain the same with the exception that the variable to be predicted is not a binary demographic class but rather the percentage composition for the class. ## 5.3. Ensemble Approach: Design and Content Variables

From the aforementioned experiments and analysis, we have learned that both the design elements and the content, locally available on the homepage of a web site, provide significant signals for predicting the demographics of the audience of the web site. The models that we explored can predict the demographics of the audience of web sites with an accuracy of around 70% when using just the design cues and more than 84% when using just the content cues. We expect that these two diferent types of cues (i.e., design and content) provide their own unique signals that are embedded in a web site. While we have observed that the prediction performance of the content-based modeling approach is significantly better, the design variables can still provide useful, complementary information that may help to further improve the performance of the content-based approach. Hence, with the goal of further improving the prediction performance of the classifiers, we now explore a simple method for combining the two diferent types of cues. In particular, we suggest an ensemble approach by linearly combining the prediction/output probabilities from the best performing content-based and design-based classifiers. In other words, we use the logistic regression scheme for the design-based classifier and the SVM scheme for the content-based classifier. We build the classifiers using the training web sites and generate the output probabilities for each of the testing web sites. The output probability of a classifier represents the estimated probability of a given testing web site belonging to a given demographic class (we use the two-class setup). We compute the final output probability of the testing web site belonging to a given class (e.g., female) through linear combination such as:

$$
\gamma \cdot P r _ {c o n t e n t} (c l a s s = f e m a l e | \mathbf {w _ {c}}) + (1 - \gamma) \cdot P r _ {d e s i g n} (c l a s s = f e m a l e | \mathbf {w _ {d}}),\tag{8}
$$

where ${ \bf w } _ { c }$ is a content (term) vector, ${ \bf w } _ { d }$ is a design variable vector, γ is a weight assigned to the output probability from the content-based classifier and ranges from 0 to 1. When γ is 1, the final output probability is the same as the output probability from the content-based classifier; when $\gamma$ is 0, the final output probability is the same as the probability from the design-based classifier. If the final output probability is greater than 0.5, the web site is classified as one of the two classes (e.g., female) of web site and otherwise it is classified as the other class. Given such high accuracy values, it is possible that the performance of the content-based classifiers represents an upper bound on the predictive performance in the current ensemble setup. If this is true, then $\gamma = 1$ will produce the best results. We find this to be the case for the income dimension, and the best accuracy is seen with $\gamma = 1$ . ## 6. Conclusion and Future Work

Using the home pages of hundreds of web sites, we have investigated the following question: Is it possible to deduce the demographics of the audience of a web site solely on the basis of the design and content of the homepage of a web site? Our results are in the afirmative. To the best of our knowledge this is the first study that has shown that the demographics of the audience of a web site can be efectively predicted solely on the basis of either the design elements or the content of the web site. We performed a statistical analysis of design elements and content to investigate the empirical evidence of the demographic signals in each type of cue. We also used predictive models that attempt to recognize (using the training data) the diferences in the distribution of various design elements or terms on web sites associated with diferent demographic classes. We evaluated the predictive models using held-out testing web sites and provided several metrics to describe the performance.

We found that web site demographic classifiers (especially using an SVM) can achieve high accuracy, precision, and recall when just using the text of homepages of web sites as inputs. In addition to testing the two-class classifiers, we evaluated the performance of three-class classifiers (with a class for demographically neutral sites) and observed that the strong prediction performance holds there as well. We also found that the web site demographic composition estimators can provide reasonable estimates that have high correlation with known demographic compositions. Finally, we tested a simple ensemble approach that combines the cues from both design and content to predict the demographics of the audience of web sites. Our results indicate the possibility of improving on the already strong performance of content-based classifiers through an ensemble approach.

Our proposed methods and their results suggest both managerial and practical implications. The main managerial implication of our findings is that the current practice involving behavioral tracking of users and their data across web sites may not be critical for the problem of estimating the demographics of the audience of web sites. As we show, the local cues based on content and design elements of web sites can be leveraged efectively for this purpose. The proposed method is of low cost and devoid of privacy concerns. This is in contrast to the current industry-leading process, which is costly and riddled with privacy concerns. Moreover, since the suggested approach is automated (from crawling for local cues to building demographic classifiers) and it uses only the local cues for modeling and prediction, it can be expected to scale very well with the enormous and ever-expanding Web.

Our results are encouraging and point in several future directions. In particular, the theoretical work of Zahedi et al. [71] indicates that there are “latent” attitudes in text, and we would like to use this fact in further improving prediction of the demographics of the audience of web sites. With that intent, we are currently exploring hierarchical Bayesian models such as latent Dirichlet allocation [9] that may account for latent attitudes in text before making a demographic classification or composition estimation. Motivated by the results from the current study, we are also considering other ensemble approaches that can better triangulate the cues in the design and content of web sites. We also plan to study the efectiveness of the local cues (design and content) collected from the web sites visited by a user in predicting the demographic attributes of an individual user, which can then be applied to various problems such as target marketing or online identification.

# Predicting Audience Demographics of Web Sites Using Content and Design Cues

Dear Editor,

We have submitted a research article titled "Predicting Audience Demographics of Web Sites Using Content and Design Cues" by Iljoo Kim and Gautam Pant for review by Information & Management (INFMAN). The paper focuses on the problem of predicting the audience demographics of a web site using cues embedded in the design or the content of its homepage. Through experiments involving hundreds of web sites, we show that it is possible to effectively predict different types of demographics of consumers of web sites based on the suggested approach.

Broadly, our work falls into the area of deriving business intelligence from web data. We evaluate various classifiers for the purpose of exploring an automated approach with predictive models that attempt to deduce the audience demographics of a web site using those cues that are locally available on web sites. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that has shown that audience demographics of a web site can be effectively predicted solely based on either the design elements or the content of the site. The suggested approach provides a pragmatic and scalable low-burden complement to the more intrusive and costly registration/cookie based techniques that are plagued by a number of inherent problems with cookies as well as privacy concerns.
