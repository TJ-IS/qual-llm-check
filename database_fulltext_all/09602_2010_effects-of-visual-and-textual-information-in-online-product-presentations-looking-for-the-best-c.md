# Effects of visual and textual information in online product presentations: looking for the best combination in website design

Carlos Flavia´n Blanco<sup>1</sup> Raquel Gurrea Sarasa<sup>1</sup> and Carlos Oru´s Sanclemente<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Faculty of Economics and Business Studies, University of Zaragoza, Zaragoza, Spain

## Abstract

Online product presentation constitutes a key challenge for marketers and designers who want to satisfy consumers’ needs. Visual and textual product information affect recognition and knowledge about the product. This research examines whether the presence (vs absence) of a product image and textual information in a schematic mode (vs paragraph mode) affect users’ recall and perceptions of the quality of the electronic products’ information. The moderating role of user’s familiarity with the website and product also may play a role; the evolution of these variables likely will determine trends in e-commerce research. The results show that a schematic display of textual information improves perceptions of information quality. When a picture of the product appears together with textual information, users remember more information and consider it easier to remember if that information appears schematically. However, without a product picture, users allocate more resources to process paragraph information and therefore recall more information and perceive it as easier to recall. The degrees of familiarity also have important directional effects on these relationships.

## Introduction

With the expansion of the Internet as an informational and retail channel, users have at their disposal greater amounts of information, which may lead them to experience information overload (Lurie & Mason, 2007). Once the initial fears about intensified price competition have been overcome, online firms are competing now by offering attractive, highquality contents to users to attain a competitive advantage (Lynch & Ariely, 2000). In particular, commercial websites must provide high-quality information efficiently to meet consumers’ needs. Therefore, Web design has emerged as a key success factor for virtual stores (e.g., Jarvenpaa & Dickson, 1988; Huizingh, 2000; Ranganathan & Ganapathy, 2002). A site’s design influences users’ attitudes and intentions to buy online (Jarvenpaa & Todd, 1997; Lohse & Spiller, 1999), enhances their trust (Flavia´n et al., 2006; Schlosser et al., 2006) and increases satisfaction (Liu & Arnett, 2000; Zviran et al., 2006). The various elements of website design include highquality information, interesting contents and efficient and attractive navigation (Alba et al., 1997; Geyskens et al., 1999), as well as the presentation of the focal product (Hong et al., 2004; Kim & Lennon, 2008). Specifically, the presentation of product information on the computer screen should affect users’ ability to process that information (Mu & Galletta, 2007) and thus their shopping decision (Park et al., 2005).

A well-designed user interface and appropriate presentation mode likely improve system acceptance. For example, an effective presentation mode enables users to comprehend product information easily and facilitates the shopping process (Chau et al., 2000). To present their products, most sites use visual and/or textual information, which both exert significant effects on information processing and decision making (Chau et al., 2000; Schlosser, 2003a; Hong et al., 2004; Kim & Lennon, 2008). However, most research attempts to establish whether visual or textual information has a superior effect, rather than finding the appropriate balance that leads to the most effective online product presentation.

In contrast, the present research advances the search for the ideal combination of visual and textual information to present products online by analysing whether different combinations of presentation modes affect consumers’ cognitive states. We consider in particular the effects of the presence (vs absence) of a product picture and different displays of textual information (i.e., paragraph or schematic) on perceived information quality and recall. Extant information systems and marketing research notes that not only design but also users characteristics determine a system’s acceptance and use and thus site success (e.g., Swaminathan et al., 1999; Wells et al., 2005; Lian & Lin, 2008). Therefore, we also examine the potential role of user familiarity with the website and the product. As the Internet continues to gain popularity, more online users should become more familiar with this medium. Moreover, familiarity influences information processing (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987; Thorbjørnsen et al., 2002), and different modes of presenting product information could influence such processing. In this sense, our analysis of these users’ characteristics offers some guidance about the future development of e-commerce.

To present these investigations and insights, we structure our article as follows: In the next section, we review prior literature regarding the relevance of online product presentations, especially as it pertains to the role of visual and textual information. We then formulate hypotheses about the effects of visual and textual information on users’ recall and perceptions of information quality, as well as the moderating role of familiarity. After we present the methodology, we discuss our findings. We conclude by offering the research limitations and further research ideas, as well as our main conclusions and implications.

## Product presentation in website design

Research related to website design identifies product information content as a key success factor (Jarvenpaa & Todd, 1997; Huizingh, 2000; Liang & Lai, 2002; Kim & Stoel, 2004). That is, information content affects the perceptions and behaviours of online consumers. In this line, Eroglu et al. (2001) conceptualize website users cognitive and affective states in response to a site’s information, and Ranganathan & Ganapathy (2002) claim information content is a key determinant for online consumers. In the online environment, consumers are no longer passive receivers of marketing messages but instead play more active roles in the communication process. Online users self-select information and want to process content that they choose (Pavlou & Stewart, 2000; Sanjose´-Cabezudo et al., 2009). In addition, information content has notable influences on decision making (Lurie & Mason, 2007), satisfaction with the website (Liu & Arnett, 2000; Zviran et al., 2006) and online purchase intentions (Richard, 2005; Jahng et al., 2007).

Because of the salience of information content, online product presentation demands careful consideration (Lee et al., 2004). We focus on the information search as an important stage into the shopping-decision process, which can be characterized as a problem-solving process (Bettman, 1979; Engel et al., 1995). More specifically, deliberative information search and information consid eration takes place especially for high-involvement, high cost or high-risk products (Hodkinson & Kiel, 2003). When consumers seek specific product information, they engage in extrinsically motivated, task-oriented behaviour that indicates situational involvement, focuses on utilitarian benefits and goal-directed choices and consists of directed search (Hoffman & Novak, 1996; Peterson & Merino, 2003). Yet at this stage consumers need, above all, adequate product information to make a purchase decision (Kim & Lennon, 2008), so the presentation of this information should facilitate its acquisition and processing. Product information also has an important role in the evaluation of alternatives and final purchase stages (Wells et al., 2005), so its presentation on the computer screen could determine the ultimate success of online transactions.

We also note the distinction in advertising research between rational and emotional appeals in product information (Liu & Stout, 1987). Rational appeals include factual information (i.e., objectively verifiable descriptions) and focus on product attributes; emotional appeals relate more to subjective, emotion-laden information. Although both types can have positive impacts on consumers, some evidence supports the benefits of factual information, because it elicits more support arguments and more positive attitudes toward the product than do emotional cues (Liu & Stout, 1987; Drossos et al., 2007). However, the effectiveness of the different appeals ultimately depends on users’ goals and motivations, which dictate which product features and benefits consumers find most relevant and about which they seek information online (Pavlou & Stewart, 2000). Therefore, we attend carefully to how users perceive and process factual information when they want to acquire knowledge about a product’s characteristics.

In turn, it seems interesting to analyse the impact of different product presentation modes on consumers’ perceptions. Prior literature recognises two main types of information in product presentations: visual and textual (Chau et al., 2000). Visual information provides images or pictures of the products, which can be displayed in multiple ways with different sizes, angles and perspectives and may even feature movement or animation. In contrast, textual information describes the products with words and offers specific and detailed information about product characteristics. E-commerce websites often combine visual and textual information to constitute the consumer information environment (Kim & Lennon, 2008), and the proper use of both types represents a key challenge for site designers, because the different presentation forms generate different effects on users. Therefore, we examine how different combinations of visual and textual information affect users’ perceptions.

Prior Information Processing research notes the possible conflicts between visual and textual information. A textual description of a product and its image may be perceived differently, which would prompt distinct psychological effects (Jarvenpaa & Dickson, 1988). Dual coding theory holds that people perceive, recognise, comprehend and remember visual and textual information using two separate mental systems or codes: a verbal code to represent and process language, and an imagery code for nonlinguistic objects and events (Paivio, 1986; Sadoski & Paivio, 2004). With regard to potential confrontations between visual and textual information, this theory indicates that images are easier to remember than textual information, such that people exhibit greater recall of pictures and images than of words (Hong et al., 2004). Similarly, information processing research establishes different effects for imagery vs discursive information on cognitive perceptions (MacInnis & Price, 1987; Kim & Lennon, 2008). In a shopping environment, visual information may be more effective than textual information, because visual aspects enhance the sensory experience, enhance information processing and encourage purchase decisions. Evidence further indicates that either pictures and words or pictures alone are more effective than words alone for facilitating message recall and positive attitudes toward the product (e.g., Liu & Stout, 1987).

The effects of these informational cues may depend on the motivations or goals that people pursue when they search for information. In this way, Schlosser (2003b) finds that Internet users searching for product information develop more favourable attitudes in response to passive, text-based information rather than to high levels of visually interactive information. Objective information about the product also might be more effective if the consumer is goal-directed (Drossos et al., 2007). However, interactivity produced by visual aspects, which affects the imagery processing account, provokes higher purchase intentions, regardless of the user’s motivation. In a similar vein, the elaboration likelihood model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) accounts for a discursive processing perspective, and states that there are two routes for processing persuasive messages: central and peripheral. Depending on the consumer’s motivation and ability to process the information, one route is more salient than the other (Petty et al., 1983). When consumers are highly involved and task-oriented, such as when they search for product information, the central route takes precedence, and consumers expend more resources processing central cues, such as specific information about product characteristics (Schlosser, 2003b). Yet other evidence notes the impact of peripheral cues, such as the website design or background colour, on online users attitudes and purchase intentions (Mandel & Johnson, 2002; Sanjose´-Cabezudo et al., 2009). In addition, Lurie & Mason’s (2007) framework of how visual representations affect the decision-making process indicates that visual tools may substitute for the ‘need for touch’ better than does textual information. Thus, visual information may increase consumers’ confidence in their choices and lower the proportion of time they spend on online tasks.

Empirical studies also indicate that purchase behaviours depend on how consumers perceive the information (Schlosser, 2003a), such that the product presentation mode can affect attitudes and purchase intentions (Chau et al., 2000; Hong et al., 2004; Kim & Lennon, 2008). Yet a lack of agreement persists about which element dominates. Hong et al. (2004) and Chau et al. (2000) both find that images have a greater influence than textual information in a grocery context, but Kim & Lennon (2008) reveal the superiority of verbal information for apparel products. Accordingly, different effects appear to emerge, depending on the analysis context. The type of product should affect how users process related information on the Web. Similarly, past research has shown that product attributes (Huang et al., 2009), the degree of product complexity (Jahng et al., 2007) and users’ involvement (Martin et al., 2005) all affect the way people seek, pay attention to and process product information by weighting certain informational cues more than others. Customer orientation and/or motivation also influence how a store environment affects users’ perceptions (e.g., Sanjose´-Cabezudo et al., 2009). In this way, Kaltcheva & Weitz (2006) find that task-oriented consumers (i.e., their goal is to obtain needed products or information and they wish to complete the purchase as efficiently as possible, with little or no inherent satisfaction derived from the shopping activity itself) prefer a store environment with low arousal, whereas recreational consumers (i.e., their goal is to engage in an entertaining and enjoyable experience, deriving satisfaction from the shopping activity itself) want an arousing setting. In interactive environments such as the Internet, users’ goals similarly determine the information they consider relevant and for which they seek information (Pavlou & Stewart, 2000). When those goals are primarily informative though, factual information about the product is critical, regardless of its utilitarian or hedonic nature (Drossos et al., 2007)

According to this review, there is no single, unique solution for managing visual and textual tools to induce the most positive consumer responses, and the effects of different representations likely vary across industries and product categories. Yet Internet retailers often pay more attention to visual product presentations than textual ones (Kim & Lennon, 2008), investing significantly in the development of visual information that they appear to consider more effective, a priori. For example, sophisticated visualisation tools (e.g., animation, 3D images, virtual reality) may seem exciting, but they also could slow page downloads (Lohse & Spiller, 1999). Thus, consumers may prefer smart images (i.e., high quality, proper size) and high-quality textual information that offers factual content in the proper format more quickly. By examining the effects of different combinations of visual and textual information, we try to help Web designers and marketers determine how to combine tools to create an appropriate consumer information environment that does not hinder download speeds.

For some products, simple presentations, with low levels of interactivity and adequate visual support, could enable consumers to acquire and process information properly (Jahng et al., 2007). We consider electronic products, which we define as entertainment devices based on electronic technology (e.g., televisions, stereo systems, digital cameras, videogame consoles). According to Jahng et al. (2002, 2007), such products have relatively high complexity, with many attributes to consider, some of which are difficult to understand. Therefore, these products likely demand more analytical processing of product information on a website. In these cases, plain product presentations could be appropriate to show all the relevant information in an effective way. According to the resource matching theory, information is more effective if the resources available to process the information match, rather than either exceed or fall short of, those required for the task (Peracchio & Meyers-Levy, 1997). In fact, presentations that combine visual and textual information to create a moderate level of complexity influence product attitudes and purchase intentions to a greater extent than presentations with low or high levels of complexity (Martin et al., 2005).

Therefore, we consider how different product presentation modes influence consumers’ perceptions of information about electronic products, including the effects of the product image (presence vs absence) and the textual information format (paragraph vs schematic) on perceived information quality and recall. Perceived information quality is considered as a critical variable in determining the success of electronic commerce. Highquality information helps users make better purchase choices (Ahn et al., 2007). enhances their satisfaction with the website (Muylle et al., 2004) and triggers the central route of website trust formation (Yang et al., 2006). Recall of information is fundamental to information processing (Wells et al., 2005), and greater cognitive processing, which results from effective product presentations, induces increased recall of favourable information about the product (Petty et al., 1983). In this sense, we use recall as a measure of the effectiveness of different product presentations: Higher recall implies greater effectiveness in terms of improving the user’s cognitive processing. To measure product information recall, we investigate (1) objective recall, or the number of specific characteristics that users can remember, and (2) subjective recall, which pertains to how easily users believe they can recall the information. When people make judgments, they rely on the subjective ease with which information comes to mind (Schwarz et al., 1991; Menon & Raghubir, 2003), and if they perceive a piece of information as easy to recall, they recall it better (Caruso, 2008). Therefore, we attempt to identify visual–textual combinations that both help users recall more information and affect their subjective perceptions of the product presentation.

## Hypotheses formulation and justification

## Visual information

E-commerce consumers cannot judge products as they would in a brick-and-mortar store, because they cannot touch the product or examine it physically before purchase. Thus, product images online likely have highly significant effects on consumers’ perceptions and behaviours. As a starting point, it seems logical to propose that visual aspects can display information briefly (Lurie & Mason, 2007), which may increase decision-making efficiency, because users can detect and process the information offered by the image features easily (Khakimdjanova & Park, 2005). A product presentation that uses appropriate visual elements also may help users identify the product and improve their knowledge about it. The information they gather efficiently about various product characteristics should support their decision-making process.

In addition, online shopping relies on recognition and recall (Chau et al., 2000), which reflect the effectiveness of the retailer’s communications and also affect subsequent shopping behaviours (Childers & Viswanathan, 2000). Given the great amounts of information that the Internet offers, if a website can display high-quality contents that support the recall of product information, users could gain more knowledge about the product and form favourable attitudes toward that site (Griffith, 2001). Thus product images, by increasing product knowledge, might increase purchase considerations. Furthermore, most websites use images, so consumers may reject a website that only describes its products textually, without images to help them identify the product (Kim & Eom, 2002). A lack of visual support may suggest a presentation flaw that would generate negative attitudes toward the site (Everard & Galletta, 2003). Both dual coding theory (Paivio, 1986) and research on imagery vs discursive information processing (Kim & Lennon, 2008) indicate that people remember images more easily than they do textual information (Chau et al., 2000; Hong et al., 2004), and recall of a set of products may improve when the brand name appears together with a picture of the product (e.g., Hong et al., 2004). Moreover, different shapes of product images may influence attention and memory levels (Lee & Benbasat, 2003). Therefore, we argue that the use of product images generates better cognitive responses from online consumers, including better recall of information related to the product.

Such recall of product information is a critical consumer behaviour variable, subject to several marketing and information processing studies because of its impact on the formation of judgments, attitudes and behavioural intentions (Griffith, 2001; Hong et $a l . ,$ 2004; Wells et al., 2005; Robins & Holmes, 2008). Moreover, recall helps consumers evaluate products and make correct purchase decisions (Wells et al., 2005). If the information they recall relates positively to the retailer’s products, consumers are more likely to form purchase intentions and develop favourable attitudes toward virtual stores (Griffith, 2001). In addition, the perceived ease of recalling information influences users’ judgments of the information and the store (Ofir et al., 2008). When making a decision, the ease with which people recall pertinent information influences the final result (Broemer, 2001). Therefore, product presentations that seem easier to recall should affect the factual recall of information, improve consumers’ knowledge and eventually facilitate the purchase decision. Thus, we expect that if product information is perceived as easy to recall, consumers could remember it easily, which could improve and speed up the shopping process.

## H1: On a website, the presence (vs absence) of a product image positively affects (a) the user’s recall of product information and (b) the perceived ease with which the user recalls product information.

In addition, the display of visual tools should enhance perceptions of information quality, measured as users’ impressions of the accuracy and completeness of the website information about products and transactions (Kim et al., 2008). High-quality information enables users to make better decisions, so if visual aspects offer greater product knowledge, they may determine users’ perceptions of the site’s information quality. That is, visual elements may improve perceived levels of information quality. The comprehensibility and comprehensiveness (Muylle et $a l .$ , 2004), completeness (Ho & Lee, 2002) and reliability (Lin & Liu, 2000; Muylle et $a l . ,$ 2004) of information also might constitute the construct of perceived information quality. Images enhance the acquisition and processing of information and generate quicker and more accurate comprehension (Chau et $a l . _ { \cdot }$ , 2000); they also might diminish perceived risk (Lurie & Mason, 2007) by improving perceptions of the information reliability – and therefore perceived information quality. In contrast, the absence of a product image may imply negative outcomes, such as low quality perceptions of the website (Everard & Galletta, 2003), because users likely perceive the information as incomplete.

H2: On a website, the presence (vs absence) of a product image positively affects the user’s perception of the quality of product information.

## Textual information

The other main resource for Web design is specific textual information about products. Prior research details some key features of textual information content (Schwarz et $a l . ,$ 1991; Keeker, 1997; Huizingh, 2000; Benbunan-Fich, 2001; Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Ranganathan & Ganapathy, 2002), we still need to establish which sort of textual information should be offered to consumers and how to provoke positive responses (Chau et $a l . ,$ 2000; Park et al., 2005). Information content establishes the overall quality of a website (Huizingh, 2000; Liu & Arnett, 2000; Wan, 2000; Aladwani & Palvia, 2002; Singh et al., 2005). In particular, the accuracy of contents (Keeker, 1997), the effectiveness of information (Richard, 2005), the level of fit between information and the task (Kim & Stoel, 2004), the display of complete and concise information about products and shopping processes (Ho & Lee, 2002; Kim et al., 2008), the comprehensiveness of information (Muylle et al., 2004) and its relevance or reliability (Ho & Lee, 2002) all strongly affect online users’ perceptions and behaviours.

Because the search for product information remains one of the most frequently performed activities online (European Interactive Advertising Association (EIAA), 2008), it seems necessary to determine which information characteristics are important from the consumer point of view (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002). Information related to the product likely influences purchase considerations (Eroglu et al., 2001; Kim & Stoel, 2004), as well as the user’s satisfaction with the website (Szymanski & Hise, 2000; Muylle et al., 2004; Kim & Lennon, 2008). Thus, acquiring and processing high-quality information is critical for decision making (Miranda & Sauders, 2003).

The way information appears may influence cognitive processing; textual product information displays generally adopt either a paragraph (i.e., a paragraph describing product characteristics) or schematic (i.e., product characteristics listed in tables or charts) forms. Users may process more textual information when it is presented schematically rather than in paragraph form (Miller, 1956; Tegarden, 1999), and according to Lurie & Mason (2007), decision makers consider more product attributes when they view product information presented graphically, which could help them make more efficient decisions.

However, people read information online differently than they do printed material; notably, they ‘scan’ the online information (Griffith et al., 2001). Product information on a computer screen should be more effective if the presentation mode allows them to scan and quickly locate specific, relevant information, because in this case, the resources available to process the information match those required for the task (Martin et al., 2005). Therefore, the schematic presentation of product information might be easier to remember than information presented in a paragraph, because it enables the easier identification of product characteristics. Schematic information adopts a kind of factual format, such that information is displayed in a clear, logical, to-the-point expository style (Peracchio & Meyers-Levy, 1997). In addition, if the textual information presentation aims to arouse encoding, a schematic display should result in better memory (Lee & Benbasat, 2003). We therefore expect both objective and subjective benefits of schematic information, compared with paragraph information, with regard to the recall of product information.

H3: On a website, textual information displayed in a schematic mode (vs paragraph mode) positively affects (a) the user’s recall of product information and (b) the perceived ease with which the user recalls product information.

Presentation style also should stimulate reading and facilitate the interpretation of the information (Huizingh, 2000), so the information presentation could determine perceptions of information quality on the site. A paragraph information mode requires more effort to acquire information, because the task of reading texts demands some concentration. Schematic information instead makes the information easier to obtain and improves comprehension, especially in an online environment, in which people already tend to scan information and consumers’ reading pace is slower (Hoque & Lohse, 1999). Thus, schematic information should speed up the retrieval of relevant product characteristics and enhance perceptions of the comprehensibility of the information (Muylle et al., 2004). Detailed and specific information in schematic form also could enhance the perceived accuracy of the information, because users can locate relevant information straightforwardly. Therefore, assuming the same amount of product-related information, users should perceive schematic information as higher in quality than paragraph information.

H4: On a website, textual information displayed in a schematic mode (vs paragraph mode) positively affects the user’s perception of the quality of product information.

Interaction effects of visual and textual information Finally, one of the main goals of this research is to identify those combinations of visual and textual information that provoke the greatest users’ responses. Consequently, beyond the individual effects of information presentation modes, we take into account the potential effects of their simultaneous manipulation (Lee & Benbasat, 2003). Most actual websites combine visual and textual information and present their products in several modes. We propose that treatments that produce the more positive effects interact to affect users’ perceptions of information quality and recall of product information. According to our previous propositions, a presentation that consists of a product picture and schematic information should generate the greatest responses.

H5: The effective combination of visual and textual information positively affects (a) recall of product information, (b) perceived ease with which the user recalls product information and (c) perceived quality of product information.

## Moderating role of familiarity

Consumers’ own characteristics influence their acceptance of online shopping (Swaminathan et al., 1999; Lian & Lin, 2008) and what constitutes an effective e-commerce website (e.g., Jahng et al., 2002; Wells et al., 2005). In this way, familiarity, or the direct and indirect knowledge available to a consumer (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987), is critical to the success of a virtual store (Gefen, 2000). This variable influences online business-to-consumer relationships (e.g., Thorbjørnsen et al., 2002; Peterson & Merino, 2003) and can affect decision making (Bettman & Park, 1980; Ratneshwar et al., 1987) by reducing the amount of cognitive effort required and modifying cognitive structures (Huang et al., 2009). Familiarity also influences the relative importance of different product attributes and the customer’s sensitivity to the stimulus context (Alba & Cooke, 2004). E-commerce research should take into account the evolution of users’ familiarity with online shopping and its potential impact on their perceptions and behaviours. Therefore, we analyse the potential effect of two types of familiarity on our proposed relationships: (1) familiarity with the type of website and (2) familiarity with the type of product. These variables likely affect decision strategies and thus the cognitive costs of processing information.

Familiarity with the website could affect consumers’ final decisions (Bettman & Park, 1980; Ratneshwar et al., 1987; Gefen & Straub, 2004), such that people alter their judgments on the basis of their familiarity with website usage. Because familiarity with a particular retailer also alters the way consumers process information (Huang et al., 2009), we might expect that familiarity with the type of website affects perceptions of the different product information presentation modes. They might concentrate on different aspects of the product information and process them in different ways than unfamiliar users (Casalo´ et al., 2008). We thus note the potentially strong learning effect of visiting an e-store (Johnson et al., 1999);

their knowledge of the layout should enhance their perceived control over navigation, enable them to pay more attention to important features and thus create different perceptions of the site. In addition, a more familiar consumer likely retains and recalls information better (Wells et al., 2005). Therefore, the user’s familiarity with the type of website should moderate the effects of visual and textual aspects, and their combination, on recall of product information (objective and subjective) and the perceived quality of product information.

H6.1: Familiarity with the website moderates the effect of the presence (vs absence) of a product image on the (a) recall of product information, (b) perceived ease with which the user recalls product information and (c) perceived quality of product information.

H6.2: Familiarity with the website moderates the effect of textual information displayed in a schematic mode (vs paragraph mode) on the (a) recall of product information, (b) perceived ease with which the user recalls product information and (c) perceived quality of product information.

H6.3: Familiarity with the website moderates the effect of the combination of visual and textual information on the (a) recall of product information, (b) perceived ease with which the user recalls product information and (c) perceived quality of product information.

In a similar vein, product familiarity might affect the way users collect and process information, which would influence their product learning and evaluations. Product familiarity, which reflects a consumer’s prior experience with a specific product category (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987), influences information processing in the product category (Griffith, 2001). Because they possess more knowledge about the relevant product attributes, familiar consumers likely have different expectations than unfamiliar consumers, who instead must undergo difficult and complex learning to develop knowledge structures (Zhou & Nakamoto, 2007). Thus, different levels of product familiarity may generate different outcomes in consumers’ cognitive states (Cordell, 1997). Moreover, familiar consumers can rely more on memory-based information, whereas novices likely are influenced by external cues (Mandel & Johnson, 2002). Because we focus on how external informational cues affect users’ perceptions and processing of product information, it is reasonable to propose that the degree of product familiarity moderates our focal effects: We expect differential impacts of product presentation modes on more and less familiar users, which influences their perceptions of information quality and levels of recall (objective and subjective) of product information.

H7.1: Familiarity with the product type moderates the effect of the presence (vs absence) of a product image on the (a) recall of product information, (b) perceived ease with which the user recalls product information and (c) perceived quality of product information.

## Methodology

We recruited a sample of 108 graduate and postgraduate students to participate in a 2 (product image: yes vs no) - 2 (text information: paragraph vs schema) betweensubjects full-factorial experiment. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. Participants were between 18 and 34 years of age (mean ¼ 22.31; 44% men) and had some online shopping experience (62.9% had purchased at least one product or service; 38% had purchased more than four products or services online).

We created four mock websites for the experiment, using designs derived from practices common to e-commerce, with a well-known European e-commerce site as a reference. On our hypothetical site, ‘electroshop.com'. participants viewed a digital video-camera for sale. This product is appropriate for three reasons. First, electronic products are especially attractive to the sample demographic; according to a recent report, electronic goods are among the most frequently purchased items online and the most common topics of information searches for these consumers (EIAA, 2008). In addition, such products are highly involving (Martin et al., 2005), which ensures some task-oriented or utilitarian motivation. Second, electronic products require analytic information processing, and the use of a simple product presentation (such as those created for the experiment) could offer high-quality, easy to remember information (Jahng et al., 2002). Third, digital video cameras are not as standardised as some electronic products (e.g., mp4 players, digital camera), which minimises the amount of possible generalised knowledge. Because digital video cameras offer various features, consumers must focus on information provided to understand the product.

For the image manipulation, subjects viewed a product presentation with either a picture of the product or a completely text-based presentation. For the text condition, the information appeared in either a paragraph or a table; both modes contained the same information (see Appendix A). Before beginning the experiment though, participants received a brief introduction to the study and read an abstract describing the task they had to perform:

Imagine that you want to give a gift to a person who is important to you. This person is very keen on electronic products, so you decide to give him/her a digital video camera. After browsing the Internet for a while, you reach Electroshop.com, where you find the following information about this attractive product.

This task gives the respondents a task-oriented activity with a utilitarian orientation (Kaltcheva & Weitz, 2006). Therefore, the purpose of this instruction was to induce participants to perform the task as efficiently as possible. After reading the instructions, they had 5 min to examine their assigned Web page before they turned off the monitor and completed the questionnaire.

We had conducted a pilot test in a usability lab. Several usability and marketing experts assessed the hypothetical websites to detect any possible design errors and reviewed the questionnaire. In addition, we performed an information inventory checklist to ensure the same productrelated information appeared in all four conditions (Wells et al., 2005).

For the dependent variables, we measured recall of product information by asking participants to indicate any specific characteristics of the product or other information they were able to remember (Lee & Benbasat, 2003; Hong et al., 2004; Va¨rlander, 2007). If the participant recalled each characteristic correctly, he or she received a score of 1 (0 otherwise), to which we added the number of items each subject was able to remember. For subjective recall, we used a single-item measure: ‘It is easy for me to remember the information provided by this

Web site’ (Broemer, 2001). For perceived information quality, we adopted an existing scale (Ahn et al., 2007) that includes completeness, timeliness, fitness, comprehensiveness, accuracy and reliability of information (Eroglu et al., 2001; Ho & Lee, 2002; Kim & Stoel, 2004; Muylle et al., 2004; Ahn et al., 2007). In addition, we adjusted some existing scales (Gefen, 2000; Gursoy, 2001; Flavia´n & Gurrea, 2008) to measure familiarity with the type of website and type of product. The questions used five-point Likert scales, and the definitive scale items appear in Appendix B.

## Results

We conducted reliability and dimensionality analyses to confirm the validity of our scales (Churchill, 1979; Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Regarding their reliability, we determined Cronbach’s alphas (Cronbach, 1970), with a cut-off minimum value of 0.7 (Nunnally, 1978). The results of the tests show acceptable reliability indices for the perceived information quality $( \alpha = 0 . 8 9 2 )$ , familiarity with the type of website (a ¼ 0.897) and familiarity with the type of product $( \alpha = 0 . 9 3 9 )$ scales. The item-total correlations for each element of the scales (Bagozzi, 1994) were greater than 0.3 (Nurosis, 1993), in support of internal consistency. In a principal component analysis, we evaluated the scales’ dimensionality (Hair et al., 1998); unidimensionality would enable us to calculate the average of the indicators that constitute each construct. In that case, we can use a single variable to represent each theoretical construct (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). We conducted the factorial analysis, with principal components and Varimax rotation, to determine if any eigenvalues are greater than 1. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Barlett test of sphericity confirmed the adequacy of the principal components method to determine scale unidimensionality. Moreover, we required factorial loadings to be greater than 0.5, with a significant total explained variance (Hair et al., 1998). The relevant analyses supported the scales, and we could extract only one factor from each: perceived information quality (explained variance ¼ 65.421%; KMO ¼ 0.841; Barlett test w<sup>2</sup>(15) ¼ 379.311, Po0.001), familiarity with the website (explained variance ¼ 76.567%; KMO ¼ 0.814; Barlett test w<sup>2</sup>(6) ¼ 276.683, Po0.001) and familiarity with the type of product (explained variance ¼ 84.544%; KMO ¼ 0.857; Barlett test w<sup>2</sup> (6) ¼ 375.397, Po0.001).

## Results for moderating role of familiarity

To analyse the moderating roles of familiarity with the type of website and product, we first classified participants into high and low familiarity groups. Using a median split (Martin et al., 2005), we divided the sample into highly familiar (n ¼ 53) and less familiar (n ¼ 45) with the type of website and highly familiar (n ¼ 51) and less familiar (n ¼ 44) with the type of product. With two different 2 (product image: yes vs no) - 2 (text information: paragraph vs schema) - 2 (familiarity with the type of website/product: high or low) MANOVAs, we then investigated the possible moderator effects of familiarity by looking at interactions of the independent variables.

For familiarity with the website, we found a significant two-way interaction between textual information and familiarity (Wilks’s l ¼ 0.857; F(3, 88) ¼ 3,829, P ¼ 0.013; power ¼ 0.798) and a marginal three-way interaction among visual information, textual information and familiarity (Wilks’s l ¼ 0.911; F(3, 88) ¼ 2.237, P ¼ 0.096; power ¼ 0.543). The interaction between visual information and familiarity was not significant (Wilks’s l ¼ 0.986; F(3,88) ¼ 0.315, P ¼ 0.815; power ¼ 0.108), in contrast with H6.1, but the degree of website familiarity moderated the effect of textual information on product information recall (F ¼ 9,644, P ¼ 0.003, power ¼ 0.865), in support of H6.2a. ## Discussion

Our analysis results have demonstrated that the mode for displaying textual information online significantly affects consumers’ perceptions of the quality of the information provided about product characteristics. Specifically, information presentation can provoke positive responses from online consumers. By considering the dependent variables separately, we have established that when the textual information appears schematically (e.g., in a table), consumers can process the information more easily, which improves their decision making. In the prepurchase stage of the shopping process, a channel’s ability to help consumers identify relevant product information is a critical driver of success (Frambach et $a l . ,$ 2007). Moreover, because online users scan rather than read information (Hoque & Lohse, 1999; Griffith et al., 2001), schematic information can be comprehended more easily, which enhances their perceptions of information completeness, detail and accuracy.

However, consumers recalled no more information when it was presented schematically, nor did they perceive the information as easier to recall. This finding may indicate that the participants paid attention to the experimental task and made specific efforts to recall the information. The same product information appeared in both formats, and participants may have possessed sufficient cognitive resources to process it, regardless of the format (Peracchio & Meyers-Levy, 1997). In a more realistic context, in which online users have different levels of motivation toward and involvement with the search activity (Childers et $a l . ,$ 2001) and the amount of product information varies more, information recall might vary more, depending on the format of the information.

We found no significant effects of the presence (vs absence) of a product image, which again might indicate a higher degree of engagement in the task than is common in real-world settings. The experimental setting was intended to make participants as task-oriented as possible, such that their goal was to acquire and process product information, which should make the central route of information processing more predominant; the respondents therefore focused their attention on textual information, leaving peripheral cues (e.g., visual aspects) to the side (Petty et al., 1983). Unlike the experimental setting that provided a specific task assignment, a realworld setting often entails the search for entertainment (Sanjose´-Cabezudo et al., 2009), and users might be more attracted by appealing, peripheral elements of a website.

We also note the potential influence of the type of product analysed. Electronic products contain many specifications, and some key information (e.g., weight, battery life, zoom) cannot be conveyed by an image. Thus, consumers of these products may need to obtain more product information from textual descriptions and thus feel more knowledgeable about the product (Kim & Lennon, 2008).

Despite this lack of finding pertaining to visual effects, we have uncovered some interesting results regarding the interaction between visual and textual information and recall of product information. When subjects viewed a picture of the product together with textual information, they recalled more information if the textual information was in schematic form. The participants also considered it easier to recall information when schematic information appeared together with a picture. The time available to review the websites was the same for all participants, such that those in conditions with pictures may have had less time to consider the textual information. Because schematic information is easier to remember and because this presentation format required a distribution of cognitive resources between visual and textual information, participants in this condition may have been able to recall more information and perceived it as easier to recall. In contrast, when participants saw no product image, they recalled more information and perceived it as easier to recall in paragraph mode. Perhaps these participants had a higher need for information, because they could not grasp details quickly through the image, so they used their additional time and resources to concentrate on the text. In accordance with past research, these results indicate that when the product presentation matches the task resources required, cognitive elaboration occurs, and recall of information increases (Petty et al., 1983; Peracchio & Meyers-Levy, 1997; Martin et al., 2005).

User familiarity (website and product) moderates the effects of the relationships between presentation mode and the dependent variables. Users familiar with the type of website recalled more product information in a paragraph format; those less familiar with it recalled more product information in a schematic mode. These different cognitive structures suggest that the textual format affected their information processing differently. Because highly familiar users possessed better knowledge structures with regard to the type of website, they could devote all their available resources to processing the textual information. Reading text in paragraph form likely required more concentration than scanning information in a table (Lurie & Mason, 2007), and the resulting greater attention may have produced increased levels of recall. In contrast, less familiar users may become confused or ‘lost’ during their site navigation (Galletta et al., 2006), such that their cognitive resources must spread out more. The schematic information therefore helped them recall more product information than the paragraph information, because it demanded fewer resources.

The users familiar with the website also perceived higher information quality when it was presented schematically, regardless of the presence of a product picture. Thus, they show a higher preference for the text format that allows them to quickly scan the information. Those unfamiliar with the type of site instead perceived the best information quality in the picture–schematic condition. This presentation might help less familiar users improve their quality perceptions of the site and enhance their trust in the e-retailer (Yang et al., 2006).

Regarding familiarity with the type of product, our finding, though marginally significant, that less familiar participants perceived higher information quality when there was no product picture was unexpected. This result might suggest that these users were also unaccustomed to processing information about such products and focused their attention on processing textual information. The marginal significance of this result does not allow us to extract any firm conclusion though, so further research should examine possible explanations. As in the case of familiarity with the site, users who were familiar with the product showed a stronger preference for the schematic text format that required less effort to locate relevant information. When visual and textual information appeared in combination, highly familiar participants also perceived higher information quality from a schematic presentation rather than a paragraph one, and less familiar participants preferred the picture–schema combination in terms of information quality. These results mirror those we obtained for website familiarity. When consumers become more familiar with a product domain, they come to prefer a stimulus that requires less effort to process. For less familiar consumers in particular, a product presentation that combines product images with schematic information seems most appropriate for improving information quality perceptions.

These moderating roles of familiarity are in line with prior research into discursive information processing with similar consumer characteristics (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987; Mandel & Johnson, 2002). That is, experienced consumers rely more on memory-based information and are less influenced by external stimuli than are novices. Our results indicate a similar pattern. Figures 3 and 5 show that highly familiar participants are less affected by different product presentations. In contrast, less familiar participants show greater variability in their perceptions.

## Limitations and further research

This study opens several avenues for ongoing research. First, though the simple presentations we created for this experiment displayed the product information appropriately, it seems necessary to examine these effects with real-world online shopping scenarios. In particular, it would be interesting to analyse the effects of different product presentations that use advanced tools, such as multiple image displays, the use of 3-D movement or movies. Future studies might directly manipulate the visual and textual elements of the product presentations by modifying the number and size of product pictures or the style and font type of textual information. Besides, it should be noted that we manipulated the ‘wrapper’ instead of the content of the message itself, but the effects of textual messages could depend on whether it contains evaluative or emotional appeals (Liu & Stout, 1987; Drossos et al., 2007). Additional research should analyse the effects of different types of appeals on users’ recall and perceptions, as well as determine which ‘wrappers’ best match such message contents.

Second, this study examines only one type of product, but the effects of different presentation modes likely vary across product types. For our specific product, consumers may be guided by more utilitarian benefits, such as functionality and ease of use, than by other, more hedonic benefits. Because product characteristics influence the acceptance of online shopping (Ratneshwar et al., 1987), it would be interesting to assess different product presentation modes for products that rely on more hedonic benefits, pleasure, fun or playfulness.

Third, we intended our participants to adopt a utilitarian motivation, such that they would focus on the relevant information and complete the task as efficiently as possible. Research also should consider more hedonicoriented users (Childers et al., 2001) and examine the effects of product presentation mode on users who browse the Web, usually without any specific goal in mind, looking for an enjoyable navigation experience (Kaltcheva & Weitz, 2006). Such an affective-laden motivation changes the way people process information; for example, aesthetic elements are likely to have a major influence (e.g., Schlosser, 2003b). Hedonic-oriented users also might react differently, depending on whether the message appeals are factual or emotional (Liu & Stout, 1987).

Fourth, beyond the scope of our study, we find some evidence that people scan information on the Internet rather than read it. Further research should analyse the validity of this feature of online consumer behaviour. If users must exert extra effort to read text rather than scan a table, designers should take care to provide information in a way that facilitates the shopping-decision process.

Fifth, familiarity with the type of website and product influences the relationships we propose. Further investigations should extend our research by considering other consumer characteristics, such as degree of product involvement (Berens et al., 2005; Park et al., 2007). Involvement affects the way people process information (e.g., Petty et al., 1983), so for example, if a product is more appealing for some consumers, it could generate different informational needs (Martin et al., 2005) and affect responses to visual and textual stimuli.

## Conclusions and implications

In an e-commerce environment, product presentations can make the difference between successful and unsuccessful interfaces. We have analysed the effects of different ways to present electronic products information on users’ recall, perceived ease of recall and perceived quality of product information. We also have identified the combination of visual and textual information that improves online users’ perceptions and processing, as well as considered the potential moderating roles of familiarity with the website and the product.

Our experimental findings confirm the importance of product presentation modes online. When the online user seeks specific product information, the product information presentation mode affects both perceptions and information processing. A schematic display of textual information (e.g., table, chart) improves perceptions of information quality; when a picture of the product appears together with textual information, consumers remember more information and consider it easier to remember. People’s tendency to scan rather than read information on the Internet (Hoque & Lohse, 1999; Griffith et al., 2001) seems to apply as well when they evaluate product characteristics.
