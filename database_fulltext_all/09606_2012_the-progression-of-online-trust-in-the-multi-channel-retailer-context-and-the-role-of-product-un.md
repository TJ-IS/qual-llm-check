# The progression of online trust in the multi-channel retailer context and the role of product uncertainty

## a b s t r a c t

This research attempts to evaluate the effects of antecedents of online trust in the context of multi-channel retailers at different phases, taking into consideration the moderating effects of product types. The results reveal that multi-channel retailers' trust is transferred from the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel to the online channel. Secondly, the customers' initial interaction with the retailers ameliorates the effects of non-direct experience, such as Internet-based structural assurance and word-of-mouth. With increases in product uncertainty, the effects of word-of-mouth, of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust, and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions on online trust are greater for experience products than for search products.

## 1. Introduction

The creation of adequate initial customer trust in e-commerce is critically important because unless customers form positive beliefs and intentions toward a website, they will probably not be willing to purchase products from the website [31]. However, although customers are likely to have trust in retailers' websites initially due to prior experience with their of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores in the multi-channel retailer context (retailers with both of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online business operations), certain established multi-channel retailers continue to lag behind [21] and are currently struggling against “pure-play” online retailers [16,42]. Thus, the principal objective of this study was to determine why multi-channel retailers are unable to transfer their of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust to their online business even though they are clearly in a much better position from a customer trust perspective, than a lesser-known pure online website. This research question is crucial because the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine retailers establishing websites as one of their major sales channels is currently trending upward [10].

This study further recognizes that customers move through multiple phases in e-commerce. Trust theorists have developed frameworks regarding the manner in which trust progresses through phases. In the context of multi-channel retailers, customers can form trust in retailers' websites prior to actually interacting with the websites on the basis of prior interactions with the retailers' offline stores. Hence, this study attempts to evaluate the evolution of customers' online trust in multi-channel retailers over time – more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, not only in the initial-interaction phase on which most of prior research has focused but also in the before-interaction phase.

Finally, this paper assesses the moderating effect of product uncertainty in the development of trust in the multi-channel retailer context. Previous studies concerning customers' trust in retailers are based on products that can be readily described through a variety of search attributes, and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these prior studies may not be generalized to experience products, regarding which the customers' experiences of the products are crucial to inferences of product quality and characteristics [13]. As e-commerce offerings progress from search products to experience products, particularly in the context of multi-channel retailers, it is essential to incorporate the manner in which online trust differs according to these product types into a trust research model.

In summary, this study attempted to address three major research agenda on trust previously raised by Gefen et al. [13] in the recent issue of the Journal of MIS: namely, the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of context (multichannel retailers') on trust, longitudinal nature and trust, and trust and product uncertainty which have had little attention so far. Although some other studies [2] have examined the progression of trust development with some consideration of product type, these studies did not evaluate the progression of trust in the multi-channel retailer context.

## 2. Literature review

Trust in online environments is predicated on beliefs in the trustworthiness of a trustee; trustworthiness is composed of three distinct dimensions – integrity, ability, and benevolence [13].

## 2.1. The context of the study – multi-channel retailers

Previous studies have focused principally on exploring the antecedents of trust toward pure online retailers despite extensive studies of online trust. Previous research has approached online trust primarily via three categories: retailer characteristics such as company size [3] and reputation [20,24], user characteristics such as the disposition to trust [30] and familiarity [12], and website and Internet environmental characteristics such as institutional structures [26,35].

Although certain studies have taken the of<sup>fl</sup>ine element into account, these studies have simply been an extension of previous trust research conducted with pure online retailers, including examinations of the impact of displaying the of<sup>fl</sup>ine address on the website on trust in the retailer's online operations [41].

However, as retailers increasingly embrace the concept of multichannel retailing (retailing via both of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online operations) [9], it is important to assess trust in the multi-channel retailer context. Trust in multi-channel retailers involves a fundamentally different process than that exploited by pure online retailers. Customers are likely to have previous purchasing experiences at a retailer's physical stores [25], and thus, they may form trust in the retailer's website even before they ever actually interact with it. Therefore, the possibility exists that trust in a retailer's of<sup>fl</sup>ine operations can be transferred to its online business with the advantage redounding to the multi-channel retailer.

## 2.2. The longitudinal nature of trust

The fact that customers are quite likely to have previous purchasing experiences with a retailer's physical stores [25] even before <sup>fi</sup>rst accessing the retailer's website is associated not only with a channel problem, but also with the development of trust over time. Komiak and Benbasat [23] have proposed that online trust development in online business be examined essentially via three phases: before-interaction, initialinteraction, and repeated-interaction.

However, extant trust research has focused principally on the initial-interaction and post-initial purchase phases [24] as the majority of prior research has dealt with only pure online retailers. These studies implicitly assume that trust development does not occur in the before-interaction phase with the possible exception of the study conducted by McKnight et al. [31], which evaluated customers' trust during an experimental introduction phase in the pure online retailer context, rather than in the pre-interaction phase, during which users have yet to experience a speci<sup>fi</sup>c website and are in the midst of assessing the website. As most people currently have ready access to websites, it is actually quite dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to differentiate between the experimental introduction phase and the initial-interaction phase; it is, however, far easier to distinguish the before-interaction phase from the initial-interaction phase in the multi-channel retailer context.

As a matter of fact, Kuan and Bock's [25] study on multi-channel retailers demonstrated that some trust development does occur even before the customer has even visited the website. Thus, in this study, we compare two phases – the before-interaction and the initial-interaction phases – in accordance with Komiak and Benbasat's [23] classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of trust development phases, in an effort to determine how trust evolves and to identify the factors that contribute to the evolution of trust over time [46], particularly in the context of multi-channel retailers.

## 2.3. Product uncertainty

In previous studies of e-commerce, the concept of trust is associated with products that can be readily described through search attributes and for which little product uncertainty exists – books and CDs are two excellent examples of this [13]. This paradigm, however, cannot be generalized to the experience products category in which the experiences of customers are critical to their perceptions of product quality and characteristics. This is particularly pronounced in online environments where it is not nearly as simple for retailers to convey to the customer the characteristics of a product [13]. This implies that, in the online shopping context, the ways in which customers interact with online retailers and form trust in the retailer's online operations must necessarily differ with different product types [18].

Although trust is known to depend on the type of products being purchased online, researchers have neglected thus far to elucidate why and how online trust development differs for certain product types. Hence, this study assesses the development of trust under different product uncertainties as e-commerce progresses from primarily offering search products to offering experience products.

## 3. Research model and theoretical development

With greater understanding of previous research concerning trust and the necessity of assessing the longitudinal development of trust in the context of multi-channel retailers under varying degrees of product uncertainty, we have developed a comprehensive research model for both the before-interaction phase and the initialinteraction phase, as shown in Fig. 1. The before-interaction phase (BI) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the period prior to the customer's initial visit to the website of the multi-channel retailer [23] whereas the initialinteraction phase (II) spans the period following the customer's <sup>fi</sup>rst visit, but prior to the customer's initial online purchase [24].

Because trust is a strong and positive predictor of a customer's intention to purchase, which is in turn a good predictor of purchasing [28], and as major antecedents of trust have been supported in a variety of previous studies, this study concentrates more on testing hypotheses regarding the unique features of multi-channel retailers, comparisons between BI and II, and the moderating functions of different product types.

## 3.1. Antecedents of trust

Many studies have attempted to categorize antecedents of customer trust. However, Kim [22] previously proposed a simple category after a careful review of the diverse trust antecedents and their category literature. He argued that trust is built either by self-perception predicated on direct experience or interaction with a trustee, or is transferred from a trusted entity to another entity. Because this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not only easier to understand but also parsimonious, we have adopted his viewpoint with only slight modi<sup>fi</sup>cation in this study, in an effort to identify the major antecedents of trust. The antecedents of online trust are categorized in this study by combining the self-perceptionbased and the transference-based forms of trust elucidated in Kim's study [22], as well as the calculus-based trust concept to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the characteristics of the multi-channel retailer context; cross-channel sanctions in particular [1,27].

## 3.2. The multi-channel retailer context

The research model in Fig. 1 contains four antecedents of trust at BI and one additional antecedent, Perceived Website Quality (PWQ), at II. There are no self-perception based antecedents at BI because a customer in BI has had no direct experience or interaction with the website of a multi-channel retailer. Rather, two well-known transference-based antecedents – structural assurance of the Internet (PSA) and word of mouth (WOM) – are included. Perceived structural assurance in the Internet milieu can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the belief that structures on the Internet are in place to promote successful ecommerce transactions [30].

Fig. 1. Research model.

If customers perceive the structures on the Internet to be generally adequate for safe transactions, they should be more likely to trust the multi-channel retailer's online operations, and should also be more likely to make online purchases from the multi-channel retailer [30]. Word-of-mouth is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in this study as the extent to which people in the customer's social network provide positive information about online purchases from the retailer [14].

Transference-based trust involves transference from a trusted “proof source” [11]. This “proof source” might be another individual or organization (including a third party) that corroborates the WOM or PSA, respectively. In the multi-channel retailer context, however, one more proof source must be added – namely, the individual consumer. As we mentioned previously, customers may already have a certain level of trust in a multi-channel retailer's online operations based on their of<sup>fl</sup>ine purchasing experiences. Hence, we would like to assess whether this of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust from one's own experience can be transferred to online trust. As such, we have added of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust (OFF) to the model, assuming that of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust positively affects online trust:

H1. Of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust positively affects trust in the retailer's online operations at the before-interaction and initial-interaction phases.

Effective sanctions are considered a powerful form of social capital that in<sup>fl</sup>uences trusting behaviors [7]. This is normally enforced through the social actor's relations and networks. Yamagishi and Yamagishi's [45] theory on trust also addresses the importance of deterrence or sanctioning measures. They argue that trust exists because the truster knows that the trustee fears the consequences resulting from untrustworthy behavior. Their concept is quite similar to Doney and Cannon's [11] calculative process of forming trust, Lewicki and Bunker's [27] calculus-based trust, and Shapiro et al.'s [37] deterrence-based trust.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions (PES) as the expectation of benign behavior from a multi-channel retailer based on the sanctions that customers can impose on the retailer [7,37]. In the multi-channel context, PES consists of two major sanctions – direct and crosschannel sanctions [39]. Direct sanctions are online measures employed by individuals to contact the retailer directly in order to obtain redress for disappointing purchases (i.e., email, feedback forms), while crosschannel sanctions refer to measures that enable individuals to employ the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel (such as physical stores) to seek resolutions to online purchasing problems. Several previous empirical studies have shown how customers impose sanctions on companies that violate their goals and values [i.e., 39]. When customers know they can impose sanctions on the retailer, they believe that the retailer fears the consequences of being untrustworthy, and is thus constrained to behave in a trustworthy manner.

As the customers have an additional channel for sanctions – namely, of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores – we would anticipate that this construct will carry out a more prominent function in the formation of online trust in the multi-channel retailer context relative to the pure online retailer context:

H2. Perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions positively affects trust in the retailer's online operations at the before-interaction and initial-interaction phases.

## 3.3. Longitudinal nature

Customers who decide to use the website of a multi-channel retailer enter stage II. During stage II, the self-perception-based antecedent from direct interaction with a trustee [22] will carry out an important function. Perceived website quality (PWQ) has been previously regarded as a representative factor [19,28] among the self-perceptionbased antecedents. A customer would evaluate the retailer's online trustworthiness by navigating the website. The assessment of the retailer's website quality consists of system quality and information quality [29]. System quality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the system on the website possesses the attributes of access and usability whereas information quality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the information on the website possesses the attributes of content, accuracy, timeliness, and usefulness. As research attempting to tie website usability or quality to trust has yielded rather mixed results [28, p. 200], this study, <sup>fi</sup>rst and foremost, evaluates the impact of PWQ on customer trust in the retailer's online operations at stage II.

H3. Perceived website quality positively affects trust in the retailer's online operations at the initial-interaction phase.

During the initial interaction, trust is likely to be increasingly predicated on the direct experience of the truster with the trustee [31]. As such, we may anticipate that PWQ plays a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in stage II than other antecedents. In particular, the effects of the perceived structural assurance of the Internet (PSA) will become less profound at II than at BI as customers acquire speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge regarding the website (PWQ) at stage II.

In other words, once people visit the website, they can make decisions about safety on the basis of their direct experiences with the site, including their impressions of the quality of the website. This experience with the website will attenuate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the overall structural assurance of the Internet [31].

H4. Perceived structural assurance of the Internet affects trust in the multichannel retailer's online operations more signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly at the before-interaction phase than at the initial-interaction phase.

The effects of other antecedents might also differ in different stages because experience-based knowledge dominates tentative assumptions [31]. When individuals accumulate speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge about a website, both transference-based and calculus-based antecedents are replaced by direct knowledge. WOM is a proxy and heuristic cue that re<sup>fl</sup>ects how believable an online store is when customers have no experience with that online store. The same logic applies to PES. After consumers gain experience with an online store, both antecedents will in<sup>fl</sup>uence online trust to a lesser degree than was the case previously.

However, this question resides principally in of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust. As offline trust derives from a customer's self-experience unlike other antecedents, despite its transference-based nature, it may have a stronger effect at stage II than at BI when the customer's online experiences reinforce his or her prior of<sup>fl</sup>ine experience. This assumption might be explained by information integration theory (IIT), which explains how people will most likely process existing nodes and associations and create new inter-nodal links [28].

According to IIT, beliefs are integrative in that the old states are gradually modi<sup>fi</sup>ed and are combined with new information and experiences in the formation of new states [46]. That is, positive experience from initial interaction with the website may function as retrieval cues to nodes for previous of<sup>fl</sup>ine experience at BI. By linking the online website to the attributes of the of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust might be substantially increased. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H5. Word of mouth and perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions affects trust in the multichannel retailer's online operations more signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly at the before-interaction phase than at the initial-interaction phase.

H6. Of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust will be more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust in the multichannel retailer's online operations at the initial-interaction phase than at the before-interaction phase.

## 3.4. Product uncertainty

Customers' trust in retailers is determined by whether the retailers describe a product faithfully, particularly in online environments, where it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the retailers to adequately describe the characteristics of experience products [13]. Despite its importance, few trust studies have seriously addressed product uncertainty. In the context of online trust, Vijayasarathy's [43] <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that intangible products are generally perceived as more appropriate for online purchases than for tangible products (products that can be touched). Similarly, Hassanein and Head [18] determined that customers would tend to have more online trust in retailers when purchasing intangible products than when purchasing tangible products.

However, these two studies do not allow for empirical examinations as to whether the process of online trust formation does indeed differ according to the product types being compared. Furthermore, the choice of product categories (tangible and intangible) may not be adequate to explain why certain products are currently not doing well online. For example, books are currently performing well in online sales, whereas beauty products and furniture are doing relatively poorly [47] although all these products are tangible. We would, therefore, need to utilize a more appropriate product typology in order to differentiate effectively the process of online trust formation according to different product types.

This study adopts Zeng and Reinartz's [47] product typology – namely, experience and search products. Experience products require multiple senses for evaluation whereas search products require only the senses of sight and hearing. Customers are likely to encounter greater levels of uncertainty when purchasing experience products online as they are not afforded the opportunity to evaluate the products through their physical senses. The evaluation of varying levels of uncertainties in purchasing experience and search products online results in differences in risk perceptions. Such greater levels of uncertainty may lead to increased dependence on other factors including PSA, WOM, OFF, PES, and PWQ. Thus, we hypothesize that the relationships of the antecedents of trust are moderated by product type.

H7. The relationships between trust in the retailer's online operations and its antecedents are stronger for experience products than for search products.

## 4. Research methodology

In order to evaluate the proposed hypotheses, this study employed surveys, administering online questionnaires to actual customers. The department store industry was selected for focus in this study for two reasons. First, the broad range of products offered by department stores <sup>fi</sup>ts neatly into Nelson's [34] categories of products: search products such as computers, cameras, shavers, and electronic toothbrushes, and experience products including shoes, clothes, sunglasses, and perfumes. Second, researchers have previously pointed out that even popular department stores such as Wal-Mart and JC Penney have yet to achieve the level of online success they have sought [47].

Thus, in order to determine why online department stores are not generally performing well, we have targeted the three most popular online department stores in Korea: Lotte, Hmall (Hyundai), and Shinsegae. The growth of the department store industry in Korea is principally led by these top three national chains; these three chains accounted for 76% of the total sales of the segment in 2008, which represents a large increase from 41% in 1998, and three sites were ranked in the top 10 of session visits in the online shopping mall category: Lotte.com – 4th, Shinsegae – 6th, and Hmall – 8th (Dec. 2008) according to a website research company (www.rankey.com, website analysis/evaluation organization).

## 4.1. Pretest for classification of product types

This study <sup>fi</sup>rst involved a pre-test to classify the available product categories into Zeng and Reinartz's [47] classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of experiment and search products. Upon examination of several online websites (i.e. Sears, Wal-Mart, Target), we listed approximately 20 popular product categories in common such as clothes, shoes, and digital cameras. A questionnaire regarding the 20 product types was administered to 124 undergraduates, all of whom were frequent shoppers. They were instructed to rate, on a scale of 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), whether the products required direct physical contact to determine whether the given product would ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their needs. Ward's method was used to form hierarchical agglomerations as it generates tight minimum variance clusters and is currently regarded as one of the most effective hierarchical clustering techniques [44].

Using SPSS, all 20 of the product categories are regarded as separate clusters. Subsequently, two clusters are selected at each step, and their union leads to the lowest increase in the squared distances from each case to the center of the cluster to which it belongs. There were 19 clustering steps, and the <sup>fi</sup>nal step generated one cluster that encompassed all the cases [40]. Each agglomeration schedule demonstrated that the largest jump in coef<sup>fi</sup>cients occurred between stages 18 and 19, indicating 2 clusters of products (experience products and search products).

## 4.2. Procedures and data collection for the main survey

As previously suggested by Moore and Benbasat [32], we carried out a three-stage process for the development and validation of the survey instrument. The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase was item generation, and its objective was to identify items from existing scales and adapt them to suit the study context (see Appendix A for all items). The next phase was scale development, wherein a panel of judges sorts the items from the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase into separate categories based on the similarities and differences among items. Based on their placement, the items could be re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and ambiguous items could be eliminated. The various scales are then subjected to an instrument testing phase wherein we conduct a preliminary test of the reliability and validity of the scales. Moreover, because this survey was conducted in South Korea, backward translation was applied to ensure consistency between the Korean and the original English version of the instrument [33].

The survey was conducted by a professional online survey company. Emails were sent out to 33,800 members of the company's panel, inviting them to participate in the online survey. This yielded a response rate of 16%. This response rate is regarded as reasonable for a solicited email survey [15], especially considering the decreasing trend in the average response rates in online surveys [38].

As we were uncertain as to the eligibility of the respondents prior to emailing, several criteria were used to select eligible respondents for the survey. First, they should have purchased either experience goods or search goods from one of three of<sup>fl</sup>ine department stores. Secondly, customers who had never visited the online websites of these department stores were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as BI respondents, and those who had visited but had never purchased any products from the website were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as II respondents. If they had already purchased something from the website, they quali<sup>fi</sup>ed neither as BI nor II respondents. Moreover, they should also have had encountered information regarding the website via word-of-mouth. Only respondents who satis<sup>fi</sup>ed all of these requirements were regarded as appropriate constituents of the sample in this study. We collected 222 responses for each product type for BI, as well as 209 responses for search products and 200 responses for experience products for II. A total of 853 responses were collected for analysis.

The demographic details of the respondents are summarized in Appendix B. The frequency of Internet purchases per month of our sample is quite similar to that provided in the Korea internet status report which asserts that 85.5% of online shopping users make online purchases at a frequency of three times per month (National Internet Development Agency of Korea, http://isis.nida.or.kr/). Hence, we consider our sample to be generally representative of the Korean online population. A monetary incentive of 6000 Won ( USD 5.00) was offered to respondents for completing the questionnaire.

## 5. Data analysis

Partial Least Squares (PLS) Graph version 3.00 was employed to evaluate the hypotheses of the two phases across search and experience products. PLS is an appropriate statistical technique, as some of the constructs in the model are formative. PLS, a component-based protocol, incorporates both formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators [4].

## 5.1. Instrument validation

In order to determine whether the data of the product types could be integrated, we utilized the Chow test with regard to the simultaneous equality of all the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. The null hypothesis of parameter stability is not rejected when the computed F value in an application does not exceed the critical F value obtained from the F table at the selected signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level. The F value does not exceed the critical F value in both BI and II; thus, we do not reject the null hypothesis. We can, therefore, conclude that the models of the search product group and the experience product group are the same, and the samples can be combined to test the research model. The new latent variable modeling approach was employed to evaluate the interaction effects of product types [6].

We subsequently proceeded to ascertain whether the remaining items evidenced convergent and discriminant validity. For all phases and product types, composite reliabilities were in excess of 0.7 and the AVE values were higher than 0.50 [17] (Tables 1 and 2). The constructs also evidence discriminant validity because the square roots of the AVEs are greater than the correlations between constructs. All measures are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant on their path loadings at a level of 0.01. When we applied Harmon's single factor test [36] for both phases, no single factor emerged; this demonstrated that no common method variance problems existed.

## 5.2. Hypotheses testing

## 5.2.1. Before-interaction phase

Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 are supported. The sub-constructs of the formative construct, perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanction (PES), are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and contribute greatly to online trust (OnT). The trust antecedents account for approximately 50% of the variance in trust in the online operations. The perceived structural assurance of the Internet (PSA) affects online purchasing intentions (OPI) less profoundly than does OnT. The direct effect of PSA on OPI changed the indirect effect of PSA through OnT from 0.585 to 0.548. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between OnT and OPI is affected directly by PSA by approximately 10%. With regard to the interaction effects of product types, only H7c was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. This result demonstrates that product type, which has been thus far neglected in online trust research, performs a crucial moderating role in the trust transfer of multichannel-retailers.

## 5.2.2. Initial-interaction phase

With regard to main effects, hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 are all supported. All sub-constructs of the formative constructs proved signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant and were shown to contribute to trust in online operations. The sub-constructs of the formative construct (PES) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and also contributed to online trust (OnT). The trust antecedents accounted for approximately 63% of the variance in trust in the online operations. Word-of-mouth, of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust, and PES exert signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger effects for experience products (H7b, H7c, and H7d), respectively. Similar to what was determined for BI, this indicates that product type is a likely candidate with regard to moderating effects during trust development.

## 5.2.3. Changes by phase

In an effort to evaluate hypotheses 4, 5, and 6, the statistical comparison between the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of two phases was conducted using the procedure described by Chin [5]. The impact of PSA on OnT became insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for II relative to BI, with a t-statistic of 28.03 (pb0.001) (H4 accepted). Although the effect of WOM on online trust is not altered by the initial interaction, PES is less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in its effects on OnT at II with a t-statistic of 18.05 (H5 partially accepted). Conversely, of<sup>fl</sup>ine cognitive trust (OFF) has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger effects on OnT for II as compared to BI with a t-statistic at 49.30 (pb0.001) (H6 accepted) (Figs. 2 and 3).

## 6. Discussions and implications

Along with different trust development across product types, this study uncovered the key factors in customers' trust in multi-channel retailers' online operations over time. Despite these signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we must exercise great care in the interpretation of our results, considering the following limitations of the study. First, although Korea is one of the most advanced countries in the world in terms of IT adoption, its unique culture may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the entirety of this research. This may reduce the generalizability of the study.

Next, future research may further examine the post-purchase stage in order to complete our investigation into the evolution of trust. Last but not least, although we re<sup>fl</sup>ected the direct online experience in indirect and direct ways in phase II, many other factors were not included in this research as we focused principally on trust. In II, WOM is related to indirect online experience whereas ‘perceived structural assurance of Internet (PSA)’, and ‘perceived website quality (PWQ)’ are related with direct experiences, which are more speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors associated with online experience [22]. Perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions (PES) in II is also re<sup>fl</sup>ective of online experience because individuals can perceive the possible ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions in terms of retailer's benign behaviors through the direct channel and crosschannel after they visit the website and gather product information.

However, we did not take into consideration other factors related to online experiences such as merchandizing, inventory, delivery etc., which were more closely related to convenience or satisfaction with online experience. Despite these limitations, the results of this research are intriguing. They are summarized below in three regards: the multi-channel retailer context, the longitudinal nature of trust, and the role of product uncertainty.

## 6.1. Discussion of results

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, <sup>fi</sup>rst of all, show us that of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust (OFF) from a customer's of<sup>fl</sup>ine experience with a retailer does transfer to his or her online trust in the same retailer in the multi-channel retailer context based on the transference-based trust theory. This result, then, naturally leads us to the question we initially raised herein: why do some renowned multi-channel retailers fail to achieve the expected level of online sales when competing against lesser-known pure online retailers? A plausible answer to this question can be inferred from the greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence of OFF from a customer's selfexperience standpoint at II than at BI, which itself is explained by the information integration theory (IIT).

That is, if a customer has either positive or negative experiences from an initial online interaction, which does not correspond to his or her prior of<sup>fl</sup>ine experience with the multi-channel retailer, the customer's level of online trust in the retailer can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed. This may be why some established multi-channel retailers perform dismally online from the customer trust perspective, and also represents, to the best of our knowledge, the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical explanation of this phenomenon.

In the context of multi-channel retailers, the role of perceived ef-<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions (PES) must be more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally explored owing to the sizeable impact of cross-channel sanctions, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally at BI. One unique aspect of multi-channel retailers is that customers expect to be able to solve emergent purchase problems both online and offline – which is not the case with pure online retailers. Our results demonstrate that the perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions – including the cross-channel sanction – will increase customers' online trust even at BI in addition to II. Hence, although multi-channel retailers must take advantage of their of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores in this regard, many of them fail by not allowing customers to solve the problems related to online purchase at their of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores.

With regard to the longitudinal nature of trust, the principal <sup>fi</sup>nding of the study is the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation that speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge regarding a website can replace trust-related inferences [31]. The power of PWQ predominates whereas the effects of WOM and PES are reduced at II. The impact of PSA actually disappears at II as customers accrue experience with the website. So long as customers understand that impersonal structures exist to safeguard their transactions during the initial visit, they tend to perceive the website as safe for online transactions. As a consequence, PSA is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern for customers when they actually begin to make purchases from the retailer's website; however, PWQ is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern. Hence, if PWQ is low, a company's of<sup>fl</sup>ine reputation may be irrelevant.

Finally, our results show that product uncertainty performs a moderating role in trust development, as anticipated. At both BI and II, OFF has been shown to exert a more profound impact on trust in online operations for experience products. As search products have standardized characteristics and require no physical contact for assessment, customers tend to be less likely to experience problems related to such purchases; thus, the need to contact the online retailer directly to exercise sanctions or to seek information from their social contacts is attenuated (and vice versa).

However, the more noteworthy <sup>fi</sup>nding here is that the moderating function of the product type becomes more active at stage II than at BI.

That is, as customers progress toward their <sup>fi</sup>rst online purchase, they pay more attention to the expected in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product type in their purchase. They become more prudent in purchasing experience goods by taking more seriously WOM and PES as well as OFF. Accordingly, initial interaction and trust formation are critically important for retailers who deal in experience goods. In cases in which multi-channel retailers fail to impress customers upon initial interaction through WOM or PES or to meet customers' of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust via the online website, prior trust, in particular prior trust in regard to experience goods, might be rendered irrelevant. Although we formulated no hypotheses concerning this, we could infer it from our results; this subject may need to be explored further in future studies.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to multi-channel retailer research not only by the simple insertion of of<sup>fl</sup>ine elements such as an address, but also in that it focuses on trust development through different time phases: how of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping experiences are transferred at BI and how the antecedents change following their online experiences at stage II. Considering these issues in the multi-channel retailer context, this study enhances and broadens the current academic understanding of online trust development in several ways.

First, this study extends the notion of transference-based trust by suggesting the ‘self’ as an additional proof source. In the transference-based trust theory, trust is transferred by another individual, group, or organization such as WOM and PSA. However, the customers' own experience can also be considered a viable proof source. Customers have their own previous purchasing experiences with the retailer's physical stores in the multi-channel retailer context. Our results demonstrated that OFF affects online trust to a greater degree than WOM and PSA in both phases. Therefore, the “self” should be considered an important and stable proof source in trust transfer at BI and II. However, this “self” source should be consistent across channels; otherwise, it could work negatively.

Secondly, this research suggests a comprehensive trust framework which extends Kim's trust framework via the addition of a calculusbased trust [22]. Kim [22] previously proposed a trust framework using self-perception-based trust and transference-based trust. This study suggests adding calculative-based trust to observe the effects of antecedents of online trust in a more comprehensive manner because it can cope with all sources of trust, which are generally similar to sources of knowledge. Trust is similar to knowledge, <sup>fi</sup>rst, in that it derives either from the self or from others (transference-based), and self-based trust can be derived from either experience (self-perception based) or inference (calculus-based). Thus, we propose this novel framework for future research into new antecedents of trust.

Third, this research also contributes to effective sanction research. The ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions provides modes through which customers can deal with multi-channel retailers in the case of dissatisfactory purchases. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that online direct sanctions and cross-channel sanctions can be used effectively to achieve customers' respective purposes. Furthermore, this research extends Day and Landon's [8] and Singh's [39] framework of complaining behavior by including cross-channel sanctions – a characteristic unique to multi-channel retailers.

Fourth, this study solidi<sup>fi</sup>es the information integration theory (IIT) via its application to the multichannel retailer context. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research demonstrate that IIT may explain the phenomenon in terms of the phase change from BI to II in the multi-channel retailer context, thus answering the question: why do some renowned multi-channel retailers fail to achieve the expected level of online sales when competing against less well-known pure online retailers? The IIT proposes that customers do indeed modify their offline trust in a retailer according to their interaction with the same retailer's online operations.

Finally, this study contributes to the current body of literature on trust by differentiating online trust development across product types. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that customers place differing degrees of emphasis on a variety of factors to determine trust in a retailer's online operations for each of the two product types. Furthermore, this study provides evidence to suggest that the moderating role of product uncertainty becomes more critical in stage II than in BI, as customers become closer to actual online purchases.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Multi-channel retailers should leverage customers' of<sup>fl</sup>ine trust for online trust development during phases BI and II. In order to mitigate the higher uncertainty surrounding the online purchase of experience products, physical stores can be used to allay customers' fears of not selecting the most appropriate experience products for their needs. Customers can be provided with the option of reserving the product online, going to a physical store to try the product, and then determining whether or not to complete the online transaction at the physical store.

Customers' greater reliance on the perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of sanctions has been shown to be greater for experience products during stage II. In order to entice customers to purchase experience products, retailers may wish to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their sales and return policies with discretion, tilting more in the favor of customers, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with regard to the characteristics of experience products. One of the principal advantages of multi-channel retailers is the provision of customer service via cross-channels. Multi-channel retailers should focus on developing not only purchase programs such as “buy online, then pick up at the store” programs (Sears.com), but also cross-channel customer services designed to increase customers' online trust and help them in making their <sup>fi</sup>rst online purchases.
