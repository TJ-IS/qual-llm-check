# Changing information retrieval behaviours: an empirical investigation of users’ cognitive processes in the choice of location-based services

Ioanna D. Constantiou<sup>1</sup>, Christiane Lehrer<sup>2</sup> and Thomas Hess<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, Denmark; <sup>2</sup>Institute for IS and New Media, Ludwig-Maximilians-University Munich, Germany

Correspondence: Ioanna D. Constantiou, Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, Frederiksberg 2000, Denmark. Tel: +4538152353; Fax: +4538152401; E-mail: ic.itm@cbs.dk

## Abstract

The introduction of smartphones and the accompanying profusion of mobile data services have had a profound effect on individuals’ lives. One of the most influential service categories is location-based services (LBS). Based on insights from behavioural decision-making, a conceptual framework is developed to analyse individuals’ decisions to use LBS, focusing on the cognitive processes involved in the decision-making. Our research is based on two studies. First, we investigate the use of LBS through semi-structured interviews of smartphone users. Second, we explore daily LBS use through a study based on diaries. The findings highlight that the decision to use LBS can be described by either a comparative mode based on the value of LBS in relation to other available options, or an intuitive mode in which past experiences trigger the use of heuristics. These modes in turn have positive influences on the continuance of LBS use and indicate changes in individuals’ information retrieval behaviours in everyday life. In particular, the distinct value dimension of LBS in specific contexts of use changes individuals’ behaviours towards accessing location-related information. ## Introduction

The profusion of smartphones in mobile telecommunications markets around the world offers new opportunities for innovative mobile data services. In addition to making calls and writing text messages, more complex mobile data services have slowly become part of the everyday life of mobile phone users. One category of these services is location-based services (LBS). LBS are services that use the current geographic position of a mobile user to provide personalised services (Perusco & Michael, 2005). A number of positioning technologies, such as Cell Identi<sup>fi</sup>er (Cell ID) or the Global Positioning System (GPS), allow pinpointing of an object’s or an individual’s current position (Rao & Minakakis, 2003). The literature points out various advantages of LBS compared to other options available for retrieving location-related information, such as desktop-based PCs. In particular, timeliness and positioning are suggested as the key dimensions of the value propositions of LBS (Xu & Gupta, 2009). Additionally, automatic positioning reduces the effort for the user of having to enter location information manually (Küpper, 2005). LBS facilitate location-based information retrieval by making it less time-consuming and more accurate, especially when individuals are mobile (Rao & Minakakis, 2003; Robinson et al, 2012). LBS also allow for new forms of information retrieval, such as momentary and spontaneous use while on the move (Kaasinen, 2005).

Having failed to live up to the expectations of becoming mass-market products for a long time (Bellavista et al, 2008), LBS have in recent years become highly popular due to technical advances and new marketing strategies (May et al, 2007). Key drivers of this popularity are the smartphones, the widespread availability of broadband wireless infrastructure and a change in pricing models for fast data connections (e.g. <sup>fl</sup>at rates for mobile Internet instead of users paying by the megabyte). Today, every mobile operating system allows the download of complementary services, generally developed by third parties, through their applications stores. Services vary in cost, from free downloads to expensive ones, depending on their purpose of use. The top ranked free LBS include point of interest search tools (e.g. for restaurants, bars, hotels, banks, petrol stations, pharmacies and so on), train and public transport information and traf<sup>fi</sup>c monitoring. Topselling paid LBS include car navigation software, <sup>fi</sup>tness applications and city guides.

Yet, as LBS have only been used by mobile users for a short time, there is limited research on their actual use. Most previous studies have focused on the adoption of LBS. Cost, security and privacy issues, quality of information (e.g. accuracy and update) and lack of knowledge about LBS were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the main barriers to adoption (e.g. Chang et al, 2007; May et al, 2007). Users’ privacy concerns have been studied as well (e.g. Sheng et al, 2008). LBS use was investigated by Pura (2005), who found that conditional value (i.e. in speci<sup>fi</sup>c use context), commitment and monetary value had the strongest in<sup>fl</sup>uence on behavioural intentions to use LBS. This indicates that the value of LBS is context-dependent and that people lack motivation to use LBS unless they create value in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations.

As LBS have become increasingly popular, we are now able to analyse user behaviour in more depth by focusing on adopted services. However, LBS use cannot be viewed in isolation. Users have traditionally acquired locationrelated information through other means such as desktop-based PCs, paper maps or face-to-face interactions with others. These traditional channels have been complemented by the mobile channel. Thus, users do not choose to use LBS in isolation, but take into consideration available alternatives. Technology adoption theories have proven useful when studying a single application or technology, but the use of LBS takes place in an environment where the services compete with existing technological and non-technological means for acquiring similar information. The technology adoption theories, due to their deterministic nature, do not support investigation of the actual choices made by individuals in various contexts of use in everyday life. Instead, they postulate relatively stable perceptions and beliefs, for example, perceived usefulness of a service or perceived ease of use, as determinants of the user behaviour. In this paper, we set out to investigate the cognitive processes involved in the decision to use LBS and the ways in which they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the user’s information retrieval behaviours. Our research question is:

What are the cognitive processes involved in the choice of a location-based service and how do these processes in<sup>fl</sup>uence information retrieval behaviours?

We address this question by applying theories of behavioural decision-making (Kahneman, 2003) and conducting empirical research based on interviews as well as a diary study of daily LBS use. Our contribution is twofold. First, we contribute to mobile user behaviour research by underlining the cognitive processes involved in LBS use and highlighting the importance of the context of use on perceived value of LBS. We claim that LBS use should be investigated in relation to speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of use, as a choice among alternative options. The introduction of an alternative theoretical approach helps us to enrich our understanding of post-adoption behaviours. Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasise the importance of LBS in everyday life, by providing evidence of changing information retrieval behaviours and depicting emerging behavioural patterns.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section describes the theoretical approach and positions the proposed framework. Then, the conceptual framework, based on insights from behavioural decision-making, is presented. The research approach is described in the following section and the next two sections present the studies’ <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which are then discussed. The conclusions and the future research directions are presented in the last section.

## Theoretical background

Research on LBS use falls into the core stream of IS research on user behaviour. Various theoretical perspectives have been used in IS research to understand the adoption and use of services or technologies. Different models have been introduced to explain adoption intentions, focusing on users’ perceptions of a technology’s performance and ease of use, for example, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al, 2003), as well as focusing on the social and cognitive determinants of the user, for example, the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991). Recent empirical studies of people who grew up with the Internet, various interactive applications (e.g. video games) and mobile phones, such as the ‘digital natives’, show that their perceptions of technology are only weakly affected by aspects of functionality such as ease of use and effort expectancy, but rather centre on usability in terms of application design and perceived value elements (Vodanovich et al, 2010). Moreover, the models of TRA and TPB focus on the determinants of adoption intentions rather than on actual use or value perceptions of a product or service. The underlying assumption of these models is that the adoption intentions are a good predictor of the user behaviour.

Concerning use behaviour, during the last decade IS research has paid increasing attention to the post-adoption phase, investigating the driving factors for continuing use of services or technologies. The most applied theory is the IS Continuance Model by Bhattacherjee (2001), which links satisfaction and perceived usefulness (from TAM) to a user’s intention to continue using a system.

While we acknowledge the importance of existing research in explaining the individual determinants of adoption and continuance intentions, it is our contention that the choice process for LBS use is determined by value perceptions and heuristics, as well as the context of use. Prior research <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that mobile service use is value-driven instead of technology-driven (Pura, 2005; Constantiou et al, 2009). Consumers’ evaluations of a mobile service are largely based on how valuable they perceive the content provided to be within a particular context of use and less on technological aspects such as technical complexity (Pura, 2005). Drawing on behavioural decision-making models is therefore an important approach that can provide new and valuable insights.

Context of use is an important parameter in the decision-making process. Previous research has found that the value of mobile services changes in different contexts, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences user behaviour. For example, Mallat et al (2009) found that users particularly appreciate mobile services in situations when they are in a hurry, or when no other alternatives are available. However, LBS use may not always be based on a value assessment. In fact, the individual decides in a constructive mode which decision strategy to use, acting as an ‘adaptive decision maker’ (Payne et al, 1993). For example, when people <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in a context of use already known and experienced, their decisions may be based on heuristics instead (Payne et al, 1992).

## A conceptual framework for individuals’ use of LBS

Individuals use different cognitive systems to assess information during the decision-making process. Dual process theories dominate the <sup>fi</sup>eld of social psychology (Hodgkinson et al, 2008) and rely on a distinction between intuition and reasoning, underlining the differences in mental effort required by the individual. Stanovich & West (2000) depicted two systems of thinking: the intuitive or perceptual System 1, which is fast, automatic, effortless and emotionally charged, and the reason-based System 2, which is slower, effortful and deliberately controlled. Most empirical studies in the <sup>fi</sup>eld have concluded that everyday activities are mainly intuitive (Kahneman, 2003; Kahneman, 2011). Kahneman & Tversky (1979) investigated intuitive judgement in relation to perceptual and reason-based thinking. Intuitive judgement is introduced, ‘between the automatic operations of perception and the deliberate operations of reasoning’ (Kahneman, 2003, p. 1452), as a hybrid that involves processes and content of both systems. For example, the intuitive system is not only based on current stimuli activated by impressions, but other stored concepts as well. Perceptual thinking has many similarities to intuitive thinking but also allows for dealing with changes and differences, as it is reference-dependent, enabling comparative cognitive processes (Kahneman, 2003). The three cognitive systems’ approach (Kahneman, 2003, see p. 1451) is more comprehensive than the dual process models for the analysis of everyday activities and constitutes the theoretical basis for the present research.

Our conceptual framework is based on perception and intuition, since LBS use is an everyday activity. The perceptual or comparative processes are affected by current contextual stimuli or the percept. The intuitive system involves heuristics activated either by current stimuli or stored concepts such as mental representations (Kahneman, 2003). Heuristics facilitate and accelerate the decision-making process by reducing the amount of information processed (Bazerman & Moore, 2008). Both the comparative and the intuitive processes ‘generate impressions of the attributes of objects, of perceptions and thought’ (Kahneman, 2003, p. 1452), which are monitored by the reasoning system during the evaluation phase (ibid). The reasoning system involves effortful cognitive tasks for the deliberate assessment of information related to the decision. These operations cover both impression-driven and reasoning-based approaches to judgement. Kahneman used the label intuitive for judgements that originate from impressions, thus from comparative or intuitive processes.

An individual’s choice of LBS can be viewed as a decision based on either comparative or intuitive processes, lightly monitored by reasoning processes. The reasoning system is lightly involved in everyday life activities (Kahneman, 2011) because the individual is not expected to use high cognitive effort in order to assess all parameters or to process information in detail before using an LBS, as she may do before buying a house, for example.

The external environment, where the choice is made, has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on an individual’s cognitive processes. In the literature of preference construction, researchers have examined individuals’ choices in relation to the external environment, or context (e.g. Lichtenstein & Slovic, 2006). For example, context effects may lead to assessments of trade-offs, or comparisons between services of different categories (e.g. services available on desktopbased PC, or laptops vs LBS) in various contexts of use (e.g. home vs working place with close substitutes available).

## The Context effects of LBS use

Background context refers to an individual’s beliefs about a product/service formed based on her experience with and knowledge about the product/service in question or others with similar characteristics. This information contributes to preference construction as well as to the subjective evaluation of a product/service’s attributes and trade-offs (e.g. price vs quality) (Simonson & Tversky, 1992). For example, an individual’s experience with similar online services, without positioning functionality, offered for free, may set the background context (Constantiou, 2009). If LBS are offered at a price, the service quality in terms of information accuracy should be higher than corresponding free services for people to choose them. Background context may also be shaped from experiences with speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS, due to the frequent use by the individual. The background context can be viewed as an ‘outer’ context that brings in<sup>fl</sup>uences, external to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of use (i.e. derived from knowledge or related experiences), to the individual cognitive process. As such, the background context is depicted as a ‘supra’ context in Figure 1, which interrelates with the context of use.

The local context refers to the immediate context of use, such as the situation in which the choice is made, and may or may not include other available options (e.g. presence of substitute products/services) (Blechar et al, 2006; Constantiou, 2009). In the case of LBS, a key dimension of local context involves the dynamics of location during a use session. This means that the user’s location during service use may be either temporarily static, or the user may be on the move when using LBS. The static or dynamic setting of LBS use implies differences in the context of use in terms of the number of alternative options available to the user as well as the stimuli in the environment. The use behaviours are expected to be different in dynamic and static contexts of use. Moreover, a given context will be either familiar or unfamiliar to the user. For example, in a static use condition at home, a familiar place, the availability of online services on a desktop-based PC or laptop may defer LBS use. Thus, the local context of use in<sup>fl</sup>uences the cognitive processes involved in the choice of a service through different stimuli. As another example, in an emergency situation the different prices of available options for acquiring location-related information may be disregarded in favour of an instant response service due to the immediate need (Constantiou, 2009).

The interrelations between background and local context effects are a rather complicated matter. As described by Scheepers et al (2006), a user’s perceptions, expectations, desires and satisfaction relative to the use of mobile technologies derive from multiple contexts and these multiple contextual in<sup>fl</sup>uences are indistinguishable from the user’s point of view. We argue that these claims are also valid for LBS use as the user of the service may not be able to distinguish the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of background and local contexts in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c use session. However, for the analytical relevance of the conceptual framework (Figure 1), we postulate that the background context highlights speci<sup>fi</sup>c stimuli in a local context of use that then activate the corresponding cognitive processes. In the conceptual framework, the local context of use, which is either dynamic or static, refers to an LBS use session.

The cognitive processes in the proposed framework are clustered into two groups, the comparative processes and the intuitive processes.

## Comparative processes in LBS use

Comparative processes are based on a perceptual system of thinking. According to Thaler (1980, 1985), an individual’s perception of the value of a transaction is mainly shaped by comparing a service’s actual price with a reference price, the price of the service in a ‘reference transaction’. Most research in this domain focuses on mental accounting (Thaler, 1980). However, many LBS are offered for free. In this case, comparative processes do not involve price comparisons. Instead, the individual compares service characteristics with a ‘reference service’. According to Payne et al (1993), the perceptual decision strategies of the individual can be categorised based on the dimensions of cognitive effort and accuracy. The comparison of service characteristics involves various cognitive tasks, including processing and assessment of related information, which requires an amount of cognitive effort. The higher the cognitive effort applied the better the accuracy of the decision. A thorough review of these strategies and the corresponding categorisation criteria is offered by Bettman et al (1998).

We expect that individuals attempt to reduce the cognitive effort required by the decision to use LBS as much as possible, since the purpose of the service use is an everyday activity. At the same time, the individual wishes to maintain accuracy of the decision, that is, choosing the right service for location-related information in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of use (Lehrer et al, 2011). A common decision strategy re<sup>fl</sup>ecting these objectives is lexicographic, where the individual chooses among alternatives according to the most important dimension (Bettman et al, 1998). This reliance on prominent dimension (Slovic, 1995), or prominence effect, involves ‘focused attention on the importance of reasons, arguments and justifications in choice’ (p. 369). For example, the individual may think about the effort of using LBS in order to acquire information, and compare it to a ‘reference service’ that constitutes the status quo service, usually chosen by the individual to accomplish a similar task (Constantiou, 2009). If the effort necessary for LBS use is lower, then it will be chosen.

Moreover, individuals are subject to loss aversion and thus have a strong tendency to remain with the status quo, as the disadvantages of leaving it loom larger than the advantages. This behaviour is known as the ‘status quo bias’ (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988). For example, moving from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c service to a new one can be seen as a loss solely because of the negative subjective value of the individual having to change her routines and put effort into developing new routines for the new service. Status quo bias may also positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the choice of an LBS that is already known to the user from another channel (e.g. Google Maps), and hence leads to a lower subjective loss than alternative options (e.g. other, unknown navigation services).

Context effects have strong in<sup>fl</sup>uences in the comparative processes, especially in the comparison between an LBS and a product/service representing the status quo. For example, when choosing between the use of a paper guidebook or an LBS, an individual accustomed to the paper guidebook may compare LBS to the former in terms of information accuracy, price, or another subjective value dimension. The individual will only choose LBS if it provides additional value compared to the product/service already used. Additionally, the local context may alter the prominent dimension used by an individual during the comparative process. When considering map and navigation services, for instance, a user may perceive the information accuracy of a service differently when navigating in an unknown place compared to a more familiar setting.

## Intuitive processes in LBS use

The individual choice of LBS may also be made in an automatic and associative manner through the intuitive system. This involves the use of heuristics aimed at reducing the cognitive burden of information processing (Kahneman, 2003). Three main categories of heuristics used by individuals have been analysed (for a thorough analysis of intuitive thinking, see Gilovich et al, 2002).

The availability heuristic is based on a vivid or easy-torecall characteristic of the object under investigation (Bazerman & Moore, 2008). The individual focuses on speci<sup>fi</sup>c available information that is easily and readily accessible to her memory due to its vividness or recency. This type of information about the underlined dimension may come from a recent or frequently recurring positive experience with a service. For example, a recent positive experience with a certain map service in which the user enjoyed <sup>fi</sup>nding a place easily may become the single reason that leads to the same service choice in a future situation.

The representativeness heuristic simpli<sup>fi</sup>es information processing as the individual focuses on representative information, or stereotypes, instead of considering actual data and facts (Bazerman & Moore, 2008). In this selective information processing, people use stereotypic approaches to assess information as part of their decision process.

Mobile users may generalise their opinion about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS category based on their knowledge of a single service in the category. For example, some ‘point of interest’ search tools do not always display updated and complete information in speci<sup>fi</sup>c locations. This low-quality attribute may have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the use of other LBS services in the same category even if a user does not know their respective content quality.

The affect heuristic emphasises the pivotal role of emotions in judging alternative options (Bazerman & Moore, 2008). The affective evaluation implies a fast and not conscious decision-making process. The affect heuristic can be triggered in decisions involving alternatives with hedonic value. For example, emotions such as desire may highlight the hedonic dimension of a service and motivate its use. Alternatively, fear may also induce the use of speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS (e.g. being lost in an unknown city).

Context effects in<sup>fl</sup>uence the intuitive processes. The background context de<sup>fi</sup>nes the amount and the nature of information that an individual has prior to the use of a heuristic, by highlighting particular stimuli. For example, a user’s associations with a recent experience may trigger the availability heuristic, leading to the choice of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS. Additionally, local context has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the activation of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c heuristic. Depending on the local context, different stimuli will be available to an individual, leading her to different heuristics when deciding on the use of LBS.

## Research method

We investigated LBS use in the German mobile telecommunications market, one of the most advanced markets in Europe. We aimed to study the LBS use, and how it in<sup>fl</sup>uences the everyday information retrieval behaviours of individuals. In order to shed some light on whether LBS make a difference in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of use encountered by individuals, we conducted two studies in 2010. First, we conducted in-depth interviews with smartphone users to understand LBS use in everyday life. A second study, using event-contingent diaries to investigate users’ behaviours and capture the dynamics of daily LBS use, was conducted after the analysis from the <sup>fi</sup>rst study revealed that further empirical data on the everyday use of LBS was needed.

We used non-random samples and targeted smartphone users, who were young adults, since this group was considered to contain early adopters and thus had a higher probability of LBS use in everyday life. Information about the distribution of participants in both studies is presented in Table 1. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst study there was an over-representation of males, which is in accordance with similar studies in the mobile communications industry in Europe describing the speci<sup>fi</sup>c target group (e.g. Constantiou et al, 2007).

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst study, we conducted semi-structured interviews to collect data for in-depth investigation (Lacity & Janson, 1994) of LBS use. The interviewees described LBS use in their everyday life, as well as what they thought about LBS use, through open-ended questions.

The interviews were conducted in German over a period of 2 months, and lasted between 30 and 45 min each. They were tape-recorded and subsequently transcribed and translated into English.

The interviewees were <sup>fi</sup>rst asked questions about their smartphone use. Following this, a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of LBS was provided to interviewees, along with the example of Google Maps. Then, questions were asked about speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS use and typical situations of use, factors affecting the cognitive processes such as their experiences, reasons of use, the willingness to pay, as well as about the context of use and alternative means available (e.g. PC, radio) in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations (see the interview questions in Appendix A).

Each of the authors analysed the empirical data by carefully reading and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the transcribed data, before comparing their notes and resolving differences. The data were then coded according to the theoretical concepts of the proposed framework for each service. We focused on how the decision to use speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS is made in different contexts of use. We used thematic analysis and followed a theory-driven approach in coding (Boyatzis, 1998; Braun & Clarke, 2006). Examples of the coding process are presented in Appendix B.

The second study complemented the in-depth interviews with an event-contingent diary study (Bolger et al, 2003) in order to capture the dynamics of daily LBS use and the underlying cognitive processes. We used insights from the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to describe contexts of use, alternative means of acquiring locationrelated information and to specify measures of heuristics and comparative processes. In the diary study, we focused on young professionals and not on other groups to make sure that the participants had well-structured daily routines with repeated usage situations. As time had elapsed between the two studies (i.e. 6 months), it was not possible to use respondents from the <sup>fi</sup>rst sample, and thus a new sample was established. The study included 16 participants and ran for 2 weeks. While 16 respondents constitute a relatively small sample size, it is still appropriate for a diary study in which each person contributes in-depth data over the course of 2 weeks (Reis & Gable, 2000; Bolger et al, 2003). Moreover, our aim was to gain a better understanding of the dynamic aspect of LBS use rather than to produce statistically generalisable results.

Prior to the diary survey, we met with each participant to explain how the study would proceed and how we de<sup>fi</sup>ned location-related information. We also conducted semistructured interviews with questions about channels used for acquiring location-related information, channel availability in different use contexts, LBS use and experience as well as technical skills. Over the following 2 weeks, participants were asked to <sup>fi</sup>ll in a diary entry every time they actively looked for location-related information. Each entry was made through a brief online survey (<2 min), which could be accessed through the user’s mobile phone to facilitate prompt completion. The survey questions are presented in Appendix C. The survey included four questions about context of use, one question about the channel choice, one question about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c application used if LBS was chosen, and one question about the use of heuristics or comparative processes in the service choice. In particular, two items concerned the availability heuristic: ‘It came to my mind <sup>fi</sup>rst’ and ‘I used this channel a lot recently’. Two items concerned the representativeness heuristic: ‘It offers the best information’ and ‘It’s the most trustworthy/reliable channel’. Two items concerned the affect heuristic: ‘I just like the channel’ and ‘I had an urgent need for the information’. Finally, one item concerned the comparative process: ‘After weighing the alternatives it was the quickest/easiest way to get the information’. These measures were developed by the authors based on a thorough literature review and were validated in a discussion with two domain experts. After the diary study had been completed, a post-interview was held with each participant to ensure there were not any content-related or technological dif<sup>fi</sup>culties with the diary and that the usage behaviour recorded in the diaries was representative. The data were analysed using descriptive statistics. The empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented in the next two sections.

## Analysing the use of LBS

The services used by the interviewees fall into the category of pull-based infotainment services, in which the mobile user submits a request to receive information (Schiller & Voisard, 2004). The participants did not use push-based services, which provide information without an active request (e.g. location-based advertising). The services analysed in this study were used by at least three interviewees.

Mapping and navigation services were used by nearly all respondents. The main advantage of these services is the provision of precise navigation instructions to the user while on the move. Point of interest services that enable users to discover new places around them also constituted a popular service category. Public transportation planners were used frequently on smartphones to retrieve arrival and departure times when the users were out because it was convenient. Fitness applications were also used rather frequently. LBS users can automatically record their route and their performance during activities such as running. City guides services identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study were mainly based on Wikipedia, such as Wikihood. The city guides were not used very frequently, because their use depends on how often an individual <sup>fi</sup>nds herself in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of use (e.g. travelling to new cities). Traffic monitors were not used very frequently either, but mainly when people wanted to know whether there was high traf<sup>fi</sup>c on the highways. Similarly, radar detectors were mainly used when people were following routes known for speed controls, and/or they were in a hurry.

Having observed the use patterns of the respondents, we investigated the underlying cognitive processes leading to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS use. We structure the analysis around static and dynamic local contexts. In the static condition, the user’s location during one use session is essentially stable. In the dynamic condition, the user is on the move while using LBS and is thus confronted with varying physical locations during the same use session. In fact, the services used in this condition only provide value to the user when she is moving, for example in a car or on foot, as they are meant to constantly track the user’s location and provide updated information (e.g. navigation instructions). For each local context condition, the use of LBS was analysed based on the intuitive and comparative processes of the conceptual framework, along with observations on the impact of the background context.

## Static context of LBS use

Services supporting information-seeking about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c location or about arrival and departure times of public transportation were investigated in relation to the static LBS use context. In particular, three service categories were analysed: point of interest services, city guides and public transportation services. These services were mainly used when users were situated in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c location and wanted to get information about time schedules and places around them. In these contexts of use, often a number of alternative options are available to the user to access the same information as offered by LBS, such as paper-based transport schedules.

<sup>Intuitive processes</sup> Intuitive processes were prominent in the use of point of interest services, which enabled users to discover new places, either in familiar or in unfamiliar settings. In familiar settings, participants perceived point of interest services as particularly useful when they had spontaneous needs. For example, when looking for inspiration, an interviewee indicated the use of the availability heuristic, where the service is associated with providing updated/new information: ‘I mainly use it when I am outside somewhere looking for a new restaurant and we think “okay, all of these restaurants we’ve already been to, let’s find something new”’ (Male, 18, pupil).

In unfamiliar settings, the point of interest services seemed particularly useful when the user explored unknown areas, or looked for new places. One interviewee stated, indicating the use of the availability heuristic, where the service is associated with being most useful: ‘I think it brings the highest value in unknown cities because in your home town you usually know your way’ (Male, 30, selfemployed).

However, some people expressed a lack of trust in point of interest services’ accuracy and articulated negative generalisations about such services, indicating the use of the representativeness heuristic, as shown in the following quote, where the service is associated with inaccurate performance: ‘I used it more at the beginning. But now I prefer to ask someone in the streets … an important factor is that I don’t really trust the service as I am not sure if it doesn’t sometimes mislead me’ (Female, 24, student).

Spontaneity was also observed in the use of transportation services when participants were making a time-sensitive choice on transportation means. An interviewee explained how LBS use changed speci<sup>fi</sup>c travel patterns by providing a better (faster) option, underlining the use of the availability heuristic, where the service is associated with providing accurate information in real time: ‘I am on my way somewhere and I wonder if I can catch the last subway around the corner in order to get back home, or if I can use a bus instead, of which I don’t know yet’ (Male, 28, student).

People described different situations in everyday life where transportation services were useful, providing further evidence on the use of the availability heuristic. The ordinary situation of use involved planning to go somewhere by public transport and using a service to <sup>fi</sup>nd the fastest connection: ‘Before I go somewhere I use the Fahrplan app, to find out the shortest way with the public transport’ (Male, 27, research assistant).

People also used the service when an unexpected change in transportation plans occurred and intuitive processes were used to quickly access information: ‘I use the app when I am at work, or at a friend’s place and it starts raining and I don’t feel like going home by bike. Then I look at what the next and best ways are to get home with public transport’ (Female, 29, research assistant).

City guide services were used in idle time, in a casual manner, indicating the use of the availability heuristic: ‘I use Wikihood to kill time while waiting for a friend’ (Male, 30, self-employed).

Background context effects from positive experiences with LBS in speci<sup>fi</sup>c static use contexts reinforced service use by highlighting contextual stimuli that in turn triggered the availability heuristic. Similarly, negative experiences with the service use might trigger the representativeness heuristic.

<sup>Comparative processes</sup> Comparative processes were mainly used to judge the value of LBS. For example, participants compared point of interest services with other, status quo services and chose LBS because of the higher perceived value in terms of the ease, speed or accuracy of information retrieval. An interviewee explained: ‘I wanted to find an electronics store. At first I searched via Google, but I couldn’t really find anything appropriate. Then, it crossed my mind that I had this application on my iPhone. At the end I found the location on Around Me and went there’ (Male, 16, pupil).

Despite the distinct value dimension of the services, participants did not seem willing to pay. This is likely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the background context including experiences with similar services offered for free, as described by an interviewee: ‘If Around Me cost money I would get the same information through Google Maps, which is for free’ (Male, 32, consultant).

The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of background effects from experiences related to the use of other free services on the willingness to pay was also highlighted for the Wikipedia-based services. The users acknowledged the value dimension of LBS compared to the free services in terms of targeted information about speci<sup>fi</sup>c locations but were not willing to pay for the service. An interviewee explained: ‘No, I don’t think so, because it just connects services that are free of charge, on the one hand Wikipedia and on the other hand the localization functions’ (Male, 27, student).

For transportation services, value added by automatic positioning did not seem high enough to make people willing to pay, as an interviewee explained: ‘No, because the typing in of an address is not that laborious that I am willing to pay for it’ (Male, 31, self-employed).

However, the lack of willingness to pay changed when respondents considered touristic city guides. In that case, the reference point was a paper guidebook, which the respondents were used to paying a certain amount of money for. Respondents indicated that the city guide service is useful in unknown, foreign places. However, use of such services abroad was severely hampered by high roaming tariffs.

<sup>Summary</sup> Static LBS were valuable to the users in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of use. This value enabled them to move away from the status quo in certain situations, and to use LBS instead of the services without positioning functionality used before. Background effects, involving positive experiences with LBS use, reinforced future use by triggering heuristics and spontaneous or even habitual use. At the same time, strong background effects from experiences of use of similar services for free, hindered participants’ willingness to pay for LBS in familiar static contexts.

## Dynamic context of LBS use

Services mainly used when the user is moving were investigated in relation to the dynamic LBS use context. Four service categories are presented, including map services supporting navigation, traf<sup>fi</sup>c monitoring, detection of installed speed controls and <sup>fi</sup>tness applications providing information on a user’s running activities. In this condition, the local context does not involve a high number of alternative options because the user is moving and no other information source offers the ability to constantly provide updated geographic information. Moreover, LBS may be used to cover new or unmet needs, as in the case of <sup>fi</sup>tness applications. Similar services without automatic positioning (e.g. Gmaps Pedometer) only allow people to measure the distance of speci<sup>fi</sup>c routes after manual data entry.

<sup>Intuitive processes</sup> Intuitive processes were very prominent in the case of map services supporting navigation, both in familiar and unfamiliar places when people had to <sup>fi</sup>nd directions. Service use was triggered by the availability heuristic as an interviewee explained where the service was associated with quick access to information: ‘I often use Google Maps in situations when I quickly want to find shortdistance directions. Like somebody tells me to go to street XY, I quickly look it up on Google Maps in order to get an idea where about that address is’ (Female, 27, student).

Another interviewee further explained how the representativeness heuristic, where a speci<sup>fi</sup>c map service is associated with instant and on-the-spot provision of accurate information, led to its automatic use: ‘A typical situation was that me and my friends arranged to meet in a cafe and they said that it is at the corner of X and Y streets. Before I left I looked up the nearest bus or subway stop on the Internet. When I got to the stop, I sometimes didn’t know where exactly I had to go or on which side of the street the cafe was. Or I hadn’t really looked up where exactly the cafe was and just went to the street I knew and then looked up on my mobile phone where exactly I was and where I had to go next. Because most of the cafes and shops were marked in Google Maps, this was the best way to get there’ (Female, 29, research assistant).

Intuitive processes were also observed in the use of other LBS such as radar detectors, although there was disagreement between interviewees’ perceptions of the service’s reliability. Some had positive experiences from the service reliability, triggering the availability heuristic in the subsequent use: ‘Once I went 80 km/h even though only 60 km/h were allowed and it warned me in an absolutely reliable manner’ (Female, 26, student). Others highlighted the service’s limited potential in providing location-related information (e.g. most services can only locate installed speed controls). Furthermore, participants complained about the intensive use of the smartphone’s battery by the service, generalising from a bad experience, using the representativeness heuristic: ‘The service only works when you have Internet connection and if you let the phone locate you constantly your battery is low after half an hour. That’s why I don’t use the service anymore’ (Male, 29, business consultant).

Radar detectors are a new service category covering an unmet need. They were mainly used when people were following routes known for speed controls, and/or when they were in a hurry. As an interviewee explained, when she is aware of a radars’ presence, then the availability heuristic is triggered, where the service is associated with providing reliable information about radars’ positions: ‘I don’t use it very regularly, but once in a while when I drive a longer distance or on streets known for radar controls’ (Female, 29, research assistant).

Background context effects from positive experiences and the high frequency of use of map and navigation services in speci<sup>fi</sup>c dynamic use contexts not only reinforced future use, but changed the users’ behaviours by leading to automatic use. In this case, the background context effects trigger the availability heuristic.

<sup>Comparative processes</sup> Background context effects also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the comparative processes. The common use of mapping and navigation services offered by <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Google and Microsoft has increased people’s familiarity with their functionality and value. As one interviewee stated: ‘Before I used Google Maps on my iPhone I used it through the stationary Internet, because in my opinion it is the best maps service’ (Male, 26, student).

Participants used comparative processes, such as reliance on a prominent dimension, as described by an interviewee: ‘Before Google Maps mobile you had to look up and print out the route at home. With the mobile app, finding routes is a lot more efficient and time-saving’ (Male, 27, research assistant).

It seemed that reliance on a prominent dimension was especially important in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of use, where LBS was chosen among alternatives because of its perceived higher value: ‘At the beginning I just used it as a map. For example, I completely got lost in Hamburg and thought I either pay a taxi or I find the right direction in any other way. Then it crossed my mind that I’ve got Google Maps on my phone. It really rescued me in that situation!’ (Female, 26, student).

Positive experiences with LBS allowed people to move away from the status quo and treat the use of mapping and navigation services on the smartphone as a new status quo, while reinforcing automatic future use. As the previous interviewee continued: ‘Since then I’ve been using it more and more often, because since then I knew how easy it works, that the directions are precise and that I can rely on it. Today I don’t even look up directions in advance anymore, because I know that I’ve got the iPhone with me’ (Female, 26, student).

People were willing to pay for these LBS due to underlying value dimensions and a high frequency of use. ‘If Google Maps wasn’t preinstalled, I could imagine paying for it, because the service offers a great added value and is very helpful in a lot of situations. I use Google Maps very often, almost daily’ (Male, 27, research assistant).

Similarly, for <sup>fi</sup>tness applications the interviewees were willing to pay small amounts (ranging from 1.50 to 3 euros), as they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the value elements in providing useful information about their sports activities. ‘Run Keeper is very good and I like to use it, because I get a lot of information on my running’ (Male, 30, self-employed).

Participants also compared different options available on the Internet and found additional value dimensions in the LBS <sup>fi</sup>tness applications. These services were found to offer added value for the user, and motivated a move away from the status quo: ‘But compared to Gmaps Pedometer it has the advantage that it works automatically and I don’t need to enter my route manually’ (Male, 28, research assistant).

Comparative processes were also applied to services such as traf<sup>fi</sup>c monitors, with respondents relying on the prominent dimension and comparing the services’ convenience of information acquisition with other available options such as the car radio: ‘Some Autobahns [highways] are notorious traffic jam routes, in such situations I don’t want to rely on the radio, because the announcements are over very quickly. The smartphone is at my fingertips and gives me exactly the information I need anytime and is always up-todate’ (Male, 40, post-doctoral researcher).

The time-sensitive information provided by the services, which in turn affected users’ decisions, was another prominent dimension compared with other alternatives: ‘I use the app in this situation, because it can save me time. I could also use the radio, but the information is available only temporarily and when you don’t know the area you often do not know which streets are relevant’ (Male, 26, marketing manager).

Traf<sup>fi</sup>c monitors have a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned value dimension in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations compared to alternative channels. Users were willing to pay a small amount (ranging from 0.79 to 2 euros) for such services. It appears the value generated by these services is changing the status quo of checking traf<sup>fi</sup>c information.

<sup>Summary</sup> Background context effects are pivotal for both cognitive processes in the dynamic context of LBS use. At the initial use stage, background effects in the form of previous experiences with similar services supported LBS choice by highlighting its value-adding dimension in comparative processes. At a later use stage, background effects in the form of accumulated positive experiences with the LBS use reinforce future use, by triggering the availability heuristic. Interviewees used comparative processes to judge dynamic LBS as valuable and often reported willingness to pay for them. The services’ value motivated users to move from the status quo, which in many cases was represented by a similar service without positioning functionality. For map services supporting navigation, in particular, there was evidence of a new habit creation: ‘I use Google Maps when I am on the move. I broke the habit of looking up the way in advance, with the iPhone I look up the target direction as I go. Google Maps has really changed my behaviour in this case’ (Male, 28, research assistant).

## Cognitive processes in the everyday use of LBS

The diary study enabled us to further investigate some empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the interviews about the everyday use of LBS as well as to shed some light on LBS use dynamics and how they affect information retrieval behaviours. Over the 2 weeks of the diary study, participants reported a total of 104 instances in which they looked for location-related information. The number of instances per participant varied between 1 and 19. The results show that when looking for location-related information, users mainly searched for navigation information, addresses and telephone numbers, as well as arrival and departure times of transportation. There was a strong tendency for people to use their smartphones over alternative channels to acquire location-related information. In 60% of instances, people chose LBS, in 16% they chose the mobile browser and in 10% the Internet on PC/laptop. The remaining instances involved use of the radio, the car navigation system and asking other people nearby. LBS were mainly used when people were mobile, either on foot, on a bike or in a car (45% of instances), followed by use at home (26% of instances). Interestingly, LBS were the preferred channel in all contexts of use, even when other channels were available. For example, at home or at work, people chose to use LBS on their smartphones over a PC or laptop.

Table 2 presents the percentages of 103 instances, categorised according to the cognitive processes used when choosing a channel to access location-related information. In one instance a novice user did not report the cognitive processes used. Of the total observed instances, 57 are related to experienced smartphone users (i.e. over 1 year of device use) and 46 to novice smartphone users (i.e. less than 1 year of device use). As Table 2 presents, both experienced and novice users acted mainly intuitively, using heuristics. The availability heuristic was most prominent in all usage situations. Contrary to theoretical insights, this was also true in contexts of use in which more than one channel was available. The results indicate that the individuals very rarely compared the options available, but instead acted in a habitual manner by choosing the service they commonly use. The experienced users mainly used the availability heuristic (47% of the instances). The novice users used both the availability and affect heuristics. The representativeness heuristic and the comparative process were only used on some occasions.

Turning to speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS use, mapping, navigation and point of interest applications were mainly used due to the availability heuristic. The use of traf<sup>fi</sup>c information services was mainly triggered by the affect heuristic, suggesting that the services were used in situations with an urgent need for traf<sup>fi</sup>c information. In the case of public transportation services, representativeness, availability and comparative processes were all equally used. Users weighed the options available according to how fast and easily they provided information. Additionally, users of this service category underscored information quality and service reliability.

## Discussion

Our research set out to investigate the cognitive processes involved in LBS use as well as how these processes affect information retrieval behaviours. We developed and used a conceptual framework (Figure 1) based on behavioural decision-making, which advances a new theoretical approach in user behaviour research in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. We presented empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning users’ cognitive processes leading to LBS use. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to the research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld by providing evidence of changes in information retrieval behaviours in everyday life. LBS not only facilitate information retrieval but, once adopted, have a catalytic role in changing behaviour towards location-related information retrieval.

In the next two subsections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the contribution of the proposed framework to the user behaviour research in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Then, we present the insights derived from the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and highlight new directions for further theoretical development in this research area. Four propositions are derived to serve as a basis for further research in other user behaviour contexts.

## LBS use as an outcome of cognitive processes

Existing technology adoption or use models, due to their deterministic nature, do not allow for studying actual LBS use in everyday life where LBS compete with other technological or non-technological means for acquiring location-related information. Therefore, new approaches are required to investigate user behaviour. The proposed conceptual framework is a useful tool in the investigation of LBS use and can complement existing models in user behaviour research in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

The contribution of the conceptual framework is threefold. First, by focusing on the choice process and its determinants, that is, cognitive processes, background and local contexts effects, the proposed framework does not investigate LBS adoption or use in isolation. Instead, it takes into account that LBS are chosen in an environment where other options for retrieving location-related information are available. This allows insights into the interactions between the ‘new’ mobile and more traditional channels. Second, by emphasising the importance of perceived value as opposed to more technology-focused models in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (e.g. Venkatesh et al, 2003), the proposed framework reinforces the view of the individual as a consumer who chooses the service that best suits her needs among alternative options, rather than a user adopting a solution based on technology perceptions such as ease of use. Third, the proposed framework underlines the fast or automatic mode of thinking in everyday decision making (Kahneman, 2003) compared with more rational models such as the IS Continuance Model by Bhattacherjee (2001), which assumes analytical thinking based on the user’s perceptions and expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation leading to the service choice. The simplicity and immediacy of LBS use decision-making implies fast thinking, which is captured in a comprehensive manner by the proposed framework. LBS use is an operational decision, supporting another primary activity in most cases (e.g. looking for a restaurant in the area to enjoy a nice dinner). As such, we do not expect the user to use analytical processes and compare a full set of parameters when deciding on LBS use.

With regard to cognitive processes described in the theoretical framework (Figure 1), in many cases interviewees described LBS use as the outcome of an intuitive process. For example, the use of navigation and map services covers the need for instant information retrieval. People do not need to spend as much time on thoroughly planning their outdoor activities as they did before. LBS, by providing information anywhere and anytime, allow for more spontaneous plans. As users become more experienced with LBS use, and strong background effects are developed, they tend to decide on future use based on the availability heuristic. This <sup>fi</sup>nding was supported in both studies.

Proposition 1a: Experienced users are prone to use the availability heuristic when they choose an LBS.

The high technical performance (e.g. service reliability) and usability of LBS (e.g. user interface) are preconditions for the observed behaviours. Interviewees with little experience in using LBS had strong positive or negative opinions about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technical attribute or characteristic of the service and generalised about LBS in the same category, which in turn affected their use or non-use decisions. In these cases, novice users use the representativeness heuristic. The users’ lack of experience with LBS and the weak background effects lead to the use of the representativeness heuristic.

Proposition 1b: Novice users are prone to use the representativeness heuristic when they choose an LBS.

The affect heuristic was more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to observe. This may relate to the fact that most LBS were chosen to serve operational or other functional goals and not hedonic ones.

Comparative processes were also used, especially, at the early phase of LBS use. Comparative processes were prominent when people judged the value of LBS compared to services constituting the status quo (e.g. similar online services without positioning functionality, paper guidebook). They chose LBS because of its higher perceived value in terms of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c dimension such as the ease, speed and accuracy of information retrieval. Underlying elements of usability were sometimes observed in these cognitive processes, for example, when the prominent dimension is related to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS design.

Proposition 2: In a context of use where information services, similar to LBS, constitute the status quo, mobile users are prone to rely on a prominent dimension in the choice of a LBS.

Supporting our initial assumption, the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings underline the strong effects of the context of use, which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences the decision-making process and the value perception of a service. Especially for timesensitive information needs or while on the move, people highly appreciate the positioning functionality and information accuracy of LBS. Dynamic contexts of use and unfamiliar settings highlight this value dimension of LBS. In familiar contexts of use, the background context has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence, prompting the availability heuristic. The actual interrelation between the background and local contexts is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to analyse. However, we found evidence that the background context highlights speci<sup>fi</sup>c contextual stimuli in familiar settings, which in turn constitute a basis for habit formation. In unfamiliar contexts of use, the background context effects are weak, and LBS enable developing new behavioural patterns and meeting unmet needs that are prompted by contextual stimuli.

## Emerging patterns of location-related information retrieval behaviour

Empirical research on LBS has underlined their potential to facilitate and improve retrieval of location-related information for the user (e.g. Rao & Minakakis 2003; Robinson et al, 2012). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to this research by highlighting emerging, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c behavioural patterns enabled by LBS.

The intuitive choice of LBS over other ‘traditional’ options in familiar contexts of use (e.g. at home) indicates both a shift of the status quo and the development of new behavioural patterns when retrieving location-related information. These new behavioural patterns are more prominent when users <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in contexts of use in which they repeatedly experience positive outcomes from the LBS use. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide evidence that some users change their behavioural patterns of information retrieval due to LBS use.

For some service categories, the status quo is now shifting in favour of LBS (e.g. map services, public transportation schedules). Most of the features provided by the popular LBS observed in this study are also available on the services representing the status quo. In order to move from the status quo, users seek extra value from LBS, related to the positioning functionality. For example, maps or public transportation services provide the user with more accurate information and reduce time spent while on the move.

Proposition 3: When mobile users experience LBS providing extra value compared to the other available options, they are prone to move to a new status quo, that is, using LBS instead of the previous service.

Users’ stated willingness to pay for most of LBS in the dynamic use context indicates the formation of new routines of information retrieval. There is an underlying endowment effect (Kahneman et al, 1991) since mobile users treat the respective services as part of their routines, providing extra value in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of use, and are willing to pay to maintain them. For example, <sup>fi</sup>tness applications enable the user to retrieve location-related information that was not available before their introduction. In this case, LBS address unmet needs of the users, and the explicit extra value of the services to the user facilitates the emergence of new routines of information retrieval.

Proposition 4: In a dynamic context of use, mobile users are willing to pay for an LBS when it addresses new unmet needs and enables the formation of a new routine of information retrieval.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our studies indicate that LBS may reshuf<sup>fl</sup>e or change behavioural patterns of acquiring location-related information in the physical world. For example, map services reduce the need to prepare navigation information before a trip, enabling the user to decide on the spot how to reach a speci<sup>fi</sup>c destination. Another example is traf<sup>fi</sup>c-monitoring services, where people used to listen to the radio for traf<sup>fi</sup>c information, whereas they currently switch to LBS. It appears that technology changes the physical world’s ‘reference service’ for location-related information retrieval to a digital one. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is also supported by the recent research of Kallinikos (2011) who claims that technology penetrates every activity in daily working and living and its facilities ‘embody a variety of routines and procedures at the same time as they generate a range of perceptual and cognitive stimuli that influence human behaviour in many subtle ways’ (Kallinikos, 2011, p. 3).

Collectively, the micro-behavioural insights have useful implications for future research projects as well as for practitioners such as application service providers. Further research is warranted to understand the formation of the new behavioural patterns, and to identify substitution effects and new trends in channel con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Similarly, for practitioners, it is important to understand the role of speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS in user behaviour patterns of information retrieval. For example, a strategic action of Apple to discontinue the use of Google Maps on the iPhone had ignited a backlash in the user community and resulted in a new collaboration between the two companies, enabling users to access the service again. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c service has been prominent in changing information retrieval behaviours.

## Conclusions and further research

We set out to explore LBS use enabled by smartphones and developed a conceptual framework describing the use of LBS in relation to cognitive processes. The framework enabled us to analyse the use of LBS in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts and to identify the value dimension for users. Based on insights from behavioural decision-making, the proposed framework contributes to research on user behaviour in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld by providing a new tool for analysing user choice. The framework considers the user as a consumer of services in different contexts of use where other options may or may not be available. As such, it underscores the role of cognitive processes and fast decisions in service choice.

Most of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings were based on interviewees using services available through Apple’s App Store. However, this is not expected to have a major impact on our contribution. The current, intense competition among mobile service developers and platforms such Apple and Android leads to a large supply of a variety of services designed to support the user. As such the user should not encounter dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in <sup>fi</sup>nding services that are easy to use. Moreover, most popular applications are available on all major mobile platforms. Thus, we expect our conceptual framework to be applicable to services offered by other mobile platforms such as Android.

Despite our careful design of the studies, they are subject to some limitations. The empirical data from the interview study did not allow us to investigate the use of the affect heuristic. This may relate to the type of LBS used by the participants, which mainly had functional value. The diary study is subject to two limitations. First, there may be missing data. Even though participants stated that they <sup>fi</sup>lled in the entries reliably, they may have forgotten to record entries (e.g. because they were not aware that they were looking for location-related information) or may have been selective in reporting (e.g. because they thought some events were not important enough to report). Second, although a comparison of the electronic time-stamps of the diary entries and the time of channel choice stated by the participants showed that the majority of entries were made within an acceptable time span of 1 h after the channel choice took place, a few entries were made within 2–3 h after the channel choice. Those instances may have decreased reliability, since users might not have recalled the use situation and the exact process leading to their speci<sup>fi</sup>c LBS choice.

Future research effort should focus on testing the four propositions in other user behaviour contexts. Moreover, the explanatory power of the conceptual framework should be tested through a large-scale quantitative study, or longitudinal studies of smartphone users’ behaviours applying sampling techniques that allow the estimation of general population trends. In addition, the current proliferation of new mobile devices such as tablets offers additional opportunities for further investigation. These devices, which build on and extend existing characteristics of smartphones (e.g. touchscreens), also support LBS use.
