# Out of fear or desire? Toward a better understanding of employees’ motivation to follow IS security policies

Jai-Yeol Son \*

School of Business, Yonsei University, 50 Yonsei-ro, Seodaemun-gu, Seoul, 120-749, South Korea

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 29 August 2009 Received in revised form 22 April 2011 Accepted 7 July 2011 Available online 27 July 2011

Keywords: Security management End-user security Security policy compliance behavior Intrinsic and extrinsic motivations General deterrence theory

## A B S T R A C T

Given the significant role of people in the management of security, attention has recently been paid to the issue of how to motivate employees to improve security performance of organizations. However, past work has been dependent on deterrence theory rooted in an extrinsic motivation model to help understand why employees do or do not follow security rules in their organization. We postulated that we could better explain employees’ security-related rule-following behavior with an approach rooted in an intrinsic motivation model. We therefore developed a model of employees’ motivation to comply with IS security policies which incorporated both extrinsic and intrinsic models of human behavior. It was tested with data collected through a survey of 602 employees in the United States. We found that variables rooted in the intrinsic motivation model contributed significantly more to the explained variance of employees’ compliance than did those rooted in the extrinsic motivation model.

## 1. Introduction

Security incidents can have a negative impact on the market value of a firm. Goel and Shawky [3] found that firms experienced a 1% decrease of market share after the announcement of a security breach. Surprisingly, many incidents are perpetrated by organizational members (inside the firewall). In this sense, employees at all levels are the weakest link in information security. Employees can be a threat because they can be involved in intentional abuse (e.g., data theft, data destruction, etc.) or unintentional or accidental events (e.g., forgetting to change a password, forgetting to log off, etc.). Such acts are often referred to as IS security non-compliance behavior [13]. As they have direct access to the network of the organization, employees often become the target of thieves or hackers who attempt to use social engineering techniques to gain access to an organization’s information. Nonetheless, many organizations underestimate the importance of managing human functions and rely heavily on technological solutions to their IS security. Most organizations develop their security programs without first considering the human aspect of their exposure to security breaches.

As pointed out by Siponen and Vance [9], studies in this area have relied heavily on general deterrence theory (GDT) as a theoretical basis for understanding why employees follow (or do not follow) an organization’s IS security policy (ISSP) (e.g., [1,4,6]).

Thus deterrent certainty and deterrent severity of GDT have been touted as effective strategies in preventing employees from misusing the information assets of their organizations. However, the efficacy of this theoretical solution has been questioned because studies have reported mixed results about the impacts of them as effective regulators of employees’ conduct.

Studies in organizational behavior that are drawn from the social psychology literature on human motivation have often explained employees’ rule-following behavior with two motivation models of human behavior [12]: one is an extrinsic motivation model (focusing on the perceived consequences, such as punishment or reward, of breaking the rules) and the other is an intrinsic motivation model (holding that employees follow the rules because of their innate desire to follow the rules). Past research found that the intrinsic motivation model better explained employees’ rulefollowing but the current research stream has been built on GDT and therefore is closely linked to the extrinsic motivation model. Despite its potential to explain employees’ security-related behavior, the intrinsic motivation model has not received due attention in the literature. We argued that we could improve our ability to explain employees’ violations of security-related rules with the intrinsic motivation model.

Our study, by focusing on the both the extrinsic and intrinsic models, was constructed to offer a theoretical explanation for why employees do or do not follow their organization’s ISSP. In particular, two salient variables were identified for each model: perceived deterrent certainty and deterrent severity for the extrinsic model, and perceived legitimacy and value congruence from the intrinsic model. They were assumed to be the determinants of employees behavior. The study also examined the relative contributions of the two models in understanding employees’ ISSP compliance behavior. Thus our stimulating question was: Do employees comply with an ISSP mainly out of fear (the extrinsic motivation), desire (the intrinsic model), or both?

## 2. Theoretical foundation and hypotheses

Development of a valid ISSP is a prerequisite to an effective security management program in an organization. It is a written statement that defines the goals and objectives of the policy, requirements for the organization’s security management, workers’ roles and responsibilities, and penalties and countermeasures for violations. One important element of an ISSP is to define rules for appropriate use of the organization’s IT resources by employees. Organizations often follow best practices provided by guidelines such as BS7799 and GASPP (Generally Accepted Systems Security Principles) to design ISSP which their employees must follow [10]. In addition, ISSP generally defines penalties for noncompliance.

We view employees’ compliance with ISSP as a specific case of employees’ rule-following behavior and therefore looked at the literature of rule-following behavior to understand why employees do or do not comply with their organization’s ISSP. This has suggested two models explaining employees’ rule-following behavior the extrinsic motivation model (a command-and-control approach) and the intrinsic motivation model (a self-regulatory approach). The extrinsic model has traditionally been used as a dominant paradigm in understanding employees’ rule-following behavior. Indeed, organizations appear to focus on the extrinsic model with security policies that state that non-compliance results in disciplinary actions that include termination of employment. To this end, general deterrence theory has been used as the dominant way to explain organizational efforts to regulate their employees behavior.

GDT was originally developed to explain how to prevent people from engaging in undesirable activities. The theory has been predominantly used as a theoretical foundation in the study of deviant behavior. The theory posits that individuals are less likely to commit a deviant activity when the risks of getting caught and the severity of punishment increases. These two central tenets, deterrent certainty and deterrent severity, are closely related to the enforcement of ISSP. Deterrent certainty refers to the high likelihood of sanctions for violation of policy or rules; and deterrent severity refers to the harshness of the sanctions.

However, deterrence effects may be better explained by perceived levels of sanction certainty and severity than their actual or objective level. For instance, prior studies have reported that perceived levels of sanction certainty and severity are highly correlated with the level of deterrence for behaviors such as marijuana use, shoplifting, petty theft, and speeding in automobiles. Our study therefore used perceived levels of deterrent certainty and severity. Such perceptions can result from both direct and indirect experience with punishment and punishment avoidance. That is, people develop their perception of deterrent certainty and severity by experiencing punishment or punishment avoidance subsequent to committing a deviant behavior; the perceptions can be developed by observing how others have been treated after committing a deviant behavior. Similarly, we assumed that employees would develop their perceptions on deterrent certainty and severity due to both personal and observed experience of punishment and punishment avoidance due to ISSP non-compliance actions.

Kankanhalli et al. [5] found that deterrent severity was not a strong predictor of IS security effectiveness in organizations. More recently, D’Arcy et al. [1] found that only deterrent severity reduced IS misuse by employees. On the other hand, Herath and Rao [4] found that deterrent certainty positively affected employees’ security policy compliance intention, but they reported a negative effect of deterrent severity on employees’ security policy compliance intention. In spite of these mixed findings, we followed the reasoning of GDT described and made two hypotheses:

H1. Deterrent certainty is positively associated with employees compliance with ISSP

H2. Deterrent severity is positively associated with employees compliance with ISSP

The intrinsic motivation model of human behavior holds that innate desires and preferences outweigh expected outcomes, such as rewards and sanctions. Several reasons are advanced to explain why employees will exhibit varying levels of intrinsic motivation to follow rules of their organization, including differences in personal norms and moral beliefs. D’Arcy et al. recently offered empirical evidence that employees’ moral commitment has a strong deterrent effect on IS misuse. Also, employees’ assessment of their employer may be important sources of intrinsic motivation to follow workplace rules. Drawing upon Tyler and Blader [12], we focus on two specific workplace judgments: perceived value congruence and perceived legitimacy; these are particularly important to managers because, unlike other sources of intrinsic motivation that originate from individual differences in personal norms and moral beliefs, employees’ judgments of their employer can be shaped through their interactions.

In line with the conceptualization of perceived legitimacy [11], in our study we defined it as the extent to which employees view the ISSP as appropriate, desirable, and just. Employees will view it as legitimate when the importance of following it is successfully communicated. When an organization attempts to influence the behavior of its members, it is critical to achieve legitimacy in the eyes of its members. However, its possession and use of power is not always enough to ensure that the members will act on its requests. For instance, individuals’ perceptions of the legitimacy of a law have been found to be a strong deterrent to minor offenses, such as traffic violations. Similarly, prior work has shown that an employer’s rules perceived as legitimate were likely to be followed.

Perceived value congruence was defined as an employee’s assessment of the extent to which he or she and his or her employer share the same value set. People generally tend to interact with others sharing similar beliefs because this interaction verifies and reinforces their own beliefs. Similarly, they tend to be satisfied with their job, are committed to their organization, and desire to continue their employment relationship for a long time [2]. In the employee rule-following literature, strong empirical evidence exist that employees’ perceptions of value congruence with their employer increase the likelihood that they will follow the rules instituted by the employer. We therefore formulated the following two hypotheses:

H3. Perceived legitimacy is positively associated with employees compliance with ISSP

H4. Perceived value congruence is positively associated with employees’ compliance with ISSP

Based on our review of the literature on individuals’ rulefollowing behavior, we posit that the intrinsic motivation model as well as the extrinsic motivation model can play important roles in predicting employees’ ISSP compliance. We formally tested this assertion by developing the following hypothesis:

H5. Variables drawn from both the extrinsic and intrinsic motivation models will explain significantly more employees’ ISSP compliance than would variables from either the extrinsic or intrinsic motivation model

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Scale development

Where validated scales were available in the literature, we borrowed and slightly modified them to suit our purposes. When appropriate measures were not available, we developed new measurement scales. All of the key constructs in the model were measured with multiple items, and each item was measured by using a 7-point Likert scale, from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Appendix A contains the measurement scales for the key constructs and control variables. Except for the dependent variable of ISSP compliance, a reflective measurement approach rather than a formative approach was used to measure all the constructs.

We measured perceived legitimacy and value congruence with multi-item measurement scales from Tyler and Blader’s work. We slightly modified the original scales to fit the context of our study because the original scales were developed to measure employees general rule-following behavior. The measurement scale for deterrent certainty consisted of three items that we developed for this study. We measured deterrent severity with three items that we developed by tightly operationalizing its notion.

We measured employees’ ISSP compliance with five items that were intended to tap into key areas of IS security that pertained to individual employees in organizations. Specifically, the ISSP compliance scale measured the level of employees’ compliance with ISSP with respect to (1) access and use of information assets, (2) e-mail communications, (3) use of the Internet and network resources, (4) anti-virus protection (regularly scanning and updating anti-virus software), and (5) preventing unauthorized access to computer systems. The ISSP compliance was modeled as formative rather than reflective after several criteria – such as direction of causality between construct and items, item interchangeability [8] – were taken into account. Lastly, a single-item scale was used to measure each of the control variables.

Two IS faculty members (other than the authors) who had extensive experience in survey-based research examined all the scales; their examination especially considered content validity and clarity of the instructions on the questionnaire. Based on their feedback, we concluded that all research constructs were appropriate.

## 3.2. Data collection procedure

Full-time employees who were aware of their responsibilities as specified in the ISSP of their organizations were the target population for data collection. A nationwide sample of 2000 fulltime employees was drawn from a professional data collection company in the United States that has a large number of fulltime employees as its panel members. The company sent an email invitation to the full-time employees in the sample to invite them to participate in our Web-based online survey. The e-mail told them they were only eligible to participate in the survey if their employer had established and maintained an ISSP and if they were aware of their responsibilities as defined there.

The online questionnaire began with a definition of ISSP (see Appendix A). We further ensured that only target respondents were included in the sample by asking respondents at the start of the questionnaire whether they were aware of their responsibilities (as specified in their organization’s ISSP). They were asked to complete the questionnaire only if they answered ‘‘yes.’’ Otherwise they were asked to quit the survey.

Subsequently, 602 respondents completed and submitted the survey, resulting in a response rate of 30.1%. The final dataset included 344 men (57%) and 258 women. The average age of the respondents was about 42, and 74.4% had a college degree or more education. 65% of the respondents reported that they had worked for their employer for more than five years: 0–3 months (3%); 3–6 months (3%); 6–12 months (5%); and 12–60 months (24%). Nonresponse bias was assessed by comparing the profiles of the first one-third and last one-third of the respondents. We did not find any statistically significant difference between the two groups, and therefore concluded that nonresponse bias was not a serious concern in our study.

## 4. Data analysis

A component-based PLS approach to structural equation modeling was employed to validate measurement scales and subsequently to test the research model. We used SmartPLS (release 2.0.M3) to analyze our data. We chose PLS because, unlike covariance-based approaches such as with Lisrel and AMOS, it does not suffer from the model identification issue that often occurs when a research model with formative constructs is analyzed.

## 4.1. Measurement validation

We tested the psychometric properties of measurement scales for all reflective constructs by examining their convergent and discriminant validities and their reliability. We tested the convergent validity by computing individual item loadings between an item and its posited underlying factors and the average variance extracted (AVE). As Tables 1 and 2 show, all item loadings and the AVE for all constructs were greater than the suggested minimum values of 0.7 and 0.5, demonstrating the convergent validity of the measures. Then, we checked discriminant validity: the square root of the AVE (diagonal elements) for each construct was larger than the correlations between the construct and other constructs, demonstrating the discriminant validity of the measurement scales at the construct level (see Table 1). We also found no cross-loading issues in our examination of the correlations between items and factor scores for all constructs (see Table 2): thus, the discriminant validity of the measures was demonstrated. Finally, scale reliability was demonstrated: the composite reliability values for all constructs exceeded the suggested minimum value of 0.70.

We found that only employees’ computer self-efficacy was positively associated with the employee’s compliance with ISSP. This result was not surprising, given that certain levels of computer skills are required for a person to use some of the IT capabilities (e.g., regularly scanning and updating anti-virus software).

We assessed the threat of common method variance (CMV) with two tests. First, we used Harman’s one-factor test by performing an exploratory factor analysis with measurement items for all of the reflective constructs: deterrent certainty, deterrent severity, perceived legitimacy, and perceived value congruence. The results suggested no serious threat from CMV because all four factors emerged from the analysis, and no general factor accounting for a majority of the variance was found. Second, we assessed the threat of CMV by applying a technique suggested by Liang et al. [7]. We created a method factor and incorporated this into the structural model. We found that none of the paths from the method factor to indicators were significant at $p < 0 . 0 5$ . More important, no differences were found of the statistical significances of the hypothesized paths between the two models: the original and that with the method factor. Thus, we concluded that CMV did not pose any serious threat.

## 5. Discussion

The results of our study confirmed our assertion that the intrinsic motivation model can add significantly to our understanding of employees’ compliance with ISSP. However, interestingly, our study found that the extrinsic motivation model, which has predominantly been used as a theoretical lens, explained significantly less variance of employees’ compliance with ISSP than did the intrinsic motivation model.

Although GDT has been used widely as the theoretical lens through which to understand why employees comply with ISSP, its efficacy was questioned in our study: neither of the coefficients on the paths from deterrent certainty and severity to employees compliance with ISSP was statistically significant and although the two variables drawn from GDT made a statistically significant contribution to the explained variance of employees’ compliance with ISSP, this contribution was small when the two variables rooted in the intrinsic motivation model were already in place.

At first glance, the results were somewhat surprising, given the wide use of GDT as a theoretical foundation. However, our results generally coincide with those in many studies that have attempted to demonstrate empirically the deterrent effects of variables drawn from GDT. For instance, Kankanhali et al. reported that deterrent severity did not have a strong impact on the IS security effectiveness of organizations. More recently, D’Arcy et al. reported that employees’ IS misuse was influenced only by the perceived severity of the sanctions, not by their perceived certainty. Further, the influence of the perceived severity of sanctions on IS misuse was relatively small compared to that of employees’ moral commitment, which was added merely as a control variable in our research model. It appears that the concept of moral commitment is closely linked to that of such variables as perceived legitimacy in our study, which is rooted in the intrinsic motivation of human behavior. In this regard, the findings of our study closely coincide with those of D’Arcy et al. in which employees’ moral commitment is defined as their judgment about the extent to which IS misuse behavior is morally acceptable and justifiable.

A recent work by Herath and Rao examined the impacts of variables under both extrinsic and intrinsic motivation models of ISSP compliance intention among employees. Similar to our findings, their study reported that employees’ intrinsic motivation was positively associated with ISSP compliance intention. However, their study reported that deterrent certainty was positively associated with compliance intention. Interestingly, they found that deterrent severity was negatively associated with compliance intention. The results seem to contradict our findings, because we found that variables rooted in the extrinsic motivation model were not significantly associated with ISSP compliance behavior when the effects of variables rooted in the intrinsic motivation model were controlled. However, it was not possible to compare their findings with ours because variables chosen under the intrinsic motivation model were not the same.

## 6. Theoretical and managerial implications

Our study sheds light on the role of the intrinsic model of human motivation in explaining employees’ behavior with respect to compliance with ISSP. It shows that variables rooted in the intrinsic motivation model could explain considerably more of the variance in employees’ ISSP compliance behavior than those variables rooted in the extrinsic motivation model. Hence, our findings suggest that our understanding of employees’ ISSP compliance behavior has been limited by a lack of attention to the intrinsic motivation of human behavior.

Our study also offers important implications for organizations that are concerned about managing human functions in their security operations: greater emphasis should be placed on intrinsic motivation-based approaches than on sanction-based approaches in order to increase the likelihood of employees’ compliance with ISSP. It is strongly recommended that managers forge tight connections between the objectives of ISSP and the internal values of their employees so that they will be intrinsically motivated to follow ISSP. Security training and education programs should strive to have employees recognize the legitimacy of ISSP so that they can understand how their behavior will affect them.

## 7. Limitations and concluding remarks

There were, of course, several limitations to our study. First, because of its cross-sectional design, we cannot eliminate the possibility of alternative causal arguments. That is, the strong relationships found in study could represent the effect of past behavior on employees’ currently held perceptions. Second, the selfreport method through which both perceptions and ISSP compliance behavior were obtained from a single respondent could introduce a CMV issue; our study would have been strengthened if we had obtained independent and dependent variables from different sources. Third, although we relied on prior research to identify the two most salient variables for each of the extrinsic and intrinsic models, we cannot exclude a possibility that other important variables have been omitted. Our study would have been better if we had a more complete set of variables for each of the models of human behavior. Finally, we cannot exclude a possibility that the effects of some potentially important variables were not present in our analysis: our results may have been more robust if we had controlled for the effects of other potentially important variables, such as job position, job characteristics, and professional membership. In addition, the type of industry could have been included to explain the variance of ISSP compliance to a greater extent.

The findings of this study empirically supported our assertion. We found that factors rooted in the intrinsic motivation model have even stronger effects on employees’ compliance with ISSP than those rooted in the extrinsic motivation model.

## Appendix A. Measurement items

Definition of Information Systems Security Policies (ISSP)

To maintain the integrity, confidentiality, and availability of information resources, most organizations establish a written policy statement, often called information security policies (ISSP), IT security policy, or other names. (Please note that the term ISSP will be used throughout the questionnaire for consistency.) ISSP generally describes employees’ responsibilities for protecting corporate information from potential security incidents. Examples include employees’ responsibilities with regard to use of computers, e-mail communications, and Internet/network resources.

Compliance

1. I comply with ISSP with regard to the access and use of information assets in my organization.

2. I comply with ISSP with regard to e-mail communications.

3. I comply with ISSP with regard to use of the Internet and network resources.

4. I comply with ISSP with regard to anti-virus protection (e.g., regularly scanning and updating anti-virus software).

5. I comply with ISSP with regard to the prevention of unauthorized access to computer systems (e.g., installing patches to operating systems).

## Deterrent certainty

1. My employer strictly enforces ISSP with employees.

2. I am likely to incur sanctions if I violate ISSP.

3. My employer explicitly communicates that sanctions will follow if ISSP is violated.

## Deterrent severity

(7-point scale: strongly disagree. . .strongly agree)

1. If I were caught violating ISSP, the sanctions would be very severe.

2. My employer would take strict action against violation of ISSP.

3. If I violate ISSP, the sanctions would put me in serious trouble.

## Legitimacy

(7-point scale: strongly disagree. . .strongly agree)

1. Violating ISSP is seldom justified.

2. Someone who violates ISSP hurts his/her organization.

3. Organizations can achieve high security performance when employees follow ISSP.

4. Respect for ISSP is an important value for employees to have.

5. In the long run, organizations are better off if their workers willingly follow ISSP.

6. It is wrong to violate ISSP, even if you can get away with it.

7. People should support ISSP of their organization.

## Value congruence

(7-point scale: strongly disagree. . .strongly agree)

1. I find that my values and the values where I work are very similar.

2. What my company stands for is important to me.

3. I agree with the values that define the goals of my company.

4. I am seldom asked at work to do anything that goes against my personal moral values.

Job tenure

Response categories: 1. ‘‘0–3 months’’, 2. ‘‘3–6 months’’, 3. ‘‘6–12 months’’, 4. ‘‘12–60 months’’, and 5. ‘‘60 or more months’’.

Gender

Self-reported gender, coded as 1. ‘‘male’’ or 2. ‘‘female’’.

Age

Education

Response categories: 1. ‘‘less than high school’’, 2. ‘‘High school diploma’’, 3. ‘‘Some college, no degree’’, 4. ‘‘College degree’’, 5. ‘‘Master’s degree’’, 6. ‘‘Doctorate degree’’.

Computer self-efficacy

How would you evaluate your computer knowledge skills in general? (1. no ability. . .7. very proficient).
