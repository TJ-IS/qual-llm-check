# The emergence of shared understanding: applying functional pragmatics to study the requirements development process

Marianne Corvera Charaf,\* Christoph Rosenkranz<sup>†</sup> & Roland Holten<sup>‡</sup> Economics and Business Administration, Goethe University, Frankfurt am Main, Germany, \*email: charaf@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de, <sup>†</sup>email: rosenkranz@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de, and <sup>‡</sup>email: holten@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de

Abstract. While the importance of social interactions and especially communication is generally acknowledged in information systems development and requirements development, there are few studies that investigate communication. Departing from the conceptualisation of information systems development as a language development and formalisation process, we focus on linguistic communication and the development of language-based shared understanding of requirements. We investigate and analyse the semantic alignment process by which stakeholders achieve shared understanding in requirements development. We adopt Functional Pragmatics and examine how linguistic communication is shaped and regulated during requirements development in an actual project. By developing a pattern for semantic alignment and its use as an analytical lens, we enable a systematic and insightful understanding of communication in information systems development.

Keywords: information systems development, requirements development, linguis tic communication, shared understanding, semantic alignment, social interaction

## INTRODUCTION

Information systems development (ISD) is a complex process with a high risk of failure (Kraut & Streeter, 1995; Nelson, 2007). As it confronts researchers with multiple actors and multiple influences (Chae & Poole, 2005), human and social factors are important (White, 1984), and the major problems of ISD ‘are not so much technological as sociological in nature’ (DeMarco & Lister, 1987). Research has therefore focused on ISD as a social process (Newman & Robey, 1992). However, although ISD has often been investigated from a macro-level perspective (e.g. Robey & Newman, 1996; Gallivan & Keil, 2003; Xia & Lee, 2005; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008), the details of social interactions in ISD are still not well understood (Sawyer et al., 2010), and the process as such ‘has received relatively little research attention’ (Siau et al., 2010). Not many studies go beyond ISD methodologies, and there is a general paucity of theory and studies of organisational and social processes (Kautz et al., 2007) and how they evolve from a micro-level perspective (Marakas & Elam, 1998).

Among the different socially related issues in ISD, the ‘communication gap’ between the involved stakeholders is an important factor (Kaiser & Bostrom, 1982; Siau et al., 2010). Although many different methods are suggested as having a positive impact on this gap (Byrd et al., 1992), there are few studies that examine this claim or investigate how the respective social interactions unfold (e.g. Watts Sussman & Guinan, 1999; Levina, 2005; Chakraborty et al., 2010). So even as many researchers stress the importance of effective communication in the ISD process, ‘few provide empirical evidence or concrete suggestions’ (Bostrom, 1989), and communication is still named as one of the persistent and salient problems (Kautz et al., 2007).

Our goal in this paper is to open the ‘black box’ (Siau et al., 2010) of ISD as a social process. To do so, we propose to investigate ISD as a ‘language development and formalization process’ (Lyytinen, 1985). We focus on social interactions in ISD in the form of linguistic communication as a visible means of showing how shared understanding develops. Data were gathered in an observational field study. We examine how stakeholders used linguistic communication in an ISD project to develop language-based shared understanding of requirements. We frame our analysis using Functional Pragmatics (FP) and develop a pattern for semantic alignment. The pattern serves as a lens to investigate the communication process and the development of shared understanding, and to suggest specific propositions.

In the remainder of the paper, we first outline related work and specify our research questions. Then we introduce FP as our theoretical framework and describe our research setting and method. This is followed by the development of categories and a pattern for semantic alignment. Next, we present our analysis and findings. Finally, we offer a discussion of key results, acknowledge limitations and give suggestions for further research.

## RELATED WORK AND THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Requirements development and the role of communication

ISD methodologies in practice range from sequential and iterative approaches (Black et al., 2009) to emergent and amethodical approaches (Truex et al., 2000). Agile principles have complemented these approaches during the last decade (Cao et al., 2009), and new challenges, such as ‘Internet-speed’ development (Slaughter et al., 2006) or distributed development (Ramesh et al., 2006), have emerged. Regardless of the methodology used, requirements development as ‘the elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation’ (Abran et al., 2004) of stakeholder requirements that are to be met by software-intensive information technology (IT) systems plays a central role (Jarke et al., 2010; Ramesh et al., 2010). ISD fails not because IT systems are technically deficient but because they poorly correspond to the needs and requirements of the underlying business domain (Hofmann & Lehner, 2001).

Successful knowledge transfer (Joshi et al., 2007), mutual understanding (Tan, 1994), and communication (Gallivan & Keil, 2003) are major factors that affect ISD success (Siau et al., 2010). One of the key success factors relates to bridging the so-called ‘communication gap between users and developers, who are characterised by different cultures, communities and jargons (Boland, 1978; Kaiser & Bostrom, 1982; Gallivan & Keil, 2003). It is crucial for developers to communicate with users to determine what their requirements are (Zowghi & Coulin, 2005). This becomes especially palpable considering the increasing importance of agile ISD methodologies, some of which rely ‘almost completely on oral communication with the customer’ (Ramesh et al., 2010), and focus on intensive communication and constant feedback. On the other hand, ‘over’-communication has also been an inhibitor in projects with many stakeholders (Pikkarainen et al., 2008; Vidgen & Wang, 2009). Purely informal communication may not be effective when dealing with a large number of stakeholders and vast amounts of information (Cao et al., 2009).

While the importance of communication is generally acknowledged, and while the existence of the communication gap seems to be a truism, studies investigating these phenomena are scarce; the question why communication with users ‘sometimes delivers positive benefits, but not always’ (Gallivan & Keil, 2003), remains understudied. More than 25 years after research first focused on ISD as a social process (e.g. Ives & Olson, 1984; Robey & Markus, 1984), researchers still voice ‘a need for theory and studies about social behavior and processes of communication, negotiation, and learning and their relation to the broader (historical, politica and social) context' (Kautz et al.. 2007). Conseguently, a fundamental. systematic. insightfu understanding of social interactions, and especially of communication, is still missing in ISD.

## Linguistic communication in requirements development

Communication obstacles have different causes; one fundamental cause is the lack of a common or shared language between stakeholders (Byrd et al., 1992). Building on an established tradition in IS research (e.g. Goldkuhl & Lyytinen, 1982; Flores et al., 1988; Gopal & Prasad, 2000; Clarke, 2001; Holten & Rosenkranz, 2011), we focus on linguistic communication during requirements development. Departing from linguistics, we find in semiotics that a sign is conceptualised as a combination of a concept (the signified) and a sound image or symbol (the signifier) (de Saussure, 1974). Every language consisting of signs is based on conventions related to the symbol-concept combination. These conventions emerge through practical use in a given situation (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1984; Bühler, 1990). Communication is more effective once shared conventions and language have emerged (Selten & Warglien, 2007).

Linguistic communication in ISD has been previously examined in different contexts and using different approaches, for example, linguistic narratives (Alvarez & Urla, 2002), collective hermeneutics (Hansen & Rennecker, 2010), conversation as artefacts (Cohn et al., 2009), semantic structuring of inquiry (Marakas & Elam, 1998), or conversation strategies (Urquhart, 2001). The most influential contribution has been provided by the Language-Action Perspective (LAP; Winograd, 1988; Schoop, 2001). LAP builds on Speech Act Theory (SAT; Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969); it focuses on the analysis of linguistic communication as the basis for understanding established and for designing new IT systems (Dwivedi et al., 2009). All these approaches, as well as ours, share the assumption that language use can be observed as a social act in IS-related communication.

Furthermore, we assume that a shared language needs to be collectively built (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1984) and adjusted (Schegloff et al., 1977). This building and adjusting is called semantic alignment (Brennan & Clark, 1996; Garrod & Pickering, 2009) and is fundamental for successful communication and promotion of shared understanding (Pickering & Garrod, 2004; Branigan et al., 2010). Communication is successful to the extent that interlocutors come to understand relevant aspects of the world in the same way as each other – in other words, the degree to which they align their representations of the situation under discussion (Pickering & Garrod, 2004; Brown-Schmidt & Tanenhaus, 2008). Semantic alignment then refers to the shared assignment of meaning to symbols as well as to the repairing activities in cases of perceived misunderstandings (Schegloff et al., 1977); such alignment takes place embedded in linguistic communication (in the sense of, e.g. ‘the user is a . . .’) and is caused by a communication breakdown (Winograd, 1988), or is explicitly motivated (in the sense of, e.g. ‘the word “user” stands for . . . ,’ also addressed as meta-communication (Hoppenbrouwers & Weigand, 2000) ).

In our research we ask how relationships between symbols and concepts are built and adjusted during requirements development. We analyse how language develops over time as an instrument (language use) and as a product (language-based shared understanding of requirements). Our research questions are:

RQ1: How is semantic alignment structured during requirements development?

RQ2: What factors influence semantic alignment during requirements development?

## FRAMING LINGUISTIC COMMUNICATION DURING REQUIREMENTS DEVELOPMENT

## Functional Pragmatics

Among work that examines linguistic communication as social interaction, two main streams of theories can be differentiated (Bührig & Thije, 2005): on the one hand, theories that build on SAT (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969) and, on the other hand, theories that build on the work of Kar Bühler (Bühler, 1990). Functional Pragmatics (FP) (Redder, 2008) builds on the tradition of Bühler and focuses on language use as an instrument to achieve purposes of social interaction. The underlying assumption is that socially determined process structures influence the way different purposes of social interaction are achieved (Ehlich & Rehbein, 1979). These process structures, or communication patterns, are continually reproduced, actualised and reflected in language use (Ehlich & Rehbein, 1979). The reconstruction of communication patterns as typified and standardised action sequences is a unique way to analyse the achievement of purposes from a process perspective (Titscher et al., 2000). The purpose in our research case is to achieve shared understanding of system requirements.

FP shares with SAT the conceptualisation of linguistic actions as social interaction (Bührig & Thije, 2005), the ‘idea of critical reception’ by the interlocutor, and the existence of ‘speech acts’ (Bührig, 2005). However, FP differs from SAT in the following fundamental aspects (cf. Bührig, 2005 for details): First, while SAT characterises language as an institution (Searle, 1983), FP considers language as an instrument used to fulfil institution-related purposes. Second, FP uses authentic data of utterance sequences instead of isolated speech acts, enabling a systematic consideration of the interlocutors’ roles beyond sentence boundaries. FP shares this focus on authentic communication sequences with Conversation Analysis (e.g. Sacks, 1995), and both approaches also share basic analytical assumptions, for example, ‘organization of repair’ (Schegloff et al., 1977) or ‘turn-taking’ (Sacks et al., 1974). However, while Conversation Analysis focuses on the analysis of interaction in specific situations, FP concentrates on the development of an interaction pattern as a basis for further research; it is not just a way of analysing every kind of conversation on its own, but it observes conversation hermeneutically, in the context of purposes and of a systematic configuration of actions (Rehbein & Kameyama, 2004). Finally, while SAT-based approaches, such as LAP, focus on ‘language as an act of social creation’ (Winograd, 1988) and thereby on language as a precondition for action, our research observes the act and the pitfalls of how a shared language itself is being created.

Our choice of FP as our analytical lens is strongly linked to the motivation of our research and to thorough considerations of the differences from SAT. We focus on linguistic communication processes or sequences (more than single speech acts) (1) from a micro-level perspective and (2) as purpose-driven social interactions. Especially the underpinnings of the LAP framework, as shown above, are based on SAT and do not fully fit with this objective. We chose FP because it offers an explicit, theory-supported and systematic way to analyse communication processes and the development of language-based shared understanding of requirements.

## Application to requirements development

Within the framework of FP, studying sequences of utterances reveals how stakeholders achieve purposes (Titscher et al., 2000). We observe how semantic alignment during requirements development unfolds on the level of language use in context (pragmatics). On this level, the meaning of utterances is identified by analysing the meaning symbols obtained during the interaction of actors in the specific linguistic context and the constitution of the semantic relationship of utterances (Hoppenbrouwers & Weigand, 2000). The analysis of semantic alignment is based on the development of a standardised process structure of linguistic interaction (Titscher et al., 2000; Redder, 2008). This standardised pattern is used to investigate the action–reaction process to derive propositions about factors influencing the interlocutors’ interactions.

The development of such communication patterns requires authentic communication data, collected through fieldwork and analysed hermeneutically (Titscher et al., 2000). The core of the developed pattern consists of the reconstruction of relevant categories of linguistic interaction on the observable linguistic domain. The categories are derived from the iterative analysis of the linguistic domain (Redder, 2008, cf. phase I of our research in Section ‘Data analysis’). Once these linguistic categories are derived, the further reconstruction of the whole pattern is possible by the deduction of suitable categories for the mental domain. These mental categories complement the pattern. It is essential that the cause-and-effect chain between actions in the linguistic domain (observable) and the mental domain (non-observable; deducible) can be traced and that a notion about the whole action–reaction process emerges.

## RESEARCH DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT OF THE PATTERN

## Data collection

We conducted an observational field study, which is suitable for in-depth analysis of social interaction in IS contexts (Cavaye, 1996; Silverman, 1998). We observed an ISD project that developed an internet-based application for analysis, storage and retrieval of market-specific and user-customised data. The project ran from December 2006 to September 2009 in Germany. The natural language used in the project was German. The stakeholders involved were three project managers (PM-1–3), six members of an enterprise team (ET-1–6), five developers (DE-1–5) and two members of a requirements engineering team (RE-1–2). The latter included the first author as a project participant. Project meetings took place in differen stakeholder configurations.

We included observations of nine meetings in the analysis. Five of these meetings were also audio-recorded and transcribed (cf. Appendix S1). This resulted in about 15 hours of audio recordings and 238 transcript pages with a total of about 108 000 words. Our choice of data collection goes in line with the aim of (1) ‘using naturally occurring data’ (Silverman, 1998) and (2) separating data collection from data analysis (Kleining & Witt, 2001). During data collection, the first author was involved as an observing participant (Alvesson, 2003), which is appropriate for research fields that the researcher is familiar with and has a natural access to. There were no actions purposely influencing the research besides her project-related tasks, making notes and operating the recorder.

## Categories and code items

We categorise actions that lead to triggering, changing or adding to a shared language unde four major categories: definition (D), request (Q), reassurance (R) or adjustment (A). They represent an active contribution to the development of a shared language between stakeholders. The category continuation (C) refers to a concluding acceptance or rejection of prior alignment actions. As we argue that a stakeholder’s (re-)action is led by an assumption about whether or not semantic alignment is necessary, we included the category of alignment (not) required. It represents a placeholder category that cannot be observed, but only inferred from the context (only mental domain). Table 1 explains and summarises all categories, and Figure 2 illustrates their relationships that lead to the pattern of semantic alignment.

As semantic alignment is related to the symbol-concept combination of a sign, we focus on investigating which part of the symbol-concept combination is being targeted by every single alignment action. The theoretical frame of our research leads to three subcategories: symbol, concept and symbol-concept. We add the subcategory unspecified for cases where the action does not specify which part of a symbol-concept combination is targeted (only necessary for ‘request’ and ‘adjustment’). ## Pattern for semantic alignment

Figure 2 shows the developed pattern for semantic alignment. We depart from two idealised roles of interlocutors: speaker and hearer (stakeholder A and B). The interaction begins with an action with language-defining character (D) performed by stakeholder A. Stakeholder B first analyses whether an alignment of the utterance is required. If required, stakeholder B has different options to react according to prior knowledge and understanding of symbol-concept combinations. After this action, stakeholder A again has the same options of reaction, and the thereupon following actions can be understood as reactions, leading to an iterative process. The typified roles can be applied in situations with more than two stakeholders. At every stage of the process it is possible either to achieve the required alignment and proceed with the interaction, or to abort the alignment process and proceed without alignment.

## ANALYSIS OF RESULTS

To understand how the semantic alignment process is shaped and which factors influence it (RQ2), we coded and analysed the OCs in phase II (Appendix S2). The following results present a characterisation of the process as reflected in the codes. Furthermore, we found deviations from the pattern that could be traced back to the following reasons: stakeholders (1) repeated the pattern of semantic alignment several times; (2) used unspecified alignment actions; or (3) aligned several symbol-concept combinations in the same sequence. Based on these findings we developed propositions (cf. arrows and proposition number in parentheses, e.g. ‘→ P1’). Table 3 summarises the set of propositions that stems from our analysis.

In most cases, reactions after the definition of a concept were continuation with alignment or the alignment action focused on the concept.<sup>2</sup> Many fewer reactions concerned the symbo itself<sup>3</sup> (→ P2, cf. examples in Table 7).

This uneven distribution of alignment actions in favour of concepts is not surprising, as the purpose of interaction lies in the ‘meaningful’ part of language. Respectively, we found that only a few OCs showed discussions that focused just on the adequacy of symbols to address a concept (18 of 155 OCs).<sup>4</sup> This leads to an important question: we have a need for unambiguity and completeness for a to-be-developed IT system in ISD; what is the necessary degree of effort to be devoted to the discussion of these symbols?

## Analysis of unspecified alignment actions

We observed that the number of unspecified actions (U) in both cases of request and adiustment is high compared with the other three subcategories (C. S and SC). Nearly 40% ot the codes for request and 20% of the codes for adjustment refer to unspecified actions. This indicates that the use of unspecified actions is a relevant issue in requirements development (→ P5). In the case of requests, the unspecified actions refer to utterances where it is not clear whether concept, symbol or something else is the subject of the request.<sup>6</sup> These unspecified actions can only be solved by observing the context, that is, the previously given utterances. And even then, the situation is ambiguous and it is an issue of interpretation to know which of the previously mentioned concepts or symbols are addressed.

Table S2 in Appendix S3 gives an example. OC-9.27 depicts a situation where RE-1 defines the concept (D-C) of one of the previously given symbols after an unspecified reques (Q-U) of DE-3. Although it is not clear whether the request of DE-3 refers to links or to method description, RE-1 interprets the request as referring to the latter. This and other OCs suggest that the interpretation of an unspecified action requires more than the attention on the actual utterance, but also an overview of the already established shared language. For adjustments, the unspecified actions are in many cases the result of a correction given by a negative definition,<sup>7</sup> that is, the action contains specifications of what the symbol-concep combination is not, for example, ‘I do not mean the robots.txt, but the other’ (OC-11.3). This adjustment is also only successful if all stakeholders know what the negative definition refers to (→ P6).

## Analysis of overlapping alignment of symbol-concept combinations

In 42 of the OCs<sup>8</sup> the alignments of two or more symbol-concept combinations were discussed simultaneously. Based on the categorisation of these OCs, we present three main reasons for the overlapping of alignment structures. The first and most relevant is the definition of interrelated concepts and symbols.<sup>9</sup> For example, OC-9.32 (Appendix S3, Table S3) refers to the alignment of case study linking (OD-9.32). The therein embedded OC-9.33 refers to the alignment of case study (OD-9.33) and is necessary for the alignment of OD-9.32. RE-1 needs alignment referring OD-9.32 (R-C). However, before DE-3 can go in detail into the alignment of OD-9.32, DE-3 needs to make sure that his or her understanding of OD-9.33 is correct (R-C).

Second, different symbol-concept combinations are defined in one utterance.<sup>10</sup> We found cases where continuation with alignment occurred after different symbol-concept combinations are defined at the same time. For example, OC-9.1 and OC-9.2 (Appendix S3, Table S4) illustrate such a situation in which it is not clear what the continuation with alignment refers to. Stakeholder RE-1 introduces two different symbol-concept combinations. The first one refers to situations where anonymous visitors are allowed to use the system (OC-9.1). The second one (OC-9.2) deals with registered visitors. The reaction of stakeholder DE-3 can mean that he agrees to OC-9.1, to OC-9.2, or both. This indicates that the stakeholders may have difficulties following an alignment structure properly if many different symbol-concept combinations are simultaneously discussed (→ P7).

Third, contrasting concepts/symbols are differentiated from each other.<sup>11</sup> OC-11.20 and OC-11.21 (Appendix S3, Table S5) show a situation in which the alignment of synonym grouping occurs over the differentiation of synonym. These examples indicate that due to overlapping symbol-concept combinations people may need to concentrate on the different structures of alignment to be able to bring all simultaneously running alignment processes to a satisfactory result. This presents a challenge during semantic alignment (→ P8).

By the development and application of the pattern for semantic alignment, we enable the analysis of socia interactions and especially communication from a micro-level perspective. Different models in prior literature have similarities with our pattern of semantic alignment, although developed in different contexts or with other purposes (e.g. Winograd, 1988; Pickering & Garrod, 2004). In contrast to these models, the novel aspects of our pattern are (1) its use as an instrumen for process analysis and (2) its sharp focus on alignment concerning symbol-concep combinations.

Within our results, we present a characterisation of the semantic alignment process and show which linguistic actions are frequently performed. We propose that alignment mostly occurs as actions of definition focusing on the alignment of concepts as the meaningful part of language (P1–P3). P4 makes a point about the importance of symbols for the maintenance of a shared understanding and the impact of the absence of such understanding on the emergence of ambiguity or repeated alignment actions. We argue that it is worthwhile to find ways in requirements development that are beneficial for establishing a long-term shared understanding. Moreover, P5 and P6 focus on the presence of unspecified alignment actions in the alignment process as well as on their negative impact on the effectiveness of that process. Therefore, stakeholders should try to avoid unspecified alignment actions early on. Finally, P7 and P8 present causes for the complexity involved in the alignment process and how this complexity leads to difficulties concerning the assignment of hearers’ reactions to the different speakers’ actions. All of these findings point to increasing difficulties in situations with many involved stakeholders. This result enables us to understand how risks related to communication and shared understanding emerge (Mathiassen et al., 2007). Consequently, our findings offer several advantages to researchers and practitioners interested in the role of socia interactions in ISD.

First, the exemplary application of the pattern for semantic alignment illustrates that it can provide a sharp lens for analysing linguistic communication and for tracing the cause-andeffect chain that precedes the development of language-based requirements. On the one hand, we observed linguistic actions of request and reassurance that were indeed appropriate and necessary for clarifying misunderstandings. On the other hand, we identified many situations in which the pattern was repeated several times due to an inappropriate formulation of a request. The application of the developed pattern as a research lens not only opens up the possibility of observing, classifying and comparing the different alignment actions, but also allows tracing and characterising the different alignment processes – their duration. particularities and subtleties – by using the pattern iteratively. This is a new and required sustainable way to understand processes of communication and interaction within ISD. For example, most ISD methodologies aim to facilitate communication. Many traditional approaches rely on means of formal communication such as specification documents. But in rapidly changing environments, it is hard to react quickly using formal communication (Herbsleb & Mockus, 2003). By contrast, agile ISD methodologies rely on intensive face-to-face communication and are often suggested as a solution to these challenges. However, these approaches can also be overwhelmed by ‘over’-communication (Vidgen & Wang, 2009). Here, the pattern for semantic alignment offers considerable value as it allows us to analyse systematically and to compare in detail the effects of different ISD methodologies on linguistic communication and shared understanding.

Second, existing work investigating ISD as a social process can be complemented by ou approach. For example, Newman & Robey (1992) analyse stakeholder interaction using the concept of ‘encounters’ or episodes that relate to a macro-level perspective of ISD processes. Newman & Robey (1992) argue that it is problematic to predict how encounters lead to a modification of the social structure. Our approach extends their model by proposing an instrument that allows us to focus on encounters from a micro-level perspective and to analyse what linguistic actions lead to modifications of the social structure.

Third, our results support the idea that there are strategies during linguistic communication which ‘are not necessarily learned from practice’ (Marakas & Elam, 1998) and need to be improved in a structured way. Our approach can be used as an instrument for practi tioners to evaluate their practices-in-use as regards the development of shared understand ing, to learn whether requirements were successfully translated into a useable and useful artefact, and to inform further design efforts. Such a deep understanding of the ISD process is deemed to have a great impact on the factors that determine its success (Curtis et al., 1988).

## CONCLUSION

In this paper, we transferred FP to the study of ISD as a linguistic communication process, and showed how it can be used in ISD studies. By applying FP and the pattern for semantic alignment we provide a useful approach for future research. Although our data was limited to one project, our results provide evidence and have the capability to develop analytical gener alisation for future application to various ISD projects. However, we also advise caution since we only deal with shared understanding and linguistic communication. Other conditions related to other factors – for example, stakeholders’ capabilities, characteristics and goals; or institu tional contexts, power or culture – may play key roles as well.

Further research could use the developed categories as an observer coding scheme and focus on the design of experiments to understand the impact of alignment strategies, the effects of different practices and ISD methodologies on resolving related risks, and the impact of other factors on linguistic communication and the emergence of coherent requirements. For example, does the importance that stakeholders give to the definition of symbols or of concepts change in relation to specific factors? Such an analysis can be complemented by interviews with or surveys among stakeholders, but does not rely on them. Despite calls for investigating the relationship between project performance and communication, there are few studies that have explored these phenomena empirically or systematically. Our approach provides a building block for investigating this type of question with a focus on linguistic communication.
