## Abstract

As concerns about personal information privacy (PIP) continue to grow, an increasing number of studies have empirically investigated the phenomenon. However, researchers are not well informed about the shift of PIP research trends with time. In particular, there is a lack of understanding of what constructs have been studied in what contexts. As a result, researchers may design their study without sufficient guidance. This problem can lead to unproductive efforts in advancing PIP research. Therefore, it is important and timely to review prior PIP research to enhance our understanding of how it has evolved. We are particularly interested in understanding the chronological changes in contexts and research constructs studied. We use a chronological stage model of PIP research we develop, a set of contextual variables identified from prior literature, and the four-party PIP model suggested by Conger et al. (2013) as theoretical foundations to conduct a chronological literature review of empirical PIP concern studies. We find several PIP research trends during the last two decades, such as the quantity of PIP research has drastically increased; the variety of contexts and research constructs being studied has increased substantially; and many constructs have been studied only once while only a few have been repeatedly studied. We also find that the focus of PIP research has shifted from general/unspecified contexts to specific ones. We discuss the contributions of the study and recommendations for future research directions. We propose a fifth party as an emergent player in the ecosystem of PIP and call for future research that investigates it.

Keywords: personal information privacy concerns, chronological literature review, stage model, context, four-party model, research construct

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The advancement of information and communication technologies (ICT) such as smartphone, mobile commerce, big data analytics, artificial intelligence (AI), and Internet of Things (IoT) enhances the quality of our everyday experiences (Yoo, 2010). However, as these technologies become sophisticated and pervasive, our personal information is collected and shared extensively with known or unknown entities, often without our knowledge (Gomez et al., 2009). In a recent survey, 85% of companies say that their customers have very little insight into how their personal information is used (Accenture, 2015). In fact, concerns about personal

##

information privacy (PIP) have significantly increased recently because of not only the emergence of a hyper-connected network and of advanced technologies but also widely publicized data breach incidents (Conger et al, 2013; Malhotra et al., 2004).<sup>2</sup> For example, the breach by Target, the U.S. retail giant, leaked millions of its customers’ personal information including debit and credit card information. The data breach of Equifax, a consumer credit reporting agency, could raise the risk of identity theft for more than 100 million consumers. In 2018, Facebook shared 87 million users’ personal data with Cambridge Analytica without user consent. Naturally, individuals are increasingly concerned about losing control over their PIP (Clay, 2013), which makes them hesitate in adopting new technologies or using online services. Therefore, it has become crucial for companies to develop a sound understanding about the nature and degree of consumers’ PIP concerns in various contexts.

Privacy concern refers to the concern that individuals have with the information privacy practices of organizations, which could compromise the individuals’ ability to control personal information (Smith, 1996). Bélanger et al. (2002) define PIP as the desire of individuals to control data about themselves. In response to the growing privacy concerns, researchers have investigated various issues pertaining to PIP concerns (Preibusch, 2013). However, researchers are not well informed about what esearch contexts and constructs need to be studied to produce significant contributions to literature. This is not only because research contexts and constructs seem to evolve with time as technologies advance but also because researchers have divergent foci and rely on many different theories to investigate the phenomena of PIP concerns. This problem can lead to unproductive efforts in advancing PIP concern research. As research on PIP concerns (hereafter, PIP research) continues to increase in quantity, it is important and timely to review prior PIP research to enhance our understanding of how the research field has evolved with time. Such understanding would help researchers to assess the current state of literature, identify knowledge gaps, and design their research to address important issues that are understudied or warrant further investigation.

We are particularly interested in examining how PIP research has evolved in terms of contexts of PIP concerns and research constructs studied. The nature and degree of PIP concerns may vary in different contexts because privacy means different things to different individuals in different contexts (Bennett, 1992; James et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2011; ; Xu et al., 2011; Conger et al., 2013). For example, although 67% of individuals are willing to share data with companies, this percentage drops to 27% if the business is sharing data with a third party (Accenture 2014). As such, one size does not fit all when it comes to studying PIP concerns. Recently, researchers have called for research that investigates contexts of PIP concerns (Nissenbaum, 2009; John et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2011). The U.S. National Science and Technology Council recognizes the influence of context as a key challenge for privacy research (U.S. National Science and Technology Council, 2016). Furthermore, there has been a criticism that PIP research lacks core constructs guided by a common theoretical model (Li, 2011), which makes it difficult for researchers to build a cumulative tradition in PIP research. As a first step to address these problems, it is necessary to understand what contexts and research constructs have been studied and how they have evolved.

Through a comprehensive chronological review of PIP research, the study aims at discovering temporal patterns and evolution of relevant PIP research constructs in different contexts that prior empirical research has studied. More specifically, we analyze contexts in terms of i) what types of individuals are concerned about, ii) what types of personal information being collected by or shared with, iii) what types of organizations, and iv) what types of technology or service contexts. In addition, we analyze PIP-related constructs including antecedents and outcomes of PIP concerns. To draw focused and meaningful insights from comparable studies following the same research approach, we limit the scope of this chronological review to empirical PIP studies that have used quantitative research methods. To conduct a systematic literature review (SLR), we develop a chronological stage model by using a grounded theory approach, adopt the four-party model suggested by Conger et al. (2013), and identify a set of context variables based on prior literature. We use them as theoretical lenses to analyze literature on PIP concerns.

In the next section, we first discuss how the current study differs from previous review studies and complements them. We then present theoretical foundations for our chronological literature review. In the following section, we discuss the research method including the grounded theory approach, literature search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the coding and analysis procedure. We then discuss the results of analysis and findings of the study. The last section concludes with a discussion of the contributions, limitations of this research, and recommendations for future PIP research directions. We also propose a fifth party as an emergent player in the ecosystem of PIP and call for future research that investigates it.

Researchers repeatedly criticized the lack of research on contexts in information privacy research (Belanger and Crossler, 2011; Smith et al., 2011). Smith et al. (2011) criticized that prior research on information privacy had not investigated adequately how various contexts influence information privacy. In particular, they identified the following four contextual forces that are important to investigate: (1) the type of information collected from individuals, (2) the

#

use of information by different sectors or entities (e.g., healthcare, marketing, and finance), (3) political context, and (4) technological applications. In addition, Belanger and Crossler (2011) criticized that prior privacy research had not examined important contextual factors such as type of individual in terms of their socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds. Naturally, they have called for research that investigates contexts of PIP concerns (Nissenbaum, 2009; John et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2011). The current study intends to reponse to the call. Informed by these studies, the current research investigates the following four dimensions of context: (1) types of individuals, (2) types of personal information being collected by or shared with, (3) types of entities handling personal information, and (4) types of technology or service contexts.

The current study is unique and different from previous review studies in that it is the first attempt to review literature from a chronological standpoint. As the critical issues pertaining to PIP concerns have involved with time, the chronological review can provide important insights for researchers and practitioners. Furthermore, the current study focuses on contexts and research constructs that PIP empirical research has studied.

## Four-party PIP model

In addition to the four dimensions of context, this study uses Conger et al.’s (2013) fourparty PIP model as a theoretical background. While we can distinguish personal informationhandling entities by their objective such as commercial company versus government agency, it is theoretically meaningful to distinguish information-handling entities in terms of their roles and interactions in personal information collection and sharing process. Conger et al. (2013) propose a four-party model of PIP that depicts the interactions among relevant parties that handle personal information.

The first party refers to an individual consumer or user. The second party is a vendor/provider who offers goods and services to the first party in exchange for money and information. The first party directly engages in a transaction to obtain benefits from the second

#

party. Pratt and Conger (2009) discuss the provision of personal information as a part of tradeoff that the first party gives up some amount of privacy to obtain benefits from the second party vendor/provider. This transaction process is called a privacy decision calculus (Dinev & Hart, 2006a; Pratt & Conger, 2009).

Second parties collect personal data not only during a business transaction but also before and after the transaction. They even pile up other transactional and nontransactional data such as click streams to build a complete user profile for advertising and other marketing purposes. To use social networking service (SNS), for example, users must create a user account and provide name, email address, and password at the minimum. However, when creating an account, users must agree that the second party and others can identify them by using additional information on their profile, such as educational background, descriptions of skills, experience, and so on. SNS providers can identify their users on the basis of this additional information along with network tracking techn ologies. Your online friends in SNS can be a second party, although you are not involved with them in economic transactions (Brandtzæg et al., 2010). The second party vendor/provider also shares the first party’s personal information with external legal data sharing partners (i.e., third parties) for governmentregulated credit reporting and marketing purposes (Lichtblau & Risen, 2006; Wright et al., 2008).

The third party refers to a legal entity (individual, organization, or technology) that legally collects personal information from the first and/or the second parties and their interactions with consenting of privacy policy and user agreement. For example, assurance service companies can be described as independent third party organizations to certify and/or validate business transactions between trading partners (second parties) and consumers (first parties) (Khazanchi & Sutton, 2001). Although third parties may give users the option to upload certain information onto their own service through the second party service, third party providers can store, access, disclose, and use users’ contacts in the ways described in such third party's

#

terms and privacy policy. For example, third party middleware providers facilitate the first party’s contacts, posted content, archived communication logs, public discussions, opinions, and other activities for compliance purposes. When users have bound the second party account (e.g., Facebook) to other second party’s product/service (e.g., Twitter), third parties may easily look up profile information using basic personal information such as email address or first and las names through its profile application programming interface (API). Although some priv sensitive SNS users manage their personal information by releasing and posting different content to different subgroups (James et al. 2015), most users still worry about their privacy invasion to third party when they want release it to second party only.

Fourth parties refer to illegal entities that violate privacy laws to collect personal information and steal/abuse one’s personal data not only from the first party directly but also from second and third parties. Its examples include crackers, illegal hackers, data thieves, and third party employees who violate company’s data sharing policy. Illegal entities that collect personal information using technologies such as spyware, pharming, phishing, ransomware, etc. are also fourth parties.

## Phases of Digital Eras

While consumers have had privacy concerns long before the advent of the Internet, their PIP concerns have evolved significantly with time in part due to the emergence of disruptive technologies including e-commerce, mobile computing, social media, location-based service (LBS), radio-frequency identification (RFID), IoT, and big data analytics. PIP concern is one of the major challenges that organizations face in the current digital ecosystem environment as emerging technologies present new threats, risks, and solutions to PIP (Westin, 2003; Smith et al., 2011; Conger et al., 2013). We argue that the development of digital technologies contributes to shaping and changing the notion of and the nature of PIP concerns. In this

#

section, we discuss five phases of digital eras. These chronological stages will be used later as the theoretical lenses for our literature review.

There is no well-established stage model for the advancement of digital technologies in the academic literature. However, Owyang (2016) provides useful insights. It distinguishes four phases of digital eras: Internet era, social media age, collaborative economy age, and emerging autonomous world age.<sup>3</sup> Before the first phase, it is important to recognize the mainframe age before the mid-80s. According to Owyang (2016), since the late 1950s, we have used mainframe computers designed to handle very high volume input and output and emphasized centralized computing power. Around the mid-90s, the Internet era arrived, in which a convergence of the ICT took place. Since then, communication and commerce through the Internet became a central focus for business, consumers, government, and the media. Then, the social media age came in the early 2000s, which empowered anyone to publish almost anything with online social tools. Presently, we are in the collaborative/sharing economy age, as we use ICT to create and share products/services using peer-to-peer commerce. Although it is still emerging, the next digital evolution is going toward autonomous world age as intelligent machines (e.g., drones, robotics, autonomous cars, etc.) operate without human interventions to deliver greater convenience and efficiencies. ## 3. RESEARCH METHODS

To understand how research on PIP concerns has chronologically evolved with regard to contexts and research constructs, we first take a grounded theory approach to develop and

#

validate our chronological stage model. Then, we use an SLR approach for our literature review.

## Grounded theory approach

By using the grounded theory approach, we define distinct stages of PIP research based on notable disruptive technological development. The grounded theory approach is typically used to build testable theories based on qualitative data (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Neuendorf 2002; Parks et al., 2007, Espinosa et al., 2006). Although we do not intend to build testable theories in this study, the grounded theory approach is useful for defining distinct stages of PIP research based on experts’ insights and constant comparative data analysis until reaching for theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss 1967).

We conducted semistructured interviews with informants from both industry and academia to gain their opinions and insights about important information technologies that shaped and changed the concept and nature of information privacy and users’ concerns about it. We also asked them to suggest specific years that marked the beginning of new stages in information privacy. Each interview had duration of approximately 20 minutes and was conducted face-to-face and through phone. The interviews were recorded with permission from the participants. We recruited seven participants in total through our scholarly and professional networks. The seven participants had an average of 19.7 years of work and/or research experience in the area of information privacy. All of them had graduate degrees, and two of them were female. They were located in Singapore, South Korea, and the USA.

We interviewed five subject matter experts in the first round to identify important technologies for information privacy: two from academia and three from industry. We analyzed the interview data throughout data collection because data collection and analysis should interact with each other when using the grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss 1967). We analyzed the data after each interview and made adjustments to the questionnaire before the

#

next interview. We used the open coding method to identify emerging categories, followed by the axial coding method to put related categories into a theme (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Two of the authors coded the interviews independently. As the responses to the inquiry were straightforward, there were few differences in coding. After we interviewed the fifth informant, we concluded that our interview data reached for saturation as the last couple of interviews did not contribute to new insights into the inquiry. The informants commonly refer to important information technologies for shaping and changing the concept and nature of information privacy, including personal computer, Internet, mobile network technologies such as Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, e-commerce, social media, smartphone, cloud computing, IoT, Fintech, and artificial intelligence (AI).

We identified five distinct stages of PIP research by combining the result of the first round interviews with the insights gained from Owyang (2016)’s stage model of digital eras. The distinction between the last two stages was deemed not as clear-cut as other stages because the last stage is still evolving. In the second round, we validated the result with two subject matter experts – one from academia and the other from industry – who did not participate in the first round interviews. Both confirmed that the proposed stages made sense and were useful for this research.

We named the identified five distinct stages of PIP research as Pre-stage, Introduction, Awareness, Development, and Extension. The first stage took place before the emergence of online technologies such as the Internet and e-commerce, whereas the next four stages took place as other disruptive online technologies developed subsequently. Table 2 in section 4 shows these five stages along with timeframe, notable ICT advancement, and important data breach incidents. We use this stage model for an SLR to analyze temporal patterns of prior empirical research of PIP concerns.

In addition, we validated the four dimensions of contexts and the four-party PIP model. Because these frameworks are well grounded in prior literature, we focused on industry experts validation. Three industry experts participated in a phone interview. They confirmed that the four dimensions represent important privacy contexts and the four-party model depicts important entities handling personal information and their dynamic relationships.

## Systematic literature review

In selecting and coding relevant articles to properly answer our research questions, we followed a widely accepted method for literature review called systematic literature review, which is considered replicable, transparent, objective, unbiased, and rigorous (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015). According to SLR guidelines developed by Petticrew and Roberts (2006), literature reviews should be conducted by following the next five steps: (1) clarify SLR protocols specifying research questions, sources searched, search terms, search strategy, and inclusion/exclusion criteria, (2) search for literature using search strategy and criteria, (3) select for this study because it has a specific, well-defined research objective (i.e., to discover temporal patterns and evolution of relevant PIP research constructs in different contexts that prior empirical research has studied). In this study, we follow the SLR guidelines suggested by Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic (2015).

## Identification of relevant work

In selecting relevant studies, the first step is to identify the pool of journals in which PIP research is published. We included not only IS journals but also marketing journals because PIP concerns have also been an important topic in the marketing field. We referred to the Association for Information Systems (AIS) website to identify high-quality IS journals (Li, 2011).

As a result, we obtained more than 100 information systems (IS) journals. For marketing journals, based on Hult et al. (1997), we identified 29 high-quality journals.

To extract relevant work for the study, we searched articles published between 1980 and June 2017 from the Web of Science online database using keywords such as privacy and(or) privacy concern(s). This search yielded more than 490 IS articles and 120 marketing articles. We conducted an additional search for the journals that were not included in the Web of Science database through other online journal service portals and individual journal websites. As a result, additional 65 articles were found, which adds up to more than 675 articles in total. We examined each article to determine whether it meets the following selection criteria: empirical work investigating PIP concerns with measurement items reported. Explicitly reported measurement items were useful for coding contexts in a consistent and reliable manner. As a result, 124 quantitative empirical studies relevant to the objective of this research were retained.

In addition, we included empirical studies published in conference proceedings and unpublished dissertations because these articles may represent more recent studies and the inclusion of such articles can ensure a more comprehensive review of literature (King & He, 2005; Sharma & Yetton, 2007). To identify such studies, we searched conference proceedings databases and the ProQuest database of dissertations between 1980 and June 2017. Conferences include International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS), European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS), Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems (PACIS), and the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS). To avoid double counting of the same study, we examined each conference paper and unpublished work to see whether it meets the aforementioned selection criteria and whether it had been published later in a journal or a conference proceeding. As a result, the entire literature search yielded 205 publications including 124 journal articles, 66 conference papers, and 15 unpublished dissertations and theses; Appendix 4 lists these articles.

## Coding procedure

All three authors first read randomly selected 10 papers and coded them together to set the rule for the coding procedure. Then, two coders (one coder is one of the authors and another is a research assistant who is a Ph.D. candidate majoring in IS) were trained about the coding procedure. They independently coded the articles and compared their coding results to see whether there was any inconsistency. They coded each paper by using the chronological stage model, four dimensions of contexts, and the four-party model. When a disagreement emerged, they resolved it by discussion. To check the inter-coder reliability, we calculated Cohen’s kappa. The Cohen’s kappa of the initial coding was 0.790, which was above 0.6, the recommended level of reliability (Cohen, 1960; Landis & Koch 1977). We also calculated Scott’s satisfactory level of coders’ agreement (Scott, 1955).

As for the type of individuals holding personal information (i.e., the first party in the fourparty model), our analysis results in online consumer, Internet user, job applicant or employee, LBS user, health website user, blogger or SNS user, mobile service user, and others. As for the type of personal information being collected and shared, our analysis identified general or unspecified personal information, health or medical information, and location information.

We coded type of entities handling personal information using two subcategories. First, we coded them into the parties as defined in the four-party model. To determine such parties, we analyzed the context of the empirical study and the measures of PIP concerns. Because each party is not necessarily mutually exclusive, most studies were coded into more than one party. For instance, some of the studies using Smith et al.’s Concern for Information Privacy (CFIP) measurement instrument (1996) with all four dimensions and their items were coded as second, third, and fourth party, as each of the four dimensions of CFIP represents a different party, respectively. That is, collection and errors represent PIP concerns against second party, whereas the secondary usage dimension represents third party and improper access is related to fourth party. On the other hand, Internet Users’ Information Privacy Concerns (IUIPC), another important measure of information privacy concerns proposed by Malhotra et al. (2004), has three dimensions: control, collection, and awareness. Collection and awareness are related to the second party and control implies unspecified parties, usually third parties. Therefore, the studies that used the IUIPC measure were coded as both second party and third party. We coded other studies using different measures of PIP concerns into proper category. Second, we of the entity’s objective: commercial organizations, government, employer, and other users. These are also determined by measurement items of PIP concerns.

Finally, we categorized technology or service context into e-commerce transactions, general Internet usage, online or mobile services, government surveillance, and workplace. Unlike entities handling personal information (parties and types of entities), which have been classified mainly on the basis of measurement items or operational definitions of PIP concerns, technology/service contexts were determined by the context of the empirical study under consideration.

As for coding research constructs and theoretical frameworks, we identified the antecedents and outcomes of PIP concerns along with the background theories that each paper tested. Regarding research constructs, through an iterative process of grouping and regrouping based on their operational definitions and measurement items, we categorized them into several groups including individual factors, privacy-related factors, social relational factors, macroenvironmental factors, organizational and task factors, behavioral intentions, and other outcomes. By using the coding procedure described above, we organized prior empirical studies

#

to identify chronological patterns of context, research constructs, and theoretical frameworks that have been studied.

## Stage 0: Prestage of online PIP research

Before the introduction of the Internet and the World Wide Web (WWW), there was an embryo stage of online PIP research. In this stage, the first digital computer (Atanasoff-Berry computer), ARPANET, and personal computers were developed. In addition, CompuServe, bulletin board systems (BBS), and USENET were also introduced. Because businesses have collected customer information offline for thousands of years, privacy concerns are not a new phenomenon. However, the revolution of digital computers and network technology was in progress and held the potential to increase the human and societal capacity to collect, store, retrieve, manipulate, and transmit personal information. Therefore, since the invention of the modern computers, issues on the protection of PIP in the digital age had been repeatedly raised (Vidmar & Flaherty, 1985). There had been increasing awareness and concerns for the personal privacy that certain computer technologies may infringe (e.g., Miller, 1969; Vidmar & Flaherty, 1985).

## Stage 1: Introduction of online PIP research

After the development of the Internet, WWW, and e-commerce in the late 1980s and early 1990s, concerns about online PIP became a more pressing issue (e.g., Turban et al., 2009). The deployment of e-commerce websites particularly threatened PIP because it required the collection of customers’ personal information through user account creation, registration, order process, review survey, and additional services. Using tracking tools such as cookies, web beacons, and ad tags, many e-commerce and third par mpanies collected additional information about customers’ online behaviors including click stream activities to identify their preferences and interests. Derived from the term surveillance, a new term dataveillance was coined by Clarke (1991) to refer to the convergence of new information technologies in the monitoring of the actions or communications of individuals. Dataveillance has created a novel form of gathering enormous amounts of personal information.

E-commerce companies use the personal information collected by dataveillance not only to tailor their products and services to specific customer needs but also to boost their revenues by selling advertising space on their websites (Liu et al., 2004). Although the customized services and targeted advertising may benefit certain customers, many customers feel that their information privacy is not well protected. They feel that they should have the right to control their personal information and to know what information is disclosed to others. Moreover, PIP is compromised by information disclosure to third parties and secondary uses of personal information without consent. In this stage, online consumers’ concerns about PIP started to be viewed as a potential obstacle for the success of e-commerce.

In this stage, online consumers’ concerns about PIP triggered research that investigates the general and universal concerns of online PIP (e.g., Smith et al., 1996). The CFIP measure was developed by Smith et al., (1996), which is the most frequently used measure to date. CFIP identifies four dimensions of PIP concerns including collection, error, secondary use, and unauthorized access. Important privacy concepts such as privacy paradox (e.g., Bennett, 1995; Acquisti, 2004; Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005), privacy calculus (e.g., Varian, 1996; Ackerman, 2004), and privacy and cultural values (e.g., Milberg et al., 2000; Bellman et al., 2004) were introduced and investigated. PIP research in this stage started becoming a mainstream research area within the IS field.

Stage 2: Awareness of online PIP research

Massive changes in the ways that people interact with the Internet applications emerged in the early 2000s and became pervasive in the late 2000s, although some changes are still in progress. These changes are referred to as the second wave of web technology development, namely, Web 2.0, which includes user-generated contents, social networking services, Wiki, social commerce, crowd sourcing, semantic tagging, folksonomy, blogging, and data mash-ups. Although the precise meaning of Web 2.0 is not easy to pin down, it is generally defined as “an umbrella term that describes a set of ongoing development of Web generations which have layered conceptual ideas and newer applications/services that current technologies push and market demands pull” (Kim et al., 2009, p. 659).

Some of the key trends of these changes include user participation, collaboration, social networking, rich user experience, semantics, interactivity, and responsiveness (Kim et al., 2009). Online SNS sites are among the most popular and impactful Web 2.0 applications, where massive numbers of people voluntarily publish on the Internet their personal information (e.g., hobbies, tastes in music, books, and movies, and even personally identifiable information (PII) such as full name, date of birth, schools from which they graduated, home address, relationship information of their family, friends, and friends’ friends, and their personal photos and documents for sharing). Although the massive amount of self-disclosure and highly identifiable personal profiles offer great opportunities for companies to create value through customer segmentation, data mining, and microsegmented targeted advertising, the information can also be abused by online crooks, stalkers, and bullies, and even by one’s own friends or friends’ friends on SNS sites (Hogben, 2007; Krasnova et al., 2010). The new wave of web technology development and the increase in online users’ active participations and collaborations in the networked society divest Web users of much of their control over the flow of externalized information and engender strong concerns about online PIP.

PIP research in this stage investigated online PIP concerns mainly in e-commerce and e-service contexts (e.g., Dinev & Hart, 2006a, 2006b; Hui et al., 2007; Dinev et al., 2008; Kim, 2008). IUIPC measurement (Malhotra et al., 2004), another widely used instrument, was introduced.

Stage 3a: Development of online PIP research

In the late 2000s, ICTs were powering the concept of sharing economy such as cloud computing, Uber, and Airbnb. Cloud computing services, for example, introduce the possibility of new privacy issues because the reach of information can extend beyond the physical premises of the business organization and include information created and stored entirely on the Web. Consumer files and other sensitive data that were created and stored in-house currently reside in the cloud in the form of virtual storage and shared services.

Another recent game-changing paradigm is big data, which is different from any other data that businesses have previously dealt with, in terms of volume, variety, velocity, and veracity (Sathi, 2013). Volume refers to the increasing enormous size of data. Variety in formats

#

and sources is another salient characteristic of big data. Examples of variety include structured and unstructured text from websites, images from healthcare laboratories, videos and audios from social media, RFID tags from tollbooths, and cell phone location data from LBS applications. Velocity represents the rapid movement of data in the pipeline being embedded in business processes. Finally, veracity refers to the credibility and the suitability of the data source for the target customers (Sathi, 2013). Because most big data come from outside sources beyond user control, it is significantly vulnerable in terms of data accuracy and privacy issues.

Although big data is considered a powerful paradigm because it significantly expands data horizons, it also causes serious issues for information privacy and is seen as a troubling manifestation of Big Brother (Boyd & Crawford, 2012). Polonetsky and Tene (2012) address the privacy issue of big data clearly, as follows:

“The extraordinary benefits of big data are tempered by concerns over privacy and data protection. Privacy advocates are concerned that the advances of the data ecosystem will upend the power relationships between government, business, and individuals, and lead to racial or other profiling, discrimination, over-criminalization, and other restricted freedoms. Finding the right balance between privacy risks and big data rewards may very well be the biggest public policy challenge of our time.” (p. 25)

In this stage, research has investigated online PIP concerns mainly in the social media context (e.g., Chai et al., 2011; Stutzman et al., 2011; Mohamed & Ahmad, 2012; Jiang et al., 2013) and the mobile technology environment (e.g., Lowry et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2012; Okazaki et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2012). New PIP concerns loomed with emerging computing paradigms and technologies such as cloud computing and big data (e.g., Conger et al., 2013; Polonetsky & Tene, 2013; Sathi 2013, etc.).

Stage 3b: Extension of online PIP research

#

Emerging technologies such as autonomous vehicles, drones, AI, robots, and wearable smart devices can continually shift the critical conceptions and issues of privacy concerns into an emerging autonomous era that stimulates new perspectives and approaches in research (Conger et al., 2013). We expect these developments to set off a new wave of online research. For example, risk exists with emerging technologies such as IoT and FinTech be monitored by distant servers or people through the Internet.

PIP concerns are one of the major challenges that organizations face in the current digital ecosystem environment as emerging technologies present new threats, risks, and solutions to PIP (Westin, 2003; Smith et al., 2011; Conger et al., 2013). The concept and scope of PIP concerns have been modified and expanded as new technologies are introduced and matured (Conger et al., 2013). For example, new privacy threats emerge as mobile technology and cloud computing become pervasive and organizations start transferring applications and data from their own premises to externally hosted environments. We are beginning to see PIP empirical research on emerging autonomous technologies such as wearable devices, smart home devices, connected cars, drones, robots, IoT, and AI (e.g., Derikx et al., 2015). However, it is still relatively rare mainly because of the early stage of development/deployment of autonomous technologies.

## Overall chronological trends

Before we analyze chronological patterns of contexts and constructs studied, we analyzed the overall temporal trends across stages in terms of the volume of PIP research. When mapping the identified studies into the chronological stages of PIP research, we find that 0, 4 (0.57/year), 30 (4.29/year), 85 (14.17/year), and 86 (24.57/year) papers were published in Stages 0, 1, 2, 3a, and 3b, respectively. We conducted the Kruskal–Wallis test to compare the

#

number of publications per year across stages. We find that there are significant differences across stages (p=0.01). We then performed the Mann–Whitney U test on pairs of stages. With regard to the number of publications per year, we find that Stage 2 is significantly greater than Stage 1 (p=0.03) and that Stage 3a (p=0.008) and Stage 3b (p=0.006) are significantly than Stage 2. However, the difference between Stage 3a and Stage 3b is found to be only marginally significant (p=0.067).

As shown in Figure 2, the number of studies continuously increases in most categories of publication outlets. The number of studies starts to leap in Stage 2 both in top eight senior IS scholar’s basket of journals and in other IS journals (Li et al., 2014). This trend continues in Stage 3a as the total number of the papers more than doubled that of the previous stage in all publication outlets. This result suggests that empirical research on PIP concerns has been expanding significantly in quantity and became a mainstream area of IS research. Interestingly, however, there is a drastic decrease in the number of studies published in top IS journals in Stage 3b. By contrast, a large number of studies were published in other IS journals (37 papers, 10.57/year) and major conferences (33 papers, 9.43/year) in Stage 3b, thus suggesting that the amount of PIP research will continue to increase in the foreseeable future.

## Type of personal information

We find that a majority of prior empirical studies tend to not define a specific type of personal information and leave it unspecified or treat it as “general” personal information. General or unspecified personal information accounts for 134 cases (5.06/year, 65%), whereas the type of personal information was explicitly specified only in 40 cases (1.51/year, 19%). Many studies published even in Stage 2, 3a, and 3b did not clearly specify types of personal information. Nevertheless, we observe a modest yet meaningful increase in the number of

#

studies investigating specific types of personal information from Stage 3a. These studies often modify the existing measurements such as CFIP to study specific types of personal information of interest. Specific types of personal information include not only typical personal information such as name, date of birth, and social security number but also health/medical information and an increasing attention from researchers lately.

Type of entities handling personal information

Information privacy concerns are no longer limited to data exchanges between the first party and the second party (Conger et al., 2013). The concept of information privacy has extended to interorganizational data sharing among first, second, and third parties. Furthermore, the threats from illegal fourth parties seem increasingly important. As shown in Table 3 and Figure 4, we find that privacy concern toward second party (198 cases, 7.47/year) has been most frequently studied consistently across stages, followed by third party (133 cases, 5.02/year). However, the percentage of the third party increases from Stage 2 to Stage 3a by 11%, whereas that of the second party decreases by 10% during the same period. In Stage 3b, the proportion of the third party reaches 41.7%, which is a significant increase from 26.2% in Stage 2. Although only a few studies have investigated fourth parties’ illegal use of personal information, we find a small but meaningful increase in quantity across stages. ## 5. DISCUSSION

## Findings of chronological reviews

This chronological review study provides several important findings. We can observe a certain trend of PIP research. While there were not many empirical studies published in Stage 1, the quantity of empirical research on PIP concerns has significantly increased in Stages 2, 3a, and 3b. That is, PIP has gained much more attention from researchers and has become an increasingly important area of research. We find that Smith et al.’s seminal work (1996) played an important role to stimulate the beginning of empirical research on PIP concerns. Further, the word cloud analysis suggests subtle, yet meaningful shifts in research keywords as chronological stages progress, thus reflecting emerging technologies and issues. For example, consumers became an important keyword in Stage 2 and social appeared more frequently in Stage 3a.

We also find that the variety of contexts studied in all four contextual dimensions has increased with time, whereas some contexts remain dominant in each dimension throughout different stages. Regarding the type of individuals that hold PIP concerns, early research focused on general Internet users or online consumers rather than on specific types of individuals. This pattern changed particularly in Stage 3a when researchers began to investigate more diverse and specific types of individuals such as employees, LBS users, health website users, bloggers, SNS users, and mobile service users. This suggests that researchers began to consider different types of individuals as important contexts for PIP concerns.

Early PIP research tends not to clearly define specific types of personal information of interest. A number of studies published even in Stages 2 and 3a are not exceptions in this pattern. This is surprising because researchers have long recognized the importance of understanding different degrees of sensitivity of different types of personal information (Phelps et al., 2000; Malhotra et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2011). However, we are encouraged by a small but meaningful increase in Stage 3 in the number of studies focusing on specific types of personal information. Given the increasing public concerns about privacy of health, medical, and location information, we expect more studies to look into these types of data in the future.

This study shows that second parties and commercial organizations have been studied most frequently as entities handling personal information that individuals are concerned about. From the four-party PIP model perspective, a majority of prior research studied PIP concerns toward the second party, whereas the third and fourth parties have been much less studied. This pattern appears to be stable with time, as no significant changes are observed in Stages 2,

#

3a, and 3b. It is a surprising result given the rapid adoption of cloud computing, big data, business analytics, and IoT and thus the increasing importance of third and fourth parties in collecting and using personal information. For example, fourth parties’ activities such as identity theft, hacking, and data breaches have become the central information privacy issues. We interpret that this discrepancy is partly due to the time lag between technological development pace and the research and publication process. We speculate that it could take some time for the impact of emerging privacy ecosystems to be reflected in academic research and publication.

We find that most prior studies have investigated privacy concerns toward commercial organizations, whereas very few studies have looked at privacy concerns toward governments. Governments are playing an increasingly important and complex role in influencing users’ information privacy. On one hand, governments collaborate with commercial organizations to protect users’ privacy concerns. On the other hand, governments often need to set national security over users’ information privacy. For example, government’s surveillance programs and data mining activities have raised users’ privacy concerns. The Apple vs. FBI case (Berinato, 2016) showcases that these complex interactions and tensions between commercial organizations and governments have become a critical issue for information privacy. However, we have little knowledge about the relationship between governments and users’ privacy concerns. We believe that the lack of research in this area needs to be addressed in future studies.

The results show that PIP concerns have been studied in increasingly more diverse technology and service contexts. We observe a clear shift in recent years from e-commerce and the Internet to online/mobile services including SNS. This reflects the paradigm change from transaction-based e-commerce to social and mobile-based online services. However, we find that privacy research tends to fail to incorporate these new technological contexts into theory development and testing. Furthermore, it seems that there is a time lag between the advent of a

#

new technology and studies investigating the implications of the technology for privacy concerns. A great example is user privacy concerns about SNS. Unlike e-commerce transactions, peers’ access and use of information about the user can impose significant privacy risks. However, very few privacy studies have explicitly included peer-related constructs in their research model. We find that, although SNS was introduced in Stage 2, only one study investigated the SNS context in that stage. Similarly, Ozdemir, Smith, and Benamati (2017) found that only two of 42 privacy studies published in the AIS Senior Scholar Basket from 2006 to 2016 tested models that included privacy concerns or risks associated with peers’ use of personal information. They also found that even the two studies that incorporated peers as a context did not attempt to make the context the focus of the study. We speculate two possible causes for this problem. First, recontextualization of a reference theory requires a deep understanding of both the phenomenon at hand and the theory being used, which is a daunting task. Because information technology has become increasingly complex, it takes substantial time and effort to fully grasp the implications of new technology for privacy concerns. In addition, we find that there is no dominant reference theory in the privacy concerns literature. As a result, it is likely that privacy researchers apply many different reference theories to their research. This makes it difficult for the researcher to develop a deep understanding of a theory. Second, incorporating a new context often involves theorizing of new constructs and development of their measures. This process is notoriously difficult and time consuming, thereby resulting in a lengthy time lag for publications. For example, we find that a much larger number of studies (23 cases) including 14 conference papers started to examine SNS users’ privacy concerns in Stage 3a.

With regard to research constructs studied by PIP research, we find that many antecedents and outcome constructs of privacy concerns have been empirically tested only a few times. This result shows that it is still an exploratory stage of theory development because few core sets of theoretical relationships have yet emerged in the PIP concerns literature and there is a lack of well-established theoretical paradigms in studying PIP concerns. Nevertheless, we find that some outcome constructs are repeatedly tested a number of times, thus suggesting convergence in action. In terms of temporal patterns, we find that several antecedents and outcome constructs emerge in Stage 3b.

## Suggesting an extended five-party PIP model

Privacy is now much more than a simple data management issue between a consumer and the corresponding company as the first party and the second party of the online transaction. Additionally, legal or illegal data sharing between second parties and others are becoming more common. Therefore, the scope of PIP concerns should be expanded beyond the traditional boundary of second parties to consider the complex ecosystem of production, distribution, and consumption of personal information. We used Conger et al.’s (2013) four-party PIP model for this study to analyze different parties that handle consumers’ personal information. Although the model is still useful, we believe that the model needs to be extended to incorporate recent concerns related to current emerging technology phenomena.

Personal data collection is not limited to Internet-driven automated data collection such as web browsing, click-streaming, shopping, ordering, reviewing, and complaining on the Web. Beyond the Internet boundary, a variety of sources for online and even offline personal activity data (e.g., social media blogs, music download, airline tickets, city parking tickets, retailer transactions, etc.) are combined or aggregated for new business opportunities such as target marketing. For example, Facebook has recently announced its data partnership with data vendors to help advertisers target users by matching its user profiles with offline purchase habits and other attributes. In addition, there are unknown hidden parties that can legally or illegally obtain all kinds of personal information from a variety of online and offline sources and match them to obtain a holistic view of customers (Zuboff, 2015). To address these emerging phenomena, we propose an extended five-party PIP model by adding a fifth party, which refers to massive multisource data collectors/aggregators that use advanced ICT to collect data across the boundary of online and offline activities without first parties’ notice or consensus.

#

The massive multisource data collectors/aggregators are not simply data-sharing partners. They cover the data tracking space with a variety of ways of collecting user information through different technologies in different contexts (Martin 2016). They are invisible and unreachable to the first or even second parties and are increasingly capable of tracking individuals across devices and interactions, thereby associating information collected from online or even offline services in disparate contexts. A Web beacon, for instance, can seizure detailed consumer information such as clicking, typing, and browsing behavior and then relay that information to professional tracking companies and data aggregators that are hidden from the first and second party. As a specific case, a mobile software company such as Carrier IQ – with which no end customer has any direct contact – can log customer activity on mobile devices down to the keystroke for later analysis without the knowledge of the user. It is possible for them to compile and distribute extensive profiles of personal data far more easily than before. Even information that is accessible to the public, when assembled from different sources into a comprehensive profile, can create a revealing picture of a person. ## Theoretical contributions and implications for future research

This study has several theoretical contributions to PIP literature. First, using the proposed stage model of chronological progress of PIP and Conger et al.’s (2014) four-party PIP model, it is a first attempt to conduct a chronological review of the empirical research investigating PIP concerns and relevant constructs in various contexts for the last two decades. Because information privacy is dynamic and context sensitive in nature, this study takes a theoretically meaningful step to understand the chronological development and evolution of contexts and constructs of PIP research. This work provides a unique view point (i.e., time dimension) that prior literature reviews of PIP research did not explicitly consider. We hope that this study serves as a keystone bridging extant literature and future PIP research such that researchers benefit for their research.

Second, as discussed, this study identifies the gaps in current PIP concern studies. One important gap is the lack of empirical research on third and fourth parties. Because of emerging ecosystems, individuals are increasingly concerned about unauthorized uses of PII by third and fourth parties. While the third party was more studied in Stages 3a and 3b than in the previous stage, the fourth party is seldom studied even until very recently. Therefore, future research needs to focus more on understanding this understudied area and addressing how and why individuals’ privacy concerns differ toward the second, third, and fourth parties.

Third, we think that another important challenge for future research is to balance between developing underlying theories that are immune to different contexts and developing parameterized, mid-range theories that are suited for specific contexts. According to the results of the study, we find that prior research is not well balanced in that regard, thus lacking systematic efforts to develop underlying theories. As discussed earlier, research on PIP concerns is very divergent in antecedents and outcomes, as it has continued to investigate different constructs. While it is important to study emerging technologies and their implications for PIP concerns, it is the correct time to begin to converge on a core set of constructs derived from a few strong underlying theories. Without such efforts, PIP research will keep revolving around new stuff and fail to build powerful theories. Therefore, future studies need to start converging on central theories and variables while incorporating important new contexts. By doing so, a cumulative tradition in this area can be established, and a reliable, robust theory can be developed.

Fourth, the trends of increased automation of data collection and the popularity of emerging technologies such as wearables, AI, drone, connected car, and IoT usher us in a new wave of PIP concern incursions and invasive marketing. Presently, we are witnessing an increase in unknown and hidden parties that legally or illegally collect personal information without consent from a variety of online and offline sources for their business opportunities. There is a need to extend the four-party PIP model to reflect current phenomena of emerging technologies. Hence, given these looming threats to PIP, we argue that future research should investigate the PIP concerns toward the unknown or hidden fifth parties based on the proposed five-party model. The proposed five-party model along with the stage model can be used for structuring future literature reviews as well as guiding future PIP concern research.

Fifth, we recommend that researchers pay more attention to the understudied, yet important contexts related to health/medical information and government. These domains are particularly sensitive to privacy issues and closely intertwine with any organizations. As such, these domains would warrant more in-depth investigations and perhaps mid-range theories that reflect their unique characteristics. In addition, future research should expand the scope of privacy context to include legal, policy, social, temporal, spatial, emotional, and process-related contexts because privacy concerns are also significantly influenced by these factors.

Sixth, future research should investigate privacy concerns in the context of emerging technologies including online platforms, the IoT, and big data (Lowry, Dinev, & Willison, 2017). These technologies may require recontextualization of some of the existing theories because they might be qualitatively different from other existing technologies. For example, with the IoT, private information may be shared through ordinary everyday things such as cars, light bulbs, thermostats, and speakers. A new theoretical perspective may be required to address some of the critical privacy issues relating to this ubiquity of privacy threats.

Last but not least, we call for a quantitative meta-analysis of the empirical findings of the key theoretical relationships between PIP concerns and other related constructs. Recognizing the lack of cumulative knowledge on PIP concerns, a few studies have attempted to integrate and synthesize the empirical findings on the relationships between PIP concerns and related constructs (Li, 2011; Smith et al., 2011). While they provide valuable insights and develop

#

integrative research models, they mainly rely on qualitative analysis methods. As a result, one important gap is that we have little knowledge about how empirical findings of PIP concern research add up statistically. Naturally, researchers have recently called for a quantitative metaanalytic review to resolve inconsistent, mixed empirical findings (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Li, increase, it is the correct time to respond to this call and conduct a meta-analysis to find out what cumulative effects are statistically significant and what are still inconclusive.
