# Network ties and the success of open source software development

Gang Peng <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Yun Wan <sup>b,1</sup>, Peter Woodlock <sup>a,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Williamson College of Business Administration, Youngstown State University, Youngstown, OH 44555, United States <sup>b</sup> Department of Computer Science, University of Houston Victoria, 14000 University Blvd., Sugar Land, TX 77479, United States

Keywords: Network ties Knowledge flow Learning Open source software (OSS) Project co-membership Social networks Project success

## a b s t r a c t

Prior network-based research on open source software (OSS) development has focused on the benefit of network ties and assumed all network ties play the same role. We adopt a fine-grained view of network relations to investigate the impact of network ties on the success of OSS development. Through examining the development of OSS projects hosted by SourceForge, we find that co-membership among project teams is an effective mechanism for building network ties, through which knowledge and expertise flows across projects in OSS community and, therefore, contributes to the success of OSS development. However, network ties among projects not only confer benefit, but also incur various cost, and due to the different growth patterns of cost and benefit, network ties have a diminishing return to project success. In addition, we find network ties of leader–follower type and follower– leader type are more beneficial to OSS success than other types of ties, and network ties connecting to projects of later development stages are more beneficial than those connecting to projects of earlier stages. Our study provides useful guidelines and suggestions as to how to leverage the knowledge and expertise of others for successful development of OSS projects.

## 1. Introduction

Despite its deviation from the principles and practices advocated by traditional software development, open source software (OSS) development has gained increasing recognition (Raymond, 1999; Scacchi et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2011a). Over time, a significant amount of research has been conducted to investigate the success of OSS development from various perspectives (e.g., Colazo and Fang, 2010; Paulson et al., 2004; Raymond, 1999; Scacchi, 2004). In particular, realizing that social behavior and performance are not isolated but relational (Burt, 1992), researchers have adopted the social network approach to examine the success of OSS projects (Colazo, 2010; Grewal et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2011a).

For software development, knowledge and expertise are inherently rooted in the minds of individual members (Robert et al., 2008; Singh and Tan, 2011). Network ties can facilitate knowledge flow and exchange across OSS projects, and eventually influence the success of OSS projects. However, a closer examination of this literature reveals a theoretical gap: most studies focus on the benefit of network ties with few considering the cost of network ties; more importantly, although network ties have been found to affect OSS development in general, it is not clear which types of ties are more beneficial. We intend to bridge the above research gap in this research. By doing so, our study not only contributes to the network-based research on OSS success, but also bears practical implications as to how to leverage knowledge and experience of the others for successful development of OSS projects.

Specifically, we adopt a fine-grained view of network relations to examine the following three issues. First, we propose that project co-members establish network ties among OSS projects and co-membership is an effective mechanism for knowledge flow across OSS projects. Second, we argue that network ties confer benefits as well as incur obligations and costs, and this is particularly relevant for OSS development since network ties are built through project co-memberships. Due to different growth patterns of cost and benefit, OSS project success is not a linear but a concave function of the number of network ties, i.e., network ties have a diminishing return to the success of OSS projects. Third, realizing network ties differ in their capacity to channel and assimilate information and knowledge, we categorize network ties based on the roles played by the co-members as well as the development stages of connected projects, and further investigate which types of ties are more beneficial to OSS development.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we introduce the background of this study and explicate the importance of co-memberships for knowledge flow across OSS projects. This is followed by Section 3 on theory and hypotheses development. Section 4 describes our dataset, variables, and methodology for hypotheses testing, and estimation results are presented in Section 5. In Section 6, we discuss contributions and limitations of the study, and offer directions for future research. The paper is concluded in Section 7.

## 2. Research background

Previous literature has examined success of OSS projects from various perspectives. Early observations of OSS development reveal that OSS workspace is like a bazaar that follows two broad principles: ‘‘given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow,’’ and ‘‘release often and release early’’ (Raymond, 1999). Crowston and Scozzi (2002) suggest that, to achieve OSS success, project members must develop necessary competencies, identify and understand opportunities, and marshal these competencies to achieve the collective goals of a project. In addition, researchers have proposed that motivation (Lakhani and Wolf, 2005; Raymond, 1999), leadership (Fleming and Waguespack, 2007), license type (Colazo and Fang, 2009; Stewart et al., 2006), code architecture (Jensen and Scacchi, 2005; Peng et al., 2011), communication (Singh and Tan, 2011), coordination (Bonaccorsi and Rossi, 2003), and temporal dispersion (Colazo and Fang, 2010) all play important roles for OSS success.

Among the various perspectives to OSS research, the one that has received increasing attention is that of social network theory, which argues that the contexts and relationships in which actors are embedded can potentially influence the information and knowledge they receive, as well as the resources and opportunities they access, which in turn can lead to im proved outcome and performance (Burt, 1992).

In OSS community, each OSS project consists of multiple developers or project members, and each developer can participate in multiple projects (Xu et al., 2006). In this study, a developer who belongs to two or more projects is called a ‘‘comember’’ between these projects. Correspondingly, OSS projects that share one or more co-members are called neighboring projects. When a co-member works on a neighboring project, he can exchange ideas and discuss issues with other members through such tools as discussion forums, email lists, and tracker systems (Xu et al., 2006), and subsequently he learns from others and gains expertise and knowledge from participating in that project. Indeed, learning has been identified as one of the most significant motivations for OSS participation (e.g., Fang and Neufeld, 2009; Lakhani and Wolf, 2005). Furthermore, when the co-member works on the focal project, other members in the project learn from him as well. Therefore, through learning, knowledge and expertise can flow from the neighboring project to the focal project. Effectively, co-memberships constitute the nexus between OSS projects and act as conduits for knowledge flow across boundaries of OSS projects. Through co-members, useful information and knowledge, including innovative ideas and techniques for OSS development, can be channeled across the connected projects, influencing the success of OSS projects (e.g., Grewal et al., 2006; Zaheer and Soda, 2009).

In the area of information systems, network approach has been adopted to examine a variety of issues related to OSS development, such as incentives of network formation (Singh and Tan, 2011) and future cooperation for OSS developers (Hahn et al., 2008). In particular, a few studies have investigated how network ties affect OSS success. For example, Grewal et al. (2006) argue that in general network ties contribute positively to OSS project success. In contrast, Colazo (2010) finds while network ties may improve OSS proiect quality, they may slow down the speed of project development, In addition to the number of network ties, the density of network ties is also of interest to researchers. It has been found that dense connections between developers within an OSS project can potentially speed up project development, but this is achieved at the expense of project quality (Colazo, 2010). On the other hand, Singh et al. (2011a) argue that a moderate level of connections between developers as well as among projects would be most beneficial for OSS success.

However, two issues need to be further explored in this literature: First, most studies adopting network approach for OSS success focus on the benefit of network ties, with few considering the cost of network ties. Literature suggests that building and maintaining relationship with others takes time and effort and consumes resources (Adler and Kwon, 2002), and increased number of ties may unduly create information overload, increase coordination and cognitive cost, and thus hinder performance and outcome (Colazo, 2010; O’Leary et al., 2011). Second, this line of research typically assumes that all network ties play the same role in influencing the performance of OSS projects. Obviously, this is not necessarily true. While network ties have the potential to channel information and knowledge, the effectiveness of this activity depends crucially on the resources that can be accessed and channeled as well as the ability to make use of these resources (Balkundi and Harrison, 2006; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Rodan and Galunic, 2004). When network ties are used to tap into useful information and resources, increased network ties can enhance performance or outcome. However, when resources channeled through network ties play such a role that can possibly inhibit or restrict the ability for exploration, network tis may not play a positive role (Adler and Kwon, 2002). In studying firm innovation performance, for example, it has been found that performance may be negatively influenced by connections to less innovative others (Zaheer and Bell, 2005). Because of these, researchers argue that the ‘‘active ingredient’’ of network ties is the content of information that flows across the ties (Balkundi and Harrison, 2006; Rodan and Galunic, 2004), and the quality of the information held by connected others sets the upper limit for the resources that can be channeled (Zaheer and Bell, 2005). The implication is clear—when studying the impact of network ties, a fine-grained view of network relations based on the potential content of information flow across network ties is more desirable.

Our units of analyses are the OSS project teams within SourceForge.net, one of the largest OSS communities on the Internet. We regard individual project teams as social actors and co-members among project teams as network ties. Furthermore, we focus our analyses on the impact of direct ties on knowledge flow. There are several reasons for this: First, while it is possible that an indirect tie may exert influence as well, the strength of such an influence is likely to diminish rapidly as the distance increases (Jackson and Wolinsky, 1996). Second, indirect ties exert influence indirectly, which may have already been incorporated into direct ties. Third, current literature typically examines direct ties to study how knowledge flow affects performance and outcome (Grewal et al., 2006; Zaheer and Soda, 2009).

## 3. Theory and hypotheses

## 3.1. Diminishing return to network ties

Network ties can channel useful information, increase knowledge stock, enhance performance, and they are particularly important for innovative and knowledge-intensive activities such as OSS development. First, OSS development typically requires performing non-routine tasks that need to draw from a variety of expertise and knowledge, some of which may exist outside a team’s boundary (Singh and Tan, 2011). Therefore, connections with other teams holding this expertise and knowledge, as well as complementary resources, are crucial to the success of OSS development (Cataldo et al., 2008). Network ties can help OSS projects transfer or bridge domain-specific knowledge at different development stages or different sections of software systems. For example, by observing the development activities of Debian, a well-known OSS project, Sowe et al. (2006) revealed that active interaction among project members can enhance knowledge sharing and facilitate information and expertise exchange, which in turn has a great impact on the organization and coordination of the overall resources for OSS development.

Second, to achieve OSS development success, project teams need to actively search for new ideas, opportunities, and available resources (Feller et al., 2008; Ohira et al., 2005). Network ties can potentially reduce search cost since they provide easy access to potentially beneficial information, untapped opportunities, and new resources that are unknown to other teams. The importance of network ties to software development is frequently documented. When American Airlines and IBM developed the first real-time online air ticket reservation system, SABRE, many of IBM’s developers working on SABRE had participated in a similar U.S. Air Force project, SAGE, which had been used to process radar signals collected across the United States in real time. As a result, SABRE was able to not only benefit from technical innovations from SAGE, but also avoid many of the development pitfalls in terms of system requirements, programming, and project management, etc. (Scacchi, 1984). Field studies of software development teams have also found a positive link between teams’ boundary-spanning activities and the stakeholders’ rating of teams’ performance (Guinan et al., 1998).

Third, network ties also help reduce costs for decision-making by project teams (Boh et al., 2007). OSS development often requires evaluating alternative solutions to a wide range of problems from specific algorithms to overall software architecture, which consumes scarce resources, such as time, cognitive and computational efforts. Co-members may bring in effective solutions already adopted by other teams for the same problems or contexts (Zaheer and Soda, 2009). Therefore, by channeling in new knowledge and taking advantage of others’ experience and expertise, teams can save scarce cognitive efforts and resources, and improve the odds of making the right decision (Espinosa et al., 2007). For example, when being asked why they chose Mozilla Public License for releasing its software, NTXShape, a database OSS project team at Source-Forge, explained that many of its members had used various types of OSS licenses in other projects, and these previous experiences provided them opportunities to better understand the purposes, restrictiveness, and benefits of various license types, and eventually helped tremendously in making the final license choice decision for NTXShape.<sup>3</sup> of all these possible benefits, network ties have been claimed to have a positive impact on OSS success (e.g., Grewal et al., 2006).

However, network ties as conduits for knowledge flow also incur various costs (Adler and Kwon, 2002; Burt, 1992), and this is particularly relevant for OSS development. First, it takes time and effort and consumes resources to build and maintain connections with other projects. In OSS development, co-memberships indicate obligations of co-members to contribute to all projects they are involved (Colazo, 2010). According to a survey by Lakhani and Wolf (2005) on 684 OSS developers at

SourceForge, each developer spends on average about 14.1 h per week on all the projects he participates and 7.5 h on the focal project, indicating that OSS developers spend significant amount of time in developing other projects at the same time. Second, when involved with too many projects concurrently, developers frequently switch between these projects, and their time, commitment, and attention tend to be fragmented, which in turn can cause their work to be less efficient (Hallowell, 2005). Third, making use of the knowledge and experience from neighboring projects is not a trivial task, as it requires evaluating alternative solutions and coordinating activities across projects, which consumes cognitive or even physical resources. For example, one of the big advantages of OSS is the reusable architectural and functional components, which can be freely accessed through network ties and integrated into the focal project. However, in order to use these components, project teams need to clearly understand the current code bases and reusable components, effectively design interfaces between them, and ensure that the integrated software can function properly as a whole package (Boh et al., 2007; Fleming and Waguespack, 2007; Haefliger et al., 2008). Therefore, network ties through OSS project co-memberships are not just an asset, they could be a liability as well.

Taken these together, we speculate that the overall impact of network ties may not be linear. On one hand, as network ties increase, information and knowledge accessed through multiple network ties are more likely to be redundant, decreasing the value for connecting to additional project teams. In other words, the benefits of network ties tend to grow at a decreasing rate. In contrast, while the cost of network ties may be negligible initially, with an increasing number of projects to participate and contribute, project members face increasing challenge for allocating and diverting their limited resources across multiple projects, eventually meeting their bottlenecks and impairing task performance (O’Leary et al., 2011). Thus the costs of network ties tend to grow at an increasing rate. The implication is that network ties may initially contribute positively to OSS success, but as the number of network ties continues to grow, the marginal benefit of network ties starts to decrease, and eventually the positive impact of network ties reaches its plateau. So we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. The number of network ties has a diminishing return to the success of OSS projects.

## 3.2. Developers’ roles and network ties

As we argue earlier, not all network ties play the same role in affecting future performance. The primary reason is that network ties exert their influence through the ‘‘active ingredient’’, the content that flows across these ties (Balkundi and Harrison, 2006; Rodan and Galunic, 2004). In OSS development, since a co-member may play different roles on the focal project and a neighboring project, the benefits conferred to the focal project by a network tie may also depend on the roles the comember plays on the two connected projects—the roles a co-member plays will affect the content of knowledge that flows into the focal project as well as the ability of the focal project to make use of the channeled knowledge and expertise.

One of the well-recognized facts from network research is that actors in a social network are not equal in their statuses and influence, and they can be classified into leaders or followers based on their statuses and influence in the network (Fleming and Waguespack, 2007). Accordingly, network ties among OSS projects can be further categorized based on the roles played by co-members on the projects (Grewal et al., 2006). At SourceForge, projects are initiated by project leaders, who may then afford interested members, or followers, access to the code base to develop the projects together (Pavlicek, 2000; Raymond, 1999).<sup>4</sup> Thus, on an OSS project, a developer plays the role either as a leader or a follower (Giuri et al., 2008; Pavlicek, 2000; Raymond, 1999). In this study, we categorize a network tie as a leader–follower tie if the co-member is a leader on the focal project and a follower on the neighboring project. Accordingly, the ties between the focal project and the neighboring projects fall into the following four types: leader–leader ties, leader–follower ties, follower–leader ties, and follower–follower ties. Since each type of ties may differ in their effectiveness for channeling and making use of knowledge and expertise, next we investigate which types of ties are more advantageous to the success of OSS projects.

Prior literature suggests that network ties maintained by members of higher statuses, like team leaders, possess both political and informational advantage over those by other members (Fleming and Waguespack, 2007). Between two projects, a tie maintained by a co-member serving as a leader on at least one project differ from that maintained by a co-member serving as a follower on both projects in its ability to access and assimilate external information and resources (Faraj and Sambamurthy, 2006). Status confers legitimacy and translates into access to and control of relevant resources (Feller et al., 2008; Fleming and Waguespack, 2007). As a result, leaders have timelier access to greater amount of information and knowledge held by other teams within a network, and network ties maintained by team leaders can more effectively cut across team boundaries (e.g., Giuri et al., 2008; Majchrzak et al., 2004). In addition, compared to followers, leaders may have acquired unique skill sets through leading and guiding project activities. Therefore, in OSS development, a network tie maintained by a co-member acting as a project leader on one or both sides of a tie should be more effective than a tie maintained by a co-member acting as a follower on both sides. In other words, network ties of the follower–follower type would be the least beneficial to OSS success.

However, as discussed earlier, network ties not only confer benefits, but also incur obligation and cost outlay (Lakhani and Wolf, 2005). In OSS development, the obligation and cost outlay associated with network ties are particularly onerous for project leaders. Project leaders play important roles in guiding and coordinating the activities of OSS development. They are usually the individuals who write initial code bases, guide development activities, motivate team members, and make decisions on a variety of important issues such as project features, code architectures, and future development directions (Peng et al., 2011; Raymond, 1999). Thus if the project leader of the focal project also serves as a leader on the neighboring project, he has to allocate limited energy and resources to lead activities of both projects, which is very difficult to achieve. In addition, doing so would expose him to too many information-processing tasks, causing cognitive overload and poorer work performance (e.g., O’Leary et al., 2011), and eventually hindering the success of the focal project.

There is an informational disadvantage for a leader–leader tie as well. By definition, the carrier of this type of tie serves as a leader on both sides of the tie. Since he is a leader, he plays a primary role on the two connected projects, and other members play supportive or secondary roles; therefore the leader contributes most of the ideas and knowledge to the two projects, particularly when team size is small. Consequently, the information and knowledge channeled to the focal project through this type of tie tends to be not as novel and valuable since what the neighboring project knows is quite already known by the focal project. Therefore, it follows that ties of the leader–leader type would also be less beneficial to OSS success.

In comparison, leader–follower ties and follower–leader ties are different. In the former case, by participating in another project as a follower, the leader of the focal project can effectively draw in and make use of the channeled non-redundant and useful information and resources without burdening himself with the duty of leading the neighboring project. In the latter case, by learning from leaders of the neighboring project, the focal project can gain access to novel information, insights, and expertise that would otherwise be difficult to obtain. In other words, both the leader–follower ties and follower–leader ties would be able to take advantage of their political and informational advantages as well as avoid the heavy duty and cost outlay for maintaining these ties with other projects. Thus we propose:

H2. Network ties of leader–follower type and follower–leader type are more beneficial to OSS success than other types of ties.

## 3.3. Project stages and network ties

As network ties can potentially serve as channels for knowledge flow, the benefit of network ties also depends on which other OSS projects these ties are connecting—the knowledge and expertise held by neighboring projects set the upper bound of the resources the focal project can tap into (Zaheer and Bell, 2005). Here, we argue that network ties connecting to projects of later development stages are more beneficial than ties to those of earlier development stages.

First, ties to projects of later stages than the focal project itself can provide experience, knowledge, and expertise proven to be effective and beneficial, thus reducing the risk and cost of learning, and increasing the chance of success. Second, pro jects of later stages typically have accumulated a large pool of both tangible and intangible resources (Hahn et al., 2008), and consequently, ties to these projects can provide timely information and valuable experience that can be used to cope with a variety of contingencies, thus improving the chance of success for the focal project. Third, since these projects have already reached their later stages, they usually require less workloads from the co-members, whose efforts and resources can thus be allocated to working on the focal project. Fourth, the mature stages of these projects may serve as signals to others about their ability to develop successful projects, and therefore their suggestions and opinions are considered as more valuable and trustworthy. Prior literature suggests that when uncertainty arises about the quality of someone or something, their status cues are frequently used to infer their quality (Podolny, 1994), since statuses represent prestige or honor in a social network (Hahn et al., 2008). Therefore, ties to projects of later stages also help build trust between projects and increase the focal project’s willingness to learn from these neighboring projects, so as to facilitate knowledge flow from them. For example, when designing its code architecture, FreeBSD benefited greatly from 386BSD, a relatively mature and stable operating system software (Dinh-Trong and Bieman, 2005). Indeed, many of the developers on FreeBSD used to work on 386BSD, and the lessons, experience, and expertise gained from development of 386BSD afforded FreeBSD the opportunity to avoid many design pitfalls and coding problems which otherwise would be difficult to deal with (Hubbard, 2009). Therefore, we propose:

H3. Network ties connecting to projects of later development stages are more positively related to OSS success than those connecting to projects of earlier development stages.

## 4. Dataset, variables, and methodology

To empirically test our hypotheses, we make use of the OSS development data obtained from SourceForge.net. All independent variables for this study are extracted and derived from the January 2006 SourceForge data dump. For each of the focal projects, we identify all its neighboring projects connected by the co-members. Accordingly, variable network ties is measured as the total number of neighboring projects of a focal project. Network ties and its quadratic term, network ties<sup>2</sup>, are used to test H1.

As discussed earlier, we categorize network ties based on the roles played by the co-members on the connected projects, hence variable leader–leader ties, leader–follower ties, follower–leader ties, and follower–follower ties. We also distinguish the ties with regard to the development stages of the neighboring projects. At SourceForge, each active project takes on one of the following six possible stages, from lowest to highest: Planning, Pre-Alpha, Alpha, Beta, Production/Stable, and Mature. We define the total number of neighboring projects that are of later stages than the focal project as later-stage ties, those of earlier stages as earlier-stage ties, and those of the same stage as same-stage ties. The above variables are used to test H2 and H3. It is worthwhile to note that the above network variables are derived based on the whole projects at SourceForge

An illustration of the calculation of the network variables is shown in Fig. 1. The focal project P1 has three members, m1, m2 and m3 (m1 is the project leader on P1), who are co-members on six neighboring projects, among which two are of later stages than P1, denoted by L, and one is of the same stage as P1, denoted by S. In addition, m1 and m3 also serve as project leaders on P3 and P7. Thus, for P1, variable network ties = 6, leader–leader ties = 1, leader–follower ties = 1, follower–leader ties = 1, and follower–follower ties = 3. In addition, variable later-stage ties = 2, same-stage ties = 1, and earlier-stage ties = 3. A similar approach to building network ties in OSS communities was used by prior studies as well (e.g., Colazo, 2010; Grewal et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2011a).

We use several control variables for this study. First, prior literature suggests that team capability is important for OSS success (e.g., Crowston and Scozzi, 2002). Thus we control for team capability in this study. This variable is obtained by averaging the skill experience (in years) of all project members, including leaders, on the focal project. We also control for project size and project age since these two variables have been found to influence team dynamics and performance (Moreland and Levine, 1992). Project size is measured as the total number of developers on each project. Project age is measured as the time (in months) elapsed since a project registered at SourceForge.net until January 2006. The next variable we control for is temporal dispersion of a project (Colazo and Fang, 2010). This variable is measured as the total number of unique time zones that are covered by members of a proiect, In addition, we also control for other project characteristics, such as project intended audience (5 dummy variables), supported operating systems (5 dummy variables), project topics (19 dummy variables), and project license types. Consistent with prior literature (e.g., Singh et al., 2011a), we categorize project licenses into three broad types: viral, copyleft, and others. Earlier studies have shown that these variables are important for OSS project decision mak ing and the success of OSS projects (e.g., Lerner and Tirole, 2005).

The dependent variable in this study is OSS project success. In literature, OSS success has been defined as the degree to which the developed software meets the needs of a project’s users (Crowston and Scozzi, 2002). Unlike traditional firm-dri ven endeavors, OSS projects are not always driven by direct profit, and, therefore, the success of OSS projects is primarily based on the feedback from the developers themselves and the users, and is usually measured by non-profit indices. In this research, we follow the approach by earlier studies (e.g., Grewal et al., 2006; Hahn et al., 2008), and measure project success using two indices: software downloads and code released; both are counted during the period from February to October 2006 and are further taken the logarithm. These two indices correspond to the commercial success (Grewal et al., 2006) and the technical success (Hahn et al., 2008) of OSS development.

Finally, of all the projects enlisted at SourceForge, we only consider projects in the Java foundry, i.e., in Fig. 1, the focal project, P1, is from the Java foundry only, but this is not necessarily the case for P2 to P7. There are several reasons why we study Java foundry only. First, Java is one of the major foundries at SourceForge and many projects are developed using

## 5. Results

We first present the descriptive statistics and correlation matrix of the variables in Table 2. The results show that the dependent variables software downloads and code released are correlated positively and significantly with all independent variables except leader–leader ties and earlier-stage ties. This pattern is consistent with our hypotheses that these two types of ties do not play as important a role to the success of OSS projects. Although correlations between some of the variables seem to be high, these variables typically do not enter into the estimation models simultaneously.

We use ordinary least squares (OLS) regression for our analyses. We test for multicollinearity among the independent variables in all the models in the tables. This is done by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all the independent variables in all the models. The VIF-values are all below 5, and consequently, multicollinearity should not be a problem in our models (Neter et al., 1996).

Our estimation results are shown in Tables 3 and 4. The dependent variable is software downloads in Table 3 and code released in Table 4. The results from both tables show that in Model 1, the base model, team capability is positive and signif icant, confirming findings from prior research that team capability is important for OSS success (Crowston and Scozzi, 2002). In addition, temporal dispersion is positive and significant in both tables. This result is consistent with previous finding that team temporal dispersion has a positive impact on OSS development (Colazo and Fang, 2010). Project size is shown to positively affect OSS success, suggesting the importance of slack resources for team performance (Moreland and Levine, 1992).

Model 2 adds the variables network ties and network ties<sup>2</sup>. ## 6. Discussion

This research makes several theoretical contributions. First, we propose and confirm that co-membership is an effective mechanism for constructing network ties in OSS communities. Through these ties, useful information and knowledge can flow across OSS projects. Although prior literature has suggested an association between network ties and OSS project success, it is not clear what the underlying mechanism is for the association. In this study we clarify that learning across OSS projects through co-members can result in knowledge flow across OSS projects, which further leads to performance improvement, thus filling in this ‘‘missing link’’ in prior literature. Through co-members, network ties among projects can be established, and networking benefits can be derived and appropriated for OSS development. Our anecdotal evidence and empirical analyses show that network ties based on project co-membership are indeed valid and effective for knowledge flow across OSS projects.

Second, existing studies typically propose a positive and linear relationship between network ties and team performance (e.g., Grewal et al., 2006; Robert et al., 2008). We contend that network ties have both cost and benefit, which need to be considered simultaneously, particularly under the context of OSS development. Correspondingly we propose and empirically show that network ties may actually have a diminishing return to OSS success. This finding has important implication for future studies of the impact of network ties on OSS projects. Unlike other communities, OSS communities mainly exist in virtual workspace, since face-to-face interaction among members is rare. However, co-membership requires code contribution or participation in other activities on the projects, thus the cost outlays are particularly onerous and cannot be overlooked Therefore when analyzing the impact of network ties, it is suggested to take into consideration of the cost as well as the benefit of network ties, not only for OSS development, but also for performance improvement in general.

Third, we explore the nature of the network ties and the potential information flow through these ties. We do this in two ways. Since members play various roles on OSS projects, network ties differ in their ability to access and make use of information and resources. We find that network ties of leader–follower and follower–leader types benefit OSS projects more than those of leader–leader and follower–follower types. Moreover, since projects in different development stages vary in their stocks of knowledge, expertise, and skills that have been accumulated, we argue and confirm that network ties reaching out to projects of later stages are more beneficial than ties to those of earlier stages. By examining the roles played by the co-members on the connected projects as well as the stages of the neighboring projects, this study extends our understanding of the impact of social networks on OSS success (e.g., Colazo, 2010; Grewal et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2011a). In other words, it is important to realize that not all the ties play the same role in channeling information and knowledge, and some ties may be particularly effective and thus require more attention and effort to create and maintain.

This study also bears practical and strategic implications for successful development of OSS projects. As pointed out by prior studies, social actors are seldom self-sustaining, and to survive and prosper, they need to actively exchange ideas with others, scan for potential opportunities, and garner resources to their own advantage (Burt, 1992). During this process, network ties are particularly helpful in pulling in useful information so as to maintain one’s effectiveness and competitiveness (Fleming and Waguespack, 2007). In the context of OSS development, while OSS project teams are largely emergent, project leaders do, to certain degree, have control over who can make direct changes to the code base, and therefore influence the composition and activities of project teams.<sup>6</sup> Thus, the results from our study suggest that to increase the odds of success, team leaders not only need to motivate contribution from team members, but also need to monitor the activities of team members (Singh and Tan, 2011), and consider and even strategically maneuver its network ties with other projects whenever appropriate (Colazo, 2010; Singh et al., 2011a).

Specifically, on one hand, project teams need to recognize the informational benefits conferred by network ties; on the other hand, it is equally important to keep in mind that maintaining network ties incurs cost outlay, which may eventually offset their benefits once the number of network ties exceeds a certain threshold. Realizing that network ties of follower– leader type is particularly helpful, leaders of the focal projects may intentionally invite members who have led other projects to join the focal project. To capitalize on network ties, when deciding on which other projects to join, developers are encouraged to work on projects that are of later development stages or more mature projects, and this way, the focal project can potentially channel in more high-quality knowledge and information as well as avoid pitfalls so as to improve the odds of success.

This study has its own share of limitations. First, we infer network ties among OSS projects from project co-memberships. However, there may exist other channels through which projects connect with each other, such as physical meetings (Fleming and Waguespack, 2007), thus the knowledge flow between the projects may not be perfectly captured by co-memberships. Second, our study is essentially cross-sectional. Future studies may conduct longitudinal analyses to reveal dynamic patterns of the impact of network ties (e.g., Qureshi and Fang, 2011; Singh et al., 2011b). Third, it is possible that members may become inactive after joining projects and thus the impact of network ties through co-memberships may be over-estimated. Future studies may explore this issue further. Fourth, in addition to learning across projects as argued in this study, developers may learn from each other within a project. However, we do not differentiate between these two types of learning in this study. Last, in this research, we employ large sample quantitative analyses to examine the impact of network ties on OSS success. Researchers are encouraged to apply qualitative methods such as in-depth interviews, case studies, and observations to investigate the issue further. Through these methods, details about the content of information flow across network ties may emerge, and this may further deepen our understanding of the impact of network ties.

## 7. Conclusion

In this research, we take a fine-grained view of network relations to examine the impact of network ties on the success of OSS development. We identify co-membership among project teams as an effective mechanism for building network ties among OSS projects. We also find that network ties have a diminishing return to project success. More importantly, the effectiveness of network ties depends not only on the number of ties but also on the types of ties and the development stages of the connected projects. Network ties of leader–follower type and follower–leader type are more beneficial to OSS success than other types of ties, and network ties connecting to projects of later development stages are more beneficial than those connecting to projects of earlier development stages. Our results contribute to the understanding of the impact of social networks on OSS success
