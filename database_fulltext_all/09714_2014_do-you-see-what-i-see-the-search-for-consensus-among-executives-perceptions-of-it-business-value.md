## Abstract

Since firms rarely collect data on the precise economic or financial impacts of information technology (IT), perceptions play a key role in assessing IT impacts. To the extent that executives in the same firm evaluate IT impacts similarly, it can be easier to approve future IT investments or to initiate corrective action for failing IT investments. In this study, we use distributed sensemaking theory to investigate the conditions under which executives will reach a consensus as to the extent and locus of firm and process-level IT impacts in their firm. Using data from surveys of 133 top-level business executives in 13 firms, we show that consensus is a function of CIO-led sensegiving in the form of IT promotion, CIO leadership, information systems (IS) engagement with end users, and IS-business communications. The absence of consensus – discord – suggests IS disengagement, a lack of effective CIO leadership, weak IT-business communications, and ineffective promotion of the role of IT. Sensegiving does not mean telling executives what to think about IT but rather how to think about IT and its impacts at various points within the firm. Whether IT impacts are rated high or low, efforts to create increased consensus among executives can greatly enhance value from IT. ## Introduction

With the advent of new forms of information technology (IT) such as mobile apps, software-as-a-service, and virtualization, information systems (IS) practitioners and academics face renewed challenges in measuring the impacts of IT. Although firms rarely have objective data linking IT to sales, profitability, or other business metrics, executives can still have an opinion on the scale and locus of IT impacts in their firms (Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). Through a cognitive sensemaking process, executives are able to notice, filter, weight, and aggregate multiple informational cues as to the performance impacts of IT into a single, overarching view of IT just as jurors arrive at a final verdict based on their review and synthesis of a wide body of evidence, testimony, and argument presented in court (Starbuck & Milliken, 1988; Weick, 1995).

What is especially intriguing about executives’ perceptions is the extent to which they accurately reflect the true reality of IT impacts in firms, notwithstanding a long-held belief that perceptions are open to bias, error, and distortion and so should be viewed with suspicion, particularly in an area as complex as IT business value (Mezias & Starbuck, 2003). Building on reports of perceptual accuracy in other areas of the IS literature (see Venkatraman & Ramanujam, 1987; Miller et al, 1997; Maule & Hodgkinson, 2003; Karimi et al, 2004; Ketokivi & Schroeder, 2004), Tallon & Kraemer (2007) reveal that executives with visibility into a range of different business processes in a firm can sense from personal experience or from interactions with peers, subordinates, and reports where and how much business value is being created by IT. What the literature is less certain of, however, is whether executives in the same firm will perceive IT impacts similarly or whether they will rate IT impacts differently based on divergent personal goals for IT or overly narrow domains of functional responsibility that mask the broader effects of IT at the firm level.

The extent of consensus among key decision makers in the same firm as to the extent and locus of IT impacts is key to defining and maintaining a strategic direction for IT. Whether executives perceive IT impacts as high or low, consensus allows IT resources to be allocated in ways that fit the strategic goals of the corporation (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Bassellier et al, 2003; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). If there is consensus among top executives that IT is underperforming, steps can be taken to remove, rectify, or otherwise redirect problematic IT investments. If there is consensus that IT is performing as planned, it can be easier to obtain agreement to continue supporting existing IT or to fund new and potentially risky IT. If consensus is lacking, discord or disagreement can make it harder for an executive to justify the need for greater IT spending if some executives view IT as contributing handsomely to firm performance while others feel that IT is underperforming and that increased IT spending would be wasteful. Discord can also weaken the role of the CIO as a strategic business partner, relegating IT to the status of a tactical asset and putting the firm at risk from an abrupt market change (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Sambamurthy, 2000; Bassellier et al, 2003; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). Building on the IS literature on perceptions of IT impacts, the goal of this study is to explore the means by which executives can reach a consensus as to the scale and locus of IT impacts in their firm. This is separate from asking what practices firms can deploy to boost IT impacts. Instead, recognizing that consensus is useful and worthwhile, we are asking what firms can do to create a consensus around IT impacts, regardless of whether IT impacts are seen as high or low.

As presented in Figure 1, our model uses two related theories: distributed sensemaking, an area of sensemaking theory centered on sensemaking in groups or distributed settings (Weick et al, 2005; Kendra & Wachtendorf, 2006) and sensegiving, a theory centered on communications and knowledge sharing as a way to allow sensemakers (individuals or groups) to engage in more directed sensemaking (Rouleau, 2005; Weick, 2005; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007; Vlaar et al, 2008). Distributed sensemaking refers to a variety of situations when individuals in a group setting must independently form an opinion that may or may not fit with what others see (Weick et al, 2005; Kendra & Wachtendorf, 2006). Distributed sensemaking does not require individuals to reach a consensus nor does it preclude the possibility that individuals might fail to reach a consensus. Sensegiving, defined as a process or a sequence of practices meant to ‘influence the sensemaking and meaning construction of others toward a preferred redefinition of organizational reality’ (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991, p. 442), can create a context that is conducive to and supportive of consensus. The goal of sensegiving is not to tell others what to think but how to think. In the context of IT, CIOs can use sensegiving practices to educate users and other non-IT executives on ways to think about IT impacts. This could entail, for example, a forum for the exchange of ideas on objectives and expectations for IT or making the IS function more communicative, engaging, and responsive to users (Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007).

We focus, in particular, on four critical forms of IS sensegiving: CIO Leadership, which is defined as the extent to which CIOs play a leadership role in business activities; IT Promotion, which is defined as attempts by the IS function to promote greater awareness of IT; IS Function Engagement, which is defined as IS outreach to users as a way to resolve IT issues; and IS-Business Communications, which is defined as bidirectional exchanges between IT and business executives as to their goals for, and satisfaction with, IT. These four sensegiving practices are drawn from a review of the IS literature on the relationships between IT and business executives and their respective roles in using IT for competitive advantage. On the basis of this review, we suggest that IS-led sensegiving activities can help firms to build a shared firm-wide consensus as to the level and locus of IT impacts (Reich & Benbasat, 1996; Lefebvre et al, 1997; Bassellier et al, 2003; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004; Preston et al, 2008). Studies reveal that CIO leadership and outreach to users can develop an awareness of the role of IT and, through education and advocating for more effective use of IT, can help to extend the range of impacts from IT (Fiegener & Coakley, 1995; Huff et al, 2003).

On the basis of a sample of 133 executives drawn from the executive steering committees at 13 Fortune 500 firms, we find evidence of both consensus and discord. Using a binary-logit regression model created around a cross-level model (Klein et al, 1999), we find that sensegiving helps build consensus around the extent and locus of IT impacts whereas a lack of sensegiving can allow discord to take hold. As such, this research contributes to the literature on executives’ perceptions of IT by improving our knowledge of how executives assess IT impacts. Our results can also help practitioners as they set about building consensus around IT. Sensegiving gives users the confidence and tools to better rate IT impacts, potentially showing them what steps are needed to address underperforming IT investments that fail to create sufficient value.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We first review how distributed sensemaking informs our search for consensus around IT impacts. We then probe different sensegiving practices to see how they might spur consensus. After outlining our hypotheses, we present our survey data and empirical analysis. This is followed by a discussion of our findings and their implications for research and practice. Lastly, we conclude by reviewing our contributions, research limitations, and future research possibilities.

## Theoretical discussion

Thomas et al (1993) describe sensemaking as, ‘the reciprocal interaction of information seeking, meaning, ascription, and action’ (p. 240). Weick (1995) extends this definition, using the seven properties of sensemaking (identity, social, ongoing, cues, plausible, sensible, and retrospective) to portray a process in which ‘people concerned with identity in the context of others engage ongoing events from which they extract cues and make plausible sense retrospectively, all the while enacting more or less order into those ongoing events’ (p. 18). Plausibility rather than accuracy is the primary goal of sensemaking. Individuals iteratively process informational cues until such time as they feel confident that their views are defensible and stable. However, plausibility does not mean that sensemaking will be inaccurate. Tallon & Kraemer (2007) show that despite well-grounded fears that sensemaking can be distorted by any number of factors that individually or collectively might serve to undermine accuracy, executive perceptions can still mirror reality to such a degree that perceptions can be used in lieu of hard-to-find objective or financial measures of IT business value. However, knowing that sensemaking can (and often will) yield accurate and truthful results does not mean that every executive in the same corporation will think alike. Diversity of opinion is often necessary and highly desirable for effective decision making, particularly in the face of uncertainty, but at a certain point, opinions can become so diverse and polarized as to stifle the entire decision making process (Surowiecki, 2005). The emergence of a consensus view among executives of the perceived value of IT is critical even if all executives are not in complete agreement. Proposed IT projects are increasingly reviewed by steering committees whose members, drawn from the ranks of IT and non-IT executives, will often consider the impacts of existing IT investments before deciding to accept or reject funding requests. Although sensemaking allows individual executives to derive a personal opinion of IT, from a theoretical perspective, it may be more meaningful to consider the entire body of opinions across all executives in the same firm. Even when sponsored by top executives such at the CEO or CFO, proposed IT investments are still subject to careful scrutiny by cross-functional steering committees (Ward et al, 2008). Hence, there is an evolving need to consider distributed sensemaking within groups, not just as a means of understanding how groups make decisions but the diversity of opinions in these groups as to the perceived impacts of IT.

## Distributed sensemaking

Although distributed sensemaking is not explicitly defined in the organizational studies literature, Weick (2005) uses phrases such as distributed cognition and heedful interrelating to highlight the essence of sensemaking in a distributed setting where opinions are shaped by shared experiences and interpersonal exchanges. Weick presents distributed sensemaking as an attempt by a group of individuals to collectively make sense of a situation that no one person, in isolation, is likely to solve. Under sensemaking, the focus is on individual-level noticing, filtering, and weighting of cues albeit in a setting where an opinion may be revised to fit within organizational norms and expectations. With distributed sensemaking, the focus is on group-level noticing, filtering, and weighting of cues. Some cues may be common among all members of the group (public cues) whereas other cues are associated with a single individual or a subset of the group (private cues). In each case, individuals are free to voice a personal opinion but their opinion can become an input into another person’s sensemaking and may, depending on their status in the group, influence the overall viewpoint of the group. Quoting Taylor & Van Every (2000), Weick notes that if a group fails to make a correct decision, ‘we may find an explanation for their [mistake] in the way people are organizing [so] the degree of intelligence manifest by a network of nodes can be [set] by the quality [and] not just the quantity of its interconnectivity’ (Weick, 2005, p. 53). The loss of the Columbia space shuttle in 2003 is a particularly sad example of how group-think and top-down decision making can exclude opinions that, in hindsight, might have averted a tragedy if they had been properly considered rather than being censored. Where firms have a heavily routinized decision making structure, distributed sensemaking can be open to distortion if individuals think that their views will not be taken seriously or if it is preferable to agree with senior executives rather than to risk offense by offering an alternative opinion (Hambrick & Mason, 1984; Starbuck & Milliken, 1988; Miller et al, 1998; Weick, 2005). Reducing the presence of political or social pressures encourages more thoughtful, reasoned, and effective decision making. Hence, a highly desirable feature of distributed sensemaking is the need for independence between observers to minimize the risk of copycat opinions, diversity as a way to draw upon insights from individuals with private information, and decentralization in order to ensure that individuals are exposed to private information (Surowiecki, 2005).

Despite fears that distributed sensemaking can be limiting whenever independence, diversity, and decentralization are lacking, there is evidence that distributed sensemaking has practical value. In the case of the unscripted evacuation of persons from lower Manhattan following the collapse of the Twin Towers, Kendra & Wachtendorf (2006) marvel at the leaderless efforts of boat captains who, over several hours, ferried several hundred thousand residents to safety. The authors argue that if, ‘the operation was directed from above by a centralized control, the distributed aspects of sense would be more difficult to find since the controller might be more heavily involved in setting the parameters of sense’ (p. 4). When faced with uncertainty, captains were able to think and act independently without being burdened by a rigid chain of command or the desire to foist their views on others. Each captain used local knowledge but the collective effect of applying that knowledge was a successful (unplanned) evacuation with no injuries or loss of life.

## Distributed sensemaking and perceptions of IT business value

As outlined by Tallon & Kraemer (2007), the determination of IT business value in the absence of confirming objective data calls for executives to engage in an ongoing sensemaking process to notice, weigh, and filter informational cues from various sources in order to reach a reasoned, balanced judgment of where IT is delivering value and whether that value is sufficient to justify the initial IT investment. IT business value exhibits numerous forms of uncertainty not just in terms of whether value can be expressed monetarily but whether value can be captured by firms rather than being lost to consumers or competitors (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1996). Even with this degree of uncertainty, executives can still infer from their surroundings whether IT is having its intended effect. For example, marketing executives may be able to see the potential gains from

CRM through customer acquisition and retention rates or from an analysis of customer satisfaction. Equally, production/operations executives may be able to discern the impacts of production systems that minimize error rates, raise throughput, and streamline the link between design and production. Beyond this, other function-level executives may be able to translate what they see happening with IT in their areas into a general perception of how IT is shaping overall firm performance.

The various sources of cognitive bias and distortion noted by sensemaking theory as undermining the accu racy of individual perceptions are just as likely to affect the accuracy of distributed sensemaking. While Tallon & Kraemer (2007) find that these biases are largely non-fatal or immaterial for individual-level sensemaking, insofar as perceptions correlate strongly with objective measures of IT business value, we cannot deduce from this that perceptual biases are immaterial for each and every firm. The population of firms may be unaffected by perceptual bias but in the case of distributed sensemaking within a specific firm, bias could still exert a presence causing one executive’s opinion to diverge from another executive’s opinion. In Table 1, we consider the seven properties of sensemaking as they apply in a distributed setting (Weick, 1995). We focus in particular on how each property can, through bias, error, and distortion, lead to discord among decision makers who, despite noticing the same cues, can reach very different conclusions.

Chief among concerns for perceptual accuracy is the potential for projection and false inference – executives project what they see happening with IT at other businesses or functions to their own situation. Executives may see their firm as similar to others (same size, market reach, product mix, etc.) with known IT impacts and simply infer that their IT impacts must be similar – an error of attribution. They could also infer a link between IT spending and IT business value, believing that if their IT spending is high relative to an industry average, then commensurately IT business value must also be high – a situational error – or they may extrapolate data from the performance of a single application to the entire company – an error of omission. If these biases are systematic, individual-level sensemaking could give rise to a perception that is patently false and with no basis in reality. Notwithstanding the potential for systematic bias, distributed sensemaking could still give rise to a consensus view of IT impacts, albeit one that is demonstrably false.

The key difference between sensemaking and distributed sensemaking is how executives interact with one another and how this interaction shapes their personal opinions of IT business value. Executives may be functionally related to each other but they can still act as independent decision makers by virtue of their personal use or experience with IT. Of course, the reality in many corporations is that an analysis of IT impacts does not take place in a vacuum. Besides the social aspects of distributed sensemaking that allow perceptions to evolve through personal interactions with peers, superiors, and direct reports, the maturation of a perception of IT impacts can often be a two-way street with informational cues moving back and forth between the sensemaker and the sensegiver. While the role of the CIO and the IS function has morphed in recent decades from service provider to strategy enablement, the one constant throughout has been a desire on the part of the entire IS organization to educate users as to the capabilities of IT and the need to couch those capabilities within an impacts framework (Watson, 1990; Grover et al, 2011; Hansen et al, 2011; Kettinger et al, 2011). These endeavors have been variously defined in the organizational management and strategic planning literature as sensegiving (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991; Rouleau, 2005; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007; Vlaar et al, 2008).

## Building consensus through sensegiving

Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991) first coined the term sensegiving in an ethnographic study of a college president who was trying to communicate a new strategic vision to his colleagues and so win their support for its execution. As several researchers have since observed, sensegiving can be persuasive and educative when individuals set out to craft a message or to build a structure that others will use in their sensemaking but it is not guaranteed to, nor purposefully meant to, usurp or distort their sensemaking (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991; Bartunek et al, 1999; Rouleau, 2005; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007). As such, sensemaking and sensegiving are complementary. Sensegiving can be precipitated by sensemaking failures as seen, for example, by firefighter retraining efforts following the Mann-Gulch disaster (Weick, 1993). Sensemaking can also react to sensegiving through a re-interpretation of informational cues or through the promotion of rules through which cues can be filtered, weighed, and joined (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991). Sensegiving is different from managerial edict in that individuals have the option to think for themselves and to accept or reject the views or advice of the sensegiver. More recently, Maitlis & Lawrence (2007) probe triggers and enablers of sensegiving among organizational stakeholders and leaders. Sensegiving is often a response to sensemaking gaps in the form of problem solving irregularities, inadequate guidance from leaders, and the desire to better understand ambiguous events. Sensegiving enablers require some degree of subjectmatter expertise on the part of sensegivers before their views can be reasonably allowed to enter the sensemaking process. Effective sensegiving also requires a degree of trust and confidence on the part of sensereceivers (Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007). Maitlis & Lawrence (2007) regard sensegiving as, ‘a highly institutionalized form of social practice, with context-specific rules and resources’ (p. 79). Organizational actors routinely filter out sensegivers who lack legitimacy, expertise, resources, or social standing within the organization. In the context of perceptions of IT, undue pressure from CIOs to support a certain view of IT impacts can backfire causing business executives to become more critical and demanding of IT (Enns et al, 2003).

Sensegiving can take on different forms in the search for consensus around executive perceptions of IT business value. As reported in Table 2, the IS literature is replete with examples showing how CIOs can engage with end users to develop their knowledge and understanding of IT. Managerial IT knowledge has been repeatedly identified as an essential predictor of increased IT use and IT assimilation (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Boynton et al, 1994; Bassellier et al, 2003), while Broadbent & Kitzis (2005) regard CIO outreach to end users and efforts to shape their opinions of IT as a requirement for supplyside leadership. More broadly, CIO outreach translates into IS being more active in business planning and educating users to understand the full complement of opportunities that IT can present to firms. As earlier studies confirm, CIO participation in top management teams where CIOs can help to set and direct business goals helps to educate business executives on what IT can do to expand firm performance (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Kearns & Lederer, 2003). Hansen et al (2011) and Kettinger et al (2011) note that the digitization of organizations and processes has shifted the leadership role of the CIO to one of active engagement with business leaders. This role is not meant as a forum for CIOs to air their grievances or to press necessarily for higher budgets but to forge relationships that deliver a greater understanding of IT and its capabilities. Echoing Keen’s claim that ‘the CIO position is a relationship, not a job’ (Keen, 1991, p. 55), the true role of CIO leadership is one of educating, informing, and sensegiving with the ultimate aim of establishing a shared set of perspectives for what IT can achieve. From a theoretical viewpoint, CIO leadership can help business executives to – in the words of sensemaking theory – enact a sensible environment in the form of a cause and effect model linking IT projects and capabilities to possible outcomes. Thus, if executives are better educated and informed about IT in their organizations, they may be better able to reach a consensus on the scale and locus of IT impacts. These arguments lead to the first of four sensegiving hypotheses:

The broader IS function can also play a role in educating and informing users as to the role of IT. The relationship that Keen (1991) outlines for the CIO is built on the back of the IS function and what it has done to educate and inform users through seminars and workshops. The strategic planning process by which IT projects are proposed and ultimately funded is heavily influenced by users and business process managers (Segars & Grover, 1999). If through better training and overall promotion of IT, the IS function allows the user community to become aware of new innovations and ways that IT can help them to optimize business performance, business executives’ expectations for IT impacts may be more consistent. Consensus can be problematic when different business executives have different expectations for what IT is meant to do. Through IT promotion, a more informed and strategically engaged group of business executives may be more likely to rate IT impacts in a like manner. These arguments lead to the following hypothesis:

## H2: IT Promotion has a positive impact on consensus among executives’ perceptions of IT business value.

The technical nature of IT continues to antagonize users. A particularly negative experience with IT can hurt the reputation of the CIO and the broader IS function, leading to a rejection of IT project ideas and a tendency to minimize IT use (Sunstein. 2009). Research shows that commitment to IT is affected by how CIOs or the IS function engage with users (Enns et al, 2003). If CIOs employ rationally persuasive arguments for why IT is a key organizational resource and decline to put undue pressure on users to adopt IT without a clear imperative, executives are more likely to commit to IT. The sense giving literature notes that the operations of a central decision making authority can hurt organizational success to the extent that it insists on conformity to bureaucratic norms and routines (Kendra & Wachtendorf, 2006). However, this does not mean that CIOs should permit users to set their own IT standards – the result would be a chaotic mix of standards. Clearly users often have idiosyncratic needs that fuel the desire to selfmanage IT or to deviate from firm-wide IT standards. Sensitivity toward users’ needs, their fears for IT – anxiety around using IT or attendant risks – and concerns around user adoption of IT could mitigate the effects of bias on executives’ perceptions of IT. The risk of an ‘us vs them situation is that non-IT executives will see the IS function as an obstacle to progress rather than a partner in busi ness success. An IS function that tries to engage users, to understand their needs and concerns, and to help users overcome whatever barriers might restrict their successful use of IT is likely to be seen as a partner in success (Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004). The social property of sensemaking is potentially influenced by how users perceive their interactions with the IS function. For example, if the IS function is perceived as disengaged and unhelpful, low levels of IT business value could be blamed on them whereas if IT is a success, it is in spite of the IS function and due solely to the efforts of the user community. If, instead, the IS function is seen as engaged and helpful, the business value impacts of IT – regardless of their scale – could be seen as jointly attributable to users and the IS function. Prior studies show that uncertain issues and complex stakeholder environments present an opportunity for sensegiving (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991; Weick et al, 2005; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007). IS function engagement provides such an opportunity. By affecting the social aspects of sensemaking that bifurcate how users view the IS function and the impacts from IT in turn, IS function engagement is likely to lead to consensus among executives’ perceptions of IT business value. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

## H3: IS Function Engagement has a positive impact on consensus among executives’ perceptions of IT business value.

Finally, as indicated in Table 2, the IS literature has repeatedly found evidence of the value of IS-business partnerships. Just as sensemaking is an ongoing process in information gathering, weighting, and filtering, sensegiving is also an ongoing process of discernment, guided in part by the perceived presence of sensemaking gaps (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991; Weick, 1995; Weick et al, 2005; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007). Sensemaking gaps may be triggered by change in IT that threatens the organizational status quo or that signifies opportunities for future business growth. Increased communication between users and the IS function is a vital step in bringing IT innovation to the attention of users and for users to develop opinions of IT. Sensemaking is a social process that relies heavily on inter-personal communication (Weick, 1995). Sensegiving is equally communicative (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991). Hence, IT-business communications can feature prominently in the search for consensus among executives’ views of IT impacts if, as previous studies show, communications create a shared understanding of goals for IT and how IT should be used to greater effect (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Boynton et al, 1994; Rouleau, 2005). Communications will allow the IS function to gauge the success of IT through end user surveys and to gauge their sensegiving if IT is viewed as ineffective or if IT resources are being underutilized. The net result is that executives and other users will be better informed about the magnitude and direction of IT investments and will be more likely to hold similar views as to the scale and locus of IT business value within their firm. Communications are a necessary and important precursor to the availability of public and private cues. Without cues, there can be no sensemaking and little potential for consensus as to the scale and locus of IT business value. Hence:

H4: IS-business communications has a positive impact on consensus among executives’ perceptions of IT business value.

Overall, the above hypotheses depict critical aspects of sensegiving on the part of the CIO and the IS function. Next, we assess the impact of these sensegiving activities in terms of their ability to achieve a consensus view of the scale and locus of IT business value among groups of executives in the same firm.

## Methodology

To test our model, we devised a two-part survey directed at senior business executives. In the first part, we included 24 sensemaking items derived from research by Tallon & Kraemer (2007) that evaluates the perceptual impacts of IT at the process (20 items) and firm level (4 items). The four firm-level items – capturing executives’ perceptions of the impact of IT on firm profit, market share, labor costs, and selling, general, and administrative costs – correlate positively and significantly with objective measures based on financial accounting data (Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). On the basis of the argument that the first-order impacts of IT materialize at the process level (Barua et al, 1995; Melville et al, 2004), the process-level items in our survey are designed to assess a range of IT impacts across the value chain as represented by the following standard business processes (supplier relations, production & operations, product & service enhancement (R&D), sales & marketing, and customer relations) – four items per process. To the extent that our survey targets persons with responsibility for primary processes or functional areas, it is likely that their noticing and sensemaking will initially involve specific IT impacts at the process level. Perceptions from different processes outside their immediate area of responsibility can later be weighted, filtered, and combined into an overarching assessment of IT impacts at the firm level (Starbuck & Milliken, 1988; Klein et al, 1999; Mezias & Starbuck, 2003; Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). Consensus among this grouping of executives can be assessed at both the process and firm levels as Tallon & Kraemer (2007) suggest in previous studies of IT business value. However, since process-level perceptions are further aggregated at the firm level, research on crosslevel or multilevel analysis suggests that a more precise or appropriate test of sensegiving would involve consensus around the impacts of IT at the firm level. This would allow us to control for potential confounding effects of executives’ opinions on IT business value at the process level (Klein et al, 1999).

The second part of our survey focused on sensegiving. Thirteen items were developed from prior research as noted in Table 2: CIO Leadership (4 items), IT Promotion (4 items), IS Function Engagement (3 items), and IS-Business Communications (2 items). Comments were received from three IT executives and three business executives at three Fortune 500 firms as to the interpretation and relevance of all items. Although sensegiving originates from inside the office of the CIO, rather than asking the CIO to report on their sensegiving activities, a better approach is to rely on the views of non-IT executives in the same firm. We also created a series of four items to ascertain executives’ views on the effectiveness of the IS function: service quality, helpfulness, responsiveness to requests, and user satisfaction. These items are useful controls recognizing that if executives have an especially favorable opinion of the IS function, they may falsely infer that IT value must be high. Certainly, there could be a positive correlation between executive perceptions of IS effectiveness and their perceptions of IT business value but we do not want to bias our model by failing to control for potentially misleading attribution errors (Mezias & Starbuck, 2003; McKeen et al, 2007; Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). For pretest purposes, we mailed surveys to all executive members of the steering committees at two services and two manufacturing firms; their feedback clarified word choices for a tiny number of items and facilitated an initial test of the psychometric properties of all survey items. All survey items were retained (a copy of the complete survey is included in the Appendix).

## Data collection and analysis

The population of interest to this research comprised single-segment, single line of business firms in the Fortune 500. Since our intent was to survey all members of the executive steering committee – that is all C-level executives except the CIO, Chief Legal Counsel, and outside directors – there is a possibility that in a conglomerate or multi-divisional company, IT impacts could vary between business units and so asking an executive about IT business value at a certain point along the firm’s value chain could introduce a degree of uncertainty, bias, and confusion into their response. By focusing on firms whose activities are confined to a single line of business, we control for the exigencies of multidivisional corporations. Using the S&P Compustat database for 2004, we identified 63 single segment firms having one NAICS code or whose secondary and primary NAICS codes pointed to the same line of business. We then contacted the CIO at each firm to obtain their backing and tacit support for a survey of their senior business colleagues. CIOs were told that the research team would manage all correspondence and that they would be unable to receive individual-level data. Only aggregate data would be supplied to them in the form of a final report. If CIOs gave their approval, we would merely say in our covering letter to executives that the project had the support of the CIO and that their direct responses to the research team would be treated in confidence. Data collection was scheduled to begin in late 2005 and to continue through the summer months of 2006.

After contacting the CIOs at these 63 Fortune 500 firms by mail and phone, 12 CIOs reported that their firms had a formal policy prohibiting participation in academic research, four declined to participate due to reorganizations which would have impacted their executive steering committees, and a further nine felt that the project was of little interest to them. However, 14 CIOs indicated that they would participate in the research and would lend their support to a survey of their executive boards. One CIO later resigned before formal mailing of the surveys began and so our final sample frame consists of 13 firms. Using the Hoovers website, annual financial reports, 10Ks, and press releases, we proceeded to identify members of the executive steering committees at each firm, limiting our search to senior non-IT executives, directors, and Senior Vice Presidents with functional responsibilities. We also omitted from this list executives with interim appointments, executives residing outside the U.S., and anyone who joined the firm within the last 2 years. Using these criteria, we obtained a list of 212 C-level executives across our sample of 13 firms.

After two survey mailings with telephone follow-up, we received responses from 135 executives. Two surveys were removed due to missing data and so our final sample, shown in Table 3, comprises 133 executives (a 63% response rate). For response bias purposes, we randomly contacted 20 non-respondents who cited travel commitments as their primary reason for not replying. The average tenure of respondents was 7.9 years at the same firm. We also compared the 13 firms with those that did not respond on a series of financial measures (profit margin, assets to sales ratio, and return on equity). No significant differences were noted and so the results from our later analysis may be generalized to our larger population of single segment, single line-of-business corporations drawn more generally from across the Fortune 500.

In order to test the psychometric properties of our survey items, we first undertook a confirmatory factor analysis. Most of our items – particularly those measuring IT business value – have been repeatedly tested in previous research and so the factor structure underlying the items was relatively well known and understood (Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). General descriptive statistics on each survey item is given in Table 4. Using PLSGraph, we tested an 11-factor model: five process-level IT impact factors, one firm-level IT impact factor, four sensegiving factors, and one IS effectiveness factor that was used for control purposes. The results of this analysis are shown in Table 5. All primary factor loadings are statistically significant at $P { \leqslant } 0 . 0 0 1$ . Forty of the 41 survey items had a primary factor loading above 0.70; one item (ISE3) reported a factor loading of 0.67. Cross-loadings were also examined. Just under 11% of cross-loadings (44 of 410 cross-loadings) had values above 0.5 but all were less than 0.6; 30 of these 44 cross-loadings represent strong positive correlations between IT business value in the primary processes of the value-chain and are likely attributable to the pervasive effects of enterprise IT systems whose effects touch multiple processes or where IT impacts in one process spill over into an adjoining process (Barua et al, 1995; Tallon, 2012).

We also tested the survey items for evidence of convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity verifies that items correlate higher with indicators of the same factor than with indicators of some other factor. Convergent validity is confirmed if the average variance extracted (AVE) is at least 0.5 (Falk & Miller, 1992). Meanwhile, discriminant validity tests if the indicators of a specific factor load higher on that factor than on different factor. Tests for discriminant validity ask if the squared correlation between each pair of factors is greater than the AVE for each factor. As noted in Table 6, our survey passes both of these validity tests. Table 6 also shows that composite reliability for each factor exceeded a recommended minimum of 0.80 (Nunnally, 1978). Reflective item indicators were used for all validity and reliability tests.

## Model analysis

Since our dependent variable is binary (discord or consensus), we use binary logistic regression to estimate the parameters of our model. The goal of logit modeling is to estimate the natural log of the odds of a certain event, in this case consensus (coded as 1). In its simplest form, logit models can be written as:

$$
\mathrm{Ln} (O d d s) = \mathrm{Ln} \left(\frac {\mathrm{P}}{1 - P}\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2} + \dots + e
$$

where P is the probability of finding consensus in a particular firm. By taking the exponential of the logit, it is possible to derive an odds ratio. This ratio allows us to evaluate the potential impact on consensus for each independent variable. For each respondent, logit modeling can also be used to make predictions as to whether there is a greater likelihood of discord or consensus. Since we already know from the analysis in Table 7 what the actual outcomes are for each respondent, this allows us to build a classification model to test the accuracy of the overall model. To predict consensus, we specify four sensegiving variables (using averages of the items under each construct) and several control variables that, on the basis of sensemaking theory, could bias executives’ opinions of IT impacts. We control first for IS function effectiveness on the basis that executives could falsely infer IT impacts from their views of the performance of the IS function. To do so, we took an average of the four items used to measure IS function effectiveness. We also control for executive perceptions of IT impacts in each of the five processes of the value chain (using averages of the individual survey items in each process), firm size as measured by the log of sales, and profitability as measured by net margin. The results of this analysis appear in Table 8 with fit statistics and data showing the predictive capabilities of our model. We also tested an alternative regression model that used the raw firm-level IT impact interrater reliability scores in Table 7 as dependent variable. The results of this alternative OLS regression model are structurally similar to that of the binary logit model.

The goodness of fit statistics in Table 8 point to an appropriate model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow $\chi ^ { \bar { 2 } }$ is insignificant while both the Cox-Snell and Nagelkerke $R ^ { 2 }$ are high. More importantly, the classification table shows an overall 90.2% prediction success rate (96% in the case of consensus alone). In terms of the predictor variables, we note first that the coefficients for each control variable are insignificant. This is an interesting and important result for it shows that the existence of consensus or discord is not dependent on what executives believe to be the impact of IT at the process level nor is consensusdriven by their views on the effectiveness of the IS function. Sensegiving theory says that in cases where individuals are unable to process sufficient amounts of information to render a carefully weighted and independent opinion, they may resort to extrapolation and attribution to deduce something plausible that might be closer to the truth (Starbuck & Milliken, 1988; Weick, 1995). In this case, if executives were prone to equating IS function effectiveness with the value that IT provides to firms, it could mean that their sensemaking process was constrained and notwithstanding their claims to plausibility, potentially wrong. Equally, since perceptions of IT impacts are insignificant in each area of the value chain, we conclude that executives are not simply extrapolating what they see happening with IT impacts at the process level to what they think might be happening at the firm level. If this arose, it would be difficult to talk about consensus since it would mean that executives struggle to look past the process level to understand how (or if) IT is having an impact on financial performance. A legitimate question to ask about our determination of consensus and discord is whether there is a systematic bias in our sample where consensus materializes only in cases where IT impacts are high and discord in cases where IT impacts are low. To test this assertion, we use a one-way analysis of variance in Table 9 where we compare the means of IT business value in each process and at the firm level for firms with consensus and those with discord. The results of this test reveal no systematic differences in process-level IT impacts between each group with the sole exception of customer relations where consensus firms have slightly higher IT impacts. There are some differences at the firm level where consensus firms report higher IT impacts on profit margin and labor costs but no discernible differences for IT impacts on market share or SG&A. These differences in IT impacts on profit margin and labor costs may suggest a tendency toward consensus in firms where IT is more effective while discord is more prevalent in cases where IT is less effective. Arguably, if IT impacts are high, more executives may be open to sharing in that success if they feel partially responsible for that success. However, if IT impacts are weak or inadequate relative to the level of desired outcomes, executives may be more inclined to engage in a blame game as they seek to disclaim personal responsibly for why IT impacts are weak rather than uniformly agreeing that IT impacts are weak. If an absence of sensegiving is enabling discord to take root in such cases, it may be even more important for CIOs to take a leadership role to allow clearer minds to prevail and so restore IT impacts to a more effective level once consensus has been achieved around the level of IT impacts. Since costs relate to profit, discord around the impact of IT on costs could also flow through to discord around profitability.

## Discussion and Implications

Our analysis of distributed sensemaking among senior executives in 13 Fortune 500 firms reveals a clear delineation between firms where IS-led sensegiving facilitates consensus and those where a lack of sensegiving likely prevents consensus from taking hold. Just as previous research outlines a number of IS initiatives that fold up under the umbrella of sensegiving (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Weill & Ross, 2004; Broadbent & Kitzis, 2005), we evaluate the impacts of sensegiving from four related angles. From the perspective of the IS function, it matters whether the IS function engages with users, whether they educate users as to how to use IT, and whether they communicate with business partners on matters relating to IT. Yet, in the search for greater consensus around IT impacts and what this means in terms of eliminating IT waste or enhancing strategic IT investments, our results show that CIO leadership is especially important. CIO leadership means that CIOs have a role to play in strategic planning and business problem solving at the most senior levels of the firm. The IS research literature is already replete with examples of why CIOs should be a member of the executive management committee since this allows IT to be directly involved in shaping business performance. Our results potentially go beyond what we already know about the role of the CIO in that we can now show that the CIO is a key lynchpin in how non-IT executives can begin to think about IT and how IT might be affecting their firm performance. CIOs can educate users on how to make sense of what they see happening around them with IT. In this way, CIOs can help steer executives’ sensemaking toward consensus. This may require some level of sensebreaking, a term coined by Vlaar et al (2008) to depict efforts to discredit false perceptions of IT or to identify more realistic expectations for IT. CIOs can use their involvement in strategic planning activities to create more elaborate business cases for IT. CIOs could also promote post-implementation reviews of IT as a way to ascertain if IT is living up to its promise, using the lessons learned from these reviews to reinforce what users need to know about IT in order to come to a broad informed consensus on the scale and locus of IT impacts (Tallon et al, 2000).

## Research contribution

This research contributes to the literature on IT business value in three respects. First, we build on past research by Tallon & Kraemer (2007) that shows that executives’ perceptions of IT business value are accurate, reliable, and consistent with objective measures of IT impacts. IS researchers continue to voice frustration at the lack of objective data on IT impacts, notably at the process level.

Executive perceptions are – when accurate – a proxy for objective measures. If, as this research reveals, there is a possibility that executives in the same firm might not agree, there is a concern that not all executives should be trusted. If executives do not notice the same things, then – on the basis of distributed sensemaking theory – their views are distorted and so should not be used by IS researchers unless deliberate precautions are taken. Objective or financial performance metrics will always remain the gold standard by which IT impacts are judged but if there is consensus among senior executives as to the perceptual impacts of IT, there is every reason to use perceptions in lieu of objective measures. In that sense, our findings reinforce the conclusions in Tallon & Kraemer (2007) that researchers should not be so quick to dismiss perceptions entirely. The critical result of this research is to advise IS researchers to remain cautious and distrustful until consensus is confirmed.

Second, we extend previous research on sensemaking in IT by considering the potential impact of sensegiving activities on the extent of consensus among senior business executives. Whether IT is having its desired effects or not, consensus is important for deciding how firms can use IT for optimal effect and, in those cases where IT business value is weak, consensus can help to define a course of corrective action. Sensegiving can help to build consensus but only insofar as the sensegiver is credible and in a position of authority that allows their views to be shared with key decision makers. Sensemaking is entirely cognitive but sensegiving can help to shape the thought processes through which informational cues are filtered and combined into an overall perception of IT. Sensegiving does not mean that a CIO is telling others what to think about IT but rather how to think about IT. Our results show that efforts to build a rapport with users, through which users can be better educated and informed about the potential for IT and how to determine if that potential is realized. can help bring about consensus. Even if IT is failing, it helps if executives can discover this fact and react to it. As shown in the strategic management literature, managerial sensegiving can bring a degree of order and control to stakeholder sensemaking, in effect helping to stem the negative consequences of respondent bias that can push most executives to misinterpret or misreport what they see (Maitlis. 2005) Third. we make a theoretical contribution to the literature on IT business value by linking dual theories of distributed sensemaking and sensegiving as a way to build consensus between executives in the same firm. Distributed sensemaking speaks to the potential for diverse opinions or interpretation of uncertain events. When individuals are sufficiently independent to avoid real or implied pressure to adopt copycat opinions, diverse in terms of their backgrounds or experience, and decentralized in terms of their ability to garner private information, it is more likely that key decision makers can reach valid judgments by taking full and unbiased advantage of all available information (Surowiecki, 2005;

Weick et al, 2005). Sensegiving is an important input into this process in order that sensemakers have an accurate sense of the informational cues around them and how that information can inform their decision making. In a practical sense, our depiction of sensegiving reveals a two-way relationship between the IS function and end users. Sensegiving requires CIOs to know their audience and to recognize that executives can be intimidated and confused by IT. Knowing this, CIOs can better design activities to educate and inform users about how to think about IT, how to pursue innovative uses of IT, and how to participate more fully in IT management.

## Implications for research and practice

The identification or measurement of IT business value continues to rank among the top issues in IS research (Melville et $^ { a l , }$ 2004). The question of whether IT is strategically important is not without its detractors although firms continue to see significant value from IT (Carr, 2003; Luftman & Kempaiah, 2008). Tallon & Kraemer (2007) added a word of caution for IS researchers who wish to use perceptual measures of IT impacts because of the fickle and potentially biased nature of perceptions. This study adds a further note of caution. The potential for error and distortion is ever-present but, on balance, perceptions can be used in lieu of objective measures of IT impacts as long as researchers have an ability to know that a single respondent’s sensemaking capabilities are in sync with what others are thinking. If discord exists in a firm, researchers could end up targeting a group or a person whose views are not representative of the firm as a whole. This is a problem shared with other academic disciplines that rely on a single respondent (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986; Podsakoff et $^ { a l , }$ 2003). When using the views of a single individual to convey key data about IT impacts, researchers must ask whether that individual’s views are representative of their colleagues. Without this insight, IS researchers could reach flawed and potentially erroneous conclusions.

Our research contribution showing the association between sensemaking and sensegiving and the potential impact of IS sensegiving on consensus among business executives is both timely and relevant at a time when sensemaking is receiving greater attention from IS academics. Sensemaking has been used to investigate software design (Griffith, 1999), IT adoption (Seligman, 2000), global IT deployment (Kirsch, 2004), and outsourcing teams (Vlaar et al, 2008). Rather than pursuing further research around occasions for sensemaking (Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007), future research could investigate how sensegiving can steer sensemaking in a direction that is likely to be most beneficial to IT impacts and overall firm performance.

Our research also has implications for IS practitioners. The value of sensegiving cannot be under-stated. Even if CIOs and their staff are overwhelmed with technical issues around technology integration, standards, and software selection. there is a need for CiOs to reach out to their constituents (or customers) to educate them as to the value of IT. The fact that IS effectiveness has no discernible effect on consensus confirms that this is not a popularity contest. CIOs should not see sensegiving as an opportunity to engage in self-promotion or to force an agenda on unsuspecting users. Sensegiving is meant to be informative and educative, to bring IS and business executives closer together, and to foster greater understanding of what IT can do for firms. With a growing focus on responsible IT governance and greater use of IT for strategic gain, it behooves CIOs to consider sensegiving activities that can create an awareness or knowledge of IT among business executives with the eventual goal of improving the scale and breadth of impacts from IT.

## Limitations and future research

While every effort was made to minimize critical weaknesses, our research suffers from a number of factors that could impact its generalizability. First, our sample size – 133 senior executives in 13 single segment, single line-of-business Fortune 500 firms – is small. While this was a deliberate effort to control for extraneous factors that could affect our ability to identify consensus or discord across a conglomerate-type organization, a larger sample size would have allowed for greater generalizability. Hence, we caution against extending our results across the Fortune 500 or to others outside this group. Future research could consider the effects of sensegiving under such circumstances where best practices from one division of an organization are allowed to spill over into other divisions. Second, we confined our analysis to a subset of sensegiving activities based on their prevalence in the IS literature. Other IS sensegiving activities may be evaluated in future research as a way for CIOs to rank order the most important activities at their disposal for shaping how business executive think about IT. Not all sensegiving activities may be equally effective – some may be easier or less costly to implement than other. Third, our research is cross-sectional. We do not consider whether sensemaking changes over time in response to changes in sensegiving activities. Our data were primarily collected in 2006. Since then, IT has radically evolved through software-as-a-service, cloud hosting, mobile applications, and other innovations that have, in some instances, shifted the locus of IT decision making from the CIO to functional leaders. As such, there may be less need for sensegiving if non-IT executives are more educated and confident on what IT can do for functional performance. Future studies could use post-implementa tion audits as a vehicle for assessing how sensemaking and sensegiving interact over time and how key events such as an IT failure presents opportunities for further sensegiving. Lastly, we measure sensegiving through the eves of business executives rather than using the CiO Future research could look at both groups to test whether in the case of consensus. CiOs and business executives are likely to judge sensegiving equally or whether, in the case of discord, business executives are likely to judge sensegiving at a much lower level than CIOs might judge themselves. The fact remains that IT and non-IT executives still disagree on a host of issues. IT is not a universal strategic resource across all firms. The CIO and the IS function are still distrusted in some firms. There is potential, therefore, for researchers to reassess the relationship between IT and business executives but to do so using the lens of sensegiving.

## Conclusion

The complexity (and importance) of IT investment evaluation has expanded tremendously under the exigencies of a progressively turbulent and unpredictable environment characterized by globalization, rapid product development, and shrinking product lifecycles. Much of the determination of IT value is left to blind faith as executives are left to gather and filter a mountain of data from disparate applications and processes before deciding whether IT impacts are sufficient. Despite what seems to be an intractable task, executives can make sense of these data and, indeed, their perceptions have been shown to be accurate even as researchers question whether these perceptions are so biased as to be irrelevant. In the absence of data, perceptions may be the only thing that executives have to go on and so distributed sensemaking is seen as a critical process in helping executives to understand what they see and, if necessary, to reach a collective decision on whether IT is achieving its potential. Consensus around IT is especially important considering the enormous amounts spent on IT by firms worldwide and the associated risk of failing to halt or reverse losses from a failing IT investment. Past research notes that firms are often slow to halt failing IT projects due to differences of opinion as to what represents failure. Sensegiving could help to detect failure sooner.

Ongoing changes in the role of the CIO have allowed CIOs to serve as an advocate and cheerleader for IT. Recent research on sensegiving recognizes that CIOs can reach out to their non-IT colleagues in an attempt to help them make sense of what is happening with IT. Sensegiving is not forced dogma. It is not management by decree but a well-intentioned effort to educate and inform others as to the value of IT and how they might approach an analysis of the value of IT under their control. Sensegiving, therefore, shapes the enactment of sensible environments, a central property of sensemaking where executives build mental models for how IT impacts can be evaluated. Sensegiving is meant to be educative, informative, sensitive to end user fears about IT, and above all else, mindful of the growing need to drive greater value from IT.

As IT becomes more complex and critical to business activities in the years ahead, IS sensegiving may become a necessary ingredient in new IT governance models. Consensus among executives as to the actual value of IT may be historical in nature but consensus may be equally important for clarifying future goals for IT. Future studies can offer useful insights into the dynamic interplay between sensemaking and sensegiving, not just in businesses where IT is viewed as successful but where IT is struggling to generate sufficient value in the face of often turbulent change. As research turns to consider issues of agility and IT responsiveness to change, sensegiving-based IT governance and its ability to forge a consensus around IT business value may prove transformative in how key organizational decision makers perceive the value of past, present, and future IT projects.
