# The design and evaluation of accessibility on web navigation

Benjamin P.-C. Yen

School of Business, The University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong, China

Received 30 June 2004; received in revised form 13 July 2006; accepted 30 July 2006 Available online 18 September 2006

## Abstract

Web sites have been deployed to create and sustain business competitiveness in a trend of emerging Web technologies and growing e-commerce. One critical success factor of e-commerce is the ability to allow information to be retrieved from a Web site in an efficient and effective manner. Such ability, being determined by both the Web site structure and the Web page organization, can be measured in terms of accessibility and popularity of Web pages. The relationship between accessibility and popularity of web pages is dynamic in nature and can be analyzed to enhance a Web design. Having observed the lack of means to measure information retrieval of a Web site, this paper purports to introduce a guideline to evaluate Web page accessibility based on several structural-based accessibility models where an innovative accessibility–popularity (A–P) analysis is deployed to measure and, thereby, to modify a Web structure. Both push (i.e. demand driven) strategies and pull (i.e. design driven) strategies are incorporated into such guideline. Further, accessibility models are analyzed and compared in order to identify appropriate applications for each model. The paper is concluded by a summary of future directions of the accessibility models. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Accessibility; Internet; Information retrieval; Accessibility–Popularity plot

## 1. Introduction

Information is indispensable in the modern world. Web and information systems have changed the way people communicate as well as expedited the process in which they obtain information. Millions of transactions take place on the Web within a split of second. Not surprisingly, the numbers of Web pages on Internet has grown explosively in the past few years and such growth is only expected to be even more acute in the future. Not only browsers benefit from the resourceful URL's that only keep them away clicks away from their desired information on the Web, but also Web page owners who profit from advertisement income generated by every visit the browsers pay to their Web sites. In the mean time, burdens are created by large quantity of unorganized information that results from poor search performance. To improve search performance, tremendous efforts are required on the part of Web page owners to continuously update and reorganize information on their Web pages and, in doing so, they strike to find answers to the following questions: What brings browsers and advertisers the most satisfaction? How to balance the interests of the two? How can a Web site better serve the browsers and advertisers in terms of information retrieval? And, finally, how to justify the costs of such improvement in information retrieval?

Research has been conducted on four aspects in relation to improvement in search performance and resource allocation, namely, human computer interactions, navigation, reconfiguration, and evaluation.

Human computer interactions mainly focus on issues of human factor, such as usability. Nielsen [17] studies Web usability in page content, link structure, site organization, and design issues for users with disabilities. Shneiderman summarizes prevailing theories, principles and guidelines for effective user interface designs for various websites [23] and points out the challenges in attaining universal usability for web-based service such as technology variety, user diversity and gaps in user knowledge [24]. Ivory and Hearst [11] surveyed popular (automated) website evaluation methods and graphical design practices, highlighting the importance of such design elements as architecture, page, link, text and graphics. Palmer [19] reports a series of studies that develop and validate website usability, design and performance metrics. The performance metric includes the subconstructs user satisfaction, the likelihood of return, and the frequency of use.

Web navigation is related to the modeling and analysis of Web structure and traverse. Chakrabarti et al. [4] develop algorithms that exploit the hyperlink structure of the WWW for information discovery and categorization, the construction of high quality resource lists, and the analysis of on-line hyperlinked communities. Sarukkai [21] uses a Markov Chain model based on the user access information for link prediction and path analysis. In addition to link prediction, the Markov Chain model provides web server HTTP request prediction, adaptive web navigation, web tour generation, and personalized hub/authority. Chakrabarti et al. [5] and Gibson et al. [7] define the Web sites as “authorities” and “hub” in isolation. They conclude that a respected authority is a page that is referred to by many good hubs and a useful hub is a location that points to many valuable authorities. Zin and Levene [33] propose that information on the topology is important for useful exploration and can also help to reduce the feeling of disorientation that users experience. They model a web database as a probabilistic grammar and present several algorithms to calculate its weights, expressed as probabilities attached to transition rules. Levene and Loizou [13,14] formulate a hypertext database as a graph and propose a probability approach to navigate in the graph to find the trail to match the query. They utilize both the theory of probabilistic automata and the theory of random Turing machines. Huberman et al. [10] assume that users make a sequence of decisions in proceeding from one page to another as long as the value of the current page exceeds some threshold, yields the probability distribution for the number of pages that a user visits within a given Web site. The model is verified by comparing its prediction with detailed measurements of surfing patterns and explains the Zip-like distribution in page hits observed at Web sites.

Reconfiguration concentrates on the automation, customization, and improvement issues. Goecks and Shavlik [9] propose a client-side approach to label Web pages so that an agent can automatically track the user's interests in browsing Web pages. Wang et al. [28] describe a personalized filtering model to record and update user's preferences on different products. Yan et al. [30] present a customization approach based on clustering technique to dynamically generate links for navigation. Perkowitz and Etzioni [20] describe two cluster-mining algorithms, called PageGather and IndexFinder, which automatically gathers Web pages that are not linked but related to the same topics in the mind of a user. Berghel et al. [3] present a web browser, “Cyberbrowser”, to extract keyword and customize the information access for the content within the web page. Teng et al. [26] propose a scalable method for parallel processing in both information crawling and processing. An automatic cyberspace partitioner is deployed to balance the traffic load dynamically among processors. Teng and Lu [25] propose a solution for access control problem in the case of a large number of users and access groups. The interval tree and a data structure in computational geometry are used to efficiently execute this access-right checking method.

Evaluation concerns the assessment and taxonomy of Web access. Bakos [2] describes the role of buyer search costs in markets with differentiated product offerings and analyzes the impact of reducing searching costs in the context of an electronic marketplace. Some insights obtained from the results are extensively explored in this study. Gilleson et al. [8] establish taxonomy of Web site traversal patterns and structures. Dhyani et al. [6] classify and discuss a wide ranging set of Web metrics for quantifying Web graph properties, Web page significance, Web page similarity, search and retrieval, usage characterization and information theoretic properties to improve Web information access and use. Nakayama et al. [16] address a technique that determines the gap between Web site designers' expectations and users' behavior, which is measured by the inter-page conceptual relevance and access co-occurrence. Joachims et al. [12] introduce the Web-Watcher, based on a learning approach with the user feedback to improve the quality of advice for interactive navigation. Similarly, Liaberman [15] introduces the intelligent agent, Letizia, which works with conventional web browser to keep track of the user browsing behavior and interests. Zhou et al. [32] use average connectivity to evaluate the Web site structure utilizing both the structure and user interaction information.

The literature review shows that most papers are based on the technical issues, static structure and system performance. There lacks discussion on requirement analysis of business applications, evolution of information demands, and balance between the designer's expectation and the user's desire. In addition, the improvement of Web performance is typically based on the assessment of a single measure (e.g. connectivity [32]) rather than tradeoff analysis among various measures based on structure properties and navigation patterns. This paper focuses on analysis of tradeoff between structure properties (such as the easiness of page access) and navigation patterns, and provides a guideline to evaluate page accessibility, to adjust site structure and to improve the performance of accessibility. Four accessibility models are defined to assess the easiness of page access. An innovative accessibility–popularity $( A { - } P )$ analysis framework is proposed to evaluate and improve Web structure. The analysis is similar to management of shelf space in a supermarket. The decision of location and size of shelf space for a particular product is based on both marketing strategy and sales feedback. The popular product or product to be promoted in a supermarket is usually displayed on the shelf that is easy for the customers to locate and retrieve. In a Web site, similarly, popular pages deserve high accessibility. The A–P analysis framework is mainly based on both push (e.g. demand driven) strategy and pull (e.g. design driven) strategy to keep the balance between accessibility and popularity for Web pages.

The paper is structured as follows. Definitions and notations are introduced in the Section 2. In Section 3, four exemplary accessibility models, formulated as graph traversal problems, are proposed to assess the readiness of page access. Section 4 focuses on the accessibility–popularity (A–P) analysis model to evaluate and improve Web structure. Details of guideline of the $A { - } P$ analysis model are also provided. A case study is carried out to demonstrate the application of accessibility model. The four accessibility models are compared and discussed in terms of their complexity and applicability in Section 5. The paper concludes with a summary and suggested future research direction.

## 2. Preliminary

## 2.1. Definitions and notations

A Web site consists of Web pages. Each page may connect to other pages through hyperlinks. The Web site can be modeled as a directed graph, G = (V, A).

Vertices $V { = } \left\{ \nu _ { 1 } , \ \nu _ { 2 } , \ . . . \nu _ { n } \right\}$ , where $\nu _ { i } ~ ( i { = } 1 , ~ 2 , ~ \dots ~ n )$ denotes a page.

Arcs $A = \{ [ \nu _ { i } , \nu _ { j } ] \mathrm { o r } e _ { i j } \mathrm { o r } ( i , j ) |$ the hyperlink from the source page i to the destination page j}.

Definition: The distance from page i to page j is the minimum number of links to follow in order to reach the destination page j from the source page i. The level of a page is defined as the distance from the root page. The level of the root page is 0, the level of the pages that are linked from the root page is 1, etc. In this paper, level and distance are used interchangeably.

Even though some Web site might have multiple root pages or entrant pages, only the cases with one root page are considered in this paper. For simplicity and clarity, some links, such as “go-back” links and cross-links, are ignored. The breadth-first search mechanism can be used to determine the level of all the pages on a site. Details about graph traversals can be found in Ref. [22].

Definition: The weight of a level reflects the importance or availability of that level. It can be defined as a function of the level as $w _ { l } { = } w ( l )$ , where l is the page level. For example, if $w ( l ) { = } 0 . 5 ^ { l }$ , the weights of level 1 and level 2 are $0 . 5 \ : \ : ( w _ { 1 } = 0 . 5 ^ { 1 } )$ and 0.25 $( w _ { 2 } = 0 . 5 ^ { 2 } )$ , respectively.

Definition: The impact of a link $( i , j ) , L _ { ( i , j ) } ,$ is defined as its priority or attractiveness in the source page. A link highlighted or at the top of the page has higher impact over others. For example, link impact is 1 for ordinary links and 2 for the blinking links.

## 2.2. Accessibility of web pages

It is more convenient for the user to access the page if more links are pointing to it. Hence, the total number of links pointing to the page is regarded as an important factor for page accessibility. The influence of individual link on the accessibility of destination page depends on both the impact of the link in the source page (e.g. how important the link is) and the level of the source page (e.g. where the link comes from). Taking into account of both the link impact and the level of source page, a tailored value called expected number of links (N ) is defined as a total weighted link impact. The $N _ { j }$ of the destination page j can be denoted as follows:

$$
N _ {j} = \sum_ {(i, j) \in A} \left(w _ {i} \times L _ {(i, j)}\right),\tag{1}
$$

where $w _ { i }$ is the level weight of the source page i and $L _ { ( i , j ) }$ is the impact of link $( i , j )$

Definition: The accessibility $P _ { i }$ of a Web page i is defined as the degree of easiness that the page can be reached via the other pages and hyperlinks.

The definition of “accessibility” here is different from that in “Techniques for User Agent Accessibility Guidelines $1 . 0 ^ { \dag }$ [27] by W3C. The premise at where the users start to navigate a Web site is the homepage. This may not be true since there are many other ways in which the users can find a site. According to a survey of ZDNet [31], the “word of mouth” is one of the most common ways that the users can find a site, and thus the start point to navigate a Web site is the homepage. The accessibility of a Web page is determined by several factors:

• The location of the page at the Web site.

• The properties (e.g. size) of the page and all other pages on the paths leading to it.

• The number of links in other pages pointing to this page.

• The properties (e.g. highlight) of the links pointing to this page.

The loading time of a Web page is roughly proportional to its size which has a much less dominant influence on the accessibility than other factors since the sizes of most web pages are relatively small [1]. In addition, such size introduction remarkably increases the computational complexity and workload. There are many other factors, such as page layout and data types (e.g. images, video clips, etc.), not considered in this paper.

One exemplary page accessibility $P _ { j }$ of page j is the expected link number $N _ { j }$ in Section 2.1 —

$$
P _ {j} = N _ {j} = \sum_ {(i, j) \in A} \left(w _ {i} \times L _ {i, j}\right)\tag{2}
$$

The definition can also be generalized as an increasing function with respect to $N _ { j } ,$ for example, $P _ { j } =$ $a \times N _ { j } + b ,$ , where a and b are constants.

In practice, the user may need to navigate several pages, and thus it is important to consider the accessibility of a group of pages. The group is simply regarded as a whole without considering the internal links in the group. Similar to the accessibility of a single page, the accessibility of a group is computed as follows:

$$
G = \sum (w _ {i} \times L _ {(i, j)}), \quad i \notin S, j \in S, (i, j) \in A\tag{3}
$$

where $w _ { i }$ is the level of the source page $i , L _ { ( i , j ) }$ is the impact of link $( i , j )$ , and S represents the set of pages in the group. The summation of accessibility of each page in the group results in an approximation of $G \mathrm { : }$

$$
G ^ {\prime} = \sum_ {j \in S} N _ {j} = \sum_ {j \in S} \sum_ {(i, j) \in A} \left(w _ {i} \times L _ {(i, j)}\right)\tag{4}
$$

In addition to the external links, $G ^ { \prime }$ might also include the set of the links that are internal to the group and point to any page in the group, so $G ^ { \prime } \ge G ^ { \prime }$

Suppose there is a Web site whose structure is shown in Fig. 1(a). Assume level weight function w(l ) equals $0 . 5 ^ { l }$ where l is the level of the source page, and all link impact (L) equal 1. The accessibility of node 3 is $P _ { 3 } = N _ { 3 } = 0 . 5 ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 5 ;$ the accessibility of the group {1,2,3} is $G = 0 . 5 ^ { 0 } = 1$ . Adding links may improve the accessibility as shown in Fig. 1(b). Now all nodes other than the root are at level 1. The accessibility of node 3 is $P _ { 3 } = N _ { 3 } = 0 . 5 ^ { 0 } + 0 . 5 ^ { 1 } = 1 . 5$ while the accessibility of the group {1,2,3} is $G { = } 0 . 5 ^ { 0 } { + } 0 . 5 ^ { 0 } { + } 0 . 5 ^ { 0 } { = } 3$ . There is a significant improvement in accessibilities due to link addition. In fact, it is intuitive that after adding the links, it is easier and more convenient for the user to visit the pages. The Web designer cannot continuously add links without any restriction. One extreme case is to add links to make the Web site become a complete graph so that a user can directly reach all other pages from any page. Clearly this is not practical, especially for large-scale Web sites. In addition, cramming the links into each page may cause a side effect on link impact. Therefore, the Web designer should set some constraints on the links. However, the constraints may change over time, which is not considered in this paper. The user may start navigation from a page in bookmark, search engines or other sites, where the start page may not be the homepage. All the accessibilities pages can be defined based on the new root page. However, in this paper, the main focus is the case in which the start page is fixed as the homepage.

## 3. Accessibility models

In this section, four exemplary accessibility models are proposed, namely Expected Link Number Model, Accumulated Accessibility Model, Sum of Distance Reciprocal Model, and Sum of Expected Distance Reciprocal Model, to assess the easiness of the page access. These models are based on Web structures and other properties (e.g. such as expected value, accumulation and distance). We discuss the corresponding definition, algorithm, and example for each model.

## 3.1. Model 1 — expected link number (EN)

The page accessibility of this model is mainly based on the number of links to the destination page, and it is defined as a simplified linear function of expected link number (N ) in Section 2:

$$
P _ {j} = N _ {j} = \sum_ {(i, j) \in A} (w _ {i} \times L _ {(i, j)}), j = 1,..., n\tag{5}
$$

where $w _ { i }$ is the level of the source page i and $L _ { ( i , j ) }$ is the impact of link $( i , j )$

To compute the accessibility, breadth-first search is used to compute the page level, level weight and link impact. Either breadth-first or depth-first search can compute the page accessibility as shown in Fig. 2. The linear function can be further simplified by letting level weight function $w ( l )$ equal $0 . 5 ^ { l }$ and all link impact L equal 1. An example of simplified model is illustrated in Fig. 3.

In a nutshell, the way a Web site organizes its pages resembles the manner a convenience store utilizes its shelf space. ## 4.1. Accessibility–popularity (A–P) analysis

The accessibility and popularity of a page should be co-related. “Popularity” of a Web page can be regarded as page importance, which can be measured in terms of “views” or “visits”, the number of times a page is viewed by users, or the number of times a page is retrieved in different sessions. Other measures include average length of time users spent on a page, or the number of visits of a particular page as a percentage of total visits of all pages of a Web site, etc. Since user preferences change over time, Web designers need to continuously examine the navigation behavior of users and its association with the Web pages.

The A–P (Accessibility–Popularity) plot illustrates the relationship between Web designer's anticipation and users' expectation as shown in Fig. 10. There are four zones in the plot, I, II, III, and IV, each represents a different group of Web pages. Zone-I stands for pages with high accessibility and high popularity which are the most preferable cases of A–P consistency in a Web site. The pages in Zone-II have high accessibility but low popularity, and they may be moved to the “corner” in order not to occupy too much of the “prime” area. The pages in Zone-III have low accessibility and low popularity; these pages are either newly launched for adjustment or obsolete for removal. Pages in Zone-IV have low accessibility but high popularity; their accessibilities should be enhanced.

In the A–P plot, the x-axis (accessibility) represents the direction that Web designers can actively maneuver and the y-axis (popularity) reflects changes resulting from navigation behavior of users. The evolution of pages on the A–P plot can follow either Push-strategy or Pull-strategy. In a Push-strategy, the evolution is based on user demand or popularity as shown in Fig. 11(a). The decision of accessibility adjustment is dependent on popularity that reflects the user preferences. The pages in Zone-I (high accessibility and high popularity) might gradually move into Zone-II due to decrease of popularity, where the designer should decrease the accessibility of these pages accordingly and move them into Zone-III for adjustments. Pages can be removed from the Web site after staying in the Zone-III for a certain period of time; alternatively, they may regain its popularity and be moved into Zone-IV, where the designer should increase accessibility of the pages and move them back into Zone-I.

In a Pull-strategy as shown in Fig. 11(b), the evolution is based on site design or accessibility. Increase in accessibility is expected to result in improvement in popularity. Pages in Zone- III can be promoted or consolidated by increasing their accessibility and thereby be moved into Zone-II, and they will be ready to be moved into Zone-I upon attracting attention and gaining popularity. Sometimes, the site designer may need to change the page portfolio (such as keeping the ten most popular or important pages) or split some pages to keep the accessibility/popularity balanced within the Web site. Pages in Zone-I might be moved to Zone-IV. Pages in Zone-IV may gradually lose their popularity and move to Zone-III for elimination. Both Push-strategy and Pushstrategy may serve as fundamental guidelines for Web management.

## 4.2. Investigation and application of A–P analyses

Basically there are two approaches to improve the accessibility of a Web page: (1) decreasing hierarchy of pages, or (2) increasing the number of hyperlinks connecting to a page. Generally, pages with high popularity enjoy priority for improvement in accessibility. The improvement procedure is a continuous process as popularity or “importance” of the Web pages changes over time. Accessibility is determined by a static view of the Web site structure while popularity is dependent on a dynamic aspect of user navigation stored in the log file. Improvement of a Web site is based on an analysis of relevancy between accessibility and popularity.

The diagonal connection between Zone-I and Zone-III is critical; it delineates a desired balance between accessibility and popularity. This diagonal link also provides a necessary baseline for assessing and adjusting page accessibility by Web designers. Each page of a Web site has a corresponding target reference point. As shown in Fig. 12, point A represents a spot for contents with both high accessibility and high popularity and, thus, may appropriately be considered for significant items or those worth of promotion. On the other hand, point D denotes an adequate placement of cold pages. A change in the horizontal axis reflects the designer's adjusting the existing page-organization structure, whereas a change in the vertical axis reveals shifting content-access behaviors by users over time.

Intuitively, we can examine accessibility of a particular page through the following process:

(1) Choose a target point on the balance diagonal, based on an initial accessibility value.

(2) Analyze users' content-access behaviors to assess popularity of the page and chart the page on the A– P plot, using the observed popularity and the initial accessibility. The resulting point suggests a new target reference point on the diagonal to which the designer should adjust the page's accessibility.

(3) Select a value between the original and adjusted accessibility as the new accessibility for the page.

To illustrate the above process, let us consider a newly launched page having an initial target reference point of 1 on the diagonal and an initial accessibility value of x as shown in Fig. 12. After a period of time, popularity of the page is observed to be at point 2, with the initial accessibility value unchanged. The new target reference point is point 3 on the diagonal, suggesting an accessibility value of y. In this case, the designer should select an accessibility value between x and y for this page. If the page is observed at point 4 on the A–P matrix, point 5 becomes a new target reference point, suggesting an accessibility value of z. Similarly, the designer should select a value between x and z as the new accessibility for the page. Central to the described A–P evaluation are selecting particular pages for the purpose of this analysis and using the result of this analysis for performance improvement, each of which is discussed as follows.

Three groups of pages are essential for the purpose of this analysis — those with (i) leaf nodes containing important information (e.g., product/service information); (ii) all nodes; and (iii) selected nodes based on (navigation) path, page level (e.g., general category versus specific product), or page function (e.g., hub versus authority).

Performance improvement may involve both critical and non-critical pages in the A–P analysis. Analysis of critical pages directly contributes to page-organization effectiveness; side-effects (such as noticeable changes in non-critical pages or constraint violations) should be avoided. General guidelines for performance improvement are as follows.

(1) Start with the most critical pages.

(2) Swap the pages in opposite directions on the A–P matrix.

(3) Follow a bottom-up approach; e.g., use deep leaves.

When applying the A–P analysis to improve the page-organization design, several issues need to be addressed, including hyperlink strategy, A–P threshold, and update policy. Hyperlink strategy can be classified as adding hyperlinks (AH ) or modifying hyperlinks (MH ). With the AH strategy, only additions of new hyperlinks are allowed. We often observe practical constraints on the (maximum) number of outgoing hyperlinks on a page. Page accessibility can be monotonically increased by using hyperlink additions. On the other hand, the MH strategy allows both addition and deletion of hyperlinks on a page. A–P analysis requires selecting threshold values for page accessibility and page popularity. These threshold values can be defined by using absolute and relative terms. An absolute threshold is based on some statistical analysis of accessibility of all the pages and visitors' content-access behaviors recorded in the log book, whereas a relative threshold is a normalized value derived from the absolute threshold.

Rule 2. Swap two pages in Zone-II and Zone-IV: Pages of high accessibility but low popularity (H–L) as well as those of low accessibility but high popularity (L–H ) are desirable targets for adjustments. Rule 3. Swap two pages on the same path through Zone-I and Zone-III: A popular page might be a derivative of its popular descendent page(s). That is, users must visit all the antecedent pages before reaching the target page; i.e., a popular descendent page. The popularity of a descendent page should not exceed that of its antecedent pages. Swapping these pages may avoid plausible popular derivatives. Pages in Zone-II and Zone-IV need to be projected to the diagonal path connecting Zone-I and Zone-III to select the appropriate accessibility for the observed popularity. This might not be obvious when the move is analyzed or executed using the original site graph. Resembling a continuous application of Rule 3, this rule swaps the pages on the same path. A horizontal move, on the other hand, moves a page close to a leaf node with the adequately projected accessibility. The described operation can proceed in a progressively interleaving fashion. A page can be moved down to a leaf node vertically and then horizontally to another leaf node with an accurately projected accessibility, and ultimately move up vertically to complete the adjustment. ## 5. Evaluation of accessibility

## 5.1. Comparisons of accessibility models

Though all four accessibility models can be applied for performance evaluation as illustrated in the case study, they are suitable for different cases based on performance tradeoffs in complexity and applicability. Complexity concerns the problem characteristics, such as monotonic property, algorithm complexity, adding links, adding node, and modification. Applicability concerns the structure types, such as multiple root nodes, multiple destination nodes, tree structure, acyclic directed graph and general directed graph. The following is the discussion on the comparison of four models in both aspects of complexity and applicability.

## A. Complexity

(1) Monotonic property. The monotonic property here mainly is the property of non-decreasing accessibility if adding a link $e _ { i j }$ to graph G without changing its structure, i.e. if $\mathsf { \bar { \boldsymbol { G } } ^ { \prime } } \mathrm { = } \boldsymbol { G } \mathsf { \cup } \boldsymbol { e } _ { i j } ,$ then $P _ { j ( G ^ { \prime } ) } 2 P _ { j ( G ) } ,$ where $P _ { j ( G ^ { \prime } ) }$ and $P _ { j ( G ) }$ denote the accessibilities of page j in $G ^ { \prime }$ and G respectively. Intuitively, EN model has monotonic property. The value of level (or distance) might change by adding links, and the change can only be decreasing. As a consequence, DR model has monotonic property. For AA model, it is also obvious that the accessibilities of page i, page j and all other pages cannot be decreased by adding links; however, it is not applicable for ED model. This property is limited to certain applications (e.g. navigation in the electronic catalogs) and is constrained by upper limit on the number of the links in a page.

![](/api/attachments/YXV6K2C7/fulltext/images/a634b0c2f346f5066086a340195b18d073eb907022171f1c10b748b10b934c99.jpg)  
Fig. 18. A–P plot of the example where $w ( l ) { = } 0 . 5 ^ { l } .$

(2) Algorithm complexity. Both AA and ED models need to solve longest path problems, so the algorithms are non-polynomial; whereas EN and DR models only concern breadth-first or depthfirst search, so the algorithms are polynomial.

(3) Adding links. EN model needs only to update the accessibility of destination page after adding a link. Similarly, DR model needs to modify accessibilities of both source and destination pages. The candidates for modification in AA model include all the pages directly or indirectly (propagationally) involved in the link addition. It might need to re-compute the accessibilities of all the pages for ED model.

(4) Adding nodes. EN model needs only to compute the accessibility of the new page. Both AA and DR models are required to update all the successors of the new page. It might need to recompute the accessibilities of all the pages for the ED model.

(1) Multiple root pages. All four models are applicable to a Web site with multiple root pages. A vector of levels (or distances) and accessibilities is required for each root page. (4) Acyclic directed graph. All four models are applicable.

(5) Cyclic directed graph. Since both EN and DR models are dependent only on level (or distance), they can work for the Web sites with cyclic directed graphs.

## 5.2. Application mapping of accessibility models

The complexity implies the dynamic requirement of Web site whereas the applicability reflects the static requirement of site structure. We can further classify complexity issues into scale and stability and, applicability issues into search and structure as shown in Table 3.

(1) Scale (small, large). The scale of a Web site concerns the monotonic property and algorithm complexity which is about the computational requirement for the accessibility models. Web site varies from small scale applications (e.g. personal home page) to large scale ones (e.g. product information of multi-national corporate)

(2) Stability (low, high). Stability refers to the volatility of the Web site. The changes include link addition, page addition, and modification. High stability indicates constancy of the Web site and low stability means the frequent adjustment is required.

(3) Search (single, multiple). Single search involves only one destination page and multiple search engages more than one. This may also include the cases of multiple root pages in the Web site.

(4) Structure (simple, complex). The structure concerns the degree of the complexity of the Web organization. Both tree and acyclic directed graph are simple structure and cyclic directed graph is defined as a complex structure.

## 6. Conclusions

In order to design and maintain a friendly Web site, Web designers need to decide how to adjust the Web structure in a dynamic environment. In this paper, we propose several accessibility models to measure the degree of easiness for users to retrieve the Web pages in navigation. An accessibility model can be adopted to provide an overview of site structure, to improve the page organization, and to enhance the retrieval performance. The A–P (accessibility and popularity) plot shows the A–P classification of the pages in a Web site to guide Web designers to manage the site by either Push strategy (e.g. popularity leads accessibility) or Pull strategy (e.g. accessibility drives popularity). A case study is carried out to demonstrate the application of accessibility model. A comparison based on the complexity and applicability for four accessibility models is made to illustrate suitability of applications. Advantages of the guideline in the proposed framework are summarized as follows —

(1) Flexibility. The guideline is based on the tradeoff between accessibility (e.g. structure property) and popularity (e.g. user preference) rather than on the assessment result of one specific factor. In addition, the tradeoff criteria can be tailored based on the application requirements. Furthermore, various accessibility models can be devised for different applications.

(2) Scalability. The linear or monotonic properties of accessibility models imply the high scalability. In addition, the push and pull strategies can focus on the applications of longer time horizons.

(3) Applicability. The application suitability maps the accessibility models to various Web applications based on the requirements of scale, stability, search, and structure. The guideline and the accessibility models are applicable to a wide range of applications.

(4) Details. The guideline provides thorough explanations and step-by-step procedures with examples in addition to the general rules of improvement.

The evaluation strategy proposed in this paper mainly focus on the dimension of evaluating the page accessibility (i.e. the easiness of page access). Extensions can be made on the proposed framework to take into account other dimensions. For example, page size can be easily incorporated to the framework in Sections 2 and 3 to denote the information richness or volume. Other potential directions for extension are as follows —

(1) Evaluation measures. Explore other evaluation measures (e.g. relevancy, timeliness and attractiveness) and extend the A–P analysis to a higherdimension framework.

(2) Optimal accessibility. Define and analyze the optimal accessibility of a page, group of pages, and a Web site. Investigate the impact of the update of links and pages on accessibility.

(3) Popularity extension. Extend definitions of the popularity to include both time aspects (e.g. length of visit session) and temporal aspects (e.g. path analysis of page predecessors/successors).

(4) Accessibility extension. Generalize definitions of weights (e.g. dynamic weight and page weight) and accessibilities (e.g. normalized accessibility and link accessibility) for further extension of accessibility models.

(5) Model comparison. Consider other comparison criteria (e.g. runtime performance) in addition to complexity and applicability.

(6) Semantic relationship. Include conceptual network framework for the requirement of semantic relationship between pages. The semantic relationship may impose constraints on rules of improvement (e.g. page swap).

(7) Performance evaluation. Analyze the discrepancy between the perceived performance from subjective analysis (e.g. user satisfaction) and system performance from objective assessment (e.g. accessibility).
