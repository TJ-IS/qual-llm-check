# Research on radical innovation implementation through knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow: A case study on academic teams

Jianyu Zhao<sup>a,</sup>\*, Xi Xi<sup>b,</sup>\*, Baizhou Li<sup>a</sup>, Tienan Wang<sup>b</sup>, Hang Yin<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Economics and Management, Harbin Engineering University, Harbin, PR China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge reuse Knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow Radical innovation Space Time Mechanism

## A B S T R A C T

To better understand and apply knowledge reuse through knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow when radical innovation is expected, we select an extremely important reuse knowledge industry, i.e., academic research <sup>fi</sup>eld, and adopt the “spatiotemporal” perspective to analyze the principles and processes of knowledge reuse. We have divided knowledge reuse into four stages, namely, knowledge search, knowledge evaluation, knowledge recombination, and knowledge creation, thereby developing a knowledge reuse mechanism model centered on these stages. This study di<sup>f</sup>ers from the previous ones primarily in our adoption of the new perspective in knowledge management research. and dynamically analvze knowledge reuse in the context of knowledge flow

## 1. Introduction

Radical innovation is a kind of creative innovation behavior that involves discontinuous development where unprecedented improvements or performance features are achieved [1]. In the era of knowl edge economy, radical innovation has become the most desirable, valuable, and strategically- signi<sup>fi</sup>cant innovation way for organizations or innovators. It has been noted that radical innovation would cause great di<sup>fi</sup>culties for innovators [2], who would not possess all the knowledge [3], and, at the same time, would take much time and re quire high costs to create all knowledge by themselves. In this context, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, the process by which an entity’s knowledge is captured and shared (Szulanski, 2000), becomes the precondition for innovators to implement radical innovation [4,5]. However, Majchrzak et al. [6] indicated that knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow is not a su<sup>fi</sup>cient approach when the objective is radical innovation because radical innovation not only requires the acquisition of knowledge through knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow but also needs to apply and even recombine specialized knowledge across a variety of sources. Consequently, given that knowledge reuse can provide the innovators with the combinative capabilities to apply existing knowledge in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations [7], it plays a critical role in radical innovation.

In this paper, we focus on knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow for the purpose of facilitating radical innovation. From a knowledge reuse perspective, reuse for radical innovation is the exploitation of existing diverse knowledge previously unknown to the innovators when creating the development of radically innovative solutions. Nowadays, a growing body of research has gradually paid attention to the issue of how knowledge reuse a<sup>f</sup>ects the radical innovation ([6,8]; Szulanski, 2000; [9–11]; Alavi and Leidner,1999; [12];[13]). Notwithstanding, prior literature has opened new horizons and enhanced our understanding of radical innovation from the perspective of knowledge reuse, and the four important respects have largely been ignored in the existing literature.

First, much of the literature has ignored the potential impact of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow on knowledge reuse. Because innovators may face the challenge that inventing their own innovation solution was deemed too expensive due to severe time and/or cost constraints [6,8], thus, reuse existing knowledge become an e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ective approach for achieving the purpose of radical innovation. Innovation, particular radical innovation strongly relies on the high-value tacit knowledge. However, the inexplicable characteristics of tacit knowledge lead to the circumstance that individuals cannot exert the high value of tacit knowledge for innovation through knowledge replication. In this context, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow provides an e<sup>f</sup>ectual means which provides the opportunity to access the reusable knowledge for innovators (even unknown or untraditional knowledge), and increases the common cognition of conveying information and clarifying tacit knowledge’s e<sup>f</sup>ect through discussion and interpretation [14]. Therefore, Roper et al. [15] suggest that there is a pressing need to explicit potential impact of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, because that it may help innovators deeply understand the meaning of reusable tacit knowledge and then choose knowledge reuse for radical innovation. However, extant literature has paid much attention to the content and form of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, instead of analyzing the e<sup>f</sup>ect of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. To address this limitation, drawing on the view of Hautala and Jauhiainen [16] that “the best innovation progress is the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in space and time,” we use a spatio-temporal perspective along with the theory of cognition to explicitly consider the particularly important role played by knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow.

Second, the theoretical principle of knowledge reuse is still being under-researched. Szulanski (2000) emphasized that knowledge reuse replication (KRR) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the reusers through the process of replicating the essential elements of the source’s knowledge. Although the framework of KRR is helpful for transferring ambiguous, incompletely codi <sup>fi</sup>ed, and complex knowledge, it may be unsuitable to analyze knowl edge reuse characterized by the di<sup>f</sup>erent principles. Actually, any knowledge is likely to be reused [17], and thus, it is necessary to con<sup>fi</sup>rm under what situations (whether there is divergence and shared experience), and what types of knowledge (is it familiar or from other domain) have the reuse and adapted potentiality for radical innovation [7]. Unfortunately, the existing literature has failed to analyze the principle of knowledge reuse for radical innovation. To address these gaps, several theoretical principles were proposed through strict case summary. Such principles are able to analyze how knowledge is reused and the characteristics of knowledge reuse to implement radical innovation.

Third, although the mechanism model of knowledge reuse has been stressed in Majchrzak et al. [6], studies mainly concentrate on the contents, determinations, and performance outcomes of knowledge reuse. Very few literature has delved into the general mechanism model based on the knowledge reuse process. Radical innovation does not have an initial purpose and re<sup>fl</sup>ect signi<sup>fi</sup>cant discontinuity, leading to opportunity recognition, idea generation, and solution design, which do not occur at the given location as in incremental development [1,18] but occur sporadically throughout and often in response to unanticipated situations [19]. Therefore, di<sup>f</sup>erent from incremental innovation, the mechanism model of knowledge reuse for radical innovation may have certain particularities, which distinguish it from the regular stage of the KRR model (Szulanski, 2000) or knowledge transformation of the SECI model [20]. Hence, developing this model is valuable to gain a deeper understanding of the role of knowledge reuse in radical innovation. In this paper, we establish a general mechanism model based on the complete knowledge reuse process to explain the nature of knowledge reuse for radical innovation.

Fourth, in contrast with the profusion of conceptual research, the existing literature has o<sup>f</sup>ered only limited empirical evidence on the mechanism and process of knowledge reuse in knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Much empirical works are conducted using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c contextualized or small sample or as in-depth research within a few organizations or industries. In fact, although the model of knowledge replication for innovation (KRI) has been used as the subject of many theoretical studies, it remains empirically under-researched. Further, when it comes to the more general, advanced theories, a common state of frustration seems to prevail. For instance, many famous researchers have suggested that the most theoretically interesting research is that requiring broad stu dies of nontraditional and under-researched <sup>fi</sup>elds (Szulanski, 2000; [17]). Similarly, Zhang and Li [21] reported that there is a clear need to <sup>fi</sup>nd a general conclusion to guide practice. Ruiz et al.,further empha sized that empirical research on the rules of knowledge reuse should track the complete process and present persuasive conclusions and evidence. In response to these critical observations, we chose to in vestigate this valuable and under-researched empirical subject to depart from speculative grounds and provide empirical support for knowledge management (KM) research.

We establish a spatio-temporal framework and combine with the cognition of innovators to analyze the process of knowledge reuse. This framework is a new perspective on the KM to reveal the mechanism of knowledge reuse for radical innovation and is valuable for identifying how knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in<sup>fl</sup>uences the dynamic process of knowledge reuse. This framework addresses three points simultaneously: First, knowledge reuse is connected not only to linear time but also to rela tional experienced time. Second, knowledge reuse has a clearly causal logical relationship in time and space. Third, within the dynamic process, the reused knowledge should be searched, evaluated, and recombined in space and time. In particular, we engage in answering the three central questions: In the context of the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in space and time, how does knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow promote knowledge reuse? What are the principles and processes of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow? And the most important one, what is the general process of knowledge reuse to achieve radical innovation?

Following the introduction, the second section introduces our theory. In the third section, the data and methods of the empirical study are presented, followed by the empirical analysis and results in the fourth section. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section draws conclusions about the results and contributions.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Knowledge reuse based on time and space

According to the KM theory, when knowledge reuse is taken to be the behavior of innovator searching, reconceptualization, and reutilizing existing knowledge through knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow [8], time would be a condition and implicit assumption through which innovators promote the advancement of radical innovation [16,22,23]. In the KM theory, time includes linear time and relation time. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of linear time focuses on managerial time-lagged representations of the knowledge processes [24,13]. For example, the cases, investigations, and interviews that are usually answered by the researchers or managers and collected after the results of innovation processes are known to be in linear time. Therefore, linear time is usually regarded as the underlying basis in research that analyzes knowledge process problems, in other words, conducted studies on “here and now” processes of KM [25]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from linear time, relational time is the beginning and ending time when the knowledge unit moves, representing a certain time region where knowledge unit transfers between di<sup>f</sup>erent innovators [16,26], and the integration and recombination of several knowledge units as well as sequences of created knowledge [27]. Some scholars described relational time as “chances to acquire knowledge” [5], which is because relational time would make innovators feel an obvious time movement [20]. Therefore, relational time, where there are time region limitations, would challenge the seeming universality of linear time [26]. However, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow always occurs among innovators unintentionally, therefore, linear and relational times are not always clearly separable.

In the KM theory, space can be divided into interior and exterior space. Exterior space grants the reuse of knowledge of its basic conditions and primarily focuses on the geographical proximity between innovators (Coccia, 2008; [28]). Exterior space is the basis for knowledge production, and the proximity of space determines whether the two parties of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow are close in geographical distance [29] and whether the exterior network that might be formed is able to promote knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Interior space emphasizes the interaction between innovators, and it is taken as an area of exchange and collision between thinking, knowledge, and ideas of innovators ([30];[31,32]) as well as the “venue” in which innovators search for, evaluate, and recombination knowledge. In the KM theory, the notion of interior space is evidently derived from the “Ba” of knowledge generation and inter active space, developed by Nonaka and Konno [25]. Although geographical proximity (exterior space) alone is not enough to foster knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow or any other knowledge activities, it facilitates other important forms of proximities (Boschma, 2005) and allows them to share high-value knowledge within a closer distance, even face-to-face [3], hence helping the <sup>fl</sup>owing knowledge enter into the interior space. Generally, the interior space consists of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge, and it is a place where knowledge units communicate and recombine with each other there. Consequently, interior space is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the <sup>fi</sup>eld environment to explain how innovators reuse knowledge to implement radical innovation.

In studies on knowledge reuse for radical innovation, time, space, and knowledge are connected by knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, ordinarily expressed within linear or relational time. Knowledge reuse is the method adopted by innovators to search for, evaluate, and recombine knowledge within a given space, thereupon lead the innovator to create new knowledge. Therefore, knowledge reuse exists as a pathway for radical innovation situated in the intersection between the integration and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of space and time, occurring during knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. This intersection consists of two important aspects: First, knowledge reuse is the process of innovators relying on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow to acquire and recombine existing knowledge within a given space in linear or relational time [26]. This process may not follow the sequential order of distinguishing opportunities and the dialectic process of knowledge transformation (Szulanski, 2000; [20]), because knowledge reuse connects the past, present, and future, and permeates the entire process of innovative activity and not merely the beginning and the end. In this process, relational time consumed by knowledge reuse may decide the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of knowledge acquisition and recombination by innovators within a given space [33]. The second aspect is that the use of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow stands as the crux of knowledge reuse. This kind of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow can not only overcome the constraints of geographic or space distance to acquire previously unknown or nontraditional reusable knowledge for innovators but also increase the shared experiences to improve the understanding and facilitate quicker access to existing knowledge be tween the source and the recipient. Therefore, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow is usually regarded as a necessary condition for knowledge reuse, which is a bene<sup>fi</sup>t for innovators to overcome geographical constraints and space distance to build a common cognition [16].

Time, space, and a variety of knowledge are connected mainly as “spatio-temporal.” The result of knowledge reuse depends on the in terconnectedness of time, space, and knowledge. Conversely, scattered time-space is perhaps not conducive to innovators in their location and utilization of reusable knowledge during the process of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow [34]. “Constructionists” who subscribe to the cognitive theory not only support the “spatio-temporal” notion of knowledge reuse but also emphasize that time and space cannot be separated during the process of knowledge reuse. The KM theory holds that the process of knowledge reuse exists within a demarcated spatio-temporal region. During the process in which procedural and dynamic “<sup>fl</sup>ow”, but not “transfer,” is emphasized, innovators, based on temporal sequence, re<sup>fl</sup>ect the power dynamic between “source” and “recipient” with regard to orienting, integrating, and utilizing knowledge within a given space. As long as innovators choose to reuse knowledge, they will in<sup>fl</sup>uence and interact with other innovators and spaces [35], making space a determinant factor for knowledge acquisition and recombination brought to bear by knowledge reuse [36]; therefore, knowledge reuse takes place in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c “spatio-temporal” region.

## 2.2. Spatio-temporal of knowledge reuse based on cognition

To reveal the nature of knowledge reuse, the cognitive theory serves as a reference. The cognitive theory is used to analyze and explain judgments and thinking patterns formed depending on the cognition, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and understanding of people to objective things [37]. In our study, the greatest strength of the cognitive theory rests in its applicability to explain the e<sup>f</sup>ects and value of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. According to the cognitive framework, knowledge reuse belongs to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of knowledge cognitive result as proposed by Grant [38], because the most important indicator of whether innovators will reuse knowledge rests squarely in their own cognitive judgment as to the value of reuse knowledge. Therefore, in the bidirectional transfer of directional knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, knowledge reuse is often thought to increase with shared experiences and similar cognition between the source and the recipient. The innovators’ perception and judgment toward interactive targets, as well as knowledge, determine the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, tendencies of knowledge reuse, and its contribution to radical innovation.

According to the cognitive theory, knowledge reuse is a decision made by innovators, which they continuously deliberate during the process of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow based on their cognitive structure and mental models [39,40]. If innovators judge that their present knowledge content can satisfy new demands, then knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and the established scope thereof will be restricted to their present reciprocal knowledge targets or knowledge network resources [1]. In this situation, knowledge reuse exists as the knowledge acquisition and replication possessed by reciprocal knowledge targets. When innovators believe they must produce new knowledge for radical innovation, knowledge reuse becomes the pathway by which innovators, relying on obtaining heterogeneous, scarce resources from knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, bring about development in unfamiliar <sup>fi</sup>elds of knowledge [41]. Furthermore, radical innovation primarily involves the <sup>fl</sup>ow of high-value, tacit knowledge, and such knowledge is likely nonencoded and complicated. These characteristics require innovators who choose to utilize knowl edge <sup>fl</sup>ow, which are su<sup>fi</sup>ciently able to both catalyze the <sup>fl</sup>ow of tacit knowledge and assist their own understanding of tacit knowledge [42,43]. Such methods assist innovators in connecting and interpreting the logical results and connections of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, as well as elucidating hidden meaning within reuse knowledge; therefore, knowledge reuse takes the <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge between innovators as its precondition.

In the framework of the cognitive theory, knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow can explain the three interconnected space dimensions of knowledge: distributed cognitive space, situational cognitive space, and valued cognitive space. When innovators conduct innovative ac tivities in any given <sup>fi</sup>eld, their choice to reuse knowledge is primarily centered within the distributed cognitive space, in which they go through the process of perceiving, understanding, and deliberating on scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological research. Innovators also undergo the process of searching, evaluating, and recombining various knowledge scattered throughout this space. Analyzing encountered di<sup>fi</sup>culties during the implementation process, deciding on strategies of knowledge reuse, and explaining knowledge reuse occur within the innovator’s situational cognitive space and bear the classic code of possessing a temporal sequence [44]. Whether the reuse of knowledge results in the attainment of satisfactory performance depends on the judgment made in the subjective value space. Consequently, knowledge reuse based on cognition is a procedural experience that occurs within the bounds of time and space, as well as during the interaction between innovators and knowledge [43]. Take, for example, the case of an innovator tackling a di<sup>fi</sup>cult task, in which the innovator relies on reusing knowledge, situated within knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, to handle the task. As the innovator “innovates,” he or she also experiences a sense of accomplishment at having created something [45]. Making the innovator rely on or tend toward reusing transmitted knowledge during future practical production processes is precisely the result of cognitive procedural experience.

Furthermore, in the linear and relational time of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, innovators, following progressive improvements to their cognitive structures and mental models, make increasingly clear judgments regarding the value of knowledge within a given space. This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst pathway whereby the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of knowledge reuse is increased. Evidently, knowledge reuse not only exists within the space where knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows between innovators but also draws upon innovators’ personal cognitive structures or is interpreted by language as innovators communicate and discuss among themselves [16]. For instance, through mutual exchange and discussion, innovators can rely on cognition from their personal mental models to complete their con tinual evaluation of the research project status, projected timeline, and problems encountered, as well as the technology and market thereof [19]). Such a process is essentially the combination of cognitive space and time-space. Therefore, whether on a microscopic, individual-based scale or an intermediate, team-based scale, cognitive structures are the basis on which innovators within a given space choose to reuse knowledge, weigh the feasibility thereof, and execute strategies. Si multaneously, the shared mental models based on the cognition of innovators are also important to support innovators in relational time, as they become closer and more familiar with each other. Such support may in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence the occurrence of knowledge reuse.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Case selection

Our case studies center around an important and very common, yet extremely understudied micro <sup>fi</sup>eld of knowledge reuse: academic research. We try to adopt a more normative and rigorous case study to prove how the knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow a<sup>f</sup>ects and implements radical innovation. There are four primary reasons for this. First, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in academic research is very frequent; even more frequent is the reuse of knowledge by researchers during innovational activities. Second, in every case in which innovators focus their attention on singular “points” and in which there exist cognitive di<sup>f</sup>erences, it is possible to distinguish and analyze di<sup>f</sup>erent performances of different innovators during radical innovation. Third, academic research can avoid environmental factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge reuse. Fourth, academic research is a <sup>fi</sup>eld of knowledge research in which knowledge is interconnected to a relatively high degree, and radical innovation in academia is currently facing new and previously unseen challenges.

The scope of the present research is limited to management science and features a major program (MP) and two general programs (GP) funded by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC), in addition to a key project (KP) funded by the National Social Science Foundation of China (NSSFC). In all four of these radical innovative case studies of knowledge reuse, the population of cases has met the analytical demand of controls for extraneous variation [46] and con trolled the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the knowledge reuse task di<sup>f</sup>erential [47]. In ad dition, MP-NSFC, GP-NSFC, and KP-NSSFC all stand as projects of the highest quality within Chinese academia. The managers of these cases are very respected and trusted scholars, who have controlled for the e<sup>f</sup>ects of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences and scholastic issues relating to radical innovation [48]. In the process of receiving these projects, a<sup>fi</sup>liated scholars were required to <sup>fi</sup>rst participate in many competitive, jurybased selection processes, which demands that project managers and their teams possess outstanding scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and innovative ability before they proposed radical results with regard to actual issues. Thus, we are con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the scholars engaged in the four cases are specialized in developing concepts that have not been used previously, satisfying the context conditions of analysis on knowledge reuse proposed by Majchrzak et al. [6,8]. Finally, this study utilizes interactive evaluative methods taken from project managers, research observers, as well as the managers of other projects to establish a plan to guarantee the evaluative objectivity of these four cases in terms of their degrees of radical innovation, reliability of results, and methodological fairness. Basic information of these four case studies is given in Table 1.

We used the guidelines for theory-building case study research proposed by Eisenhardt [46,49], the guidelines for hypothesis-generation case study research o<sup>f</sup>ered by Klein and Myers [50], and the requirements of ethnographic research methods (Schultze and Boland, 2000) to complete the research design. The detailed steps are as follows:

1. De<sup>fi</sup>ning research questions (getting started): Used the contents and constructs of theoretical literature in <sup>fi</sup>elds such as the KM theory to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and delineate research questions and theory.

2. Case selected from speci<sup>fi</sup>c population: The cases involving reuse of knowledge, which was initially unknown to the innovators and that led to radical innovation were considered. Selected 4 successful knowledge reuse cases where con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the solutions being continued into development was high. The number of the 4 cases fell well within the recommendation of Eisenhardt (1989) that 3–10 cases were required for theory building, and case researchers possessed a high degree of connection to knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, and research questions belonged to the same academic subject. Simultaneously, the degree of radical innovation of the 4 cases was strati<sup>fi</sup>ed by four indicators, namely, the degree of discontinuity in form, <sup>fi</sup>t, or function [6]; pub lication level; citation of publications in the next year; and project achievement score.

3. Data sources and investigators: Identi<sup>fi</sup>ed cases with archival data and analyzed the fundamental structure and logic of archival data re-<sup>fi</sup>nement research questions. The main role of the researchers in the cases was to act as participant-observers (Burgess, 1987), conducting regular interviews with each project manager and associated team members (there were no fewer than four interviews in each case), and requiring that interview respondents explain their understanding and practice of research results.

4. Data input: Searched and integrated repositories of interview transcripts and data from literature in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and regularly accessed the implementation of cases to con<sup>fi</sup>rm whether additional cases or interviews were required to obtain more data.

5. Data analysis: Set up analytical catalogues for each case, through which research sta<sup>f</sup> – after analyzing the results for every interview – sent research conclusions to respondents by email. The research personnel kept their promise of maintaining strict con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and required the respondents to report their progress, results, and experience from the research project. This provided the foundation for the next level of analysis. Each interview cycle lasted for four months.

6. Re<sup>fi</sup>ning de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of constructs: Following the continued progress of case analysis, the research sta<sup>f</sup> frequently discussed hypotheses about possible logical relationships, which made up research questions and utilized additional cases to replicate or modify hypotheses, advance discussion, and improve initial research conceptions.

7. Comparison of literature: Compared the results of case analysis with those in the current literature on KM, organizational management, and innovational theory, and examined the reasons for divergence between <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

8. Reaching closure: Matched summary records for the 4 case analyses with the re<sup>fi</sup>ned conclusions of the research personnel and inter preted to participants not involved in analysis and ended the research with 4 cases.

## 3.2. Methods and data collection

A variety of methods have been applied to the spatio-temporal perspective in the research on knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. This study is rooted in the use of literature tracking methods to re<sup>fi</sup>ne ideas, with which we designed the timeline for each case analysis to generate archival data. We have followed the case design rule proposed by Eisenhardt [46] and referred to the timeline design method proposed by Hautala and Jauhiainen [16]. First, according to the project research progress submission time of the 4 cases (December 10 to December 31 each year), we divide the time of projects combining with the research content of each project. Second, we shelter the key problems to be resolved in the project research content proposed by the project holders, building on tradition in the innovation literature of using retrospective tracer studies, and choose the interconnection part that is close to both the former and later parts to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the time axis (to be divided into months). Third, according to the review of documents (AO, <sup>fi</sup>nal proposals, e-mails, and project notes), as well as interview data in each case, we form the timeline of each case. Finally, according to the research situation of the case, we improve the behavior marking method proposed by Szulanski (2000) and marked each stage in the timeline as: “Beginning,” “Discussing and allocating,” “Preliminary achievement,” “Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant achievement,” and “The next longitudinal project.” Further, combining with the spatio-temporal marking method of Hautala and Jauhiainen [16], we have marked the radical innovation behaviors caused by knowledge reuse (taking the innovation results being accepted by the top 15 % international journals in the <sup>fi</sup>eld as standard). To facilitate the comparison and analysis, we also marked the “small innovation,” “important event,” and “Associate innovation.” The selected cases are executed for long enough (at least 3 years), and there are enough similar behaviors to be marked; therefore, cases are able to be sorted according to their sizes, and eventually, a timeline with stage marks and innovation marks can be obtained, which can meet the requirements of the comparative analysis of a normative case study [49].

## 4.1. Principles

Radical innovation is a challenging proposition to the implementation of academic research projects and a mandatory reason for innovators to reuse knowledge. Although the project managers and researchers in our study broke down their projects into continuous stages which ran in terms of relational and linear time because of the overlap between the three types of cognitive space, knowledge reuse was not the primary motivation underlying knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in any of the cases. Rather, the primary motivation was the methodology chosen by researchers to resolve problems when they encountered obstacles or attempted to de<sup>fi</sup>ne problems during projects. It was necessary to set an implementation phase for projects, regardless of whether the solution would eventually be reused or newly designed. Then, in the time-space stage of the implementation process, we used points in relational time to explain the principles of knowledge reuse, which occurred during the time-space stage. These points represent the sequential direction of stage transformation during the project and bind time and space together.

First, knowledge reuse often occurs in the discussion and allocation stages, as well as those that follow. This shows that following their involvement in the implementation of the project, the researchers gradually formed clear awareness and judgment as to whether to reuse knowledge, when to reuse knowledge, and the value of reusing knowledge. This means that the reuse project begins not with the formation of a “seed,” as suggested by Szulanski (2000), but with a de<sup>fi</sup> nition of the problem. Consequently, we assert that the judgment and decision making of researchers in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research projects are extremely important. These factors determine, in relational time and the 3 overlapping types of space, whether researchers will select knowledge reuse as their innovational strategy and means of problem solving during the project. For instance, in Case 1, which displayed the highest levels of innovation, in the initial phase of implementation, the researchers attempted to create a new model that was entirely suitable for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research institutes and subsequently used this model to explain the hierarchical roles in innovation in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research in stitutes. However, during the course of the project, they gradually realized that the traditional knowledge they commanded was unable to serve as a model, and if they continued to use such a model, then it would consume an unpredictable amount of time and energy. To solve this problem, the researchers spent nine weeks searching for related theoretical literature and utilized analogies from the interdisciplinary theory to expand the lines of thinking established in their model. One example is Mindruta’s (2013) value creation matching model, which borrows the co-evolutionary theory from biology to provide analogies for development in industry and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research institutions and use case analysis to verify the value and feasibility of established models. This kind of analogy spurred researchers, making them aware that knowledge reuse possesses a high value for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research projects.

Second, after analyzing the interviewees in the 4 cases, it is found that di<sup>f</sup>erent from the conclusions of Szulanski (2000) and Majchrzak et al. [6] on knowledge reuse, innovators would be faced with a choice when they spurred by knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of academic research:

to create a solution with the knowledge in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (they all think that it is the top choice for managers) or to create a new solution by reconstructing problems with knowledge that may be reused. Innovators of the 4 cases said that they all admit that they may fail to master interdisciplinary knowledge totally (nontraditional management <sup>fi</sup>eld, or knowledge that has not been mastered by them) because knowledge is complex. However, if they insist to develop a solution with their own traditional <sup>fi</sup>eld knowledge (management <sup>fi</sup>eld), the cost to create the solution is the highest (especially Case 1 and Case 2), and there is also a risk that the problems cannot be solved. In contrast, reusing knowledge to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne questions and subsequently implement innovational methodologies were not only just as e<sup>f</sup>ective but also saved substantial time and resources. As said by the project manager in Case 1,

If we develop and learn knowledge in the field of management further, we would enter into an unsolvable dilemma, and it would ask cost, and we had to finish the project with high eficiency within the shortest time. When the innovators realize that they cannot solve the problems with knowledge in the traditional field or methodologies from the same discipline and fail to achieve the expected innovation, they would admit that they cannot create a solution with their theory and methods within their field; therefore, they would choose knowledge reuse.

Third, reusers in all 4 cases indicated that participating in knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow allowed them to select knowledge reuse more easily than thinking individually. Considering that actions of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow can contract the distance between subjective space [53,54], we posit that within linear time, as distance between spaces contracts, the likelihood of researchers conceiving ideas of knowledge reuse increases, and the space becomes more conducive to both the reconstruction of problems and the search for knowledge. This is because the concentration of heterogeneous knowledge is higher and the characteristics of exchange between di<sup>f</sup>erent types of knowledge are more explicit in time-space in which the spatial distance is relatively short. Such characteristics not only increase the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and scope of researchers’ knowledge sear ches within distributed cognitive space but also help researchers form clearer and more accurate situational cognitive judgments toward obstacles encountered during projects, thereby stimulating them to reuse nontraditional or non<sup>fi</sup>eld methodologies capable of bringing about higher-level innovation. At the same time, in time-space, where the spatial distance is short, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow between “source” and “recipient” is more direct. Such time-space is also where researchers can, to the greatest possible degree in overlapping space, synchronize temporal trajectories and reduce loss or interpretive bias to heterogeneous knowledge caused by complications and spatial-temporal di<sup>f</sup>erences. As expressed by the project manager of Case 3,

I encourage and am willing to provide knowledge flow requirements for the project team. On the one hand, we have team members engaging in research in diferent areas and fields. When we decide to solve problems by exploring, positioning and reusing others’ knowledge, the spatial scope of exploring reusable knowledge is enlarged by heterogeneous knowledge transported in knowledge flow. On the other hand, our team members are able to explore and filter knowledge quickly and accurately with the help of idea collision generated from knowledge flow, thus locating the reusable knowledge used for solving problems within the space in the shortest period.

Further, reusers point out that although knowledge reuse would involve certain heterogeneous knowledge in other <sup>fi</sup>elds, and the incomprehension of knowledge maybe lead to ambiguity in knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in the initial stage, the ambiguity would not bring further e<sup>f</sup>ects on knowledge reuse. Reusers in the 4 cases <sup>fi</sup>gure out 3 common points: <sup>fi</sup>rst, they are all researchers who have good scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge and rather mature knowledge system; moreover, they are all experienced in innovation (responsible for top tier scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research and publishing papers in top journals in their <sup>fi</sup>elds), and they are able to make decisions on the application of di<sup>f</sup>erent heterogeneous knowledge in existing problems. Second, although the <sup>fl</sup>ow of heterogeneous knowledge, especially that of interdisciplinary heterogeneous knowledge, may cause di<sup>fi</sup>culty to the implementation of the project initially, making participators negative to the expected innovation degree, and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in the team is the basis for them to acquire heterogeneous knowledge. Team members would help reusers understand and judge knowledge, and seek help from experts in this <sup>fi</sup>eld to explain the heterogeneous knowledge completely and carefully. It would not only bring con<sup>fi</sup>dence to reusers to carry out assumption and reconstruct heterogeneous knowledge but also provide a challenging assumption. Third, although there are several knowledge reuse providers in each case, and their di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge may lead to heterogeneity, these di<sup>f</sup>erences would not cause paradox in knowledge content because the heterogeneity is caused by di<sup>f</sup>erent roles of knowledge providers. Therefore, reusers are able to comprehend the implicit conceptual dif ference of reused knowledge to reduce ambiguity and potential risks. Reusers in Case 2 have commented on the ambiguity caused by het erogeneous knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow:

We got an idea to reuse knowledge after discussion and communication, although knowledge is heterogeneous, and beyond our understanding, they make us feel hopeful to solve problems. We have got new ideas, which are helpful for us to solve problems in the future and to save time, money, and vigor.

## 4.2. Process

Fig. 2 shows the knowledge reuse process based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. After collecting and analyzing the data of respondents’ opinions from the four cases, we divided the process into four stages: knowledge search, knowledge evaluation, knowledge recombination, and knowledge creation.

## 4.2.1. Knowledge search

Searching for knowledge in relational time and cognitive space is the precondition for innovators to choose knowledge reuse as their means to implement radical innovation. After rede<sup>fi</sup>ning project problems and associated notions, innovators form time-space networks with the help of analogy, metaphor, or expansive methods and turn knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow into a pathway for searching and re<sup>fi</sup>ning reusable knowledge [16]. This is because when obstacles are encountered during projects, researcher discussion, interpretation, utilization, and updating of obstacles can be taken as one “node” together [55]. The temporal connection of nodes is related and linear, whereas space represents the researchers’ collection of knowledge, information, and content of multiple nodes. Therefore, time-space turns the <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge into a network. The meaningful combinations of communicative and cognitive spaces produce di<sup>f</sup>erent strategies in time. For our respondents, should they wish to achieve their goals of attaining radical innovation, they must search for many types of strategies to help them interpret and solve problems and, depending on the knowledge of cognitive results, select knowledge that best allows them to solve present problems. In this moment of search, knowledge networks formed by knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow serve as knowledge search platforms, which contain many nodes of knowledge. On these platforms, researchers can actively distinguish knowledge within these networks and clarify, evaluate, and analyze that knowledge, after which they adopt actions corresponding to the knowledge. Thus, this network of time-space is a product of the process of knowledge search. For example, in the computing simulation process of Case 3, innovators, including the project manager, had acknowledged that they had lack of computing knowledge utilized in addressing key problems of the project. This was largely due to their engagements in the innovation management. To achieve a high-quality project, they had to overcome the liability of knowledge in the computing <sup>fi</sup>eld. They began to search for a wider spectrum of knowledge and proactively communicated with group members. Finally, the cross-time and space knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow networks were preliminarily formed though the knowledge interaction between inter-group space. They tended to narrow the search, as they had gradually identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of visual simulation method of ABS based on R-language in overcoming the challenges they encountered. Moreover, they tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd examples that had been successfully addressed by this method because of the more time, cost, unexpected outcomes and harmful academic reputation associated with adopting this method. To accurately analyze the applic ability of R-language, the project manager provided multiple forms of commutation opportunities and environments for their group members and professors in R-language research, such as direct discussions or video conferences. By doing so, the complexity of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow networks increased with the involvement of new members and more intensive knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows, then facilitating innovators to understand and improve the reuse knowledge of R-language. After team meetings and communications for 14 times, the innovators mastered related knowledge, whereby the developing bundle of the project became clearer.

## 4.2.2. Knowledge evaluation

Knowledge evaluation is ordinarily the foundation for innovators searches for knowledge, as well as the pathway-taking process for re-<sup>fi</sup>ning the knowledge of innovators within the space of knowledge networks in relational or linear time [16]. In contrast to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Majchrzak et al. [6] on KRI, we <sup>fi</sup>nd out that researchers in academia are more circumspect in their evaluation of knowledge, because they not only value their academic reputation but also try to o<sup>f</sup>er the greatest bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the project through reuse knowledge. As the respondents indicated, the researchers put forth an evaluative metric based on their own metacognitive knowledge, composed of <sup>fi</sup>ve aspects, as their mental framework of the metaknowledge of reuse.

After this, the researchers decided whether to introduce and im prove accuracy (whether the expressed knowledge was complete, and whether the establishment and derivation of the corresponding models were precise and accurate, and whether the application of knowledge and models was achieved in similar domain), learnability (whether they could, with relatively little cost, grasp a kind of knowledge or core theoretical content; that is, the degree to which knowledge reuse can be translated or replicated), improvability (whether a given theory or method could be appropriately modi<sup>fi</sup>ed given the situation and problems of our research, includes the speci<sup>fi</sup>c contents and approaches which need to be improved), relevance (degree of interrelatedness in terms of conception, form, or characteristics to avoid scholastic di<sup>f</sup>er ences and prevent confusion), and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness (the time when the knowledge was proposed, and whether a given knowledge had recently been used by other scholars, and the degree of innovation that the scholars achieve through using the given knowledge). Researchers not only adopted methods of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, repeated discussion, analysis, and examination to determine whether the theories could help them solve problems but also focused on the ways in which they should adopt or introduce a given theory and methodology (such as hiring personnel in the <sup>fi</sup>eld to help them, using their own learning to solve the problem Sor cooperating with third parties to complete the project). For instance, during the process of evaluating theories and methodologies in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of chemistry in Case 4, the project manager evaluated knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow as follows:

We acquire knowledge from other fields that might help us to solve problems through a wide range of knowledge communication and transfer. Some of the knowledge has been introduced in relevant studies, but some ha never been used in our specialty. In accordance with our perception and past experiences with similar issues, we realized that if it is proved to be reasonable and correct in strategic alliances research to introduce the knowl edge, we may draw a highly valuable and innovative series of conclusions. Therefore, we need to specify the restrictions and context of the projects, thus evaluating the basic models, data and adaption dificulty of these methods. Actually, we do not think much of completely or partly mastering these methods; we only hope that if time and energy permit, we can continuously acquire theoretical models of the reusable knowledge, thus solving our problems.

As the decision stage, knowledge evaluation was necessary to set the state for radical innovation, regardless of whether the decision would eventually be reuse knowledge or not. We have summarized the di<sup>fi</sup>- culties encountered by researchers in four cases as they evaluated knowledge reuse: Although knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in continual, linear, and relational time o<sup>f</sup>ered researchers a knowledge network of a larger spatial scope, which helped them obtain more knowledge to reuse because of the large variation in the types of such knowledge, as well as their inadequacies and inexperience in this academic <sup>fi</sup>eld, they were forced to spend a relatively large amount of linear time evaluating the feasibility and value of the knowledge. This process is subject to the constraints of cost, time, and learning ability. First, to solve problems (such as direct cooperation) and pay overly expensive fees (such as those associated with hiring expert help), many team members were inclined to abandon their conceptions of knowledge in the given <sup>fi</sup>eld, as those conceptions would limit their use of funds in other ways. Second, if it took a long time to change such knowledge, then, considering meeting project time constraints, the researchers may have abandoned the reuse of knowledge in the given <sup>fi</sup>eld as a means of solving the problem. Third, recognition of the learnability of reusable knowledge also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced researchers’ selection and evaluation of knowledge. Reusers believed that as the learnability of knowledge in creased, it became easier to combine their ideas with such knowledge to the e<sup>f</sup>ect of realizing radical innovation. In generally, the innovators needed to balance the apparent and potential paradox of knowledge reuse.

## 4.2.3. Knowledge recombination

Even though researchers re<sup>fi</sup>ne reusable knowledge by way of evaluation, problems can still occur in knowledge networks when researchers reuse knowledge during projects. Thus, researchers must integrate and bundle di<sup>f</sup>erent nodes of knowledge content in time-space, turn “Is it possible?” into “It is possible and reasonable,” and thereby complete the challenge of reusing interdisciplinary knowledge. It is at this point that every researcher in the team should set a clear target, explain the content in every node of knowledge, and ultimately choose the appropriate pathway to facilitate the assimilation of di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge nodes within time-space.

We have discovered that the more similar the cognitive space of researchers (researchers possess similar experiences, thinking, or goals), the more conducive the space becomes to the formation of mutual understanding and to the rapid, high-quality integration of information and content from di<sup>f</sup>erent nodes of knowledge to realize innovation. Four respondents from Case 3 expressed that their team experienced failure due to knowledge reuse because reusers were unable to reach a consensus with the project manager in any cognitive space. Reusers’ ideas neither formed new bundles of knowledge nor focused on co-development in solving imminent problems. After becoming aware of this problem, they chose to abandon the idea and began reevaluating other possibilities for reusing knowledge. Afterward, they chose to hold a symposium to more closely interpret reusable re<sup>fi</sup>ned knowledge. They also invited two respected scholars in the <sup>fi</sup>eld to attend program discussions, analyze whether their method was appropriate to the project, and examine the size of any risks or possible issues they might encounter. Lastly, all project administrators reached a consensus to knowledge reuse, solved all problems related to the integration of di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge nodes within networks of knowledge, and opened up new pathways to realizing project goals.

The adapters of concepts of knowledge were critical during the process of knowledge recombination. In Case 1, which displayed the highest levels of innovation, the associate professor explained, “I am willing to reuse knowledge to better complete the project, but I must use knowledge that has already been adopted, regardless of whether I or specialists in the associated <sup>fi</sup>eld adapt it.” This viewpoint indicated that if knowledge is to be reused, then the adapters need to be clearly identi<sup>fi</sup>able. However, we found that even though researchers utilized the <sup>fi</sup>ve criteria described above to evaluate reusable knowledge, the lack of appropriate methodologies to evaluate adapters and the e<sup>f</sup>ects of improvement (such as reasonability, reliability, accuracy, and suitability), especially in academic research, could create di<sup>fi</sup>culties in the process of judging knowledge reuse. First, even if reusing knowledge can help researchers realize radical innovation and obtain valuable results, such <sup>fi</sup>ndings can still fail to be recognized by other scholars, who may believe that tapping into knowledge of other <sup>fi</sup>elds is neither rigorous nor sound. As a result, researchers may abandon the adapta tion and application of interdisciplinary knowledge. On the other hand, di<sup>f</sup>erent from Majchrzak et al. [6] who have selected the intersections of specialized knowledge within our chosen academic discipline as the target of their case analysis in the manufacturing industry, it is found that interdisciplinary research did not make themselves create com monality. Consequently, if researchers choose to collaborate with scholars from other <sup>fi</sup>elds, they could lack the appropriate means to con<sup>fi</sup>rm whether the improved knowledge is truly applicable to solving their problems.

Additionally, although researchers are una<sup>f</sup>ected by passive factors, they will consult previous cases when making decisions during the process of knowledge recombination. Successful cases will motivate them, spurring them to <sup>fi</sup>rmly believe that reliance on knowledge reuse can result in new perspectives and methodologies, which will thereby resolve encountered obstacles. Consistent with the views of Markus [7], we have further discovered that within relational time, reusers who have been spurred, including colleagues with nontraditional and non-<sup>fi</sup>eld backgrounds and knowledge networks of scholars, will spontaneously expand the scope of the space of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and refer to the past methods of integrating interdisciplinary knowledge. This expansion e<sup>f</sup>ectively realizes the “restructuring between di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge” proposed by Majchrzak et al. [6] and lowers the risk generated by knowledge reuse during the project’s relatively short linear time. If the previous case failed or they accepted high amounts of risk, then they might abandon innovative and risky pathways of knowledge integration. Note, for example, how reusers in Case 4 abandoned op portunities to integrate informational <sup>fi</sup>eld knowledge because they discovered that the risk of this method could lead to the discontinuance of the project.

## 4.2.4. Knowledge creation

We have discovered that during the process of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, time produces visible <sup>fl</sup>uctuations. We take the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance in which innovators encounter problems during project implementation as our starting point, from which innovators pass through a stage in which knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow pauses (Fig. 1). Borrowing the insight of Hautala and Jauhianinen [16], we have labeled this stage as “disrupted time-space,” which is described as “interrupted” during project implementation. This is because the researchers must rely on methods of knowledge reuse by rede<sup>fi</sup>ning problems they encounter to create new knowledge with which to solve problems, while perfecting, grasping, and utilizing such knowledge. We maintain that in the cognitive perspective, discovering and accurately recognizing “disrupted time-space” are of paramount importance. Regardless of whether the researchers successfully utilized reused knowledge to solve problems by the end of the project, they were virtually unaware of the passage of time, as long as they were within this stage of concentration and creativity. Furthermore, after they communicated, searched, integrated, and absorbed di<sup>f</sup>erent scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge, their existing cognitive space expanded. In this expanded space, recombining di<sup>f</sup>erent information, knowledge, and content can lead researchers to generate new cognition and judgment toward problems and establish a new system of knowledge structure. If they succeeded in solving problems, then “disrupted time-space” served as the key to produce radical in novation within the projects. In the weekly research journal of Case 2 (week 7, number 22), knowledge reusers who realized radical innova tion described this sort of disrupted time-space as:

In this stage, I was wondering about an exciting but uncertain and unexpected result. I thought I should use the data mining method from the theory of precision instrument control and calculate the fitness in every cluster. I was working day and night, which was a dificult but fun work because the result is significant for our team to continue (to the next stage). When I was finished, I sighed. The time had gone by surprisingly quickly and I remembered only the time when I prepared to begin (my work). But more importantly, from the result, I discovered a peculiar phenomenon, and I began to analyze the reasons and the project was disturbed. At last, I got four propositions from the results and improved the cluster mark. Right now, almost all my partners are using this mark to calculate the processing of the agent clustering in knowledge and social networks.

“Disrupted time-space” is a critical stage in which researchers carefully examine knowledge. In Case 1, which displayed the highest degree of innovation, the project manager expressed,

Although my team members have solved the problems with interdisciplinary knowledge, we have to recheck the processes and results many times, which is much more detailed, prudent, and comprehensive than using traditional management methods to acquire the research results. We have obtained a very interesting conclusion, but only after checking the reasonableness, scientificness, and universality could we publish our conclusion in journals and proceed to the follow-up research. Once our method is questioned or proved to be wrong, not only will our research sufer attack, but our reputation and ability will also be damaged.

In addition, disrupted time is the critical period – of great value to researchers – in which they overcome setbacks and “the dark side.” We found that in academic research, researchers not only attempted to rely on reusing knowledge to solve problems of innovation but also hoped to understand and completely grasp knowledge from other <sup>fi</sup>elds as well as produce new knowledge. This is because such knowledge can serve as inspiration, which can assist the researchers in publishing articles or applying to projects in high-level publications. However, knowledge reusers in all four cases stated that studying interdisciplinary knowl edge was extremely di<sup>fi</sup>cult and that such di<sup>fi</sup>culty could lead to feelings of failure, angst, or despair. Despite this, they would continue to work hard as they studied and searched, as they were subject to the pressure of meeting project deadlines. At the same time, being researchers, they saw setbacks as crucibles of self-transcendence and thus possessed su<sup>fi</sup>cient motivation and a mindset active enough to overcome present challenges and produce outstanding knowledge within this stage. Fig. 1 shows that the time-space of knowledge reuse in the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow process supports radical innovation and creative knowledge in disrupted time, indicating that these disruptions were important for the knowledge creation in the project:

If it is valuable for my present work to improve and reuse interdisciplinary knowledge and create new knowledge, I will take the initiative to do this. Although I am not skilled in this area, and some of the knowledge is new to me, I am willing to understand and learn. Besides, I do not think of it as extra work, as I could fulfill my stage objective. Therefore, I have passion toward this work and try to complete it with high quality. If I succeed, I think I could not only get recognition from my colleges and peers but also master a brand-new method as well as ability, which is of great importance to my future research.

## 4.3. Discussion

From the results of the three-years follow-up-analysis in all four cases, we have broken down the process of knowledge reuse in the spatiotemporal perspective into <sup>fi</sup>ve steps:

a. Encountering di<sup>f</sup>erences: reconstruction of the concept of the problem

b. Knowledge research: analysis and communication of the theories and methods that may solve the problem; analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of traditional KM and other scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theories of knowledge.

c. Knowledge evaluation: further screening of searched knowledge according to the <sup>fi</sup>ve criteria.

Fig. 3. The mechanism model.

d. Knowledge recombination: de<sup>fi</sup>ning screening pathways in knowledge networks and, in conjunction with the problems of the project itself, assimilating knowledge content from di<sup>f</sup>erent nodes of knowledge.

e. Knowledge creation: comprehensively developing new, novel, and high-value knowledge that can solve problems and realize radical innovation.

In the spatio-temporal perspective, the process of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow dynamically dictates that knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow must face uncertainty; however, we can construct a clear and complete model of the fundamentals of knowledge reuse (Fig. 3) around these <sup>fi</sup>ve stages. According to this model, innovators can accurately grasp and control the meaning of each stage to lower risk and improve the stability of radical in novation.

Although radical innovation is essentially unique, non-linear, and even non-sequential, we have utilized four cases from academic <sup>fi</sup>elds focused on radical innovation to obtain certain characteristics and rules of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in the spatio-temporal perspective, which present opportunities to improve the KM theory.

In choosing the new “spatiotemporal” perspective to analyze the principles of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, our research not only improves theories of knowledge reuse and innovation proposed by Szulanski (2000) and Majchrzak et al. [6,8] but also improves the universality of conclusions in existing research and explains, from the perspective of a dynamically driven process, the appearance of behavior in innovators at di<sup>f</sup>erent stages during the process of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Moreover, we have successfully integrated relevant models of knowledge reuse, eliminated barriers of di<sup>f</sup>erence that exist between the KRR and KRI models, and proposed new models of the principles of knowledge reuse, which are based on heuristic cases and in accordance with a new fundamental model of knowledge reuse in innovative environments.

Reconstructing the concept of encountered obstacles is the begin ning of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in the spatiotemporal perspective – an observation consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of existing research. Even though our research shares many similarities with the conclusions of other scholars’ work, there exist some di<sup>f</sup>erences, which we obviate by o<sup>f</sup>ering an alternative and more generative theory of building explanations for these di<sup>f</sup>erences:

First, our research does not have a <sup>fi</sup>xed or established typology of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow (in every case, there are di<sup>f</sup>erences between problems encountered by researchers and knowledge that is reused). We also establish the characteristics of knowledge source as critical in<sup>fl</sup>uences that determine the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of reuse. Because these characteristics can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the direction of knowledge search, accuracy of knowledge evaluation, fairness of knowledge integration, and methods of knowledge creation, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research di<sup>f</sup>er from those of Majchrzak et al. [6,8]. We maintain that emphasis should be placed on the entire reuse process, not only on search and evaluation, and that regardless of whether they surface during the initial or <sup>fi</sup>nal stages of the project, the characteristics of knowledge source are of the utmost importance to realize radical innovation.

Second, our research di<sup>f</sup>ers from the previous one in that we focus on the process of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. We innovatively use the concept of "time-space” to utilize “dynamic knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow” to visualize the implied contextual hypothesis of knowledge reuse. Majchrzak et al. [8] believe it to be the translation of knowledge, but we take it to be bidirectional knowledge transfer in which there exists feedback. By means of visualization, we have discovered that in relational time, not only does knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow spur innovators to choose knowledge reuse, but the direction and range of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow in-<sup>fl</sup>uences knowledge reuse. These discoveries are seldom touched upon in current scholarship despite their signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value to furthering the development of the KM theory.

Third, we consider the dimensions of time and space both si multaneously and explicitly and unify the behavior of innovators. In accordance with process – driven thought advocated by Majchrzak et al. [6], we divide the process of knowledge reuse into stages and analyze the lifespan of the entire reuse cycle. We have distinguished the spatial and temporal characteristics of knowledge reuse, researched the com plete circulative process – from the innovator’s searching for knowledge to his or her creation of knowledge – which occurs in spaces of knowledge (knowledge networks) in relational time and investigated innovators’ performance within di<sup>f</sup>erent procedural stages. We have veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that the closer the cognitive spaces of innovators are to each other, the more conducive the spaces are to reusing knowledge and understanding and integrating knowledge from a given discipline. Further, we point out that improvement to concepts of knowledge (or chosen improvers) has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on the results of reuse. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings have not been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in practice in existing research.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings di<sup>f</sup>er from existing ones in that we have discovered that knowledge reusers not only focus on the replication and improvement of knowledge, but also emphasize their understanding and comprehensive learning of reused knowledge. The evidence rests within the <sup>fi</sup>ve criteria with which we evaluate knowledge reuse: accuracy, learnability, improvability, relevance, and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Of these criteria, learnability, improvability, and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness have not been touched upon in prior research; however, they are important factors that must be considered in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of KM and innovational research [56,57]. We believe that the reason for these di<sup>f</sup>erences may rest in the selection of di<sup>f</sup>erent practical research objectives as well as in challenges raised by the unique environment of academic research with regard to future examination of these propositions. However, although these <sup>fi</sup>ve criteria that we have found are not highly contextualized, they are of critical importance to comprehensively evaluate metaknowledge and potentially reusable ideas and reduce the unpredictability and risk in reusing knowledge.

Finally, we have veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that innovators impacted by routine knowledge reuse are more able than unin<sup>fl</sup>uenced innovators (those who have no experience in reusing knowledge) to select knowledge reuse to spur innovation. This is because the sharing of experience, history of reuse, and other cognitive routines of reuse run through and are e<sup>fi</sup>cacious during the entire reuse process. This runs counter to the beginning stage proposed by Szulanski (2000) and the comprehensive initial phase proposed by Majchrzak et al. [6], suggesting that routine knowledge reuse, from start to <sup>fi</sup>nish, in<sup>fl</sup>uences innovators’ mental models (primarily in problem rede<sup>fi</sup>nition), behavioral tendencies (primarily during knowledge search and evaluation), tendencies (primarily in integrative methods), and the methodologies and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of innovation. This discovery can serve as the basis for improving existing models of knowledge reuse.

This study also possesses substantial practical implications. First, from the perspective of KM, our research helps innovators during practical productive processes to more objectively recognize knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Our research veri<sup>fi</sup>es that to realize innovation (especially radical innovation), innovators must learn nontraditional and non<sup>fi</sup>eld knowledge more broadly and actively carry out activities corresponding to knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, such as brainstorming or attending research conferences. Second, in terms of organization management, we deem that organizational managers should attach more importance to the motivational energy of innovators and encourage them to realize self-transcendence. At the same time, they should pay attention to the creation of collaborative team atmospheres and environments, and actively provide innovators with relevant training. In particular, our suggestion for managers is to foster a team or organization that is conducive to knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, to help innovators to establish an attitude to spur innovation, and to establish a norm and perceived behavioral control that facilitates knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. It is a key element for them to improve their relationships, improve their identi-<sup>fi</sup>cations between each other, and choose the reused knowledge provided by the other. Further, innovators should consider broadening their social networks and maintaining positive relationships with research personnel and colleagues from di<sup>f</sup>erent disciplines because such steps are a part of reusing knowledge practically. Third, in the view of information technology and management, our research has provided reference for managers and innovators to establish and choose communication modes and reused knowledge that are suitable for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and knowledge reuse. It is fully illustrated by our research that an e<sup>fi</sup>cient information communication is conducive to conveying knowledge between members quickly and precisely, which is of great importance for innovators to select reused knowledge. Therefore, our suggestion for managers is to choose proper information communication equipment according to the knowledge structure of team or members, to provide a more extensive and practicable platform to share knowledge, to establish an arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligent knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow system of information technology so as to promote knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and improve knowledge reuse selection accuracy.

## 5. Conclusions

The objective of this study is to increase our understanding of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and catalyze research into the KM and innovational theory. We take the combination of the three dimensions, namely, time, space, and knowledge, as the foundation of this study and analyze the fundamentals of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, with radical innovation being the target. We explained the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of di<sup>f</sup>erent behaviors of innovators as well as the scope of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow, characteristics of knowledge source, and cognitive similarity on knowledge reuse through a dynamic, multistage process. Furthermore, we discovered the special roles of knowledge reuse routines in knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow as well as the value of disrupted time-space to the ability of innovators to realize radical innovation. Finally, we proposed a comprehensive model of the fundamentals of knowledge reuse into which existing research models can be integrated. The importance of such a model to spur theoretical development cannot be overstated.

This study makes two main contributions to the research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. First, we utilize the framework of space and time to analyze the process and principles of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow through a dynamic multistage process, overcoming complicated pitfalls in the comprehensiveness and complexity of understanding knowledge reuse in traditional scholarship, and thereby o<sup>f</sup>ering insight for future research. The proposed research framework should prove useful to scholars working under limited cognitive conditions, who face complex problems related to knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow itself a<sup>f</sup>ects the result of knowledge reuse, thus further clarifying the e<sup>f</sup>ect of knowledge reuse on radical innovation.

Second, our research is di<sup>f</sup>erent from the past ones that were relatively narrow in their KM research process. To support our research, we utilized case analysis and selected highly important scholastic disciplines in which reuse is frequent. Our study not only uncovered several major in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors of knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow but also expanded the analytical perspectives, methodologies, and corresponding models in existing theory; both factors are imperative to further developing research on knowledge reuse. At the same time, even though the process of knowledge reuse has already been partially veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by scholars, our process-driven research – in which we have divided knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow into stages and pointed out several special behaviors in innovators at each stage of reuse – provides many insights for organizational managers.

In conclusion, we have aimed to investigate a relatively undeveloped spatio-temporal perspective and set of circumstances under which we utilize a dynamic, integrated, and procedural perspective to research knowledge reuse based on knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow. Subsequently, we seek to explain in depth the ways in which knowledge is reused, which serve the function of connecting theory to guided practice. In future studies, we will conduct follow-up research in di<sup>f</sup>erent <sup>fi</sup>elds, utilizing our proposed theoretical model to analyze the characteristics of knowledge reuse in every industry, while also continuing to further improve our models.
