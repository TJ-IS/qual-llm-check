# Designing scalability in required in-class introductory college courses

Gabriele Piccoli<sup>a,b,</sup>\*, Marcin Łukasz Bartosiak<sup>b</sup>, Biagio Palese<sup>c</sup>, Joaquin Rodriguez<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, USA

<sup>b</sup> Department of Economics and Management, University of Pavia, Pavia, Italy

<sup>c</sup> Operations Management and Information Systems, Northern Illinois University, DeKalb, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital innovation Socio-technical artifact Design science research Information systems scalability

## A B S T R A C T

We posit that design science enables the creation of in-class introductory college courses that can scale to large numbers of students, under resource constraints. We build on the centrality of human interactions in learning environments and conceptualize a college course as a socio-technical (ST) artifact. Grounded in the intervention theory, we draw meta-requirements guiding the design of college courses that leverage IT to scale, while maintaining the centrality of the professor s role. We use the design-build-evaluate cycle to instantiate the ST artifact and demonstrate its feasibility using evaluation episodes as prescribed by the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science Research

## 1. Introduction

There is little doubt that digital technology has transformed society as we know it. Over the last few decades, digital innovation was in strumental in creating new products and services, new business models, and value creation paradigms. More broadly, it helped to transform entire industries [1,2]. However, digital innovation and digital trans formation engender unintended consequences and risks that are understudied by Information Systems (IS) research [3]. In this article, we turn our attention to a wicked problem [4] confronting higher educa tion: the need to balance e<sup>fi</sup>ciency with quality.

As a result of <sup>fi</sup>nancial pressure, many universities have increased classroom size and substituted online courses for campus-based activ ities. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOC) and Virtual Learning Environments (VLE) [5] have proven to be e<sup>f</sup>ective alternatives for students in remote locations [6], students who cannot a<sup>f</sup>ord traditional education [7], students who just need to refresh their academic background [8], or learners in nonclassroom environments, such as museums [9]. With respect to class size, recent research converged toward the consensus position that the learning and teaching experience degrades as the student-instructor ratio increases. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, at the college level, larger classes are associated with lower subject matter mastery, as measured by grades [10], particularly for students who are at the top of the grade distribution [11]. Furthermore, interaction and engagement decrease and students in large classes remain anonymous, leading to lower motivation for both faculty and students [12].

Two decades ago, Peter Drucker famously proclaimed: “Thirty year from now the big university campuses will be relics. Universities won't survive. It's as large a change as when we <sup>fi</sup>rst got the printed book. […] The college won't survive as a residential institution. Today's buildings are hopelessly unsuited and totally unneeded” [13]. Drucker pre<sup>fi</sup>gured a time when learning would be divorced from classroom settings to Information Technology (IT), rather than physical spaces, which would be the enabler of a college education. While we will not be able to claim that Drucker was wrong in his prediction for another ten years, enrollment in degree-granting postsecondary institutions rose from 13.8 million in 1990 to 21 million in 2010, and it is projected to grow to 22.6 million in 2026 [14]. More importantly, there is a growing consensus in the literature on e<sup>f</sup>ective college education that points to human relationships between peers and between the instructor and the students as the catalyst for high-quality college education [15–17]. The most e<sup>f</sup>ective teachers carefully motivate student learning by articulating and reinforcing the “payo<sup>f</sup>” of knowledge acquisition while giving students a sense of control over their achievement [18]. However, larger classes limit human interaction that teachers traditionally leverage to motivate students, leading to regression toward using grades as a motivator. The lack of human interaction also fosters strategic learning – which occurs when students focus “primarily on doing well in school, avoiding any challenges that will harm their academic performance and record” ([18], p. 34).

It is not the objective of this research project to compare the merits of online and in-class education. Rather, the point of departure for our work is the centrality of human interactions in learning environments. Conceptualizing a semester-long college course is an act of design – “engineering an environment in which [students] learn” ([18], p. 49). Thus, our work is primarily concerned with socio-technical (ST) artifact design and scalability, rather than learning and pedagogy. Thus, our work tackles the challenge of designing a required in-class introductory college course that can scale to large numbers of students, under resource constraints. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our work is motivated by the need to deliver an introductory IS course to prepare about 1,000 business college freshmen per year.

As demonstrated by previous research [19], the problem-solving paradigm of design science research (DSR) [20] is well suited to investigate optimal solutions to the class of problem we are addressing. Motivated by the centrality of human interactions, we conceptualize the crafting of a university course as the design of an ST artifact [3], an assemblage substantiated by the interplay of IT, people, processes, and organizational structures. For those universities where the pressure on e<sup>fi</sup>ciency is irreversible, we submit that DSR enables IS scholars to uncover and test IT-enabled course designs that optimize the seemingly competing pressures of quality and e<sup>fi</sup>cient college-level education. Building on the Design Science Research Methodology [21], and in keeping with the most recent DSR guidelines, we contend that the design, implementation, and evaluation of the artifact presented here is a substantial research contribution [22].

The paper is structured as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst frame and problematize the class of problems we are focusing on. We then introduce our kernel theory [23]: intervention theory. Next, we articulate a set of meta-requirements (MR) and design principles (DP) drawn from the kernel theory, as applied to our speci<sup>fi</sup>c class of problems. We then discuss the ST artifact and describe its implementation. Building on the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science Research [24], we adopt the human risk and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness strategy to evaluate the design and show that the artifact can be constructed, that it will work, and that it will be used by the intended audience.

## 2. Problem de<sup>fi</sup>nition

Our work focuses on a standard introductory course in a large state business school in the United States of America. The course is titled “Introduction to Management Information Systems” and it is mandatory for all <sup>fi</sup>rst-year business and economics majors. According to the de scription, the course covers “the role of IT in the business including the development and use of IS, hardware, and software, the strategic impact of IT for businesses and the nature of the IT career.” Since its inception in the mid-1990s, the course also had a practical component focused on pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency with individual productivity tools (e.g., Microsoft Excel). Over the last <sup>fi</sup>ve years, an average of 1563 students per year enrolled in the course [25]. A similar course is on the books at most major undergraduate business programs.

Over the last decade, the course increasingly migrated from the classroom to online delivery. Resource constraint, rather than pedagogical superiority, was the primary driver of this decision. After some experimentation, the department settled on a major vendor system with the following features:

Online Books: All content is delivered online.

Virtual Microsoft O<sup>fi</sup>ce environment: No software installation re quired; a simulated application runs in the Web browser.

Assignments: The instructor assigns speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills for the students to practice as graded homework and quizzes.

Gradebook: Assignments are automatically graded, and the in structor receives a standardized report.

Search: Users can navigate directly to content referring to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c skill.

Videos and interactive “Guide Me” pages: The screen captures vi deos that demonstrate how to complete individual skills.

The application automatically grades the work students perform in the simulated environment. It can also evaluate “projects” – structured assignments the student completed using the actual software application (e.g., Excel). As documented elsewhere [25], the introduction of the system resulted in positive intended consequences and some negative unintended consequences. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the migration to online delivery brought increased e<sup>fi</sup>ciency – de<sup>fi</sup>ned as student throughput per section. These e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies translate into salary savings exceeding \$5.5 million between 2001 and 2016. While it is hard to assess the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of the course without formal evaluation, instructors in dicated that an in-class delivery “is better” from a pedagogical standpoint, but infeasible with large sections [25]. Among the primary limitations of online delivery, the instructors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the inability to illustrate particularly di<sup>fi</sup>cult concepts, to respond to questions in real time, and to convey tacit knowledge (e.g., tips and tricks) that improve students’ e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. They also lamented the inherent limitations of using a simulated software environment, rather than having students practice directly in the software (e.g., Microsoft Excel). More surprisingly, the online delivery engendered some unintended consequences: role reversal, the minimization of human interaction, and strategic learning [25].

Role reversal refers to the “digitization of the professor” whereby the “course solution,” over time, disrupts the role of the teacher and takes over fundamental teaching activities. As the department gravitated increasingly toward online delivery, the control of both the con tent and the pedagogy shifted to the digital “course solution.” Practical skills (e.g., Microsoft O<sup>fi</sup>ce) became predominant over the theoretical concepts listed in the course description and the instructors were relegated to the activities of course administration and support.

Minimization of student interaction refers to a course design that steers students toward online resources rather than fostering human interaction with faculty members. In the course, only 6 % of enrolled students visited an instructor for at least one face-to-face meeting. All other communication occurred through email, with 54 out of 163 students sending at least one message. Thus, 66.9 % of students never interacted with a faculty member at all (all those who interacted face to face also sent email communications). Furthermore, 71.2 % of messages pertained to administrative and procedural questions (e.g., “I was wondering [about] the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the \$135 price and the \$180. What comes with each price?”), 26 % of the messages pertained to software issues (e.g., “I uploaded my <sup>fi</sup>le and I got 100 % on it, and when I went to press submit it would not let me, but it showed up at the bottom that I did get a 100 %”), and only the remaining 2.3 % was devoted to content questions (e.g., “I just <sup>fi</sup>nished the project but I’m confused on how to print the preview of the workbook since there is no <sup>fi</sup>le tab ). These results are based on an analysis of all communication in a section of the online course (enrollment 171 students). However, they are consistent with previous evaluations by instructors involved in the course [25] and conform to a “technology-shaping perspective’’ [26], whereby even small di<sup>f</sup>erences in ST artifact design lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences in the pattern of use over time [27].

The lack of human interaction also appeared to foster strategic learning. While strategic learning is not exclusive to online courses, it can be exacerbated when performance goals are set based on extrinsic motivators (e.g., the grade) rather than a mastery orientation.

Note that the department did not plan for role reversal, the minimization of human interaction, and strategic learning. These were unintended outcomes of the migration to the online learning environment. While we use the <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand experience described above to problematize the artifact design, it is beyond the scope of this research to compare student learning outcomes in online learning systems versus in-class course delivery. Instead, taking the centrality of human interactions in learning environments as a requirement, we focus on the design and implementation of an ST artifact that leverages IT to support and complement, rather than substitute, the professor. We seek to demonstrate both the feasibility [20] and the scalability of such a design through a series of formative evaluation episodes and summative naturalistic evaluation [24] delivering a semester-long course.

## 3. Designing the artifact: a scalable in-class course

While our context is a college of business-required introductory IS course, with our design we seek to inform solutions for a class of problem [28]: the design of in-class-required introductory college course that can scale to large numbers of students, under resource constraint. In keeping with design science fundamentals [20,23,28], we derive requirements from kernel theories and formulate DP to guide the development of the system. Once the ST artifact is implemented, we adopt the human risk and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness FEDS evaluation strategy to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the DP and to assess the artifact.

## 3.1. Kernel theory: intervention theory

In Intervention Theory and Method, Chris Argyris posits that “to intervene is to enter in an ongoing system of relationships […] An in tervenor, in this view, assists a system to become more e<sup>f</sup>ective in problem-solving, decision making and decision implementation in such a way that the system can continue to be increasingly e<sup>f</sup>ective in these activities and have a decreasing need for the intervenor” ([29], p. 15). We argue that the scalability problem under resource constraint, that we face, can only be solved by empowering students. The intervention theory is best suited to this goal, because of its focus on enabling the client, the receiver of the intervention, to become self-su<sup>fi</sup>cient. Thus, we imagine the intervention as designing a choice architecture [30] that fosters positive self-reinforcing behaviors. Our class of problem is di<sup>f</sup>erent from many other popular interventions (e.g., gami<sup>fi</sup>cation of exercises), because of the distinctive characteristics of the educational context. For example, the learning process is more e<sup>f</sup>ective when driven by intrinsic rather than extrinsic motivation [31]. Internal motivation is most appropriate when the intervention requires multiple iterations and actions of the intervener over a relatively long timespan [32,33], both typical of college courses. Moreover, students have different learning strategies [34], which can be incompatible with procedures and incentives introduced to in<sup>fl</sup>uence students’ behavior (e.g., badges or rewards).

The intervention theory identi<sup>fi</sup>es three principles that guide the design of interventions: leveraging valid and useful information, allowing free informed choice by the client, and fostering internal com mitment. Valid information is that which can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed and has been shown to a<sup>f</sup>ect the phenomena the intervenor is seeking to in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Useful information is that which the client would be able to use to “control their destiny” [29]. From an intervention theory standpoint, natural aptitude provides valid, but not useful information, while study habits provide both valid and useful information. Free informed choice points to the centrality of the client in the implementation of the intervention – and therefore in its design. A free and informed choice is particularly important in situations, such as college-level learning, where internal commitment is a precondition to the success of the intervention. Internal commitment refers to the degree of ownership and responsibility the client feels with respect to the intervention. The power of internal commitment comes from individuals’ sense of purpose for the initiative, and their beliefs about the control they exert over their actions and the outcome.

The three principles of intervention theory are interdependent. The availability of valid and useful information is necessary for the client, to make decisions that are free and informed. At the same time, the outcome of these decisions provides information that contributes to the stock of valid and useful information available to the client and the intervenor. Moreover, to the extent that the results of choices being made by the client are positive, those choices should strengthen in ternal commitment [29].

## 3.2. Meta-requirements and design principles discovery

We conceptualize the course as an ST artifact. Thus, drawing on the intervention theory, we advance MR for system design that include both IT-dominant and organization-dominant elements [28]. We organize the MR along with the three principles of intervention theory.

## 3.2.1. Valid information through digital data streaming

Since the introduction of computers in business and organizations, IS theorists have recognized the potential of IT to create digital representations of processes and activities [35]. The computer mediation of everyday activities generates digital representations of events at their inception, a phenomenon known in the literature as digital data genesis [36]. A Digital Data Stream (DDS) is a “continuous digital encoding and transmission of data describing a related class of events” ([37], p. 7). A DDS channels the digital representation of a class of events at their inception and makes them available for harvesting by organizations [38]. In some instances, the DDS is available as a byproduct of existing systems, in other words, the <sup>fi</sup>rm consciously generates it by deploying the needed technology.

Intervention theory posits that interventions must be based on valid and useful information. Information is valid when it is shown to a<sup>f</sup>ect the outcome while being independent of it. For this reason, the digital transformation of an introductory college course should focus on tapping into, or generating, the DDS that yield the needed valid and useful data. This meta-requirement calls for the proactive design of real-time data collection of students’ behaviors. We argue that DDS, with their focus on real-time digital data genesis and real-time availability of the stream for action, are the optimal framework for generating valid and useful data.

MR1: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses should record all students’ behaviors.

• DP1.1: Generate an attendance DDS for all physical activities (e.g., class sessions and lab sessions).

DP1.2: Generate a resource-use DDS for required and optional re sources (e.g., readings and homework).

DP1.3: Generate a completion and performance DDS for required and optional assignments.

DP1.4: Generate an interaction DDS for tracking communication.

The advent of Learning Management Systems (LMS) enables the digital data genesis of student activities in and outside of the class. Contemporary LMS track of such variables as login frequency, time spent on the system, download of materials and resources, completion of exercises, communication and the like [39]. This ready availability of digital data spurred the development of learning analytics to implement “analytic techniques to help target instructional, curricular, and support resources to support the achievement of speci<sup>fi</sup>c learning goals” ([40], p. 8). The research recognizes that students often do not realize how well they are performing, until it is too late to course-correct [41]. For example, recent work shows that simple interventions such as alert emails that spur faculty-student interactions lead to better retention rates [42]. In an implementation of an early alert system, a simple email noti<sup>fi</sup>cation was used to alert the student of their at-risk status as computed by a predictive model. The authors reported that 55 % of high-risk students receiving the alert moved to the moderate-risk category, and about 25 % moved to the low-risk or even the no-risk ca tegory by the next measurement. Of those who were originally classi-<sup>fi</sup>ed as a moderate risk, almost 70 % moved to the low- or no-risk categories after email intervention. In line with the results advocated by the intervention theory, noti<sup>fi</sup>cation interventions spurred recipients to continue to seek support at a rate 30 % higher than the control group even after the alerts stopped [43]. More recent work reports on the development and implementation of an open-source academic alert system, which leveraged data from an open-source LMS [44]. The system was designed to be general, rather than speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a single course, and to be able to scale across institutions. Thus, the authors relied on readily available DDS and could not capture some critical sources of predictive data, such as absenteeism and ongoing perfor mance (e.g., homework pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency). However, they introduced and tested an early alert system for at-risk students. In various introductory courses (e.g., math, sciences, business, and art) at four di<sup>f</sup>erent in stitutions, alert interventions were benchmarked against no intervention using an experimental design. Running a predictive model after 25 %, 50 %, and 75 % of the time in the course had elapsed, investigators identi<sup>fi</sup>ed at-risk students and sent an email alert suggesting two types of corrective actions. Using the <sup>fi</sup>nal course grade as the outcome variable, the study demonstrated that the alert, regardless of the corrective action indicated, had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive impact. In other words, raising student’s awareness about their risk of failing signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduced the chances of actual failure [44].

## 3.2.2. Free and informed choice through learning analytics

The second principle of intervention theory is free and informed choice, placing the locus of decision-making on students once they are armed with valid and useful information. Free and informed choice in college-level courses have been shown as a trait of e<sup>f</sup>ective teachers who “avoid using grades to persuade students to study” ([18], p. 36). Instead, e<sup>f</sup>ective teachers carefully motivate the importance of learning occurring in the course by making explicit the “payo<sup>f</sup>” associated with mastering the learning goals, and deliberately giving the students a sense of control over their learning outcomes [18].

MR2: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses must not con<sup>fl</sup>ate behavior with learning.

DP2.1: Activities tracked through DDS have no bearing on the stu dent’s learning assessment (i.e., grades).

• DP2.2: Assignments and homework are a service to students and have no bearing on the student’s learning assessment.

DP2.3: Learning assessment is measured, independently of student behavior, through dedicated ad hoc evaluations (i.e., exams).

MR2 aims at eliminating external incentives for counterproductive behaviors (e.g., coming to class only to accrue “participation” points, cheating on homework, or engaging in strategic learning). It also fosters learner control, the ability of students to “make their own decisions regarding some aspects of the path, <sup>fl</sup>ow, or events of instruction” ([45], p. 957). While behaviors and activities are not always indicative of performance and skill acquisition, they are important elements of the learning process. Previous research shows how a lack of information about knowledge mastery during the course undermines the successful implementation of learner control, leading to situations where students do not realize how well they perform until it is too late to redress the situation [41]. Thus, an important characteristic of the class of ST artifacts for introductory university courses is the ability of students to engage with the material and receive appropriate feedback.

MR3: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses treats students as self-responsible units and maximizes learner control.

DP3.1: Regular homework and practice assignments are available to students.

DP3.2: Evaluations and feedback are provided for any assignment students voluntarily submit.

DP3.3: Assignments are designed to be automatically evaluated, and students are directed to physical interactions (e.g., lab hours), to discuss the results if clari<sup>fi</sup>cation is needed.

An important tenet of intervention theory is that free choice must be informed choice, based on valid and useful information. Note that this requirement goes beyond showing students how they performed on homework assignments or examinations. Rather it calls for exposing students to data about all their behaviors, and to alert them with decision-making data that provide an interpretation of these behaviors. For example, with access to ever more comprehensive DDS (MR1), it is possible to reliably classify students into risk categories. However, these predictive data are rarely made systematically available to students themselves [44], despite the fact that simple interventions, such as alert emails, that spur faculty-student interactions lead to better retention rates [42].

MR4: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses exposes all behavioral and performance data as soon as it becomes available.

• DP4.1: Provide a dashboard for visualizing students’ individual behavior and performance.

• DP4.2: Apply learning analytical techniques to identify and alert at risk students.

MR5: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses contextualize behavioral and performance data for students.

DP5.1: Provide a dashboard for visualizing anonymized aggregated current student cohort behavior and performance.

DP5.2: Provide a dashboard for visualizing anonymized behavior and performance by previous student cohorts.

## 3.2.3. Internal commitment through persuasive technology

The third principle of intervention theory is internal commitment. Designing technology for maximum in<sup>fl</sup>uence is the realm of the emerging <sup>fi</sup>eld of persuasive technology. The computer mediation of everyday activities [35] elevated the role of IT to that of a potential agent of persuasion [46]. Persuasive technology, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “any interactive computing system designed to change people’s attitudes or behaviors” ([47], p. 1), can therefore be an agent of in<sup>fl</sup>uence by delivering persuasive stimuli [48] designed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the recipients to form, reinforce, or change their attitudes or behaviors [49]. Contemporary college students are digital natives, comfortable users of personal IT and smartphones. With such an audience, the use of triggers – those calls to action “that tells people to perform a behavior now” [47] – can be very promising. This meta-requirement calls for the use (MR6) and attentive design (MR7) of IT-enabled signal, spark, and facilitator triggers to foster students’ internal commitment during the course.

MR6: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses proactively triggers appropriate behaviors.

DP6.1: Use signal triggers to remind students of deadlines and commitments (e.g., assignment deadlines).

• DP6.2: Use spark triggers to alert at-risk students and urge them to action.

DP6.3: Use facilitator triggers to reduce obstacles to performing appropriate behaviors (e.g., prompting a “question of the day” through a conversational interface).

While the notion of triggering is intuitively appealing, the di<sup>fi</sup>culty lies in triggering the behavior at the appropriate place and time to prompt action without frustrating or annoying the recipient [50]. For example, urging action at times when students are unable to perform it risks causing frustration [48]. Thus, triggering activities must leverage individual preferences.

MR7: The ST artifact supporting large introductory college courses encourages sustained students’ use by managing triggering risks.

• DP7.1: Signal triggers are contextually aware (e.g., reminders are targeted, rather than unquali<sup>fi</sup>ed “gentle reminders”).

DP7.2: Students can customize the acceptable triggering window (e.g., time of day, day of the week) or suspend triggers (e.g., mute

for the day).

• DP7.3: Students can manage the type of triggers they receive (e.g., requesting a “question of the day”).

## 3.3. Existing artifacts

To complement the theoretical analysis and the research-driven development of the DP, we analyzed learning technologies in use at the college level. Two of the authors independently carried out an in-depth analysis of the “course solutions” currently in use at the university: SIMnet and MindTap. The two solutions are designed by large textbook publishers and command a substantial share of the US higher education market. The evaluation of the solutions included the use of the appli cations in the analyzed context, discussions with the company sales team, and interviews with instructors as well as students. The primary objective of the evaluation was to understand whether the existing solutions are suitable as the IT core of the planned ST artifact.

The analysis determined that course solutions gather reliable data about the system’s usage. SIMnet gathers data about access to the portal and each individual resource (e.g., explanation, video). Furthermore, the system tracks the completion of tasks and the practice of speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills. On the other hand, MindTap tracks activity completion percentages and a measure of student’s engagement, which can be visualized by the instructor through the use of dashboards. It also allows the instructor to download the aggregated raw data visualized in the dashboards. From the student’s perspective, SIMnet only provides limited visibility of their behavior. For instance, it shows students the tasks already completed and submitted. MindTap provides students with dashboards where students can visualize their individual aggregated measures (e.g., “engagement”) over time. Although the systems collected accurate data about the students’ system usage, the data are hardly actionable. In fact, data collected are not accessible in a programmatic manner (e.g., through an API), and the data that can be accessed are either aggregated at the level of the class or relative to only one student. Furthermore, granular interactions (e.g., the number of clicks) are not tracked by the systems. The limitations of the analyzed systems prevent the implementation of several of the proposed DP.

The analysis was broadened to other “course solutions” designed to support introductory college courses, and to complementary technologies, such as the <sup>fi</sup>ve most widely used Learning Management Software (LMS) solutions market shareholders on the LMS market - Moodle, SAKAI, Canvas, Brightspace by D2L, and Blackboard [51]. Despite the importance of IT support for college course delivery, we identify sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant coverage gaps with respect to the DP introduced above (Table 1). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while a few DP are widely implemented (e.g., DDS generation for assignments), a number of them received no at tention. More generally, and arguably, more importantly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd no solution currently available that aligns with the intervention theory and its call for fostering internal commitment by enabling free and informed choice based on valid and useful information.

## 4. Artifact implementation

In this section, we report on the implementation of the ST artifact and its design iterations. As shown in Fig. 1, the project developed over time around a series of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial and naturalistic evaluation episodes as prescribed by the FEDS artifact evaluation strategy [24]. After each set of evaluation episodes, the feedback obtained informed the redesign and improvement of the artifact. Note that while we report organizational and IT interventions separately for clarity of exposition, our focus on the ST artifact ensured the reciprocal shaping between artifact building, organizational intervention, and evaluation [28]. We also report both formative evaluations and the results from a summative naturalistic evaluation of the ST artifact implementation (i.e., the <sup>fi</sup>rst in-class section with 37 students).

## 4.1. Organizational intervention

The course was scheduled on Tuesdays and Thursdays for 80-min sessions. On Tuesdays, the instructor held interactive lectures covering theoretical material on IT foundations (e.g., networking) and IS foundation (e.g., value creation with IT) topics. On Thursdays, using a <sup>fl</sup>ipped-classroom pedagogy, the class acquired intermediate skills in Microsoft Word and Excel (see Appendix 1 for the complete list of topics). The session started with the instructor showing how to perform some of the more di<sup>fi</sup>cult or conceptually challenging tasks, using a purposely designed data <sup>fi</sup>le that students could download to follow along. After this mini lecture of 15−30 min, the students worked on practice assignments. Each assignment followed an ongoing scenario that spanned the duration of the semester, and contained links to the o<sup>fi</sup>cial Microsoft documentation (both descriptive and video sources) for the skills it required (see Fig. 2 for an example). The scenario gave continuity and realism to the work, so that students could see how their acquisition of increasingly complex skills would translate into their ability to carry out increasingly complex work.

The ST artifact design implemented MR2 by ensuring that no DDS data tracked during class or by the course application was used to compute students’ grades (DP2.1). All assigned activities, such as practice assignments, were assessed if the students completed them, but did not contribute to the <sup>fi</sup>nal evaluation (DP2.2). Students’ mastery was measured by two exams covering practical skills, <sup>fi</sup>ve checkups, and a <sup>fi</sup>nal team project covering theoretical materials (DP2.3).

The ST artifact design implemented MR3 by providing nine practice assignments over the course of the semester. As per the DP in this metarequirement, none of the assignments was required. Yet, work sub mitted within the expected one-week deadline was evaluated (DP3.1). Students received a detailed task-by-task report for each assignment through email, enabling them to see exactly which skill they had not yet mastered (DP3.2). The report listed, for each required task, the importance of the task (i.e., assigned points value), and the actual points earned by the students (i.e., a percentage of successful completion). Students had access to practice solutions through the course app. However, reports were not intended to substitute for human interaction. Rather, they directed students to meet with the instructor or the teaching assistants, for any questions or clari<sup>fi</sup>cation students could not answer independently (DP3.3).

A design imperative for the practical section of the course is the use by each student of their own personal computer. Unlike the previous online design, which forced everyone to learn the Windows version of Word and Excel by relying on a browser-based simulated environment, our ST artifact enables students to work on their own machines with a full version of the Microsoft O<sup>fi</sup>ce applications being learned. This approach is realistic, and it mirrors the way students would use the software for school projects or at work. Conversely, even the most ad vanced simulated environments, force learners to practice individual tasks, resulting in learning that is formulaic and disjointed. Moreover, simulated environments drastically limit the type of mistakes and “alternative solutions” the student can practice. Thus, students do not develop an overall conceptual understanding of how to use the application to achieve their goals $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ an analysis in Excel, standardized and e<sup>fi</sup>cient document design in Word).

Leveraging the fact that Microsoft O<sup>fi</sup>ce documents are collections of XML <sup>fi</sup>les, we used Python to implement an automatic grading system. The software program takes as an input a “key <sup>fi</sup>le” created by the instructor. It <sup>fi</sup>rst extracts the content of the documents by parsing the XML using the minidom package. For each practice assignment, a grading sequence is created by calling generic functions that evaluate each skill that comprise the practice assignment, using the numpy package to perform calculations on arrays and the prettytable and csv packages to output results. These functions seek a match between the student work and the key, but they are robust to acceptable alternative solutions. For example, if students do not construct a function exactly as the key, but necessary elements are present (e.g., the name of the function, the cell range) and results are correct, the students will receive credit for the work. Note, however, that the automatic grading software is not designed to minimize human interaction by attempting to provide de<sup>fi</sup>nite answers or pointers to self-help material. If they do not understand why a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task is incorrect, or how to correct errors they have made, they are directed to visit open labs where they receive help from the instructor or a teaching assistant.

By controlling the automatic grading system, we have full control of the entire process: from skill identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to framing of exercises through scenarios, to acquisition, and practice of speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills, to the evaluation approach, and the degrees of freedom allowed in the assessment of student work and feedback to the learners. Moreover, knowing the structure, strengths, and limitations of the grading soft ware enables us to design practice assignments that are simultaneously realistic, pedagogically sound, and evaluable at scale. For example, if students must learn how to use di<sup>f</sup>erent types of conditional formatting rules in Microsoft Excel (e.g., “Highlight Cells Rules” and “Top/Bottom Rules”) and the automatic grader cannot evaluate multiple conditions on the same column, we structure the assignment so that students apply the <sup>fi</sup>rst rule on one column and the second rule on a di<sup>f</sup>erent column. In this way, both skills are tested, yet evaluation can reliably occur at scale.

## 4.3. Enabling technology: the custom designed course application

We custom-developed the software application enabling the ST artifact on a back-end using the MEAN (MongoDB, Express.js, AngularJS, and Node.js) free and open-source JavaScript stack. It runs on a Linux OS instance from Amazon Web Services (AWS). We chose cloud hosting because it frees us from server maintenance and security responsi bilities, while at the same time providing computing and cost scalability proportional to the number of users and calls to the host server. The e<sup>fi</sup>ciency thus gained enables our small team to focus on features development and functionality evolution enabled by the reciprocal shaping between artifact building, organizational intervention, and evaluation during each semester iteration [28]. The performance of the current iteration was set to scale reliably up to 45 concurrent users using a virtual machine with one Intel Xenon family CPU with up to 3.3 GHz and 2 GB of RAM (an AWS “t2.small” instance). The code was version-controlled using Git, and it was hosted on GitHub to facilitate collaborative coding. The use of free and open-source technologies for developing and hosting the application was imperative given the re source constraints underpinning the project, but also optimal given our design objectives of high scalability and reliability.

The ST artifact design implemented MR1 by requiring students to sign-in to access course material: chapters, course slides, practice assignments, topic, and test schedules. The application tra<sup>fi</sup>c was tracked and monitored using Google Analytics' (GA) free service. GA enabled the collection of students’ behavior data at the session, page, and click event level (DP1.2). During the current iteration, the data stream from the Web application was the only one generated on a real-time basis. We manually recorded attendance to physical activities, such as lab and class sessions (DP1.1). The architecture for tracking the completion and performance of required and optional assignments (DP1.3) as well as communication outside of physical meeting venues is also in place (DP1.4), but we have yet to leverage the data for artifact evaluation.

The ST artifact design partially implemented MR4 and MR5. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the current instantiation of the ST artifact students do not have access to a dynamic dashboard, and we did not implement any learning analytics models. Instead, we experimented with a reporting system delivering two types of custom pdf reports through email. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type, a detailed task-by-task report contains results from practice assignments. We implemented an R script that, using the CSV output of the automatic grading software, automatically creates and emails to the student a pdf report with the performance feedback detailed in the previous section.

The second type of report can be thought of as a static version of a dashboard. Each report presents the results to the students, detailing enacted behaviors (e.g., attendance, access to the materials) and performance on evaluated tasks (DP4.1). For example, students received a chart comparing their lab attendance rate to the class average. For each student, we generated such a report twice during the semester – at midterm and prior to the <sup>fi</sup>nal exam. In addition to the comparison of individual activities with the class (DP5.1), the reports o<sup>f</sup>ered a comprehensive overview of individual behaviors.

## 5.1. Formative evaluation episodes

A number of formative assessments, both arti<sup>fi</sup>cial and naturalistic [24], took place during numerous design iterations that led to the ST artifact design that we deployed in a full semester course. In the below section, we provide the evaluations description in a thematic rather than chronological order, to aid in the understanding of our design decisions and the role of each evaluation event (refer to Fig. 1 for a chronological representation).

## 5.1.1. Practice assignments

The original analysis of the two “course solutions” in use at the university can be considered a <sup>fi</sup>rst formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation episode (#1). The evaluation episode is arti<sup>fi</sup>cial because the researchers, rather than the client, used the technology. Despite the data collection limitations identi<sup>fi</sup>ed early on, the original ST artifact design entailed using an o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf solution for practical skills development. The course solution did cover all of the practical skills that we deemed important for students to master. However, these o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf solutions only cover the material in the context of the Microsoft Windows plat form. Moreover, they require students to perform the tasks in a simulated, browser-based environment that <sup>fi</sup>rst shows, then guides, and <sup>fi</sup>nally tests the students on the same task. Tasks are organized in modules and the number can be customized – ranging from 68 to 85 per lesson. Despite our pedagogical design imperative to allow students to use their own personal computer, and the fact that about half of them own MacOS machines, we believed that asking students to use self-di rected learning material in the simulated environment prior to coming to class would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. However, as the objective of the course is to become pro<sup>fi</sup>cient and master the skills within the application, and not to simply mimic them, we supplemented these materials with concepts and assignments described in weekly practice chapters. As described earlier, the supplemented assignments are realistic and mirror the type of work students will perform in future classes and at work. In a series of formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation events (#3), each assignment was tested by two researchers on both macOS and Windows machines. The objective of testing the homework was to make sure that the tasks were not only feasible and clear but also that they enable the assessment of student skills in a pedagogically e<sup>fi</sup>cient and e<sup>f</sup>ective way. This series of formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluations at the end of each design-build-evaluate cycle resulted in deleting, rephrasing, or changing some of the tasks included in the original assignments.

Upon completion of the ST artifact, we tested it with a formative naturalistic evaluation episode (#4) during a pilot test of the course with a small class of 15 students. Three weeks into the course the students reported that the learning modules of the course solution were alienating and boring. Moreover, students also reported they did not <sup>fi</sup>nd the learning modules valuable. Interviews with students showed that once they had sequentially practiced the skills using the self-directed lessons in the o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf course solution, they would not remember how to perform the task and had to rely on teaching assistants in class to complete the realistic assignments we had developed. Students with a MacOS device remarked that they saw no use in com pleting the online modules, because there were substantial di<sup>f</sup>erences between the Windows-only explanations and their version of Microsoft O<sup>fi</sup>ce applications. Most students had already stopped using them and would exclusively come to the <sup>fl</sup>ipped classroom to work on the practice assignment. As a reaction to the feedback during this evaluation event, we modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the application and added links to each task in the realistic practice assignments we had developed. The external links to the materials are contextual to the students’ preferred OS. The use of the o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf course solution was abandoned.

During the summer term, one of the researchers revised all the practice assignments and external link materials. The main source of skills explanation became the O<sup>fi</sup>ce Support knowledge base, with supplemental video resources added to provide a deeper explanation of more complex tasks (Lynda.com and GCF LearnfFree.org). In the few cases where a resource was not available, a “Discovery challenge” was added to stimulate the student to <sup>fi</sup>nd a solution on their own. The <sup>fi</sup>nal redesign of the online practice assignments was subjected to a formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation (#5). Two students who had completed the course, and joined the team as teaching assistants, tested all the assignments and evaluated every link to ensure that they were both working correctly, and that they su<sup>fi</sup>ciently explained how to complete the task. At the end of this evaluation, some links were removed, others were replaced by better resources and others were integrated with more resources.

## 5.1.2. Data collection accuracy

The original analysis of the two “course solutions” served as a <sup>fi</sup>rst formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation episode (#2) also for our re<sup>fi</sup>nement of MR1. The investigation was instrumental in understanding the limitations of existing technology and reinforcing the need for programmatic access to the DDS produced by the system at the most granular level possible. The <sup>fi</sup>rst formative naturalistic evaluation episode (#4) con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed the unsuitability of the o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf solutions. Thus, while re-<sup>fi</sup>ning the materials during the summer, we developed the custom software application enabling the ST artifact (Fig. 2). To enable the collection of uniquely identi<sup>fi</sup>able, granular, and behavioral DDS, the application implements an authentication system powered by the student’s o<sup>fi</sup>cial university emails, thus enforcing a limit of one account per student.

As described above, activity tracking in the app was implemented using the GA framework. Members of the research team used the system in a formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation episode (#6) designed to test the accuracy, granularity, and depth of the default GA data tracking code. The evaluation uncovered the unavailability of important data. For example, URLs paths tracked were inconsistent in the reports, with ir relevant trailing slash generating new data points, and clicks on external resources were not tracked. More importantly, GA default fea tures do not uniquely identify users, which prevent the gathering of valid and useful information (e.g., individual level behavioral DDS). The problem was solved by integrating the “autotrack” JavaScript li brary. The library enables individual-level tracking of page scrolling as well as clicks on external links, and the active visibility of the pages.

## 5.1.3. Feedback system

The primary objective of practice assignments is to o<sup>f</sup>er feedback to students regarding their performance, feedback that in our design is not used to compute grades. One of the main challenges of a scalable solution is the ability to provide valid and useful performance information that the client can act upon. The early design of the feedback system provided, for each assignment, a sample <sup>fi</sup>le that represents the “solu tion” to the homework. With this approach, students would be expected to evaluate their performance independently. While the approach is scalable as the number of students grows, it failed during the <sup>fi</sup>rst formative naturalistic evaluation because it required the students to perform scrupulous comparisons between their <sup>fi</sup>le and the solution to detect errors (#4). The students involved in the pilot course reported that the system was both inconvenient and unclear. Thus, during the ensuing summer, the team designed and developed the automatic grading system described earlier. To ensure quality feedback, the automatic grading system is not developed as a stand-alone piece of software. Instead, the synergy between the structure of the practice assignments, and the evaluation and feedback system is a core design feature. To achieve this synergy, as it was being developed, the software underwent a series of formative arti<sup>fi</sup>cial evaluation episodes (#7) at the end of each design-build-evaluate cycle. The practice assignments provided an inventory of competencies required to complete realistic tasks and their pedagogical sequencing. Weekly meetings during the summer ensured integration between the assignments and the ability to evaluate them at scale. During these meetings, test cases were evaluated to ensure that the intended pedagogical objectives could be achieved (e.g., students understand how to use absolute and relative cell refer encing in Excel). The team overcame limitations and errors by either modifving the evaluation code or the practice assignments. Recall the example of conditional formatting in Excel, which was introduced earlier. In this case, instead of trying to improve the code to overcome its limitations, the team changed the task description to ensure that any range of cells would require at most the application of one conditiona formatting rule. The pedagogical objective (i.e., learning to use condi tional formatting rules) was achieved while maintaining the scalability of the feedback system through an automated evaluation of learners' work. Files submitted by the previous semester students and new sub missions prepared by two teaching assistants were used as compre hensive test cases to assess the reliability of the automatic grading software. Limitations and inconsistencies were discussed during weekly meetings and resolved by further re<sup>fi</sup>ning task descriptions and assignment data <sup>fi</sup>les. The result of this development was an ST artifac supporting the delivery of the six Excel and three Word practice as signments. The <sup>fi</sup>nal design of the feedback system was tested with a summative naturalistic evaluation during the fall semester.

## 5.2. Summative naturalistic evaluation

Our summative naturalistic evaluation (#8) consists in delivering one section of the course and reviewing the following sources of data: the behavioral and performance DDS we collected throughout the semester, the results of a survey at the end of the semester enquiring about individual app use, and seeking feedback about the ST artifact design (see Appendix 2), as well as the standard course evaluation

issued by the administration.

With respect to MR1, we <sup>fi</sup>nd our design to enable the generation of needed DDS. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, only 5.56 % of students reported printing all the theoretical materials and 7.43 % reported printing all the practice assignments. Thus, we are able to collect accurate resource use data (DP1.2) with an approach that is scalable because our app leverages a cloud infrastructure (i.e., AWS). We also evaluate all performance activities, required or optional, using electronic means (e.g., the automatic grading software). As a consequence, we can scale our collection of performance DDS (DP1.3). All interactions that do not occur during face to face meetings are logged in the communication channels used to support the course – thus enabling the unobtrusive generation of interaction DDS (DP1.4). The current design relies on manual tracking of attendance to all physical activities (DP1.1). This approach is not scalable (see discussion).

As with any other behavior in the course, class attendance was not required (MR2). Attendance was consistently above 75 %, despite the prevailing rhetoric at the school suggesting that unless students are forced to, they will not go to class. Of the 30 students who completed the course, four attended every class and all but three attended at least two-thirds of the sessions. While attendance did not statistically correlate to performance, it supports our e<sup>f</sup>ort to foster human interaction in the learning environment. The formal course evaluation showed that the course was well received, with an overall result on a four-point scale at 3.53 (college average 3.35). Questions in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the course design included: the perception of fairness in evaluation (3.56 vs 3.38), assessment of the feedback quality (3.44 vs 3.28), and stimulation of interest in the subject matter (3.67 vs 3.32).

While they were present and actively engaged during the sessions, in aggregate students did not consistently complete the weekly assignments (MR3). On-time completion was the only requirement to receive feedback, and completion steadily declined during the semester – from 69 % on the <sup>fi</sup>rst assignment to 7 % on the ninth assignment. The results indicate that, while students would start the assignment in class, they rarely completed the work during the allotted class time. As a consequence, they would not submit it for evaluation. A number of students stated in their feedback that when they sat down to complete the assignment at home, the length and complexity of the practice ex ercise would discourage them. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 22 out of the 27 respondents agreed with the statement that “shorter practice assignments would be more e<sup>f</sup>ective.” In other words, our summative naturalistic evaluation shows that the design failed to ensure that students would stay on track with the progression of material without using grades to stimulate activity.

We employed a diachronic approach to measuring the reliability [54] of the feedback system. We used the tasks included in the o<sup>fi</sup>cial examination of learners’ skill mastery assessment, to investigate the performance of the automatic grading system with di<sup>f</sup>erent users on a wide range of realistic tasks. We <sup>fi</sup>rst compared the degree of agreement between the four raters – the grading engine and the three graders. On a total of 1,300 elements evaluated by each rater, 90.46 % of the evaluations had zero standard deviation (i.e., every rater recorded the exact same score). Individual agreement between the three human graders was 91.77 %. Following this preliminary analysis, one of the human raters re-analyzed all the exams under the supervision of one member of the research team. She was instructed to take as much time as was needed to identify all discrepancies between the score assigned by graders to each task, and the correct evaluation of that task. Overall, this evaluation indicates that the grading engine was superior to each human rater, achieving an accuracy of 98.38 % and misevaluating only 1.62 % of the graded elements, compared to an average of 5.23 % by human graders (range, 6.92 – 4.00).

The results of this evaluation support our conclusion that the automatic grading system provides very reliable performance feedback (DP2.3). Moreover, while both the manual and the automatic grading systems scale linearly with the number of submissions, the latter was about two orders of magnitude faster when benchmarked on a Dell Inspiron 13 7000 series personal computer with 8 GB of memory and Intel Core i7-6500U CPU. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the three raters took an average of 10.63 min per exam (range, 7.23–17.04 per rater), while the automatic grading system took 5.25 s per <sup>fi</sup>le (range, 3.47–7.39). The eva luation did point to one area of improvement with respect to the scalability of the ST artifact. Manual processing of evaluations to email them injects unwanted delay in the feedback system (see discussion).

## 6. Discussion

The primary objective of this research project is to advance the design of a required in-class introductory college course that can scale to large numbers of students, under resource constraints. Instead of attempting to compare the merits of online and in-class education, our work takes the centrality of human interactions in learning environments as given. Grounded in the intervention theory, the paper advanced the MR and DP of a ST artifact that can scale to large numbers. It then demonstrates the feasibility of the ST artifact using a human risk and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness evaluation strategy [24]. In this section, we discuss the insights emerging from the artifact’s implementation and evaluation, and we draw implications for future research and future iterations of the design [52].

The <sup>fi</sup>rst consideration is that the system, as designed, is ready to scale to large numbers. All behavioral DDS are seamlessly tracked and can be used for analysis, with the exception of attendance. Hosting the technology on AWS enables almost in<sup>fi</sup>nite scalability as the number of students grows. The implementation of the DP associated with MR2 and MR3 was successful. Our evaluation shows that the automatic grading system we designed is reliable (DP3.3), but the timing of feedback (DP3.2) must be re-evaluated. The summative naturalistic evaluation presented above suggests that, while the performance feedback system can scale to a large number of learners, the overwhelming nature of the assignments and low speed of the feedback system (weekly cycles) hampered completion rates. This was a fundamental limitation of the ST artifact.

Intervention theory provides the overarching architecture for the design of the ST artifact, and points to a need for balancing task realism with the ability to provide reliable and valid feedback to the learners. However, low practice assignments completion rates hamper the goal of providing timely performance feedback. One common approach to increasing completion rates is to use grades to create an extrinsic incentive [57,58]. However, this approach runs counter to MR2. Moreover, the use of grades diverts attention away from the task, thus potentially hampering the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the intervention [59]. The progress principle suggests that emotional well-being, and motivation at work or during task completion depend primarily on the perception of progress toward a meaningful goal [60]. In our case, such a goal is skills mastery. In light of the third principle of intervention theory, fostering internal commitment, the progress principle suggests that an important metarequirement ought to be added to the original list. Namely, the ST ar tifact supporting large introductory college courses must foster a sense of progress in the learners. In the context of the introductory college course, this additional MR would call for coupling the existing design principle of practice assignment realism, with that of manageability. Manageable assignments are those that, based on current students ability at any speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time, are successfully completable. Manageable assignments are more likely to produce small wins and foster increased completion.

Beyond course material changes and the app feature changes designed to support them, the summative naturalistic evaluation suggests an evolution of DP along two vectors: improved data collection, and persuasive technology triggers. Our evaluation of actual student use patterns shows that they tended not to print material and instead work within the app. The use of embedded links signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacted students learning in the practical skills component of the course. These early results suggest that the design of the content can simultaneously improve the objective of valid and useful data collection through DDS generation and improved pedagogical value. This could be done by embedding valuable multimedia elements and links in the theoretical content. Early examples are videos, links to external resources, and embedded widgets (e.g., a password strength evaluation widget in the cybersecurity chapter). As the design of content moves away from a book format and closer to an interactive knowledge app, we expect students to further limit printing while improving their understanding of the material.

While at this point we have relied heavily on log data to capture student behaviors, another avenue for improvement consists of leveraging emerging multimodal learning analytics techniques [61] to improve the collection of valid and useful information at scale. Particularly problematic is the challenge of generating the attendance DDS. Face recognition may provide a fruitful avenue digitizing presence in all colocated learning activities: class sessions, lab sessions, review sessions, and o<sup>fi</sup>ce hours. Keeping with our DP, such a system can be used to record student behaviors to expose them through dashboards and would not be used for grading. We also expect the system to become the basis for real-time student recognition during the class, once augmented reality solutions become viable. We deem such a system as an important instrument to help reduce the feelings of anonymity that pervade stu dents in large classes.

The second challenge we face, as captured by our results, is the need to think creatively about scalable systems that aid in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing students to practice good learning habits and combat strategic learning and procrastination. Recent research shows that students tend to engage in undesirable behavior (e.g., procrastination) despite being aware of the resulting negative outcomes [62]. In smaller classes, the best teachers are able to motivate students without resorting to the use of requirements and grades [18]. How can the same objective be achieved in larger required courses? MR6 and MR7 point to persuasive tech nology [48] and digital nudging [63] solutions. One of the limitations of the current work is the fact that we were unable to implement spe ci<sup>fi</sup>c triggers. However, an ST artifact that implements MR6 and MR7 while supporting the scalability imperative underlying our work mus intervene proactively to nudge students into appropriate actions. Spe ci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the ST artifact should provide a choice architecture that en courages students into positive and self-reinforcing activities by default. This is a fertile area for future research. One of the most noteworthy cases of a digital nudge at scale is the health features of the Apple Watch, which gently reminds users to perform behaviors leading to a healthier lifestyle. As detailed in the MR de<sup>fi</sup>nition, digital nudges can assume many forms. For instance, displaying healthy options <sup>fi</sup>rst in online food ordering menu is a subtle nudge. Noti<sup>fi</sup>cations reminding you to go to the gym are more direct prompts. Future research should investigate the design of IT-enabled triggers implementing digital nudging principles. Triggers of the kind identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in MR6 and MR7 can be developed through conversational interfaces. For example, Ro driguez at al. 2018 describe the implementation of a chatbot that use natural language conversational capabilities to enable students to in quire about approaching deadlines and commitments, and to engage with the course content. Using the chatbot, students can inquire about the schedule of class commitments and activities. More importantly the bot implements a facilitator trigger designed to foster students’ engagement with the material to test their knowledge. They do so by requesting the chatbot to o<sup>f</sup>er questions about the course material and receive feedback about the accuracy of their responses. In line with MR7, the application managing the users interaction with the bot en ables the students to customize the noti<sup>fi</sup>cation window and the type of messages for which they want to receive a noti<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., set custom “do not disturb” windows). Albeit preliminary, the research suggests that the facilitator's trigger had a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ect on students’ perfor mance. Research within this stream points to the feasibility of trig gering and the applicability of MR6 and MR7. Future research should incorporate triggering systems such as chatbots into an ST artifact like the one described here, to test its contribution to the delivery of in-class introductory college courses that can scale to large numbers of students, under resource constraint. With regard to ST artifact design, our results demonstrate the importance of exploiting the generative qualities of ST artifacts such as a learning environment enabled by persuasive technology – within the bounds of deep-seeded beliefs about the acceptable outcomes (e.g., the centrality of human interactions in learning environments). Such a design is centered on leveraging digital technology to enable, rather than substitute for, human interaction. The articulation of the MR and DP provides a solid basis for future implementation, demonstration, and evaluation of ST artifacts [64]. The MR stem directly from the tenets of intervention theory, an established framework for guiding social actions designed to bring about change. They address all three principles of the theory: (1) leveraging valid and useful information, (2) allowing free informed choice by the client, and (3) fostering internal commitment, and are grounded in recent research. However, we do not claim that the MR and DP advanced in this article are either exhaustive or objectively superior to any other possible meta requirement. Instead, following the iterative process of DSR, the proposed DP serve as the basis for implementation and evaluation of the ST artifact. More fundamentally, our work shows how much of the research on digital transformation of college-level courses takes place in the context of online learning systems, and there is no a-priori reason to believe that the application of the same class of technologies (e.g., learning analytics dashboards, alerts, and triggers) will have similar e<sup>f</sup>ects in traditional courses and withstand empirical scrutiny. As future research moves through the stages of the DSR methodology [64] existing DP will be re<sup>fi</sup>ned and new MR may be produced through reciprocal shaping between artifact building, organizational intervention, and evaluation [28].

## 7. Limitations

To problematize the artifact design, we gather information from the <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand experience in a single institution. While we believe that the same issues apply to other universities that struggle with similar budgetary constraints and pressure to increase the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of in-class course delivery, generalization of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings through replication in di<sup>f</sup>erent settings is needed. As noted above, one of the limitations of the current work is the fact that we were unable to implement speci<sup>fi</sup>c triggers. While we leveraged published research <sup>fi</sup>ndings to infer the role of triggering in our design, empirical validation is necessary. Finally, the current paper does not attempt any comparison of the merits of online and in-class education, taking the centrality of human interactions in learning environments as given. Thus, we are not able to determine if the course delivery centered on human interaction actually results in students’ improvements in mastering course contents. However, such comparison of the student learning outcomes in online learning systems versus in-class course delivery is of primary interest to both researchers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and university administrators, and we in vite future research to compare in-class designs that leverage our pro posed MR with online courses delivered in VLE.

## 8. Conclusions

The higher education “industry” has not been immune to digital innovation and digital transformation. Motivated by the growing consensus in the literature that the delivery of high-quality college education hinges on the instructor’s ability to engineer a learning environment where students can learn e<sup>f</sup>ectively [18], we advocate for an approach to the digital innovation in education that leverages, rather than “digitize,” the instructor. Innovation is increasingly the outcome of dynamic problem-solution pairings [65], and we argue that the design science approach is best suited to identify and advance optimal designs. We consider our ST artifact an early example of what s possible. We continue to iterate the design-build-evaluate cycle of DSR to uncover and solidify the DP of ST artifacts. Those principles should help us address the wicked design problem of delivering a required in-class introductory college course, one that is e<sup>fi</sup>ciently scalable to large numbers of students.

More subtly, leveraging the DSR paradigm in our e<sup>f</sup>ort helps to reassert the need for explicit design, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of clear objectives, the role of MR substantiating those objectives, and the importance of building artifacts following clearly articulated DP. The explicit articulation of MR and DP grounded in established kernel theory and research <sup>fi</sup>ndings forces a continued re-evaluation and attention to the coherence of the ST artifact design and its rationale. In other words, overtly articulating the MR for artifact construction provides the instruments for “governing” the digital transformation process. It also enables an objective evaluation instrument for reevaluating the design over time to make sure that unintended consequences are surfaced and addressed.

Joaquin Rodriguez Joaquin is a Ph.D. candidate in the Entrepreneurship & Information Systems Department at Louisiana State University Ourso College of Business. Before joining the LSU community, he obtained his Bachelor’s degree from the University of Ferrara and a Master’s degree from the University of Pavia. His research focuses on the strategic use of digital technologies to achieve a competitive advantage. In particular, his work investigates how organizations can leverage digital platforms that they do not own or control to improve their strategic position. He is currently focusing on the context of food delivery platforms.
