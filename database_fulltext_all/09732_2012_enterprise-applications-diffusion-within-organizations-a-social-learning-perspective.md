## A B S T R A C T

We used a social learning explanation to explain the diffusion of an enterprise application (EA) within an organization. We viewed the implementation of EA as a pattern of key users’ behaviors to examine the effect of self-generated and external sources of influence. Such behavior is progressively developed over time. Our study was based on investigating two business areas in a single company. From our investigation, we determined that the key users’ behaviors toward EA diffusion were influenced by both cognitive factors and organizational stimuli. Although the organizational environment was similar fo both sites, one area performed better than the other in diffusing the system.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise applications (EAs) are a type of IS that are used in order to manage activity within teams or beyond organizational boundaries. This type of IS focuses upon the integration of business processes within organizations, allowing improved coordination among functional departments, business units, providers and with customers. EAs include ERP, CRM, KMS and Supply Chain Management (SCM). They are complex technologies because of their size, off-the-shelf approach, and the organizational, operational, and technological changes they introduce in organizations [3,5]. However, the high rates of failure of EA projects have resulted in a tendency to analyze this phenomenon from different management perspectives [1,10], as a cross-functional discipline that covers a broad range of management fields including IS, organization theory, human resources, accounting, and operations.

EA integration introduces new elements into the implementation process; it requires a single database and a modular constitution. Companies often implement EA using a phased approach in which each business area adopts either a set of EA functionality or just one that forms a complete business unit.

More importantly, it remains possible to implement complementary changes although the unit is already being used. Moreover, the adoption of EA in one business area may occasionally require changes in the legacy system. This raises the question: when does implementation actually end? Indeed, many EA projects are never completed. A gradual adoption approach thus requires a method to assess the implementation of an EA. Approaches following a technological innovation perspective [11], divide the process into several stages. Therefore, we felt that a complementary perspective was needed in order to effectively understand EA diffusion.

We therefore decided that the diffusion of EA within an organization could be understood as a social learning process in which the key users (those responsible for an organizational area) had to develop types of behaviors that helped the diffusion of the system within their business area; but what types of behaviors affect the final use of the EA? And what cognitive interactions predict a good EA implementation? We attempted to answer these questions using social learning theory (SLT).

## 2. Social learning theory: the foundation

SLT, also known as Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), has its roots in the work of psychologist Bandura; it is fundamentally learning by observing the actions of other people. It therefore involves three variables: the person, behavior, and the environment. The theory proposes that these three variables interact to explain individual actions (i.e. behavior).

Recently, VanSandt and Neck [13] described how the practice of self-leadership – as a construct derived from SLT – can be a relevant means to improve moral action in companies. The number of studies focused on the link between self-efficacy (a person’s belief that he or she can perform a task) and individual reactions to computing technology is particularly significant.

Although the first steps in the application of SLT to IS may be apparent, there are a number of other ways to use SLT to explain IS diffusion. The application of SLT in IS studies has focused on just one of the relationships in the SLT model: that of a person’s cognitive perceptions (e.g. self-efficacy) and behavior. However, the question of how the environment interacts with the other two variables remained unanswered. Furthermore, previous studies have focused on quantitative analyses, which make a broader study of social learning for IS phenomena using qualitative methods (e.g. longitudinal case studies) more desirable. SLT is a powerful theoretical framework that elicits a better understanding of IS transformation processes including gradual diffusion of EA.

## 3. Research method

Our study was part of a larger investigation of EA adoption and diffusion [6]. It accompanies a description and analysis of EA adoption and diffusion models. However, the motivation here was to explore the different levels of performance by and within companies. In one company that reported successful EA adoption, the authors found that the success originated in a specific single business area, whereas other business areas were performing quite differently. This led us to explore circumstances where the same environmental motivators were present in all areas, but where some areas were more successful. Thus our question was: why do EAs diffuse differently among business areas within the same company? This question led us to further study two business areas (sales and distribution, and logistics and manufacturing) in what we refer to as the Coffee Company (CC).

These two business areas, which had contrasting results, became our two sites for a multiple-case study. Studying two business areas within a company means that the company becomes the environment in which the phenomenon is observed. We selected the key EA user as our unit of analysis. The key user was the set of users who play a stated role in implementing the system and helping other users learn about the system. They were, generally, business-area managers.

## 3.1. Site description

CC is a national leader in the processing and distribution of roasted and ground coffee in a Latin American country and it has been exported green coffee to the USA and Europe since 1992. In the first years of the millennium, the company’s annual earnings were about US\$45 million and it employed 370 persons. Its supplychain consisted of several procurement centers located in coffee regions throughout the country, a modern plant where coffee was roasted, ground, and packaged, a fleet of trucks to deliver products to regional centers, twelve regional distribution and warehouse centers which distributed its products to 11,900 retailers and forty-three independent intermediaries. CC has also a sales force of 35 persons, responsible for selling to more than 700 large retail destinations including supermarkets and chains.

The two areas under study in our investigation were logistics and manufacturing (L&M) overseeing the processes of procurement, manufacturing, and distribution of products between the factory and the regional distribution centers and sales and distribution (S&D) responsible for the processes of sales and delivery from regional centers to retailers. Each area had a manager who was designated as the key user for the diffusion of EA within his/her area. Both managers were in charge of a broad set of processes in the supply chain; they were responsible for the entire value chain.

## 3.2. Data collection and analysis

We recorded the process through a combination of real time and retrospective analysis. Frequent visits to CC were carried out over a period of three years. The primary methods of data collection were 41 semi-structured interviews, each lasting about 90 min, observation, and documentary review. The interviews were with people involved in EA implementation: the CEO, the CFO, functional managers, end-users, technical specialists, members of the personnel department, and EA consultants. We also observed several Key Users Committee (KUC) meetings – each dedicated to managing and evaluating the EA implementation process and lasting about of three hours. Direct observations were performed by an observer, who was introduced as a researcher sponsored by the CEO. Long-term observations took place in monthly visits: this minimized the effect of the observer on what was being observed. As a result, the agenda of KUC meetings was not influenced by the observer. At first, sketchy notes were taken by the observer, who then rewrote them soon after the meeting. Some training sessions were also attended. The analysis also included a review of documents focusing on memos, user manual, procedures, system manuals, and reports of earlier implementation phases.

An iterative approach of data coding and analysis was followed, focusing on the development of concepts and constructs associated with the social learning process and EA diffusion within an organization. Although these methods were initially classified following an open coding technique (i.e. as suggested by the data), the emerging concepts were re-organized to take into account ‘‘some potentially important variables’’ [8] from SLT. Specifically, the analysis consisted of three concurrent flows of activity: data reduction, data display, and conclusion drawing/verification. We reduced the rewritten field notes to a more organized data set. To do this we used codes as labels attached to words. Then, we assembled and displayed data based on the SLT. The main displays we used were graphs and chronological loops. These allowed us to compare events among the two sites. Finally, we drew conclusions and verified them. Although conclusions were initially vague, they became explicit by iterating data collection, data reduction, and data analysis over time.

Once conclusions emerged, precautions were taken to corroborate the interpretations. We checked field notes during the writing process, got feedback from interviewees and from third parties (e.g., an EA consultant, two scholars, and people that participated in three research seminars). The three main SLT variables are extremely general and no previous conceptualization of them existed in the EA context.

## 4. Enterprise system adoption at CC

CC acquired its EA in 1997 by purchasing it from one of the top five vendors. A first set of EA functionality was installed between 1997 and 1999. This initially installed included features related to finance, materials management, sales administration and manufacturing. The first version of the system was installed and available for use in 1999. Since then the organization has been engaged in a process designed to diffuse the EA to support more organizational functions and business units. Our study was based on the implementation process between 1997 and 2002. In our description of the evidence we describe the projects according to the year in which they took place (see Fig. 1).

## 4.1. The initial implementation: from 1997 to 1999

CC followed a traditional implementation approach that focused on adopting a set of functionality in some business areas. To do this CC gathered a project team of seven staff members and some external consultants. It was led by the IS manager. Financial accounting functions started in 1997–1998. They progressed to rollout in the second half of 1999. This required a rework of the process model of the finance function. Over the same period, the cost accounting system was remodeled, influencing both the finance and manufacturing functions. Meanwhile, the adoption of materials management and manufacturing functionalities were already underway. These processes required the development of reporting options to meet the requirements of the finance department.

## 4.2. The second implementation: from 1999 to 2002

The year 1999 was a decoupling point in CC’s efforts to implement its EA. The company dropped its implementation model in favour of one based on a longer term perspective. No phases were stated in this new model. The implementation process was presented as being composed of an indefinite number of projects.

The diffusion process of the EA was led by the CEO. His belief in the need for post-implementation activities was critical. He explained it as follows:

I was told that many companies make the mistake of believing that the implementation of a system finishes when the system is up and running. At that moment top management stops the delivery of resources for post-implementation. This is madness. The real benefits from the system are achieved through finetuning and innovation after that time. I have taken those words seriously.

The CEO led and closely managed post-implementation activities. Through these, CC staff launched a number of initiatives related to the fine-tuning of the system and which helped to diffuse it internally. However, resources were scarce and the CEO had to prioritize initiatives according to the company’s needs. One of the first approved initiatives was to diffuse the system into the S&D areas located remotely in 12 different regions of the country. This was explained by the CEO in year 2:

We are in a highly competitive sector, which is significantly influenced by final customers and intermediaries (distributors and retailers). We had to upgrade our processes in order to meet the customer’s needs.

These words encouraged the organization to purchase Hand-Held Computers (HHC) for sales intermediaries nationwide. The S&D key user was required to oversee their operation.

The KUC was created by the CEO early in year 2, following a recommendation by EA consultants. The KUC intended to monitor the EA consultants’ service levels and the different initiatives in the post-implementation era. The KUC arranged a meeting every month. These meetings allowed the CEO and key users to share information about the progress of the different initiatives and to make decisions. The CEO took advantage of these meetings to send critical messages to key users. For the CEO, achieving the benefits of EA relied heavily on working in teams for the design of the new process and less on individual work. It was also vital to share data, information, and knowledge openly.

The CEO and senior managers of CC became convinced that the use of traditional consultancy services was inappropriate for their EA project. They decided to hire an outsourcing service providing technical services. The goal was still to continue to fine-tune the system, to implement new functionality, and to give training.

The three year EA outsourcing contract was long enough to allow the employees to acquire knowledge from the consultants, as well as allowing CC to negotiate a lower price for the service. The company paid a fixed monthly price per user and a service level agreement was put in place. Having a fixed price for everything was a positive stimulus for the EA diffusion process. Without capital limitations, the project could empower key users and top managers’ to define and implement new initiatives. The CEO continued to play a critical role, following up each initiative. He dedicated a part of all KUC meetings to monitoring the progress of each project. Finally, the CEO asked employees to continuously raise standards and achieve better performance.

## 4.3. EA diffusion in sales & distribution (S&D)

The 12 S&D centers received finished goods from the factory, stored them in the warehouse, sold, invoiced, and collected money from both local, small markets and large retailers. The S&D manager and two other employees were assigned to play the role of change agents; they were trained on the EA functionality for three months in the corporate offices.

In year 2 of our study, the S&D functionality had been installed in the factory and corporate offices and the process of diffusion had started. The S&D key user was in charge of this part of the project. He had the goal of standardizing the processes of his twelve S&D regional centers.

CC piloted the regional diffusion in just one of its distribution centers. The implementation took about 4 weeks, including system configuration, testing, and training. Monitoring was undertaken using Gantt charts and follow-up meetings took place weekly. Once the system was running in the S&D center, change agents started to coach, guide and transfer knowledge to the final users. These change agents were at the site for two weeks in order to stabilize the system and solve problems. After this, an internal help desk took over the role of giving remote support to final users. The two change agents remained on site as part of the Help Desk. They realized that most inquiries were about procedures and norms, rather than system issues; they therefore wrote a procedure manual to help final users in the regional centers.

After the successful diffusion of the system in the first S&D center, the key user started the implementation of the plan for the remaining centers. The entire implementation took about 18 months. While the change agents were implementing one center, they continued to support centers already using the system. In addition, the key user identified any type of mismatch between the system and the actual process of the company. One of these mismatches was related to new reports required by senior management. These requirements required the EA outsourcer to tailor the system.

Change agents welcomed opinions from staff in remote regions. They fostered a confident environment for change, with a gradual gain in respect. In addition, members of the KUC focused on the diffusion project. They maintained a high expectation of success, and sought to raise performance standards for the S&D team.

## 4.4. The EA diffusion in logistics & manufacturing (L&M)

The adoption of EA was expected to improve the coordination between the S&D areas and the L&M areas. The CEO’s second priority was thus the diffusion of EA through L&M. Between years 0 and 2, the L&M key user was responsible for the implementation of the manufacturing and material management functionalities. Initially, the CEO trusted him to carry out the diffusion of EA into the rest of the L&M areas.

The L&M key user had extensive experience in manufacturing and had been the area’s manager for several years, showing considerable expertise in managing to the production systems. Given that coffee production was traditional and mature, he had little need for new ICTs. He saw his area as a functional department with little influence from other areas.

From the outset, he believed that the system was not applicable to his area. Then, after using the system, he became convinced that it was difficult to use. He saw better coordination with S&D as only a distant goal for him. He did not perceive it as a priority for manufacturing processes. Instead, he used the system only for the automation of certain transactions required. He repeatedly prevented the involvement of his employees in other parts of the EA project. This behavior changed the CEO’s and KUC’s views of the diffusion process in the manufacturing area. It was decided that the L&M key user would be placed in charge of other projects. In his place, three new key users would become part of the L&M team. These users were drawn from purchasing management, manufacturing management, and transportation management. These new key users had a clear message from the CEO: ‘‘take full advantage of the system in your area’’.

The new users quickly demonstrated better performance than their predecessor. In particular, the key user in purchasing management utilized his previous experience in an EA project. He identified a mismatch between the real and configured model in the purchase-to-pay process of indirect materials. The key-user wanted to eliminate the use of paper and to encourage only electronic transactions between his clients and the purchasing department. This triggered the adaptation of the process toward that of best practice deemed by the EA.

## 5. Findings with respect to SLT

A number of studies have acknowledged the impact of environmental or contextual factors on the implementation of an EA [14]. Research has also been carried out on the role of key users and other end users in EA implementation e.g., [12], but no study has yet positioned the key users’ cognitive factors among broader EA adoption processes, nor has there been a study of the reciprocal effects among the three factors in EA diffusion. Fig. 2 shows the effect of SLT in the diffusion of EA:

SLT in the diffusion of EA results in the following claims:

1. Key user behavior is the crucial factor in the diffusion of an EA in an organization.

2. Key user behavior toward EA diffusion is conditioned by workers’ cognitive processes and environmental influences.

3. Reciprocal determinism causes key user behavior to shape the environment and thus influence a key user’s future cognition.

## 5.1. The social learning triad: behavior

Behavior has been defined as observable and potentially measurable activities invoking motor, cognitive, and physiological responses. They should be described according to the goals they aim to achieve. We decided that seven behaviors had most influence on EA diffusion. Key users who were members of the KUC exhibited one or more of these with respect to diffusing or not diffusing the system throughout their area.

## 5.1.1. Behavior 1 – routine use

By analyzing the behavior of different key users we found several different levels of EA utilization (see Table 1). For example, the S&D key user developed sophisticated uses in his business area. After rolling out the EA into the twelve sales regions, he started using the system for delivering better customer service and for improving coordination throughout his area. However, the L&M key user preferred using the system only for the automation of some transactions but he still used spreadsheets for decision-making.

## 5.1.2. Behavior 2 – learning

Learning can be split into two: by doing and through observation. CC’s key users developed both types. The transportation management key user recognized that he was learning about the EA functionality and features by using it while the manufacturing management key user started a learning process based on how other key users were taking advantages of the EA. For example, in helping him achieve better communication with EA consultants.

## 5.1.3. Behavior 3 – identification of mismatches

The identification of mismatches is an observable activity that we found in the analysis of key users’ behaviors. For example, the S&D key user identified mismatches in to reports needed by and sent to managers and changed the specification to conform to the real requirements. The identification of mismatches has been studied in other work [4].

## 5.1.4. Behavior 4 – identification of new opportunities

Such opportunities occur from the implementation of new functionality or the rollout of the functionality in other regions or locations. EA literature also provides examples of such actions [6].

## 5.1.5. Behavior 5 – project deployment

Project deployment involves planning, implementation and closing, activities carried out through the EA outsourcer. The key user plays the role of sponsor of his/her project and he/she has to convince the KUC and to plan the project. Once the project is approved and planned, the key user plays the role of leader. He/she will implement and close the project along with the final users and EA outsourcer support. Project deployment has been acknowledged to be an observable activity in EA adoption.

## 5.1.6. Behavior 6 – user development

Because the EA implementation is a continuous process, final user development also is an ongoing ‘‘observable activity’’. Key users support the development of his/her users through three activities: training, guidance, and problem solving. A number of authors report these as key parts of the EA implementation process [9]. For the S&D area, user development was a fundamental activity of the key user. Three activities were deployed: training, mainly by EA consultants and change agents; guidance to the change agents and the final end-users by the key user sending clear messages about how the system should work; and emphasis on solving problems quickly (delivered through the help desk and then by EA consultants in the back-office). This overall user development activity gave rise to the writing of users’ manuals and procedures.

## 5.1.7. Behavior 7 – retaining the current situation

This is sometimes considered to be inertia. For example, the L&M key user’s response to the EA diffusion process was one of arguing that his area did not need the system. The manufacturing management key user took a similar position at the start of his work as key user but then changed his mind after the first meetings with the other key users.

## 5.2. The social learning triad: environmental stimuli

By taking key users as the unit of analysis of our study, environmental factors were represented as organizational stimuli that influenced his or her behavior. Based on the analysis of the two areas under study, we found that four environmental stimuli were important to the social learning triad: the CEO’s beliefs and role, KUC, EA outsourcing, and the internal help desk.

## 5.2.1. Stimulus 1 – CEO’s belief and role

The CEO considered that post-implementation activities were critical to success of the EA at CC; therefore he led and closely managed these activities.

## 5.2.2. Stimulus 2 – the KUC

We modeled two critical constructs: the KUC as the environment in which key users learnt by observation, and the KUC as the catalyst that allowed the social network to accelerate the diffusion process.

KUC featured as the environment for learning by observation. As reported in several studies, the special characteristics of an EA imply deep change in the adopting organization [2]. Its sheer scale, standardized functionality, and other features, require major changes in the way the company’s operations are performed [12]. Also these changes call for the modification of beliefs, values and behaviors of organizational members. A critical factor supporting the change process is learning by observation.

The different dynamics evolving from the KUC meetings gave rise to a set of judgmental and evaluative standards modeled by those judged to be the ‘best’ key users. Stipulations were made on how members of this committee behaved, such as:

\- Everyone has to come to the meeting with clear information about what is happening in their areas.

\- Key users have to meet with their final users before coming here.

\- If you are not able to satisfy your KU role (e.g. because of time restrictions), then you must abandon the role.

\- If you are not communicating with other areas to solve common problems, you must resign from the position.

The KUC functioned as the catalyst that allowed the social network to participate in EA diffusion.

## 5.2.3. Stimulus 3 – EA outsourcing

The initial implementation of the EA was carried out by a team of seven internal people and several external consultants. The subsequent diffusion process was developed by the KUC along with an EA outsourcer. There were three reasons for outsourcing: personnel turnover, cost, and facilitating change. Of seven internal people participating in the initial implementation, five left the company, including the former CIO. Paying external consultants was too expensive for a mid-size company like CC. The EA diffusion was expected to rely on the key users and top managers’ ability to define and implement new initiatives.

## 5.2.4. Stimulus 4 – internal help desk

A number of help desks were set in place for specific functionality and organizational areas. These encouraged users to ask questions about recent implementation: the analysis of these questions allowed key users to determine what new training aids were needed or whether the system required fine-tuning. This was particularly true for the S&D area.

## 5.3. The social learning triad: personal factors

We identified differences in key users’ values, goals, and ways of thinking that changed the performance of the EA diffusion process. We found that six personal factors affected the reciprocal interaction of the social learning triad (see Table 2).

## 5.3.1. Factor 1 – thought patterns

A thought pattern is a way of thinking about our experiences. Manz and Neck defined it as a habitual ways of thinking. In our study we found that three significantly influenced EA diffusion. These were coded as dyads of opposite patterns, because of the theoretical replication design we undertook. These three ways of thinking were:

1. Individual vs. team working patterns: How a person prefers to do an activity. We found that a team-working pattern had a positive influence on achieving EA diffusion. This was true for the S&D, the purchasing management, and the transportation management key users. The L&M key user fell at the other extreme by working as an individual (see Table 2).

2. Process vs. functional patterns: How an individual understands, models and undertakes the set of activities he/she carries out. A process pattern means the person sees his/her activities as part of a whole system, that requires coordination with others. A functional pattern means that the person feels that his/her activities are isolated (see patterns adopted by key users in Table 2).

3. Openness vs. closeness patterns: The level of open-minded that a person has to new ideas or challenges. Openness focuses on constructive ways of thinking about challenging situations while closeness is the reverse (see Table 2).

## 5.3.2. Factor 2 – diffusion self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is self assessment of one’s ability to accomplish a specific task. In our study, we found that the key user’s self-efficacy in dealing with the diffusion of an EA had a major effect.

## 5.3.3. Factor 3 – self-regulatory mechanisms

Key users (and change agents in the case of S&D area) set standards of behavior for themselves and responded to their own actions with self-reward or self-punishment. In our study, we observed ‘perceptual quality’ as one of the measures adopted by key users. This is normally used for achievement-oriented activities. The judgmental function is defined by personal standards and/or a social referent. In our study, the social referent was based on the standard norms of the KUC. The different key users always evaluated themselves according to a collective comparison. Table 2 describes the norms adopted by key users.

## 5.3.4. Factor 4 – goals

Self-regulation of behavior may be driven by goals. Definite goals provide clear guides for performance. General goals provide vague guidelines for action. In our study, some areas goals were motivators while in other areas there was a lack of clear and proximal goals.

## 5.3.5. Factor 5 – prior knowledge

Individual skills play a critical role. Success requires skills and self-efficacy. We found two different types of skills influencing the EA diffusion process. These were related to key users’ prior knowledge. As a consequence organizational members can internalize and exploit it for the benefit of his/her business area. The key user’s prior knowledge is therefore coded as:

\- EA prior knowledge: Key users with prior knowledge of EA were better equipped to diffuse and encourage the use of the system. This was the case of the S&D and the purchasing management key users.

\- Project management skills: Prior knowledge by key users of project management was a key ability for the EA diffusion – using knowledge to schedule, implement, and monitor the set of activities to ensure that project goals were achieved. The S&D key user had this ability.

## 7. An SLT view of EA diffusion throughout logistics and manufacturing

Although most environmental stimuli (E) influencing the S&D area were also present for the L&M area, these two areas provided quite different results. Laws of behavior relating to external stimuli to responses are insufficient. The L&M key user showed an unfavourable set of cognitive and individual factors (P) for the EA diffusion throughout his area.

As the first triad in Fig. 5 shows, the behavior (B) of the L&M key user was that of keeping the status quo. He was not willing to diffuse the system beyond the first installation goals. We also found that the initial CEO’s prioritization left the L&M area out of the company’s first diffusion goals (E). However, this initial perspective of the CEO changed later to push diffusion within the L&M area. The individual and cognitive aspects (P) explaining this inertial behavior toward the EA diffusion are:

\- The L&M key user had extensive experience in manufacturing. He had been the area’s manager for several years and had expertise in managing tangible resources related to production systems. Coffee production is a traditional process and as a result he had little contact with new IT. He saw his area as a functional department that required little interaction with other areas. To send necessary information to other areas, he generated it internally. Changing the way things were done was perceived as being a titanic task.

\- After using the system, this key user was convinced that it was difficult to use. The need for better coordination applied to others, but not to him. Finally, his poor experience with EA, and with IT in general, meant that he lacked the confidence to introduce an IS project into his area.

Triad 2 in Fig. 5 reflects further inertial behavior toward the diffusion process. According to the L&M key user: the final users should have limited access to the system.

This way of thinking (P) prevented the development of his employees. The CEO considered that this was a serious matter. The L&M key user behavior influenced the organizational environment in a reciprocal manner.

Because EA diffusion was taking place throughout the company, this problem was visible to everyone, including users in the L&M area who were learning from their peers in other areas that the new business model was driven by EA (learning through observation (B)). Interviews carried out with L&M final-users revealed that they wanted to learn more about the system and its possibilities.

The final users’ concerns and questions were collected by the EA consultants (E) and by others, who passed them on to the key users. The KUC had an open discussion on this problem. Many conversations were initiated by the CEO with the L&M key user and subsequently the CEO reduced the key user’s power by splitting the area into three as previously explained.

## 8. Discussion and conclusions

From the two sites under study, we found that the key users behaviors toward EA diffusion were influenced by cognitive factors and organizational stimuli. Although the organizational environment was similar for both sites, one performed better than the other. Key users’ personal characteristics played a fundamental role in the process. One can see that the S&D key user and change agents were better equipped in terms of skills, prior knowledge, and self-efficacy than the L&M key user for diffusion of the EA. However, the environmental stimuli performed by the CEO, EA outsourcer, KUC and internal help desk apparently had a favourable effect on the behavior of the S&D key user and change agents. Moreover, and we concluded that inertial behavior of the L&M key user influenced the environmental stimuli. We therefore concluded that organizational factors changed their actions, as did their attempts to modify the L&M key user’s behavior. The departure of the L&M key user influenced the remaining key users and end-users, bringing about new patterns of behaviors. They learnt by observing the consequences of certain kinds of behaviors.

Defining goals for diffusion was found to be a critical factor in ensuring appropriate behavior toward diffusion. Even more significant was how the definition progressed gradually depending on the key users skills: they had to understand and assimilate basic knowledge before going ahead with more sophisticated actions such as project initiation, execution, and identification of new opportunities. Key users were continuously receiving messages, observing others, and learning from their results.

The KUC played an important role in the learning process. It was the environment in which key users exchanged knowledge and influenced one another. The KUC was the catalyst for the diffusion process. Mohrman and her colleagues [7] have explained this, saying:

New behaviors and schemata may be catalyzed through change-oriented networks such as design and implementation teams. But they take shape in the newly created work units where employees using newly developed processes and IT talk together about how best to get their work done in the new context.

## 8.1. Implications for research

A social learning perspective allows the modeling of the forces encouraging (or discouraging) the flows of learning and knowledge that explain the EA diffusion success (or failure). By using such a perspective, EA diffusion can be better understood as an ongoing process that moves backward and forward, and may never stop. From this stance, top management support remains fundamental. However, emphasis here was placed on managers being facilitators of a learning process. Project management was extremely important. Change management was also critical, but it cannot be top-down. Change must be developed through the dynamics of a bottom-up approach.

## 8.2. Implications for practice

The diffusion of an EA within an organization should follow a social learning approach based on the gradual adoption of the system throughout the firm. This implies taking a long-term perspective. This has significant implications for practitioners.

\- Organizations should put much less emphasis on the temporal aspects of projects.

\- Vendors and top managers should create the appropriate organizational conditions to encourage diffusion behaviors for different business areas.

\- Project management is not enough to manage the multidimen sional reality of EA.

The CEO should lead and create the means to facilitate the modeling of new beliefs, values and behaviors toward the diffusion of an EA within the organization.

\- Key users should be responsible for the development of appropriate behaviors toward the diffusion of a system within their business area. Also, they are responsible for creating, sharing and diffusing the knowledge appropriated from an EA transformation project.

## 8.3. Limitations

Our study was based on the behavior of key users in a single medium-sized company: thus any insight is limited to this context. In particular, our model could require adaptation when applied to larger companies.
