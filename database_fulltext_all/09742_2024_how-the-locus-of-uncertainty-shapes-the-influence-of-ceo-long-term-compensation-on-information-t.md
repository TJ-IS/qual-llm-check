# HOW THE LOCUS OF UNCERTAINTY SHAPES THE INFLUENCE OF CEO LONG-TERM COMPENSATION ON INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY CAPITAL INVESTMENTS<sup>1</sup>

Terence J. V. Saldanha and Mariana G. Andrade-Rojas Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A. {terence.saldanha@uga.edu} {andrade@uga.edu}

Abhishek Kathuria Indian School of Business, Gachibowli, Hyderabad, Telangana, INDIA {abhishek\_kathuria@isb.edu }

Jiban Khuntia Business School, University of Colorado Denver, Denver, CO, U.S.A. {jiban.khuntia@ucdenver.edu}

M.S. Krishnan Stephen M. Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, U.S.A. {mskrish@umich.edu}

Firms must allocate resources effectively to cope with uncertainty, which can manifest as a disruption and an opportunity. Although information technology (IT) is a means to cope with uncertainty, chief executive officers (CEOs) may not always support IT investments due to the risky nature of IT, especially when facing uncertain conditions. While prior research suggests that CEO long-term compensation positively incentivizes IT investments, little is known about how different loci of uncertainty impact this relationship. To address this research gap, we study how firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty shape the influence of CEO long-term compensation on a firm’s IT capital investment. Drawing on agency theory and prospect theory, we develop two hypotheses. First, we hypothesize that firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty positively moderate the influence of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. Second, we hypothesize that competitive uncertainty has a stronger positive moderating effect than firm-specific uncertainty on the influence of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. Our analysis of secondary longitudinal data from 2000 to 2007 of 357 public firms in the United States supports our hypotheses. In exploratory analyses, we found that CEO long-term compensation results in a higher riskoriented dominant logic in the firm, particularly in conditions of firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty, with competitive uncertainty having a stronger positive moderating effect. These findings uncover risk-oriented dominant logic as a theoretical mechanism that explains how CEO long-term compensation positively influences firm IT capital investment in uncertain conditions. We also conducted exploratory analyses using a different secondary dataset of 286 U.S. public firms from 2004 to 2019 to consider firm investments in transformative IT applications and found support for our theory. This finding triangulates our results across different time periods and different types of IT investments. This study contributes to theory and practice by providing a nuanced understanding of boundary conditions surrounding CEO long-term compensation, and decisions CEOs make vis-à-vis IT capital investments

Keywords: CEO long-term compensation, IT investment, chief executive officer, business value of IT, firm uncertainty, competitive uncertainty, dominant logic

## Introduction

Uncertainty, although inevitable, can serve as both a disruption and an opportunity for business. On the one hand, uncertainty can be a disruption because it “arises from unpredictable, poorly understood change, which poses knowledge problems” in understanding its effects (Rindova & Courtney, 2020, p. 788).<sup>2</sup> This means that current business knowledge has limited applicability in uncertain situations (Knight, 1921). On the other hand, uncertainty provides novel avenues to leverage growth opportunities because its effects cannot be anticipated ex ante (Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Wiseman & Bromiley, 1996).<sup>3</sup> This dual nature of uncertainty requires firms to allocate their resources effectively in response to uncertain conditions (Rindova & Courtney, 2020).

Uncertainty can impact a firm’s allocation of resources to information technology capital investment<sup>4,5</sup> (hereafter we use “IT investment” and “IT capital investment” interchangeably), which is distinctive compared to other investments (e.g., advertising, research and development).<sup>6</sup> Given the risky (Maruping et al., 2019; Xin & Choudhary, 2019), strategic, and distinctive nature of information technology (IT) investments (Dewan & Ren, 2011; Dewan et al., 2007), chief executive officers (CEOs) are often actively involved in shaping the firm’s digital strategies and driving IT investments (Cao et al., 2006; Favaro, 2016). While CEOs allocate budgets to IT functions, chief information officers (CIOs) distribute the allocated budgets across various line items (Masli et al., 2014). CEOs’ active role in IT capital investment decisions is reflected in how they champion IT in the firm and emphasize IT in investor calls (Gartner, 2016). Although CEOs play a key role in making IT investment decisions (Hall & Liedtka, 2005; Masli et al., 2014), CEOs may not support IT investments due to their risky nature, particularly in uncertain conditions. Hence, there is a need to understand how to incentivize CEOs to make IT capital investments in uncertain conditions.

Prior research suggests that compensation motivates a CEO’s actions (Devers et al., 2007). One crucial action for CEOs is resource allocation, which includes making decisions related to IT capital investments and other capital allocations (Masli et al., 2014). Since it is difficult for boards to monitor investment opportunities, long-term compensation helps boards incentivize CEOs to make certain types of investments (Baber et al., 1996). Long-term compensation includes stock options and other forms of equity-based compensation tied to objectives achieved over long periods, typically three to five years (Cummings & Knott, 2018; Hall & Liedtka, 2005).<sup>7</sup> Although prior research has studied the effect of CEO compensation on IT investment and IT outsourcing (Hall & Liedtka, 2005; Masli et al., 2014),<sup>8</sup> scant research has considered how uncertainty shapes the effect of CEO longterm compensation on IT investments.

Addressing this research gap is theoretically and practically important because prior research (Masli et al., 2014) has identified the positive effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment. Since uncertainty has not been considered, prior research has largely assumed that the impact of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment is homogeneous across different uncertain conditions in the firm and the industry. However, because firms operate in heterogeneous conditions of uncertainty, CEO long-term compensation may not have the desired incentivizing effect on IT investment. By addressing this research gap, we enrich the theoretical and practical understanding of how heterogeneous uncertainty conditions influence the impact of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment.

We focus on two types (or loci) of uncertainty. Competitive uncertainty is defined as uncertainty in the overall competitive environment—that is, the industry of the focal firm (Miller et al., 2002; Mithas et al., 2013). Competitive uncertainty can arise from factors such as economic cycles, the evolution of customer preferences, demographic changes, institutional factors, and competitors’ actions (Oriani & Sobrero, 2008; Sutcliffe & Zaheer, 1998). Firmspecific uncertainty is defined as uncertainty that is unique to a focal firm (Beckman et al., 2004; Miller et al., 2002). This includes uncertainty related to firm operations, new market entry, management turnovers, and health-related emergencies of key decision-makers (Beckman et al., 2004).<sup>9</sup> Our theoretical framework takes a holistic perspective on how CEOs make IT capital investment decisions while considering uncertainty as a disruption and an opportunity. We draw on agency theory (Eisenhardt, 1988) from an opportunity perspective and on prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) from a disruption perspective to address our research question: How does the locus of uncertainty faced by a firm (i.e., firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty) shape the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment?

Our analysis of secondary data from 357 U.S. public firms from 2000 to 2007 yields two key findings. First, firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty positively moderate the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. Second, competitive uncertainty has a stronger positive moderating effect than firm-specific uncertainty on the influence of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. In an exploratory analysis, drawing on the concept of firm dominant logic, we validated a theoretical mechanism that underlies the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT investment. This analysis supports our argument that CEO long-term compensation fosters a risk-oriented dominant logic<sup>10</sup> in the firm and explains a mechanism for how CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment under competitive uncertainty and firm-specific uncertainty. Further, using different and more recent data from 286 U.S. public firms from 2004 to 2019, we found that our theory holds for transformative IT applications, which are riskier than other types of IT applications (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004).

Our study makes three theoretical contributions at the intersection of the literatures on IT and compensation incentives, IT and uncertainty, and antecedents of IT investment (See Appendix Figure A1). First, we augment the understanding of the link between CEO long-term compensation and firm IT investment. Prior research has mainly considered this link in the context of agency theory perspectives, addressing inherent risks in IT investment. We contribute by using prospect theory and agency theory to identify uncertainty as a salient condition that strengthens the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment. Second, we uncover the locus of uncertainty (i.e., firm-specific or competitive) as crucial in shaping the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment. Third, our exploratory analysis uncovers risk-oriented dominant logic as a theoretical mechanism that explains how

CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment in uncertain conditions. Our results support our theory and offer insights for board members to set CEO long-term incentives based on the firm’s locus of uncertainty.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## Firm-specific Uncertainty and Competitive Uncertainty

Uncertainty involves business situations in which the possible outcomes and the probabilities associated with such outcomes are largely unknown to CEOs (Knight, 1921). Based on Knight’s (1921) logic, uncertainty, by definition, arises from unforeseen factors (e.g., unexpected employee errors, vendors going out of business unexpectedly, and unanticipated changes in customer preferences). Uncertainty is characterized by surprises, “black-swan” events, and a lack of predictability (Teece et al., 2016) and is defined as the “difficulty firms [or CEOs] have in predicting the future, which comes from incomplete knowledge” (Beckman et al., 2004, p. 260). Knowledge here refers to business facts, information, or knowhow. In uncertain conditions, knowledge problems pertain to difficulties in “enumerating, classifying, and estimating large number of effects among interacting factors” (Rindova & Courtney, 2020, p. 788). In uncertain conditions, there is a partial knowledge problem because business leaders take actions based on “neither entire ignorance nor complete and perfect information, but partial knowledge.” (Knight, 1921, p. 199). Such partial knowledge takes the form of a lack of information about outcomes and the probability of outcomes.

Prior research argues for a differentiated view of uncertainty because uncertainties arising from different sources act independently and have different implications (Gaba & Terlaak, 2013; Sutcliffe & Zaheer, 1998). While firms can be exposed to adverse environmental conditions, firms themselves are a complex blend of people, products, management, and operations, each with their own unpredictability (Beckman et al., 2004). Thus, research classifies uncertainty into two types: “uncertainty that is idiosyncratic to a particular organization [i.e., firm-specific uncertainty] and uncertainty that can be attributed to environmental or macroeconomic forces [i.e., competitive uncertainty]” (Henley et al., 2003; Miller et al., 2002, p. 748). Firm-specific uncertainty arises from internal factors (e.g., top management turnovers, operations) or external factors (e.g., relations with external partners, customers’ preferences for a firm’s products or services) that affect a focal firm only (Beckman et al., 2004). In contrast, competitive uncertainty arises from factors such as economic cycles, demographic changes, or institutional factors that affect all firms in an industry (Oriani & Sobrero, 2008; Sutcliffe & Zaheer, 1998). For example, competitive uncertainty may arise from external disruptions to a supply chain (e.g., pandemic, natural disasters) that affect all firms in an industry. This categorization of different loci of uncertainty provides a valuable framework for studying the moderating effect of uncertainty on the impact of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT investment.<sup>11</sup>

Integrating the concept of the partial knowledge problem with the two types of uncertainty, we suggest that the partial knowledge problem is more severe in the case of competitive uncertainty than in the case of firm-specific uncertainty for two reasons. First, competitive uncertainty involves a higher number of unpredictable interdependencies among factors in the competitive environment with unknown consequences (Rindova & Courtney, 2020). As a result, competitive uncertainty is more random than firm-specific uncertainty (Ghemawat, 1991), and the domain and knowledge space for a CEO is larger (Rindova & Courtney, 2020). In contrast, firm-specific uncertainty involves fewer interdependencies among factors, resulting in less randomness and smaller domain and knowledge space for CEOs. Second, CEOs have greater insight into firm-level changes than industry-wide changes. For example, for the CEO of an automotive firm, the unpredictability of industry-wide changes is greater than the unpredictability of changes unique to the focal firm. Thus, the partial knowledge problem is more severe in the case of competitive uncertainty because competitive uncertainty arises from industry-wide interdependencies. In contrast, firmspecific uncertainty arises from interdependencies that are unique to a focal firm. In sum, the greater randomness, larger number of interdependencies, and less familiarity of CEOs with industrywide changes imply a more severe partial knowledge problem in competitive uncertainty than in firm-specific uncertainty.<sup>12</sup>

Prior research has recognized that uncertainty can serve as a disruption and an opportunity. On the one hand, uncertainty “reflects the lack of information or knowledge, which translates into difficulties in accurately assessing current and future decision situations” (Gaba & Terlaak, 2013, p. 1847). For example, “non-disclosure, disguise or distortion of information” by supply chain partners (i.e., an illustration of firm-specific uncertainty) is a disruption that exacerbates the partial knowledge problem for a focal firm (Krishnan et al., 2016, p. 2523). Another example of firm-specific uncertainty as a disruption occurs when a firm’s vendor unexpectedly goes out of business; this situation can hinder the firm’s capacity to manufacture products. On the other hand, uncertainty can also provide novel opportunities for growth because its effects cannot be anticipated (Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Wiseman & Bromiley, 1996). For example, uncertainty in the electric vehicle industry arising from regulation (i.e., an illustration of competitive uncertainty) presents opportunities for automakers to grow their market share by developing their electric vehicle capabilities. Another example is when firms experience unexpected operational failures (i.e., an illustration of firmspecific uncertainty) that spark innovations in research and development (R&D) teams and thereby offer opportunities for new product development. ## Agency Theory and CEO Long-term Compensation

Agency theory suggests that shareholders are neutral concerning the risky investment decisions by individual firms because shareholders can distribute their wealth across firms (Milgrom & Roberts, 1992). Executives, meanwhile, are more averse to risky investments than shareholders since executives’ wealth is directly affected by the firm’s performance (Coles et al., 2006; Fama, 1980). Thus, agency costs are incurred when executives avoid risky investments and take actions that result in lower returns for shareholders (Wiseman & Gomez-Mejia, 1998). Accordingly, executives need to be incentivized to make investments that, albeit risky, can yield long-term and higher returns.

Compensation, comprising short-term and long-term components, can incentivize CEOs to make risky investments (Westphal & Zajac, 1994). Integrating agency theory with the research on CEO compensation suggests that CEOs compensated through long-term incentives have long decisionmaking horizons; in turn, they make decisions that are more in line with shareholders’ interests and risk tolerance (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Sanders, 2001). CEOs aim to maximize their returns on the stock options and restrictive stock awards linked to their long-term compensation (Banker et al., 2012). Only if the stock price is greater than the option price at the time of vesting can a CEO turn stock options and equity-based pay constituting their long-term compensation into payoffs (Sanders, 2001). Long-term compensation thus aligns a CEO’s goals with shareholders’ goals by motivating the CEO to focus on actions that maximize shareholder value (Fama, 1980; Jensen & Meckling, 1976).

## Effect of CEO Long-term Compensation on IT Capital Investment

Although prior studies have identified factors (e.g., compensation) that influence CEOs’ IT investment decisions, the question of how uncertainty shapes the effect of CEO compensation on IT investment represents a significant research gap (Appendix Table A1 shows studies on antecedents of IT investments). Addressing this gap is theoretically and practically important because prior research (Masli et al., 2014) has assumed that the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment is homogeneous across different uncertainty conditions in the firm and the industry. Our study enriches the theoretical understanding of how heterogeneous uncertainty conditions shape the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment.

The influence of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment is based on two key premises. First, IT capital investments pay off over an extended period, often three to five years (Hitt et al., 2002). Second, IT capital investments can provide extraordinarily high returns but are risky relative to other investments (Dewan et al., 2007) due to technological complexity, implementation challenges (Dewan & Ren, 2011), and the need for complementary organizational changes (Melville et al., 2004).<sup>14,</sup> <sup>15</sup>

Figure 1 shows our research model. Based on agency theory and prior research (Masli et al., 2014), we expect CEO long-term compensation to positively impact IT capital investment for two reasons. First, agency theory suggests that long-term compensation incentivizes CEOs (i.e., agents) to adopt a long planning horizon, which aligns their interests with the interests of shareholders (i.e., principals) (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Hence, long-term compensation increases CEOs’ willingness to make investments such as IT capital investments with longterm payoffs (Richardson & Waegelein, 2002). Second, agency theory suggests that long-term compensation increases CEOs’ willingness to make risky strategic decisions (Devers et al., 2007; Guay, 1999). Thus, long-term compensation aligns the risk orientations of CEOs and shareholders by incentivizing CEOs to undertake risky investments (Datta et al., 2001).

## Agency Theory, Prospect Theory, and Uncertainty

Our theoretical framework takes a holistic perspective of how CEOs make IT capital investment decisions by considering uncertainty’s nature as a disruption and opportunity. For the opportunity perspective on uncertainty, we draw on agency theory (Eisenhardt, 1988); we draw on prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) for the disruption perspective. Agency theory helps theorize how long-term compensation incentivizes CEOs to use IT capital investment to leverage the growth opportunities afforded by uncertainty (e.g., investing in business intelligence systems to analyze sales trends helps firms find new market opportunities). IT capital investments help firms grow in the long term (Mithas & Rust, 2016; Mithas et al., 2012), thus reaping benefits for CEOs in the form of stock-based compensation. Long-term compensation aligns CEO and shareholder interests by incentivizing CEOs to make IT capital investments to benefit from growth opportunities arising from uncertainty, as IT investments favor future firm and stock performance.

Focusing on uncertainty as a disruption, we draw on prospect theory to posit the moderating role of uncertainty. Prospect theory suggests that executives think in terms of gains and losses rather than in terms of their net assets; thus, they encode choices in terms of deviations from a reference point (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Levy, 1992). The reference point can be the status quo or deviations from an aspirational level (Levy, 1992). For CEOs, “carriers of value or utility are changes of wealth, rather than final asset positions that include current wealth” (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, p. 273). Research applying prospect theory to the executive compensation context notes that executive compensation motivates risk-averse or risk-seeking behaviors “depending on whether recipients frame the potential consequences of those behaviors as gains or losses” (Holmes et al., 2011, p. 1081) (For a review of the application of prospect theory in the management literature, see Holmes et al., 2011). For example, Wiseman and Gomez-Mejia (1998) suggest that executives are loss-averse and often set stock options’ exercise price as the reference point. Based on prospect theory, loss aversion concerns the “avoidance of loss, even if this means accepting higher risk” and “explains executives’ preference for riskier actions to avoid an anticipated loss altogether over less risky options to merely minimize the loss” (Tversky & Kahneman, 1986; Wiseman & Gomez-Mejia, 1998, p. 135).

Since uncertainty can be a disruption, prospect theory helps theorize why long-term compensation motivates CEOs to cope with uncertainty using IT capital investment to avoid losses. IT capital investment, albeit risky due to the likelihood of failure (Dewan et al., 2007), helps firms cope with uncertainty by improving decision-making and access to data (Andrade-Rojas et al., 2021; Mithas et al., 2012). For instance, cybersecurity investments help firms cope with uncertainty by helping them anticipate, prevent, and respond to cyber threats and cyberattacks that can hurt future firm and stock performance.

Together, agency theory and prospect theory support the development of our arguments on how uncertainty moderates the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT investment.

## The Moderating Effects of Firm-Specific Uncertainty and Competitive Uncertainty

Two fundamental premises undergird our theorizing of the moderating effects of uncertainty. First, uncertainty is a disruption that “arises from unpredictable, poorly understood change” (Rindova & Courtney, 2020, p. 78). Uncertainty poses the problem of incomplete or partial knowledge about change, thereby limiting the applicability of current knowledge to understand uncertain situations (Knight, 1921; Rindova & Courtney, 2020). For example, a global pandemic or natural disaster can disrupt a firm’s business operations or the demand for its products or services. Second, uncertainty can provide novel possibilities to leverage growth opportunities because its effects cannot be anticipated (Wiseman & Bromiley, 1996). For example, the same global pandemic or natural disaster can increase the demand for a firm’s products or services and create new opportunities for firms that support remote work.

Our first hypothesis stems from three arguments. First, IT capital investment helps firms grow in the long term by increasing revenues through the exploration of new customers, channels, products, and services (Mithas & Rust, 2016; Mithas et al., 2012). CEOs, in turn, reap the benefits of these enhanced revenue streams in the value of their long-term compensation. Due to the partial knowledge problem, the effects of uncertainty cannot be anticipated; as a result, uncertainty offers novel possibilities to leverage growth opportunities (Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Wiseman & Bromiley, 1996). According to agency theory, long-term compensation aligns CEOs’ and shareholders’ interests by incentivizing CEOs to make IT capital investments that benefit from the growth opportunities (e.g., higher future stock prices due to firm growth) arising from uncertainty. Thus, in uncertain conditions, agency theory supports the argument that the incentivizing effect of long-term compensation on CEOs in terms of making IT capital investments is strengthened. As anecdotal evidence, a CEO of a major global airline indicated that long-term compensation motivated them to invest in IT amid the uncertainty of the SARS epidemic to capitalize on future growth opportunities.

Second, under conditions characterized by a high degree of uncertainty, the partial knowledge problem is, by definition, more severe compared to situations facing a low degree of uncertainty. The severity of the partial knowledge problem increases the importance of a CEO’s risk orientation (Knight, 1921; Rindova & Courtney, 2020). When the partial knowledge problem is less severe, CEOs react more objectively because they have more knowledge to rely on. In contrast, when the partial knowledge problem is more severe due to the absence of knowledge to guide their actions, CEOs are more likely to take actions based on their risk orientation (Knight, 1921). Since long-term compensation helps shape CEOs’ orientation toward riskier investments, the partial knowledge problem makes the effect of their long-term compensation stronger in highuncertainty conditions compared to low-uncertainty conditions. Hence, the importance of long-term compensation in influencing a CEO’s actions (to make IT capital investments) is greater in high-uncertainty conditions than it is in lowuncertainty conditions.

Third, uncertainty can function as a disruption that increases the chance of poor firm performance due to partial knowledge regarding change (Koufteros et al., 2005; Rindova & Courtney, 2020). Poor firm performance can impact the value of a CEO’s stock-based holdings (long-term compensation), leading to personal financial loss for the CEO. The unpredictable change caused by uncertainty reinforces the need for CEOs to be forward-looking to address disruptions arising from uncertainty (Rindova & Courtney, 2020). By facilitating information access, storage, and retrieval (Andrade-Rojas et al., 2021; Mithas et al., 2011), IT capital investment mitigates the partial knowledge problem, which is particularly important in uncertain conditions (Sage & Rouse, 1999).<sup>16</sup> As prospect theory suggests, when a firm faces uncertainty, the need to avoid potential loss from long-term compensation shapes a CEO’s decisions in favor of IT capital investment to mitigate the partial knowledge problem regarding uncertain change.

We propose that the above arguments hold for both types of uncertainty: firm-specific and competitive.<sup>17</sup> In uncertain conditions, CEOs are more likely to reap the benefits of payouts from long-term compensation by supporting IT capital investments. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1: Uncertainty, in the form of (a) firm-specific uncertainty and (b) competitive uncertainty, positively moderates the influence of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment.

Our second hypothesis concerns the relative strengths of firmspecific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty in moderating the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. We build this hypothesis by again drawing on agency theory and prospect theory. First, the partial knowledge problem increases the importance of a CEO’s risk orientation toward the disruptions and opportunities posed by uncertainty (Knight, 1921; Rindova & Courtney, 2020). As discussed earlier, when the partial knowledge problem is more severe, CEOs lack the knowledge to guide their actions and are thus more likely to take actions based on their risk orientation (Knight, 1921). Since long-term compensation increases a CEO’s risk orientation (based on agency theory), the resultant higher risk orientation leads a CEO to make IT capital investments to leverage the growth opportunities (Mithas & Rust, 2016; Mithas et al., 2012) presented by uncertainty. Thus, the importance of CEO long-term compensation in incentivizing IT capital investment is amplified under competitive uncertainty, where the partial knowledge problem is more severe than it is under firm-specific uncertainty.

Second, due to the more severe partial knowledge problem faced by CEOs under competitive uncertainty relative to firmspecific uncertainty, competitive uncertainty poses a greater threat to the CEO’s personal wealth from long-term compensation than firm-specific uncertainty does. For example, CEOs can take steps to better cope with the effects of firm-specific uncertainty because they have more firmspecific knowledge to adapt industry best practices to their firm’s context. Although not all cases of firm-specific uncertainty may be countered effectively, the opportunities to respond to firm-specific uncertainty are greater than those available to counter competitive uncertainty. IT capital investment allays the partial knowledge problem by facilitating information access, storage, and retrieval (Andrade-Rojas et al., 2021; Mithas et al., 2011). As noted earlier, based on prospect theory, to prevent value loss for their long-term compensation, CEOs are more incentivized to make IT capital investments to address the disruption from competitive uncertainty compared to that from firm-specific uncertainty. This argument resonates with prior research, which suggests that to address the partial knowledge problem at the firm level and at the competitive level, senior executives adopt different resource allocation patterns (Rindova & Courtney, 2020). Hence, we hypothesize:

H2: Compared to firm-specific uncertainty, competitive uncertainty has a stronger positive moderating effect on the influence of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment.

## Method

## Data and Variables

We collected archival data from secondary sources on publicly listed firms in the United States from 2000 to 2007. The data sources include Execucomp, Compustat, BoardEx, Security and Exchange Commission filings, the Institutional Shareholder Services database, the Federal Judicial Center database, and an archival IT dataset collected by InformationWeek. InformationWeek is a prominent research firm that collects data annually from large U.S. firms on IT investments through surveys of CIOs and senior IT managers (Mithas et al., 2012). InformationWeek is a reliable dataset that has been used in many prior academic studies (e.g., Mithas et al., 2013; Sabherwal et al., 2019; Saldanha et al., 2017; Tafti et al., 2013).

IT capital investment (ITCapInv): IT capital investment includes investment in infrastructure hardware technology purchases and IT-related R&D. We excluded operational IT investments incurred to maintain and upgrade current systems. We first calculated a firm’s IT capital investment as a percentage of sales revenue in a focal year. Then, following Mithas et al. (2013), we computed ITCapInv as the percentage of deviation of the firm’s IT capital investment from the industry average IT capital investment (excluding the focal firm). This procedure is consistent with approaches that use industry average IT investment as a benchmark for a firm’s IT investment decision-making (Ho et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2013) and accounts for industry idiosyncrasies and industry-specific imperatives that may influence a firm’s IT investment decisions. Although we used the industry average in our analysis, the industry median does not change the results. Our results were also unchanged when we used unadjusted IT capital investment values.

CEO long-term compensation (LTC): Following prior studies, we operationalized CEO long-term compensation as the “proportion of CEO pay that is stock options or restricted stock” and computed LTC as the percentage of the CEO’s total compensation comprised of restricted stock and stock option grants (Cummings & Knott, 2018, p. 2107; Lerner & Wulf, 2007). This measure is appropriate for our study because it captures the long-term component of CEO compensation and is widely used in the strategy literature (e.g., Cummings & Knott, 2018).

Firm-specific uncertainty (FirmUncert): Following prior research (Beckman et al., 2004), we operationalized firmspecific uncertainty as the coefficient of variation of the firm’s monthly stock closing price for the previous year. As Beckman et al. (2004, p. 265) noted, “stock price volatility is a useful measure of uncertainty … because a high degree of price volatility is likely to correspond to managerial perceptions of uncertainty, which in turn are likely to impact a firm’s decision making.” For robustness, we used weekly price values and found stronger results. To ensure consistency with prior research (Beckman et al., 2004), however, we used the conservative monthly measure of firm-specific uncertainty.

The mean of the relative firm-specific variation $( \ln ( \tt { \varepsilon } _ { i } ^ { 2 } ) - \ln ( \tt { \varepsilon } _ { s } ^ { 2 } ) )$ at the industry level is the value of CompUncert (Mithas et al., 2013). For robustness, we conducted analysis using the mean monthly stock price volatility of all firms in the industry (excluding the focal firm) (Beckman et al., 2004). Although our measures of firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty are based on prior research (Beckman et al., 2004; Mithas et al., 2013), we repeated the analysis by computing firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty as the volatility of sales at the firm level and industry level, respectively (Sabherwal et al., 2019; Saldanha et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2011; Xue et al., 2012).

Control variables: We included firm size and free cash flow to control for resource and financial endowments that could impact IT investment (Mithas et al., 2013; Rojas et al., 2018). We controlled for profitability and sales growth because profitable and growing firms can afford more IT investments (Kobelsky et al., 2008). We controlled for firm Leverage because high debt may limit the funds available for IT capital investment (Kobelsky et al., 2008). We controlled for firm IT outsourcing, market-to-book ratio, and diversification since these variables can impact IT investment (Choi et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2017). We controlled for CEO age, tenure, and gender as factors that may shape a CEO’s views of IT (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). We controlled for industry concentration (an inverse measure of competition) (Ray et al., 2009; Xin & Choudhary, 2019), industry growth (a measure of industry munificence), and industry turbulence, all of which can impact firm IT investment (Mithas et al., 2013). Industry dummies (at the two-digit NAICS level) accounted for industry idiosyncrasies beyond the other industry-level variables (Tafti et al., 2013), and year dummies accounted for time heterogeneity. We lagged all the independent variables and control variables by one year prior to the dependent variable to ensure temporal precedence and to strengthen causal inference.

## Estimation Models and Econometric Considerations

We used panel regression models due to the longitudinal nature of our data. We reported fixed-effect model estimates because Hausman tests indicate that fixed-effect models are consistent, whereas random-effect models may be inconsistent (Greene, 2003; Hausman, 1978). Fixed-effect models rule out unobservable firm-level confounding factors (e.g., corporate culture) that are relatively invariant within firms and over time. We estimate the following specification:

ITCapInv<sub>i,</sub> $\mathsf { \Omega } _ { \mathrm { t } + 1 } = \mathsf { \Omega } \beta _ { 0 } + \mathsf { \Omega } \beta _ { 1 } \mathrm { \ L T C } \mathrm { _ { i , \ t } } + \mathsf { \beta } \beta _ { 2 }$ FirmUncert<sub>i,</sub> $ { t } +  { \beta 3 }$ CompUncert<sub>i,</sub> $_ { \mathrm { ~ t ~ } } + \ \beta _ { 4 } \ \mathrm { L T C _ { i , \mathrm { ~ t ~ } } } \times$ FirmUncert<sub>i,</sub> $\mathbf { \Phi } _ { \mathrm { t } } + \mathsf { \beta } _ { \mathsf { S } } \mathbf { L T C } _ { \mathrm { i , ~ t ~ } } \times$ $\mathrm { C o m p U n c e r t _ { i , t } + \beta _ { C } \mathrm { X } _ { i , t } + \varepsilon _ { i , t + 1 , } }$ (3)

where the $\beta \mathrm { s }$ are the coefficients, X is the vector of control variables, and ε is the error term. To assess whether autocorrelation was an issue, we conducted robustness tests by imposing an autoregressive AR1 structure in the error terms to account for autocorrelation. We checked for autocorrelation using the Baltagi-Wu Locally Best Invariant test, which is a modified Durbin-Watson test for serial autocorrelation in unequally spaced unbalanced panel data (Baltagi & Wu, 1999). We used Wooldridge’s (2002) test for serial correlation in panel data. Based on the idiosyncratic errors of a linear panel data model, this is a Wald test with a null hypothesis of no autocorrelation. These tests detected no evidence of autocorrelation.

## Accounting for Endogeneity

Although firm-level variables may be endogenous to firm-level decisions, our model specifications suffer minimally from this issue because (1) the independent variable CEO long-term compensation is exogenous to firm-level decisions as it is determined by the corporate board, (2) the moderator variable competitive uncertainty is exogenous to firm decisions, and (3) the moderator variable firm-specific uncertainty is only partially attributable to managerial decisions, especially in large firms such as those in our sample.

Nonetheless, we account for three key considerations related to endogeneity. First, a firm’s historical circumstances may affect its IT investment such that a dynamic effect might explain its investment in IT. Second, the relationships among the variables can be confounded by unobserved (to researchers) firm or CEO characteristics that may influence CEO long-term compensation, firm-specific uncertainty, or IT investment. Third, reverse causality can be a source of endogeneity if more IT-intensive firms provide higher longterm compensation to their CEOs. To account for such endogeneity, we used the Arellano-Bond dynamic panel model, which is a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator that addresses problems arising when the regressors can be correlated with the error term (Arellano & Bond, 1991). This model works similarly to two-stage least squares, but instruments are constructed from moment conditions, utilizing the panel’s time dimension. The Arellano-Bond model is widely used to address endogeneity because it addresses problems of dynamic endogeneity, simultaneity, and unobserved heterogeneity (Kitchens et al., 2018; Menon & Kohli, 2013; Saldanha et al., 2021; Saldanha et al., 2020; Tambe & Hitt, 2013). The Arellano-Bond model accounts for the three above considerations. First, the model incorporates dynamics by including the lagged dependent variable (Greene, 2003). Second, because the model uses a firstdifferences approach, time-invariant heterogeneity (e.g., corporate culture) is differenced out of the model. Third, the model allows for feedback effects. For example, an increase in IT investment in one year may introduce feedback to changes in firm-specific uncertainty in the subsequent year. Such feedback, if not accounted for, can violate the assumption of exogeneity.

We used the one-step version of the Arellano-Bond estimator since the two-step version can have downwardbiased standard errors, which could inflate the significances (Bond, 2002). We used robust standard errors to account for heteroscedasticity. We repeated the analyses using two-step estimators and Windmeijer (2005) corrected cluster-robust standard errors, both of which yielded similar results. Arellano and Bond (1991) showed that lagged values of the dependent variable can serve as instruments for the firstdifferenced dependent variables, and lagged values of the endogenous variables can serve as their own instruments. We used a conservative specification, treated all firm-level variables as endogenous, and used lags of the variables as instruments (Greene, 2003). We treated the industry control variables (e.g., industry concentration) as exogenous because industry-level variables are largely beyond the control of a specific firm. We performed Sargan’s J tests (Sargan, 1958) to assess instrument validity by examining the moment conditions. In all the models, the null of Sargan’s J test is not rejected, suggesting that the instruments are appropriate (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Sargan, 1958). In dynamic panel estimators, a large number of instruments may inflate the p-values (Roodman, 2009). Although prior literature provides no formal guideline on the number of instruments, prior research suggests a rule of thumb to restrict the instruments to be fewer than the number of units (Roodman, 2009). To test the sensitivity of our results, we estimated many specifications by limiting the number of lags used as instruments. Our findings are substantively unchanged and are not sensitive to limits on the number of instruments.

The Arellano-Bond dynamic panel model assumes a lack of autocorrelation of the error terms. Based on the assumption of serial independence in the original error terms, the residuals of the differenced equation, although serially correlated by construction, should not be second-order autocorrelated (Arellano & Bond, 1991). If the residuals are autocorrelated, then lagged values of the dependent variable cannot serve as instruments for the lagged difference of the dependent variable. In all the models, the Arellano and Bond (1991) tests for second-order autocorrelation in the residuals do not reject the null of no second-order autocorrelation. We used robust standard errors in all the models. In further robustness analysis, we used external instruments.

In another approach to account for endogeneity, we estimated a fixed-effect instrumental variable model that uses an external instrument. To instrument CEO long-term compensation, we used the average CEO long-term compensation of all firms in the same industry (excluding the focal firm) because the compensation structures of CEOs at peer firms in the same industry are likely to be similar since long-term compensation, such as option grants, are influenced by industry-wide option granting practices (Hanlon et al., 2003). Moreover, CEO compensation for other firms in the industry would be unlikely to directly affect a firm’s IT capital investment. In a robustness analysis, we also used an external instrument for firmspecific uncertainty.

Another concern may be that stock options may incentivize CEOs to focus on short-term benefits (McLure, 2021; Zhang et al., 2008). Another concern is whether firm-specific uncertainty may be influenced by managerial decision-making. This concern is mitigated by (1) the theoretical argument that firm-specific uncertainty is distinct from risk (Knight, 1921; Teece et al., 2016); (2) the argument that firm-specific uncertainty is only partly determined by managerial decisions, especially in large firms such as those in the InformationWeek sample; and (3) our dynamic panel models that account for the endogeneity of firm-specific uncertainty. Even so, we empirically addressed this concern by regressing firmspecific uncertainty on the lagged value of CEO long-term compensation. The coefficient of LTC is non-significant (p > 0.10, Appendix Table A4, Column 6), suggesting no significant impact of CEO long-term compensation on firmspecific uncertainty. We included further lags of LTC in the model; the coefficients of all lags are statistically nonsignificant. Our approach to mitigate this concern is consistent with studies using similar approaches to rule out similar concerns (Cheng et al., 2021; Mithas et al., 2022). Our use of dynamic panel and instrumental variable estimators strengthens model identification and allay concerns of endogeneity regarding firm-specific uncertainty.

We address a concern about cross-sectional dependence due to contemporaneous correlations arising from unobserved common factors. Due to market factors, a CEO’s yearly compensation may be correlated with the compensation paid by other firms to their CEOs in the same year. To address this concern, we used the Driscoll-Kraay estimator, which is robust to general forms of spatial and temporal correlation (Driscoll & Kraay, 1998). The results (Appendix Table A4, Column 7) remain substantively unchanged. We also estimated fixed-effect models and applied a weak cross-sectional dependence test on the regression errors (Pesaran, 2015). In all the models, the test statistic is nonsignificant $( p \ > \ 0 . 1 0 )$ , implying no significant cross-sectional dependence problem.

To assess the specificity of our results, we tested whether our findings held for other types of investments, such as R&D and advertising. The results are shown in Appendix Table A4 (Columns 8-11). We found that CEO long-term compensation positively impacted R&D investment, consistent with our argument that risk-oriented dominant logic (which includes R&D investment) explains how CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment (See the next section). We found no moderating effect of firmspecific uncertainty or competitive uncertainty $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ on the impact of CEO long-term compensation on R&D investment. We found no significant direct effect of CEO long-term compensation nor the moderating effect of competitive uncertainty or firm-specific uncertainty on advertising investment. These results differ from our results for IT capital investment, boosting our confidence in the specificity of our results for IT capital investment. In line with our theory, compared to other investments (e.g., advertising, R&D), our findings show that IT capital investment may be more effective in coping with disruption and seizing the opportunities created by uncertainty.

Finally, to address a concern that R&D investments, boards of directors, and CEOs’ confidence in their ability to pursue strategic choices (Choi et al., 2021) may impact IT investment, we conducted a robustness analysis that included additional control variables for R&D investment, board size, board independence, CEO IT education, CEO IT experience, CEO non-IT experience, and CEO experience in other firms (Table 1 shows the measurement of the variables). ## Exploratory Analysis of Underlying Theoretical Mechanism: Risk-Oriented Dominant Logic

We explore one of the mechanisms underlying our arguments. Since long-term compensation aligns the CEO’s and shareholders’ risk orientations, agency theory suggests that CEO long-term compensation increases a firm’s risk orientation. To test this mechanism, we leveraged the concept of firm dominant logic, which builds on managerial dominant logic. Managerial dominant logic is “the way in which managers conceptualize the business and make critical resource allocation decisions—be it in technologies, product development, distribution, advertising, or in human resource management” (Prahalad & Bettis, 1986, p. 490). A firm’s dominant logic is “a reflection of managerial dominant logic,” and is “an organizational-level phenomenon” comprising of “a system of expectations, beliefs, and priorities that are embedded in the firm’s routines, procedures, and resource commitments” (Bettis & Prahalad, 1995; Kor & Mesko, 2013, p. 235).

We focus on firm risk-oriented dominant logic because we argue that CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment by increasing a CEO’s risk affinity. In line with the definition of dominant logic, we operationalized firm dominant logic by resource commitments that reflect a firm’s orientation to risk (See Table 5). Accordingly, we operationalized firm risk-oriented dominant logic by computing two aspects of a firm’s risk-oriented resource commitments: long-term financial liability and R&D intensity. Long-term financial liability, measured as the debt-equity ratio, reflects a focus on long-term debt, which is riskier than short-term debt, given its reliance on future revenue. The debt-equity ratio is a measure of financial risk since the more debt a firm acquires, the greater the risk the firm may be forced to liquidate and go out of business (Faff et al., 2002; Hagerman & Ratchford, 1978). R&D intensity reflects a firm’s exploration to develop new products or services, which is more risk-oriented than exploiting existing products or services (Chen et al., 2016). Thus, long-term financial liability and R&D intensity are resource commitments that represent the firm risk-oriented dominant logic because they relate to the search for new opportunities. Since IT capital investment is risky, it is a manifestation of the firm’s risk-oriented dominant logic.

Table 6 shows the results. In Column 1, LTC is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that CEO longterm compensation increases firm risk-oriented dominant logic. In Column 2, LTC  Firm-specific uncertainty (β = 0.012, p < 0.01) and LTC  Competitive uncertainty (β = 0.183, p < 0.01) are positive and significant, suggesting that the positive effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm risk-oriented dominant logic is stronger for firms that face high firm-specific uncertainty and high competitive uncertainty. The moderating effect of competitive uncertainty on firm risk-oriented dominant logic is stronger than that of firm-specific uncertainty (F-test = 21.64, p < 0.01). These results support our argument that CEO longterm compensation fosters a more risk-oriented firm dominant logic, which is a theoretical mechanism for how CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment under firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty. This approach is similar to prior information systems studies that use exploratory analyses to theoretically validate hypothesized firm-level phenomena (Karhade & Dong, 2021).

## Validation Analysis Using Transformative IT Applications

Our core thesis is that uncertainty moderates the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investments, which are inherently risky. To validate this assertion, we conducted exploratory analysis using data on transformative IT applications. These applications are riskier than non-transformative IT applications because (1) transformative IT applications redefine business processes or introduce radical business models that disrupt industry practices (Dehning et al., 2003; Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004)

and (2) the implementation risk of transformative IT is high due to their complexity (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004). Conversely, non-transformative IT applications target wellunderstood firm activities and are intended to, for example, replace human labor or lower the cost of firm activities (Dehning et al., 2003).

We used an established approach to capture a proxy for transformative IT investments. Following prior research (Andrade-Rojas et al., 2021; Joshi et al., 2010; Saldanha et al., 2020), we collected secondary data on transformative IT applications using firms’ news announcements from 2004 to

2019 from computer journals (eWeek, Networkworld, Computerworld, eWeek Security Watch, Infoworld, InformationWeek) from the Factiva database, which includes 30,000+ news sources. These outlets offer reports of IT implementations in U.S. firms. We took three steps to cover firms’ IT initiatives and address concerns about over-reporting or under-reporting by the media. First, we screened firms’ annual reports and websites to collect IT announcements. Second, we collected press releases and public relations announcements that reported IT implementations. Third, we screened firms’ 10-K filings for IT news. After excluding irrelevant or duplicate announcements, we obtained 2,864 ITrelated news announcements.

To categorize transformative IT applications (TITA), we identified systems that enable a firm to redefine business processes, introduce radical business models, or disrupt industry practices (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004). We coded news announcements about a firm’s IT applications to measure TITA. Consistent with prior research (Chi et al., 2010; Joshi et al., 2010; Saldanha et al., 2020), we developed a systematic coding protocol and objectively identified and classified firms’ IT applications into TITA by examining IT news articles (See Appendix B). This approach to measuring TITA aligns with studies that use secondary data in the form of summative indices of binary measures to evaluate firm information systems capabilities and IT use (e.g., Banker et al., 2006; Saldanha et al., 2022; Saldanha et al., 2020). Thus, following prior research, we used the number (count) of the mapped information systems to measure TITA (Joshi et al., 2010).

We analyze this data using fixed-effect models and dynamic panel models to account for endogeneity. We also ran Poisson models since the dependent variable is a count measure. Table 7 shows the results. We interpreted the fixedeffect models (Columns 1-2), although the dynamic panel (Columns 3-4) and Poisson results (Columns 5-6) are similar. LTC has a positive and significant coefficient (Column 1: β = 2.886, p < 0.05). In Column 2, LTC  Firmspecific uncertainty (β = 0.145, p < 0.01) and LTC  Competitive uncertainty (β = 0.369, p < 0.01) are positive and significant, consistent with H1. In line with H2, the coefficient of LTC  Competitive uncertainty is greater than the coefficient of LTC  Firm-specific uncertainty (Column 2: F-test statistic = 5.53, p < 0.05). We conducted a robustness test using a binary dependent variable (TranIT), which is 1 if a firm implemented more transformative IT applications than non-transformative IT applications in a focal year, and 0 if a firm implemented the same number or fewer transformative IT applications than nontransformative IT applications in the year. The results (Table 7, Columns 7-8) using logistic regressions with this binary dependent variable are consistent. This analysis of data on transformative IT applications triangulates our results across periods and across different and nuanced types of IT investments.

## Discussion

## Findings

Our study has two key findings. First, the positive effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment is stronger for firms facing firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty. This is because long-term compensation incentivizes CEOs (1) to make IT capital investments to benefit from growth opportunities in order to avoid personal losses arising from disruption by mitigating the partial knowledge problem and (2) to take actions based on an increased risk orientation. Second, compared to firmspecific uncertainty, competitive uncertainty has a stronger positive moderating effect on the impact of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment. This is because competitive uncertainty poses a more severe partial knowledge problem and a greater threat to a CEO’s personal wealth than firm-specific uncertainty. Overall, our results suggest that the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment is contextual and depends on the locus of the uncertainty the firm faces.<sup>21</sup>

Our exploratory analysis found that CEO long-term compensation positively impacts firm risk-oriented dominant logic. This result supports our theorizing that longterm compensation increases a CEO’s risk orientation, leading to IT capital investment. The positive effect of longterm compensation on CEO risk orientation manifests in high risk-oriented dominant logic, which explains high IT capital investment. The effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm risk-oriented dominant logic is positively moderated by competitive uncertainty and firmspecific uncertainty, with competitive uncertainty having a stronger moderating effect. These results suggest that the stronger effects of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment under competitive and firm-specific uncertainty occur via an increase in the firm’s risk-oriented dominant logic.

Our results are consistent across several robustness tests, including endogeneity, simultaneity, unobserved heterogeneity, alternate model specifications, alternative operationalizations of key variables, additional control variables (e.g., CEO abilities and experiences), and crosssectional dependence.

## Theoretical Contributions and Implications

Our research is, to our knowledge, the first to study how the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment depends on uncertainty and the locus of uncertainty that a firm faces. Our theoretical contributions lie at the intersection of three literature streams: IT and compensation incentives, IT and uncertainty, and antecedents of IT investment (See Appendix Figure A1). The first literature stream studies the effects of compensation incentives on IT-related constructs, such as IT strategic posture or IT outsourcing (Hall & Liedtka, 2005; Xue et al., 2017). The second literature stream relates to IT and uncertainty and studies the effects of IT on firm outcomes in uncertain conditions (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006; Sabherwal et al., 2019; Saldanha et al., 2013). The third literature stream studies the effects of constructs as antecedents of IT investments (Ravichandran & Liu, 2011; Salge et al., 2015). While some studies lie at the intersection of the IT and compensation incentives stream and the antecedents of IT investment stream (Masli et al., 2014) and other studies lie at the intersection of the IT and uncertainty stream and the antecedents of IT investment stream (Mithas et al., 2013), we are unaware of any study that has examined the joint effects of compensation incentives and uncertainty on IT investment. Our study connects these three streams and makes three key theoretical contributions.

First, we identify uncertainty as a salient condition in which CEO long-term compensation has a stronger positive effect on IT capital investment. Prior research has employed agency theory to study the effect of CEO compensation on IT investment and on IT outsourcing (Hall & Liedtka, 2005; Masli et al., 2014). Yet the literature offers little guidance on how uncertainty shapes the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment. By taking a holistic perspective of uncertainty as both an opportunity (based on agency theory) and a disruption (based on prospect theory), our theorizing contributes to a conceptual understanding of how uncertainty shapes the effect of CEO long-term compensation on firm IT capital investment. This embodies a theoretical contribution that complements prior research, which has largely depended on agency theory to explain how CEO long-term compensation incentivizes IT investment by aligning the risk orientations of CEOs and shareholders (Masli et al., 2014). Our theorizing suggests that CEOs make decisions not just according to agency theory-based motivations from the perspective of uncertainty as an opportunity but also according to prospect theory-based motivations from the perspective of uncertainty as a disruption. We thus showcase how these two theories can help future researchers conceptualize the effects of these two facets of uncertainty (disruption and opportunity) in the context of phenomena related to information systems.

Second, we identify the locus of uncertainty as crucial for understanding the extent to which CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment. A key contribution is revealing that firms that strive to be digitally advanced need to design CEO compensation packages according to the locus of uncertainty they face. This perspective reinforces the salience of firm and environmental factors in driving IT investment (Mithas et al., 2013) and contributes further understanding of how these factors (firm-specific and competitive uncertainty) enhance the efficacy of CEO longterm compensation in driving IT capital investment. Since CEO compensation is a financial outlay that needs to be managed, our key contribution lies in theorizing and showing that the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment must be considered in conjunction with firm-specific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty. Our identification of boundary conditions surrounding CEO long-term compensation and the decisions that CEOs make vis-à-vis IT capital investments represents a salient contribution to the literature at the intersection of the IT and compensation incentives stream and the antecedents of IT investments stream (See Appendix Figure A1).

Our results imply that firms that differ in the locus of uncertainty should allocate different levels of CEO long-term compensation to incentivize IT capital investments. A theoretical implication is that firms facing high firm-specific uncertainty relative to competitive uncertainty may be better off providing CEO compensation packages with a high longterm component. In contrast, firms facing high competitive uncertainty relative to firm-specific uncertainty can offer a lower CEO long-term compensation and still incentivize similar levels of IT capital investment. This key theoretical contribution may seem counterintuitive because common perception suggests that under high competitive uncertainty relative to firm-specific uncertainty, there may be a greater need to incentivize CEOs to be risk-oriented due to the more disruptive nature of competitive uncertainty. However, this finding is grounded in our arguments based on agency theory and prospect theory.

Third, we reveal the firm’s dominant logic as a theoretical mechanism for how uncertainty shapes the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment. We unravel risk-oriented dominant logic based on the premise that IT capital investment is risky (Xin & Choudhary, 2019). IT capital investment can be viewed as a manifestation of a firm’s risk-oriented dominant logic. This contribution complements prior research on the effect of CEO compensation on IT investment (Masli et al., 2014). Although our dominant logic analysis is exploratory, to our knowledge, we are the first to theorize and test the role that a firm’s dominant logic plays in its IT investment.

Our study of transformative IT applications advances the literature on the effects of CEO compensation on IT investment (Masli et al., 2014) by introducing the role of uncertainty and taking a nuanced view of IT applications. Given the similarly risky nature of IT capital investment and transformative IT applications, this analysis triangulates our findings across different types of IT and different time frames.

## Managerial Contributions

This study makes three key managerial contributions. First, corporate boards must consider uncertainty in the firm’s context to understand how CEO long-term compensation impacts IT capital investment. Our results imply that it is crucial to assess uncertainty when designing CEO compensation schemes for firms that aim to change their resource allocation toward IT. Our findings are vital since uncertainty can serve as a disruption and an opportunity. In view of the common perception that CEOs hesitate to make IT capital investments in uncertain conditions, our study reveals CEO long-term compensation’s role as a lever to incentivize IT capital investment in uncertain conditions. Second, our study helps firms understand the conditions in which CEO long-term compensation incentivizes IT capital investment based on the locus of uncertainty that firms face. Our results suggest that long-term compensation is a stronger lever to incentivize IT capital investment in firms facing high competitive uncertainty than it is in firms facing high firm-specific uncertainty. Thus, compensation packages should be designed considering the locus of uncertainty that the firm faces.

Our exploratory analysis implies that boards can benefit from understanding the interdependency between CEO long-term compensation and the firm’s risk-oriented dominant logic. Boards need to be aware that CEO long-term compensation fosters a higher risk-oriented dominant logic in the firm. This suggests that long-term compensation modifies a CEO’s system of assumptions, beliefs, and priorities. Further, the locus of uncertainty (firm-specific versus competitive) shapes the extent to which long-term compensation incentivizes a CEO to influence the firm’s risk-oriented dominant logic.

Boards of directors seek to align the interests of executives and shareholders and to implement incentive systems that protect shareholder interests. Our findings suggest that boards can use CEO long-term compensation to ensure that executives make IT resource allocation decisions that are congruent with the long-term interests of shareholders. Our study suggests opportunities for reforming CEO compensation practices to steer digital investments, especially in conditions of firmspecific uncertainty and competitive uncertainty.

## Limitations, Future Research, and Conclusion

Notwithstanding its contributions, our study has limitations that can serve as starting points for future research. First, our sample from InformationWeek features large U.S. firms that may have advanced IT practices. Our results may not generalize to small firms or to firms in developing countries that require focused examinations (Andrade-Rojas et al., 2022; Andrade-Rojas et al., in press; Karhade & Kathuria, 2020). Second, although our exploratory analysis uses dominant logic measures based on prior research and this approach suits the longitudinal secondary nature of our data, future work can use other approaches (e.g., surveys) to measure dominant logic. Third, although the IT capital investment data is not recent, this is not a concern for three reasons: (1) the effects of CEO compensation on IT investment under uncertainty do not depend on the period when the data was gathered because CEOs continue to face challenges regarding allocation of IT investments (Xue et al., 2017); (2) our substantively unchanged findings in the exploratory analysis using transformative IT applications from 2004 to 2019 boost confidence in our theory, as it applies even in recent periods and for newer technologies; and (3)

recent information systems studies have also used data from the 1999-2007 time frame (Sabherwal et al., 2019; Steelman et al., 2019). However, future studies can validate our findings in the context of newer IT investments since 2019.

Fourth, although CEOs are responsible for allocating firm resources such as IT investments (Masli et al., 2014), we recognize the complexity of a firm’s resource allocation processes (e.g., CEOs may seek advice from other executives). Given our large sample and longitudinal data, it is practically infeasible, if not impossible, for us to know the precise processes that firms engage in to allocate IT budgets. We also recognize a complexity related to firm-specific uncertainty. Although we theoretically justified firm-specific uncertainty as a moderator, we acknowledge that some managers’ decisions may affect uncertain firm outcomes. However, this does not impact our results since firm-specific uncertainty is not fully explained by managers’ actions, especially in large firms such as those in our sample. Our treatment of firm-specific uncertainty is consistent with Knight’s (1921) conceptualization of uncertainty and aligns with studies that analyze interaction effects of firm-level constructs that may be influenced by managerial decisions (Bardhan et al., 2013; Bharadwaj et al., 2007; Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010). Moreover, our empirical analyses (e.g., dynamic panel and control function models) account for the endogeneity of firm-specific uncertainty. Finally, although we use a theoretically and empirically valid external instrument (lawsuits) for firm-specific uncertainty, finding external instruments is complex. Following prior studies, although we use dynamic panel models and other approaches to address endogeneity, we recognize that “without a randomized or natural experiment, some potential for endogeneity is unavoidable. To combat this, we used GMM estimation of a dynamic panel model, which protects against endogenous patterns” (Kitchens et al., 2018, p. 939) in data by creating instruments using lagged values (Saldanha et al., 2022; Saldanha et al., 2020).

Our study uncovers future research avenues. One avenue is to study how components or time-based elements of compensation impact IT investments. Another avenue is to explore how CEO qualities (e.g., defender/prospector nature) may shape the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment. Since data on CEOs’ risk-averse nature is publicly unavailable, such an analysis was not feasible, particularly in our longitudinal setting. Future research can include CEOs’ risk-averse nature, perhaps using primary data. Another avenue is to compare the effects of CEOs’ and CIOs’ equity incentives on IT investment and to study whether such effects depend on the CIO’s power or status relative to the CEO. Though our results are robust to accounting for CEOs’ abilities and experiences, which increase their confidence to take risks (See Appendix Table A5), future work can use primary data to study how CEOs’ abilities shape the effect of long-term compensation on IT capital investment (Choi et al., 2021). Future work can also study the implications of environmental characteristics (e.g., munificence, dynamism) for the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT investment. Finally, while we made strong theoretical arguments to develop our hypotheses, empirically testing these arguments is an area for future research.

In conclusion, this study enriches scholarly and managerial knowledge of how uncertainty in the firm and industry shapes the effect of CEO long-term compensation on IT capital investment. As uncertainty continues to grip the business environment, our results underscore the importance of CEO long-term compensation as a lever to incentivize IT capital investments.

## Appendix B

## Data Collection for Transformative IT Applications

Following prior research (e.g., Chi et al., 2010; Joshi et al., 2010; Saldanha et al., 2020), we developed a systematic coding protocol and used a recursive analytical process to enhance the coding protocol. This process resulted in the development of a comprehensive list of transformative IT applications (TITA) (e.g., blockchain, artificial intelligence) that were mapped to the theoretical category of TITA. Thus, this protocol creates an objective characterization of firms’ TITA.

First, we conducted a detailed literature review in the Basket-of-Eight information systems journals based on the definition of transformative technologies to identify IT applications considered transformative for each year in our dataset. We defined transformative IT applications following prior literature (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004) as those technologies that introduce radical business models that disrupt industry practices and market structures. Thus, transformative IT applications are riskier than non-transformative IT applications (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004) . Using this definition of TITA, we identified the IT applications that met the definition’s criteria for TITA for each year. We enhanced the initial list by eliciting synonym terms for technologies, where applicable. Next, through group discussions and independent evaluations by two external practitioner experts and two academic experts, we verified that the identified applications reflect the characteristics of TITA. For each year in the dataset, the experts re-evaluated whether the IT applications were transformative in that year. If they did not meet the criteria of transformative IT applications, they were considered non-transformative for that year.

Second, we developed a structured coding protocol, which specified a set of instructions on how to classify IT applications as TITA based on whether they met the criteria for TITA. Two well-trained coders used the protocol to independently conduct a pilot coding of a random sample of 5% of the total news announcements. Two of the authors then verified that the protocol was appropriately implemented by the coders on the random sample. Any coding disagreements were discussed between the coders and two of the authors until they reached a consensus. Clarifications were provided, and refinements were made to the protocol as necessary.

Third, two independent coders used the refined coding protocol to code all the news announcements. Any duplicated or irrelevant news announcements were excluded from the data. The coding protocol was used to code and identify the IT applications in each announcement, wherever applicable. The identified IT applications (e.g., Internet of Things manufacturing software) were then mapped to TITA (e.g., Internet of Things), if applicable, based on the coding protocol. Any disagreements were discussed between the coders and two authors until a consensus was formed. News announcements indicating stoppage or updates of IT use were also noted and coded accordingly. Unless a firm announced a stoppage of IT use, we assumed that the IT use was continued, following the approach used in prior research (e.g., Chi et al., 2010; Joshi et al., 2010; Saldanha et al., 2020).

Finally, we ensured the reliability and robustness of the coding in two ways: (1) We randomly selected a subset of the news announcements for coding by an external industry expert who was given our coding protocol. This produced an exact match. (2) We randomly selected 10% of the news announcements and asked two academic experts to code them independently. Their coding output was then compared with the classification by the coders. This also produced an exact match.

All these procedures mitigate any concerns of potential coding bias and suggest the high reliability of the coding process.
