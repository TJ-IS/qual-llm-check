# ERP evaluation during the shakedown phase: lessons from an after-sales division

Lotta Häkkinen\* & Olli-Pekka Hilmola<sup>†</sup>

\*Turku School of Economics, Business Research and Development Centre, Rehtorinpellonkatu 3, FIN-20500 Turku, Finland, email: lotta.hakkinen@tse.fi, <sup>†</sup>Lappeenranta University of Technology, Kouvola Research Unit, Prikaatintie 9, FIN-45100 Kouvola, Finland, email: olli-pekka.hilmola@lut.fi

Abstract. Integrated information systems and operational efficiency are both pivotal issues for contemporary firms. While there is substantial evidence that enterprise resources planning (ERP) systems can deliver related improvements, the implementation of these systems can turn out to be a very complex and risky task. One key aspect of managing these risks is maintaining operational momentum and preventing possible problems from escalating in the so-called shakedown phase shortly after system implementation. The objective of this paper is to examine how user evaluations of ERP system success could be used to trace down the source of potential problems, which can arise during the shakedown phase, and how and why experienced system success might vary between different user groups. The paper builds on a case study completed in the after-sales division of a large multinational organization. This context is considered a fruitful empirical setting for the study as the business sets enormous demands for ERP system functionality and its smooth implementation. Based on the case study, it is argued that in such a context the importance of sufficient user skills, data reliability and intra-organizational communication becomes emphasized in the ERP implementation process. Moreover, results illustrate how downstream operations and customer relations are particularly vulnerable to problems accumulated in upstream business processes. Related problems can potentially form a selffulfilling cycle, where the lack of skills and information constantly deteriorates both user perceptions and actual operational performance.

Keywords: ERP systems, IS evaluation, shakedown phase, case study

## INTRODUCTION

Enterprise resources planning (ERP) systems have become common in contemporary orga nizations operating in demanding business environments. The underlying idea of ERP is to use information technology (IT) to plan and integrate enterprise-wide resources beyond the scope of traditional IT solutions. This is done by bringing together several core business functions into an integrated database to provide one-time data entry and seamless access to a single facet of information (Al-Mashari & Al-Mudimigh, 2003). The software itself is designed for better longevity and is claimed to offer numerous features representing best practices (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Teltumbde, 2000; Al-Mashari & Al-Mudimigh, 2003). For many companies, the implementation of ERP has translated into dramatic gains in productivity and speed. Furthermore, numerous intangible benefits, such as improved internal processes and customer service as well as strategic enhancements, have been linked to ERP implementation (Shang & Seddon, 2000; Murphy & Simon, 2002; Al-Mashari et al., 2003).

While there is substantial evidence that ERP systems can deliver great improvements, risks associated with implementing such a system should be equally acknowledged (Davenport, 1998). The same features which make an ERP system very desirable can simultaneously cause its implementation to be a very complex and expensive task (Teltumbde, 2000). This is especially the case when pursuing benefits which affect inter-organizational interfaces and different companies operating in the same supply chain (Buxmann et al., 2004; He, 2004). For example, He (2004) reports that in Chinese companies, ERP benefits are typically supply chain-related. In such a context, implementation challenges are typically linked not only to issues such as the technical complexity of system implementation, but also to broader, firm or country-specific attributes.

Measuring and evaluating performance is a critical factor for ensuring the successful implementation of any information system (IS). In an ERP context, performance evaluation can be considered to be particularly important because of the profoundness of implementation effects. Not only do ERP implementation projects exceed typical IS projects in terms of their organizational scope, budget and project length (e.g. Yu, 2005), but creating a logical structure tha involves a number of operational entities and is supported by the adopted system can require massive cultural and structural changes (e.g. Al-Mashari et al., 2003). However, it seems that most existing literature on ERP has focused on software selection and the critical success factors (CSFs) of implementation, rather than on performance assessment after implementation has taken place (Esteves & Pastor, 2001; Jacobs & Bendoly, 2003; Yu, 2005). Several studies have suggested that ERP-related performance goes through a certain shakedown or break-in phase directly after system implementation, which may not give an accurate picture of the ultimate impacts of ERP (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Ross & Vitale, 2000; Nah et al., 2001; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005). However, it is argued in this paper that experiences gained during this shakedown phase are nevertheless valuable, and that monitoring performance throughout this period and taking actions accordingly can be seen as a vital issue for maintaining operational momentum and preventing possible problems from escalating and affecting ultimate performance. Keeping track of progress in this early stage is especially important for the acceptance of ERP systems and for managing related scepticism (Nah et al., 2001; Mandal & Gunasekaran, 2003).

The objective of this paper is to examine (1) how user evaluations of ERP system success could be used to trace down the source of potential problems which can arise during the shakedown phase, compromising both the quality of day-to-day operations and the success of the actual ERP project; and (2) how and why system success experienced by different use groups might vary in the early post-implementation phase. The empirical part of the paper reports on a case study performed in the after-sales division of a large multinational organization. Data for the study were collected two months after the system had gone live. On the one hand, this gives an excellent insight into experienced impacts and related perceptions in the shakedown phase. On the other hand, although this time is critical for the long-term success of the system, it is difficult to assess the actual impact of these events on ultimate system success. Naturally, this sets certain limitations to the research results.

The after-sales business builds on operations linked to the supplying of spare parts and related services for purposes of maintenance and repair of previously purchased products. Success in the business builds on the efficiency, speed, reliability and wide geographical coverage of operations. Thus, this context was considered to provide a fruitful empirical setting for the study. The implementation of an ERP system in this type of organization would seem to hold a number of attractive benefits, such as internal visibility within and across organizational boundaries (cf. Evgeniou, 2002). Paradoxically, the shakedown phase following system implementation can pose severe risks for maintaining efficient, speedy and reliable operations, thus exposing the core business to major problems. Prior evidence indicates that the shake down phase will typically last from four to 12 months. although the length and intensity of the phase can vary (Ross & Vitale, 2000). In an after-sales context, minimizing the length, intensity and effects of this period by, for example, tracing down and resolving problems as soon as possible would seem to be a critical issue.

This paper begins with a review of previous research on ERP systems evaluation, and the dimensions of ERP system success. This is followed by a brief introduction to the research environment of the after-sales business and the importance of ERP functionality in this context. The research methodology behind the study is then reported. The case study findings, which are based on the gathered quantitative and qualitative data, are presented in two sections: first, based on the overall data, and then through comparing the views of different user groups. The study findings are then discussed, and the paper ends with concluding remarks and suggestions for further research.

## BACKGROUND

## ERP systems evaluation

Information systems evaluation has long been of interest to both practitioners and researchers. For practitioners, the use of evaluation can help senior executives and IS analysts to assess the suitability of proposed projects, but evaluation can also be used in the post-implementation phase to compare anticipated benefits and reactions to those realized (Mirani & Lederer, 1998). From a practitioner’s perspective, IS evaluation has been described as a particularly challenging aspect of IS management (e.g. Hamilton & Chervany, 1981).

As a research topic IS evaluation is highly subjective (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003), and therefore it is hard to find one comprehensive theory or framework exhaustively covering the entire field (Zammuto, 1984; Goodhue, 1995; Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002). As a consequence, literature on IS evaluation is extremely diverse, describing a variety of perspectives on the topic (Symons, 1991; Grover et al., 1996; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998). Studies have focused on various levels of impacts (i.e. individual, system and organizational level) and have also assessed different effect types (i.e. strategic, efficiency-related and social). In general terms, literature on IS evaluation has been classified as belonging to one of three approaches (Yu, 2005):

1 The contribution of efficiency to organizational work flow (i.e. data processing saving time, labour reduction)

2 Organization and user satisfaction (i.e. ease of system use, system reliability)

3 The financial contribution to the company (i.e. cost–benefit analysis, return on investment)

Since ERP systems affect technological, operational, managerial, strategic and organizational components within the implementing firm, related assessment should reflect a holistic perspective (Al-Mashari et al., 2003). Furthermore, the implementation of an ERP system cannot be seen as an end itself, but instead reaching the ultimate aims involves continuous work and improvement over time. In such a context, evaluation cannot be simplified and understood from a single approach (Yu, 2005). Instead, multiple tools and perspectives should be used (cf. Soliman et al., 2001).

The objectives of the present study relate to applying a holistic approach to system evaluation in terms of multiple perspectives, while limiting the focus of the study to a particular temporal period of ERP operation, i.e. the shakedown phase. User perceptions of system success are of special interest. Perceptions at a higher organization level are used to confirm the existence and relevance of perceived effects. Moreover, the study underlines the value of user feedback in helping the organization to move towards a phase of stable operations, which would affect multiple organizational levels. As the focus is on the shakedown phase, the ultimate financial contribution of implementation is beyond the scope of this study.

## ERP implementation process

Although ERP system use has been shown to have numerous benefits, research has indicated that implementation projects commonly do not meet their initial objectives, and operations frequently suffer from related disruptions. This realization has produced a significant amount of literature which focuses on the management of ERP projects. For example, Kim et al. (2005) argue that managing the ERP implementation process is the key factor in determining system success.

The majority of this type of ERP literature can be considered to represent factor research, which aims at identifying CSFs that would lead to successful implementation and subsequently to system success (Aladwani, 2001). For example, Mabert et al. (2003a) and Umble et al. (2003) emphasize the importance of implementation management in terms of top managemen support and commitment, communication within the organization and among key stakeholders, and project management. Other commonly stated CSFs include ERP teamwork and team composition (Parr & Shanks, 2000), change management (Parr & Shanks, 2000; Al-Mashari et al., 2003), user training (Gupta, 2000; Soliman et al., 2001; Al-Mashari et al., 2003), coupling ERP projects with business process re-engineering (Nah et al., 2001; Akkermans & Helden, 2002; Al-Mashari et al., 2003; Mabert et al., 2003b), and the statement of a clear business plan with a vision on the project outcome (Parr & Shanks, 2000; Al-Mashari et al., 2003), the realization of which should be monitored and evaluated (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Nah et al., 2001; Murphy & Simon, 2002).

Factor-based ERP research has also received criticism. For example, Yu (2005) argues that it suffers for several drawbacks as it is limited to a rather static view that cannot explain the dynamics of the implementation process. In contrast, the process-oriented approach to ERP research aims at providing a moving picture of causal relationship chains, which span the different steps in the implementation process. Several researchers have suggested their own process models to depict the different phases which an organization will encounter during an ERP implementation project (e.g. Bancroft et al., 1998; Markus & Tanis, 2000; Ross & Vitale, 2000). Although models differ in terms of how many distinct phases are identified and how they are referred to, process models typically share a common underlying idea. Process models recognize the importance of both pre- and post-implementation phases in achieving project success. The go-live of system implementation cannot be seen as the accomplishment of ultimate project goals, but rather as a starting point for continuous improvement. (cf. Parr & Shanks, 2000; Yu, 2005)

Based on prior academic work proposing either ERP CSFs or process models, several scholars have sought to synthesize these two bodies of literature (e.g. Nah et al., 2001; Par & Shanks, 2000). While process models can provide a useful lens for understanding ERP projects, augmenting process models with CSFs for each phase could enhance the overall understanding of these projects (cf. Parr & Shanks, 2000). For example, Umble et al. (2003) suggest the following implementation procedures and related CSFs: (1) in the selection phase, select the ERP system, make sure that the hardware meets demands, and install the system with the help of qualified personnel; (2) in the subsequent system training phase, start from single transactions, and proceed thereafter to company-wide processes; (3) provide users with appropriate permissions and ensure system security; (4) ensure that data brought from previous systems are accurate and users have the ability to use it; (5) bring the system online; (6) celebrate the implementation results; and (7) continuously improve system utilization.

Thus, these two streams of literature (process and factor approaches) are not seen as entirely separate or incompatible. The study to be described in this paper primarily draws from the process-oriented approach because the underlying presuppositions of the study is that information gained from user evaluations can be applied to improve an ERP system which has already been implemented, thus underlying the importance of subjective views and timerelated dynamics. However, related causal linkages may trace back to critical actions in, fo example, the pre-implementation period, which brings the factor approach into play.

## Dimensions of ERP success

The objective of this paper relates to how user evaluations of ERP system success could be used to track down the source of potential problems during the shakedown phase. To be able to provide an evaluation of system success as well as to identify related problems, one should naturally identify the dimensions, which together constitute system success in an ERP context. Dimensions consist of a number of different quantifiable measures, for example, in an e-commerce context user satisfaction as a dimension of success could be measured with repeated purchases, repeated visits and user surveys (DeLone & McLean, 2003). Compared to critical success factors, which are basically regarded as normative, bipolar, static and management-controlled variables and related actions, dimensions seek to capture a multitude of aspects that vary over time and between reporting subjects. The joint dynamics of these elements is used as a lens to explain the perceived implementation process and its success. The updated IS success model by DeLone & McLean (2003) was used as an initial starting point for identifying these dimensions due to its wide use in general IS success literature. Contributions by other authors in this field were also taken into account in constructing and operationalizing the relevant dimensions.

Both the original and the updated IS success model of DeLone & McLean (1992; 2003) build on the synthesized results of numerous academic contributions. The original model included six dimensions: information quality, systems quality, user satisfaction, use, individual impact, and organizational impact. The revised model adds the dimensions of ‘service quality’ and ‘intention to use the system’ to the original version and combines individual and organizationa impacts into ‘net benefits’. The revised model is shown in Figure 1.

The model implies that information systems contain certain features connected to information, system and service quality. These features are experienced, through direct and indirect usage, by users who then individually develop a certain level of satisfaction or dissatisfaction towards the system. The use of the system will have certain effects on the work of individual users, which then collectively constitute overall organizational impacts. Since the model was first introduced in 1992 it has received much attention. A number o studies have empirically tested and validated certain relationships within the model, but several articles have also challenged and criticized the model (Seddon, 1997; Lassila & Brancheau, 1999).

The present paper concentrates on four specific dimensions of ERP success: information quality, system quality, service quality and net benefits. In addition to building on the DeLone & McLean model, empirical measures behind each dimension were strengthened by reviewing other studies that had specific linkages to ERP system success and related user evaluations. In particular, the service quality measures outlined by DeLone & McLean (2003), which had been adopted from marketing literature, were not directly applicable to an ERP context (cf. the quality of external services offered to e-commerce customers vs. internal service support offered to ERP users within the same organization). The studies by Goodhue & Thomson (1995) and Goodhue (1995) were used as a basis for constructing this dimension. In terms of measures related to net benefits, the adopted perspective was primarily that of individual users. Higher level organizational net benefits were mapped in an alternative manner, which was related to the specific setting of the study (see Research methodology section). The dimensions of use, intention to use and user satisfaction are only touched upon indirectly in the study. For one, in the case in question, system use was more or less mandatory. At the user level, there was no possibility to discontinue system use or switch to another system at will (cf. Goodhue, 1995; DeLone & McLean, 2003). Rather, the nature and amount of system use was more dependent on the tasks of each user than on individual user satisfaction.

## Research environment: characteristics of the after-sales business

After-sales services have become one of the fastest growing areas of business for the manufacturers of complex and mechanical durables. In this sector, it is relatively common for a manufacturing firm to generate 30–60% of its revenue and two thirds of its profits from spare parts sales and maintenance services (Suomala et al., 2002; 2004). Moreover, after-sales operations are expected to become increasingly important in the future because of severa ongoing trends: (1) product life cycles are becoming shorter and shorter; (2) machinery is used for longer periods of time; and (3) industry consolidation has created ever larger multinationals that hold multiple brands under one roof. For example, if a manufacturer has several thousand purchased parts in its manufacturing database (i.e. direct material for current production), it could easily have 100 000–200 000 stock keeping units (SKUs) in its after-sales database. As Fites (1996) has shown, in the mid-1990s Caterpillar had 480 000 active after-sales items, of which 320 000 were stocked either in centralized or decentralized locations. As the example shows, 160 000 SKUs were not stocked at all. Instead, Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) and their supply networks typically have excess component manufacturing or subas sembly capacity in order to produce certain items as they are needed. These non-stocked items as well as spare parts from centralized locations are typically transported by air to thei final destination due to short time windows.

Challenges in the after-sales business generally lie in matters related to the management and ownership of distribution. Global operations require enormous investments in facilities, inventory and human resources. For example, Fites (1996) estimated that Caterpillar’s dealers employed about 19 000 employees more than the OEM itself. Also the amount of items carried in inventory and investments made by dealers in related facilities and infrastructure represented a significant amount of tied capital. Consequently, OEMs are often required to share their profits with distribution channel members, and therefore partnerships with dealers are essential for competitiveness. As both Cohen & Lee (1990) and Fites (1996) argue, it is important for OEMs to know specific local circumstances concerning, for example, customers preferences and weather conditions, in order to forecast the future demand for high cost and low volume spare parts more accurately. This is essential information for capital intensive investments such as vessels and manufacturing facilities. Bendall & Stent (1987) illustrate that a \$40 million vessel with an estimated usage of 15 years will have a depreciation cost ranging from 300 to 800 dollars per hour. Thus, a 24-h loss in vessel time will cost \$7200–\$19 200 in terms of depreciation only. These substantial costs represent important value creation oppor tunities for spare part suppliers, and therefore a high performing dealer network is essential in this business.

As research in this field has shown, the management of distribution inventory, information transparency, the use of advanced product technology, and the efficient use of resources are vital factors for ensuring success in the after-sales business (Muckstadt & Thomas, 1983; Cohen & Lee. 1990: Fites. 1996: Huiskonen, 2001: Suomala et al., 2002), It is evident that the increasing amount of SKUs in after-sales will increase both inventory investment and the required workload. ## Research methodology

As a result of the attributes of the problem area under investigation, a case study approach was considered an appropriate research strategy for the purposes of this study. Case study research is particularly suitable for addressing problems in which research and theory are still formative (Benbasat et al., 1987; Eisenhardt, 1989), where ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions are addressed concerning a contemporary set of events over which the investigator has little or no control (Yin, 1991), and the context of action is critical (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1991).

Case studies can also serve a number of purposes. Traditionally, case studies have been considered to be most useful in exploring organizational phenomena and generating tentative hypotheses and constructs by yielding idiographic in-depth knowledge (e.g. Evered & Louis, 1981; Sutton & Staw, 1995). However, more recently, several authors have argued that case study research can be applied to a broader range of research objectives (e.g. Eisenhardt, 1989; Scapens, 1990; McCutcheon & Meredith, 1993; Ellram, 1996). For example, Yin (1991) identifies three different case study types: explanatory, descriptive and explorative.

The objectives of the present study relate to the ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions concerning user evaluations on ERP success. Consequently, the aim is to provide descriptive and explanatory information on this topic, which has not yet been thoroughly researched. Furthermore, contextual boundaries are not easy to identify in the case of ERP projects as the process includes numerous actors, stakeholders and causal links. The case study in question builds on empirical evidence from an ERP project in a multinational corporation from the durable machinery manufacturing sector. The examined project itself is one of the biggest in the company’s history and it involves the majority of the company’s employees. The ultimate aim of the project has been to improve company profitability by developing the ability to share information globally, harmonizing working procedures, and improving IT efficiency. In addition to actual ERP implementation, changes have been made to the organizational structure and practices in order to support a more process-oriented business model.

The case study focuses specifically on the effects of ERP implementation on the company’s after-sales division, which accounts for a significant part of the corporation’s net sales and profits. Its operations include the delivery of OEM spare parts, and operation and maintenance support. Figure 2 provides an overview of the ERP implementation process from the point of view of the after-sales division. However, the broader context of the empirical setting is also acknowledged.

Because of the richness of the context, case studies should not rely on a single data collection method, but should strive to use multiple sources of evidence (e.g. McKinnon, 1988; Eisenhardt, 1989). Data for the present study were gathered using multiple methods. The main body of data consists of quantitative and qualitative material collected from interviews with ERP users within the case division. These interviews were conducted two months after the system had gone live.

Interviewees either used the ERP system in their daily work or used the system as a supportive tool for decision-making. The duties of the interviewed employees ranged from operative tasks to mid-level management responsibilities. Altogether 30 interviews were conducted at the company sites. Quantitative data were gathered by using structured statement type questions, which were answered using a seven-point Likert scale (Appendix I). The interview structure followed the dimensions of ERP success outlined above. In order to gain deeper insight concerning the underlying reasons behind user evaluations, each question also included an open-ended part through which the interviewee could elaborate on the issues relating to the structured questions. The resultant quantitative data were analysed using mainl basic descriptive statistics, while qualitative data were analysed by using methods such as thematic grouping.

Altogether, the ERP project in the after-sales division touched upon 18 key business processes, of which four were covered in the interviews: (1) vendor relations and material supply (n = 7 interviewees); (2) logistics (n = 4); (3) sales (n = 13); and (4) invoicing (n = 6). These four processes were selected together with the contact group from the case organization, that consisted of personnel who were responsible for carrying out the ERP roll-out in the after-sales division as well as selected ERP key users, based on the fact that the operations these processes had been most profoundly affected by system implementation. Interviewees were also classified as either end or key ERP users. Key users (n = 9) were company employees that also served as a first level user support persons for their respective group of end-users, and they had been involved in the testing of the ERP system before its actual implementation. The remainder of the respondents represented end-users (n = 21) who had received basic ERP training.

In addition to collecting information directly from system users, the case study applied material from discussions with the contact group as well as from internal documents and correspondence. This material also served as a basis for planning how to collect comprehensive and relevant data through the interviews. The first discussions took place six weeks afte system implementation. At this stage, it had become evident that some problems had arisen in terms of carrying out certain operations. In order to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the sources of these problems, interviews were considered an appropriate manner to collect data. Later, the research results were reviewed and validated by several persons representing this broader organizational level.

## RESULTS

The results of the study are presented under two main sections. First, a review of the overall findings is provided by examining the results through each of the presented dimensions of success. In the second section, the results are analysed by comparing the views of different user groups in order to examine possible differences in user perceptions between business processes.

## Overall case study findings

As described above, ERP success was conceptualized through four dimensions: (1) per ceived information quality; (2) perceived system quality; (3) perceived quality of service offered to system users; and (4) perceived net benefits of system implementation. Basic parameters of the quantitative data are shown in Table 2 at a question and dimension level. Most of the mean values scored a value under four indicating that the interviewees did no agree with the positively worded statements. Based on quantitative assessments, users found the most problematic issues to be: (1) negative effects on the company’s overal customer service level, for example, in terms of an increase in the number of delayed or incorrect deliveries to internal and external customers; (2) increased individual workloads and decreased working efficiency; (3) negative effects on individual work quality; and (4) the poor reliability of information available in the ERP system. In the following, quantitative findings will be elaborated on based on the qualitative data, thus addressing the causal links behind perceived success.

## Information quality

As a whole, user evaluations of data quality were fairly negative. Especially, the reliability o information raised concerns. Reliability was weak regarding, for example, current levels o inventory, product availability, estimated delivery times, and the status of back orders. System users occasionally used the prior IS for double-checking, due to the simple reason that it contained more reliable static item information.

You have to consider the information behind the material number and the master data, yourself . . . is it for example correct, and use your common sense in terms of whether there’s something missing and does the information really hold up . . . for example, check this from the old system. [Vendor relations & material supply]

The reasons for inaccuracy were perceived as being partly related to flaws in the master data, but largely to mistakes that users had made while utilizing the new, unfamiliar system. The general opinion was that users did not have enough knowledge on how to use the system correctly regarding its functions, fields and definitions. Therefore, mistakes were made frequently, which yet deteriorated data quality. Furthermore, an error may not have been evident in the early phases of the order-delivery process, but will have caused prob lems in later phases, when it was extremely difficult and time-consuming to track down and correct.

The old system was better; the information was more reliable than in this one. But maybe it will be like this just in this early stage. Later on you might be able to rely on this information as well. The system contains faulty information because users do not know how to use the system in the right way . . . and as a result I charge customers incorrectly. [Invoicing]

The lack of reliability and need to double-check information from old systems or other personnel had increased the workload of employees and at the same time deteriorated the quality of their work. This connection was indicated by strong correlations between information quality and net benefit items (Appendix II). For example, information reliability had a strong positive correlation with the quantity and quality of work.

Also the comprehensiveness of information was perceived as poor. Some interviewees indicated that certain information had been lost during data conversion. Others were of the opinion that all the information needed was available in the system, perhaps even more extensively than before. However, the main problem seemed to be the users’ lack of knowl edge of where and how to find a certain piece of information. In some cases, it could be perfectly possible that an efficient way to find the needed information already existed or it could be built into the system with minimal efforts, but this had not been communicated to the users.

Nobody knows how the system actually works and where the information that is needed can be found. [Sales]

## System quality

According to the user evaluations, the actual ERP system and its basic use were not perceived as being an insuperable problem, although it was commonly felt that the old system had been better and more convenient to use. The main problems could be traced down to the lack of knowledge in terms of, for example, system functionality and terminology. As a result, most users had learned to use the system in their daily work through ‘trial and error’. Similarly to information quality, many system quality measures correlated with net benefit items, especially with the quality of work (Appendix II).

So far the new system has only complicated things. Sometimes it feels like everybody relies on the doings of everybody else, and when somebody else makes a mistake in another place, you yourself get stuck in another place. [Invoicing]; Sometimes you get the feeling that you’ve got the information you wanted from the system when you’ve checked enough places. But that’s not really any good either because even then you’re not a 100% sure that you’ve found the information you wanted. [Sales]

All in all the system’s performance was seen as relatively reliable and several respondents believed that the system entailed substantial potential both at operative and strategic levels. However, it was generally concluded that only a small portion of the system’s entire potential was currently in use, and training relating to these possibilities would be extremely beneficia for the company as a whole.

In the end, the current problems are mainly related to user learning and skills as well as the system development, which is still very much ongoing. [Vendor relations & materia supply]

This unused potential was also related to possible benefits derived from systems integration. At the time of the interviews, not all locations within the corporation were using the new ERP system. This had presented certain problems concerning the exchange of information with other distribution channel members. For example, these different information systems were not necessarily compatible, which caused problems in transferring accurate information as well as operational delays. However, when all locations would implement the standard system, benefits relating to systems integration and visibility along the supply chain were presumed to be high.

In terms of strategic sourcing, benefits regarding system integration will truly start to emerge when all other units are using the same system; as we are responsible for corporate pricing at a global level, we naturally have to have a clear picture of the purchases of these other units. In this sense, the system definitely has potential. [Vendor relations & material supply]

## Service quality

Although as a whole, responses regarding the service offered to system users did not represent the most distressing results, many setbacks in other areas can be traced to problems in internal user service. Especially the qualitative results provided evidence of this connection, while in terms of quantitative data, this relationship was not so evident (cf. low correlations between service quality and net benefit items in Appendix II). In general terms, respondents felt that application training had been carried out prematurely. For example, several inter viewees stressed that the system had evolved substantially since the training period, and much of the information given then had turned out to be obsolete. Application training was also criticized for being too brief in duration and too general. Similar comments were given on the instructive material.

We went through training when the whole application wasn’t working and overall the training we got was too brief, not enough days. [Logistics]; The training as such was ok, but the actual information we received has gone out of date, so many changes have been made since. [Sales]

To make up for deficiencies in joint application training, many of the key users provided their respective end-user group with constant information on system use. Virtually all end users appreciated these user support efforts as well as the related material coming from key users.

We constantly receive additional support material from our key user, which has been of great help. and I’ve always got help from our key user when I’ve needed it. [Sales]

However, in some cases the amount of information was seen as quite overwhelming. Finding accurate and up-to-date information concerning a certain problem was reported to be troublesome. In terms of system modifications, key users communicated with senior experts, who were employed by the separate, corporate level project organization. This level of user support received some criticism regarding its continuity and response time. The project organization was perceived as quite hierarchical and bureaucratic. For example, getting information on the current status of requests for change and possible corrective measures seemed to be extremely problematic. Ambiguity on when a certain problem would be resolved lowered spirits and gave the notion to employees that their problems were not taken seriously.

We grumble about these problems among ourselves, but I really don’t have a clear idea how feedback reaches and is handled by the project organization . . . The process of making changes to the system is extremely heavy and we really don’t get any information or feedback whether any actions have been taken to straighten things out, I at least haven’t seen any . . . It feels like they (the project organization) keeps this information just to itself. [Vendor relations & material supply]

## Net benefits

Of the four dimensions of success, perceived net benefits of ERP implementation scored the lowest average. This result was quite unsurprising as problems in other areas typically reflected on the quality and quantity of individual work, and thus also affected operations with external parties, i.e. with customers and suppliers. All in all, the workload of employees had increased substantially. The time needed to carry out a certain task had multiplied in certain processes and implementation had brought additional tasks to certain employees. This had deteriorated the efficiency and quality of work.

I have to admit that we haven’t had time to look into all the things we should have. Especially in this early phase, our time has gone to putting out fires and tending to the most urgent issues that need resolving. This has meant that at the end of the day you haven’t had the time to take care of everything you should have, but there isn’t really anything you can do about it. [Sales]

Also the lack of communication with higher organizational levels had resulted in a situation where it was unclear what kind of actions had been taken to improve the current situation. Employees had understood that the ERP implementation project would cause some problems and, accordingly, they had been flexible and tireless in carrying out their work for some time. However, with no clear signals on when the situation would possibly improve, signs of strain were beginning to show.

We won’t really be able to get a comprehensive picture of the situation (regarding the benefits of the system), before all these modifications are done. But the situation now is tha our people are mentally quite worn out, when there are so many things that should be done and so many things that we simply don’t have the time to do. [Vendor relations & materia supply]

These same problems had also affected the company’s customer service level which was considered to have decreased. Because information concerning current levels of inventory, the availability of products, and delivery times were not accurate, customers had made complaints regarding delayed deliveries, incomplete or incorrect orders, and invoicing mistakes. The whole process of sending sales offers, confirming received orders, and sending out orders had slowed down as information had to be checked from various sources before moving forward in the process. Unreliable information concerning product availability caused some dissatisfaction also in the distribution network, and the deceleration of company processes was visible for some important suppliers as well. The internal service level in the company had also decreased; internal inquiries and processes took longer than before and the time spent in sending out requests, writing and reading emails had grown.

Our customers are not satisfied. That’s the situation we’ve come to. We’re not able to serve our customers in an acceptable way, because the whole process takes so much longer. [Sales]; The system really hasn’t brought any improvements yet. Producing reports takes longer than before. It would be great if we could get it done in a day, but, for example, producing the first report took us two weeks working almost around the clock . . . The situation has improved since then, but we need more training and improvements to the reports before any real benefits come out of this. [Invoicing]

As can be observed from the description above, a number of issues seem to have contributed to the overall negative attitude regarding system success and the effects that system implementation had had. However, in certain respects, both qualitative and quantitative dat pointed to some variation in user views. Perceptions regarding the magnitude of the described problems seemed to vary. For example, issues regarding information quality and system potential divided opinions. In order to examine these observations in more detail, the quantitative responses were compared between different user groups. Results of this comparison are presented in the following.

Substantial differences can be noted in perceptions of, for example, the completeness and usefulness information as well as the overall usefulness of the system and impacts on IS integration. Similar observations can be made when examining differences regarding the net benefit dimension, except that in some cases invoicing provided higher values than vendo relations and material supply, and logistics personnel perceived impacts on decision-making to be relatively positive compared to sales personnel. From this comparison, it can be noted that, in general terms, problems which had begun in upstream processes, such as material supply, had gradually accumulated during in-house operations, such as in-house logistics, and had become more visible at the customer inter face. Respondents representing higher organizational levels and those who worked further from the customer interface did not perceive the situation as being so pessimistic. For example, problems regarding data reliability did not concern their day-to-day work, although some problems had arisen when trying to acquire information for certain reports or to support decision-making. Employees performing operational tasks and especially sales personnel had experienced the implementation project to be extremely troublesome and also felt the pressure coming from other organizational units and customers first hand. The perceived quality of intra-organizational user service was more dependent on support received from respective key users and overall experiences regarding training and the project organization. Thus, no general linkages between business processes and these user evaluations could be established.

## DISCUSSION

Based on the joint qualitative and quantitative empirical evidence, it was quite apparent that ERP users perceived system success to be relatively low, which, as such, is not surprising taking into account that the case division was going through the shakedown phase (cf. Nah et al., 2001; Hitt et al., 2002; Yusuf et al., 2004). However, what raises more concern is that the situation showed symptoms, which, if not tended to, could harm the competitiveness of the organization in its respective industry. The reasons behind both user perceptions and the actual higher level problems seemed to boil down to three major sources: (1) the lack of user ERP skills; (2) data reliability problems due to user mistakes; and (3) breakdowns in communication between different organizational levels. The implications of these problems are illustrated in Figure 7.

## Lack of user ERP skills

A number of the experienced problems can be traced down to the lack of training for ERP users. The general application training was perceived as being insufficient, and despite the enormous efforts of key users, mistakes and misunderstandings in interpreting system input and output data were frequent. The lack of training did not only deteriorate the current situation by generating customer complaints and employee frustration, but it also prohibited the whole organization from realizing the full potential linked to implementing the ERP system (cf. Gupta 2000). The design of the application-based or construct-based training courses concerning new system use can play a vital role in the employee learning process. Therefore, using pretraining with user participation to plan these courses might give better results (Shayo et al., 1999). Based on the case study evidence, it would seem that assuring that ERP users receive sufficient training and have adequate skills to operate the system correctly is even more critica in the after-sales business or in similar industries than, for example, in manufacturing organizations. Operations in this particular type of business have shorter time windows and lead times, and therefore results of system misuse are immediately visible to other supply chain members.

## Data reliability problems due to user mistakes

One of the main disadvantages of ERP systems is that, in some occasions, data errors are carried throughout the system (Gupta, 2000). As data on inventory levels, product availability, and current back orders was unreliable, system users frequently had to double-check infor mation. Once users can no longer trust the data coming from the system, they will begin to second-guess the whole system and its necessity. Also, the realization of potential benefits linked to adopting an ERP system is highly dependent on the accuracy of data. The cost of inaccuracy is higher inventories, lower profits, and a significant amount of non-value-added work (Ptak & Schragenheim, 2000), which will result in a totally reversed situation to the target scenario of the whole project. It could be argued that data accuracy is imperative for after-sales organizations, since the number of active items in sales and purchasing is far higher than in conventional manufacturing. Marginal data inaccuracies (e.g. 1–5%) may not necessarily represent a major problem in manufacturing, but in after-sales organizations inaccuracies will have direct effects in terms of lost sales and operational disruptions.

## Breakdowns in communication between different organizational levels

The management of communication throughout the organization is critical for any ERP project (cf. Barker & Frolick, 2003). Communication includes, for example, the formal advertisement of project progress and the distribution of liabilities linked to the project (Nah et al., 2001). In the case organization, the initial presentation of the project and its objectives had been successfully communicated to employees. However, after actual implementation a number of questions relating to responsibilities in user support, the status of system modifications as well as possible further application training had risen among employees, to which no one had given answers. This led to employee frustration. A specific finding relating to the after-sales business was that sales personnel seemed to be in a tight squeeze between deteriorated interna business processes on one side and the inability to respond to demanding customer requirements on the other. However, these people had little information to pass on to the customers in order to assure them that actions were being taken to improve the situation. In this context, the ability to communicate both between different organizational levels and between differen business processes would seem to be vital.

Despite the apparent limitations of the findings relating to the fact that the study gives a snapshot of the shakedown phase of ERP implementation, several lessons can be learned from these results. The abovementioned problems relating to user skills, data reliability, and communication could be resolved by taking these aspects into account already in the project planning stage. Based on this case study, it can be argued that in after-sales organizations, the importance of these issues is even more critical. While in this sense, the pre-implementation resolution of these problems through improved planning could be regarded as a critica success factor, it may be more fruitful to understand these problems through a processoriented perspective. The problems identified above can form a self-fulfilling cycle, where the lack of skills and information constantly deteriorates both user perceptions and actual operational performance. Letting these problems escalate can risk losing valuable employees due to deteriorated working conditions as well as important customers due to unreliable service. The case study results illustrate how downstream operations and customer relations are particularly vulnerable to problems accumulated in upstream business processes. Therefore, internal communication and application training should not be seen as a one-time matter and information should reach all organizational levels. This is important not only to ensure user satisfaction, but also to realize the full benefits of the project.

## CONCLUSIONS

Contemporary literature on ERP implementation argues that effective information management can be seen as a key factor in remaining competitive in a complex environment, such as the after-sales business. On the other hand, ERP implementation often represents an enormous and complex project, which involves multiple stakeholders and must take into account numerous issues, both before and after system implementation, to be successful.

This study provides insight into ERP evaluation in the turbulent shakedown phase, and is one of the first implementation studies carried out in an after-sales environment. In previous literature, ERP implementation and related success have often been discussed merely at an executive level. Less attention has been given to analysing valuable feedback from ERP users and the variance of experiences within organizations. Study findings indicate why, in the early phases of implementation, system and related operational performance may not match the objectives set by top level management, and why these signals from lower organizationa levels should not be overlooked. Instead of regarding these signals as something which is inevitable in the shakedown phase, actions should be taken to seek and understand the sources of related problems and their underlying dynamics in order to prevent problems from escalating and affecting company performance in a more permanent manner.

Through the case study, three specific problem areas were identified, which require tending to both before and after system implementation: assuring the quality of information, ensuring that users have adequate ERP skills, and communication between organizational levels. If these issues are not taken into account throughout the implementation process, it will reflect rapidly on both individual work and external customer service in a business environment with short time frames and high customer visibility. Evaluations were found to vary between users from different business processes. Employees performing operational tasks and users closest to the customer interface seemed to be in a tight squeeze between deteriorated internal business processes on one side and the inability to respond to demanding customer requirements on the other. Users at higher organizational levels and those working further from the customer interface did not perceive the situation as being so pessimistic.

One important fact, which must be taken into account, is that at the time of data collection only two months had elapsed from system implementation. Full benefits of such projects will not be fully experienced until a certain period of stability has been reached (Yusuf et al., 2004) Another limitation of the study relates to the fact that, in the case company, other changes in organizational structure and practices also were made during the same period as the imple mentation of the ERP system. Thus, not all the found impacts and related perceptions may necessarily be a result of mere ERP implementation, although efforts were made to provide a valid picture of causal relationships through the use of multiple methods of data collection and analysis.

Therefore, in order to attain a more comprehensive picture of the role of the shakedown phase and related evaluations in this dynamic and long-term process, it would be necessary to examine the related development of one organization through a longitudinal study. This type of study could examine the changes in user perceptions of the information quality, system quality, service quality, and net benefits of the ERP implementation over the phases of the life cycle. Impacts could be traced through business processes over time, to see if early negative impacts on, for example, system usefulness are magnified over time, or if they gradually even out. Such studies could increase the ability to create better implementation plans, that take into account different perspectives and consequences that relate to ERP implementation in a single organization, and thus result in more successful ERP projects.

## APPENDIX I INTERVIEW STRUCTURE

Please respond to the following statements on a scale from 1 to 7

(1 = Strongly disagree; 7 = Strongly agree):

## Information quality

1. The new system provides me with more reliable information than was previously available. Please give an example.

2. The new system provides me with more up-to-date information than was previously available. Please give an example.

3. The new system provides me with more comprehensive information than was previously available. Please give an example.

4. The new system provides me with more useful reports than were previously available. Please give an example.

## System quality

5. It has been easy to learn how to use the new system. Why is this in your opinion/why not?

6. I am satisfied with the response time and reliability of the new system. Why/why not?

7. The new system is very flexible and it has brought new opportunities to use information. Why/why not?

8. The new system has improved the level of integration between different systems. Why/why not?

9. The new system and the information it provides me with are extremely useful to me in my work. Why/why not?

## User service quality

10. The education and application training has been carried out well. Why/why not?

11. I am satisfied with the available support material. Why/why not?

12. I am able to receive user support concerning the use of the new system quickly and easily when I need it. Why/why not?

13. My requests for change concerning the new system are answered promptly. Why/why not?

14. My requests for change concerning different user authorizations are answered promptly. Why/why not?

## Net benefits

15. Using the system helps me do my work more efficiently (e.g. saves times, has eliminated overlaps). Why/why not? Effect xx h per week?

16. Using the system helps me improve the quality of my work. Why/why not?

17. Using the system helps me make decisions more frequently and/or with greater speed Why/why not?

18. Using the system helps me make decisions of higher quality. Why/why not?

19. Using the system has helped improve the customer service level and related external communication. Why/why not?

20. Using the system has helped improve the internal service level and related intra organizational communication. Why/why not?
