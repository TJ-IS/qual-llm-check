# A study of the service quality of general portals

## A B S T R A C T

An Internet portal provides not only a linkage to Web sites but also an entry point to information services. To be successful, it needs to attract a large volume of visitors and consistently deliver a high level of service quality. However, we believe that the service quality of Internet portals, differentiated into business and general portals, should be measured by different instruments, as they are used for different purposes. We conducted two studies employing exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis to identify an instrument to measure the service quality of general portals. The results indicated that the instrument is a four-factor model that includes adequacy of information, appearance, usability, and privacy and security. However, only the first two factors are significantly associated with customer satisfaction. Also, it was noted that Web page appearance was not significant for business portals but was for general portals.

## 1. Introduction

The number of innovative services is increasing with the growth of the Internet and e-commerce. One of the most promising services is the Internet portal. The information presented on it facilitates searching, communication, shopping, and many other functions. The term ‘‘portal,’’ adapted from seaport, implies a gateway for the delivery of goods from place to place and describes the entry point for direct access to Web sites. Connection, content, commerce, and community are the core functions of a portal.

An Internet portal has several distinguishing properties, such as its mission, depth of content, and targeted users. The mission of a portal may be transaction-based (designed to make a profit) or information-based (designed to distribute information). Its content may be split into horizontal or vertical: a horizontal portal or ‘‘hortal,’’ offers a broad range of content and services to a large number of users, whereas a vertical portal, or ‘‘vortal,’’ has content targeted to a particular group of users. In addition, it can be designed for public or private use; the general public can access a public portal, whereas access to a private portal is restricted to members. In general, we can also differentiate portals into business and general ones. The former are designed for a group of users and contain vertical information that is generally transaction-based, whereas the latter provide access to a wide range of information and are thus information-based.

Service quality is a measure of the users’ expectation of service from an organization, and its evaluation varies across industries and products; for example, some services cannot be evaluated before they are purchased or until experiencing them, such as dry cleaning of clothing, whereas other products or services cannot be evaluated even after the purchase, such as auto repair and legal advice. Applying service quality dimensions to customer satisfaction with Web sites draws attention to such factors of online service as responsiveness, ease of use, control enhancement, transaction reduction, service reliability, competence, accessibility, and security [26].

To be successful, an Internet portal must attract a large amount of traffic and enjoy a high degree of customer satisfaction. However, there are very few studies of the service quality of Internet portals. The study of Yang et al. found that usability, usefulness of content, adequacy of information, accessibility, and interaction were significant factors of service quality for business portals. However, there were fundamental differences between general and business portals; for example, general portals had little proprietary content to retain visitors [21]. Factors such as customer care, information benefits, and information facilitation have been found to be important for business portals. General portals, however, do not normally sell products or services directly; thus customer care may not be as imperative. In addition, a general portal is designed for the public, and its content is more diverse.

We explored service quality instruments that determined customer satisfaction with general portal services. Social and applied psychology studies have suggested that satisfaction is a function of an initial standard and perceived discrepancy from it. ## 2. Background

The most common way to locate a Web site is through a search engine (such as Google) or an Internet portal (such as Yahoo). A search engine normally allows users to enter keywords and locate information directly and efficiently. In contrast, a portal is like a shopping mall that aggregates e-services. It is then difficult to satisfy customers because the services are diversified. We investigated and developed a suitable instrument to measure service quality and identify how it affects customer satisfaction with a general portal.

The dimensions of competitive service quality have been well discussed [25], and many models of it have been proposed, particularly including SERVQUAL [6–9,18]. Its framework has also been used to assess Internet applications. Many of the service quality constructs used in prior studies are shown in Table 1.

An instrument based on SERVQUAL and WebQual [5] was developed, in [10], to measure the service quality of Web portals; it included 14 items grouped into three benefit constructs (customer care and risk reduction, information, and interaction facilitation). In [24], 19 items that assessed business portals were grouped into five constructs: usability, usefulness of content, adequacy of information, accessibility, and interaction. General portals, however, need to attract more visitors and thus require a good design, appealing to users.

The five items of appearance that we considered were therefore: (1) attractiveness, (2) organization, (3) proper use of fonts, (4) proper use of color, and (5) proper use of multimedia.

## 3.1. Study one

The objective of our first study was to provide an initial assessment of the effectiveness of a service quality framework in the evaluation of general portals using an existing questionnaire developed for the evaluation of business portals. The questionnaire was distributed in April 2006 through a professional online survey service that ensured its distribution to motivated Internet users.

A total of 120 responses were collected, of which 91 were usable. It was found that the goodness-of-fit index $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was 341 (degrees of freedom = 142), with a goodness-of-fit index (GFI) of 0.71, the adjusted GFI (AGFI) was 0.62, the comparative fit index (CFI) was 0.82, the normalized fit index (NFI) was 0.74, the nonnormalized fit index (NNFI) was 0.78, and the RMS error of approximation (RMSEA) was 0.12. As $\chi ^ { 2 }$ is sensitive to sample size and is normally significant, it was not used as an index here. Lai and Li [11] suggested that, if a model is to be considered well fitting, the NFI, NNFI, CFI, and GFI should be greater than 0.9, the AGFI should be greater than 0.8, and the RMSEA should be less than 0.08. Thus, the model was not a good fitted, and the measurement of the service quality of general portals is apparently different from that of business portals. Thus, a new service quality measurement for general portals had to be developed.

## 3.2. Study two

In our second study, the refined instrument was used in a rigorous assessment of general portals. In May 2006, the questionnaire was distributed through the same channel. Of the 380 total responses, 330 (89%) were usable. We then conducted CFA to test the construct validity. All factor loadings of items were required to be significant (their t-values greater than 1.96). In this part of our study, the t-values ranged from 9.0 to 18.1 (significant at the p < .001 level), as shown in Table 4. Discriminant validity was tested by comparing the average variance extracted (AVE) of each construct to the shared variance between the construct and all other variables. The explained variance exceeded all combinations of shared variance, which indicated that discriminant validity had been achieved [22]. The model also showed a good model fit: $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to the degree of freedom was 2.41, RMSEA was 0.065, CFI was 0.97, GFI was 0.93, NFI was 0.95, NNFI was 0.96, and SRMR was 0.051. The item loadings of the corresponding constructs ranged from 0.50 to 0.89. Hence, the model fitted well.

Our study was designed to develop an instrument to measure how service quality was related to customer satisfaction in general portals. We assumed that there existed a second-order factor of overall general portal service quality that explained the first-order factors. A second-order factor measurement was developed and is shown in Fig. 1. The data showed an excellent fit to this model, with a ratio of the chi-square to degree of freedom of 2.44, RMSEA of 0.066, CFI of 0.97, GFI of 0.93, NNFI of 0.96, and SRMR of 0.056. All four first-order factors loaded significantly on the second-order factors. We further tested the path coefficients by comparing the constrained model (with equated paths) with the unconstrained model (paths estimated freely). If the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic of the unconstrained model differed from that of the constrained model, then one factor existed that has a greater influence on service quality than had the other factor. The results showed that three factors, adequacy of information (0.81), usability (0.74), and privacy and security (0.57), contributed more significantly to service quality than did appearance (0.54).

The demographic variables in study 2 included gender, age, education level, income level, and job status. Of the respondents, 60% were female, 88.5% were aged 20 to 29, 65.5% were college educated, and 72.7% had a medium income. Following [4], to assess non-response bias and because of the small sample size in study 1, we used two groups, the first and last 20% of respondents, in each service quality construct. No significant differences were found across the constructs, suggesting no problems with non-response bias in the study 1. Next, we selected the first and last 10% of respondents in study 2. Again, no significant differences were found across the constructs. We compared the data of study 1 with those of study 2, and the chi-squared tests revealed no significant differences in any of the demographic characteristics (gender, age, education level, income level, and job status).

We found that the 14 items, grouped into four factors that could be used to measure service quality in general portals. We then examined the service quality instrument in terms of its nomological validity. Because better service quality may increase customer satisfaction, we tested this relationship. Six customer satisfaction questions, based on those of [3], were asked: (1) I am satisfied with my decision to use the portal; (2) If I had to use it again, I would feel different about choosing the portal; (3) My choice to use this portal was a wise one; (4) I feel badly about my decision to use the portal; (5) I think that I did the right thing when I decided to use the portal; and (6) I am sorry that I used the portal. To assess the nomological validity of these, we correlated the average of these with the average dimensional score for each of the four factors. All factors had positive and statistically significant correlations $\left( p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ , ranging from 0.25 to 0.48 (not shown here); this indicated the nomological validity of this scale.

Multiple regression analysis was used to analyze the contribution of each service quality factor to customer satisfaction. The F value was significant (p < 0.001), with an averaged adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.268, as shown in Table 5. This indicated that providing these service quality factors affected customer satisfaction. Looking into the relationship of individual factors to customer satisfaction, we found that only adequacy of information and appearance were significant. Based on the $\beta$ values, the sequence of factors in descending order of importance was appearance (0.40), adequacy of information (0.16), usability (0.07), and privacy and security (0.03). Thus design (fonts, colors, multimedia, attractiveness, etc.) mattered most, followed by the information provided on the portal. Other factors did not contribute significantly to customer satisfaction for a general portal.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

This study conducted two sets of analyses to develop and validate an instrument that measured the service quality of general portals plus its effect on customer satisfaction. Our work resulted in.

A four-factor instrument for measuring the service quality of general portals. It included usability, privacy and security, adequacy of information, and appearance, and was different from that developed for business portals, in which items such as up-to-date information, complete product descriptions, valuable tips on products, and detailed contact information had been found to be important. Privacy and security are apparently major concerns of general portal users and appearance was found to be a critical factor of service quality for general portals.

Of course, public access to the Internet has greatly improved in recent years and general portals do not sell products or services, however portal appearance was seen as more important in attracting the public.

Different service quality constructs of customer satisfaction. The relationship between service quality and customer satisfaction is different from that of business portals, where promptness of service and ease of navigation of the site are crucial to customer satisfaction. In the general portal, appearance and adequacy of information are important.

Information quality and quantity play important roles in Internet portals. Our findings indicated that adequacy of information was the most important part of service quality and most noteworthy for customer satisfaction. The same was true of business portals. Thus, when a user accesses either portal, he or she expects to gain unique and complete information and find links to other useful sites. Our findings should help system developers design an effective Web site and develop its functions accordingly.
