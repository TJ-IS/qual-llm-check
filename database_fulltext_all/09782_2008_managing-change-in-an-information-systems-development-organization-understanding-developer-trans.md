# Managing change in an information systems development organization: understanding developer transitions from a structured to an object-oriented development environment<sup>1</sup>

Teresa M. Shaft,\* Leslie Jordan Albert<sup>†</sup> & ‘Jon (Sean) Jasperson<sup>‡</sup> \*Division of Management Information Systems, Price College of Business, 307 West Brooks, Room 306A, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK 73019-4006, USA, email: tshaft@ou.edu, <sup>†</sup>College of Business, Department of Management Information Systems, San José State University, One Washington Square, San José, CA 95192-0244, USA, email: albert\_l@cob.sjsu.edu, and <sup>‡</sup>Department of Information and Operations Management, Mays Business School, Texas A&M University, 4217 TAMU, College Station, TX 77843-4217, USA, email: jon.jasperson@tamu.edu

Abstract. Object-oriented systems development has attracted great interest in the information systems (IS) field because of a belief that using object-oriented development (OD) makes it easier to develop and maintain software plus achieve software reuse. However, the transition from structured to OD may be especially challenging for IS developers. In fact, some argue that structured development (SD) knowledge may interfere with the ability to learn OD approaches because of a form of proactive interference.

To understand how knowledge of SD concepts influences the ability to gain OD knowledge, we assessed a team of IS developers’ understanding of structured and object-oriented concepts at the beginning of their first OD project and repeated our assessment 1 year later. Developers increased their understanding of some object-oriented concepts, but these changes were associated with increases in understanding of one of the SD concepts. Further, at the beginning and end of the project, developers’ mental models were highly consistent with one another and that of an OD expert with regard to most OD concepts. It appears that the formal training that developers received allowed them to attain a good understanding of most object-oriented concepts. These findings challenge the idea that structured developers must ‘unlearn’ SD to adopt OD approaches. Future research may wish to examine the different types of software development knowledge to ascertain which are amenable to positive transfer and which are more likely to suffer from proactive interference.

Keywords: object-oriented development, structured development, shared menta models, longitudinal study

## INTRODUCTION

Object-oriented development (OD) has attracted the interest of information systems (IS) academics and practitioners because of the belief that OD makes it easier to develop and maintain software plus achieve software reuse (Alvarez et al., 2003; Rob, 2006). In the early years of OD, experts attributed these benefits to OD’s ability to ‘reflect reality more naturally . . .’ and allow developers to ‘. . . build software that more closely models the real world (Martin & Odell, 1992, p. 41). Further, OD was considered more consistent with IS developers cognitive processes and with the way developers normally approach and categorize objects and events (Rosson & Alpert, 1990). However, despite the touted benefits of OD, adoption of OD has progressed more slowly than expected (Sircar et al., 2001; Cho & Kim, 2002).

One barrier to the adoption and spread of OD is that most professional developers have experience with structured development (SD). These developers typically possess extensive business knowledge that firms wish to retain (Shaft & Vessey, 1995). Thus, despite arguments that experienced structured developers cannot adapt, when adopting OD, firms may choose to transition experienced staff rather than hire developers with training or experience in OD (Sharp & Griffyth, 1999). As SD and OD apply different methods to develop software, the transition to OD requires IS developers to learn new to requirements modelling techniques, computer-programs languages and software tools.

While the change to any new development method can be difficult (Lee & Pennington, 1994; Fayad et al., 1996), the differences in the cognitive processes required by these two approaches make the transition from SD to OD especially challenging (Sharp & Griffyth, 1999; Sircar et al., 2001). ‘[T]he thinking process, the discovery process, and the communication between user and analyst are fundamentally different . . .’ with OD compared to SD (Coad & Yourdon, 1991, p. 192). Many believe that these differences require developers shifting from SD to OD to change drastically the way they think (Pennington et al., 1995).

It may seem reasonable that prior IS development experience would ease the transition to OD by providing developers with software development knowledge upon which to build specific OD knowledge. However, some have argued that developers who began with structured techniques are incapable of shifting to an object-oriented view (Fayad & Tsai, 1995; Fayad et al., 1996). A specific barrier facing developers making this shift is that SD knowledge leads to a form of proactive interference (Nelson et al., 2002). Proactive interference occurs when existing knowledge structures impede the acquisition of new knowledge (O’Carroll et al., 1993). When prior knowledge interferes with learning, learners must ‘unlearn’ existing knowledge to acquire new knowledge. If SD knowledge interferes with the acquisition of OD knowledge, experienced developers’ SD knowledge hinders their efforts to transition to OD and they must unlearn SD to learn OD. Thus, proactive interference offers one explanation for why experienced structured developers have difficulty transitioning to OD. However, there is limited empirical research on the shift to new IS development methods; therefore, the question remains regarding the extent to which developers’ SD knowledge facilitates or inhibits the transition to OD.

To increase our understanding of OD knowledge acquisition among experienced IS developers, we studied a team of professional developers as they conducted their first OD project. We focus on the cognitive aspects of the transition to OD because the cognitive structures of IS developers are a critical component of IS development, particularly with regard to the application of an IS development method (Lee & Truex, 2000). To assess the extent to which SD knowledge interferes with the transition to OD, we studied changes in the team members knowledge of concepts associated with each development approach. The following three questions guided our research into developers’ transition from SD to OD: (1) Do software developers ‘unlearn’ SD knowledge to acquire OD knowledge? (2) How does an individual software developer’s knowledge of SD and OD concepts change while working on an OD project? and (3) Do training and experience help structured developers create mental models consistent with those of an OD expert?

## THEORETICAL DEVELOPMENT

Research on learning suggests that one way individuals learn new concepts is through analogy, which involves relating novel concepts to concepts (or schema) that he/she already understands (Chidambaram, 1999). However, analogies may facilitate or hinder the acquisition of new knowledge. When new concepts are similar to known concepts, the learner may make a good match between such concepts. A good match enables a positive transfer of learning as the prior knowledge aids learning new concepts (Finkelstein & Haleblian, 2002). However, when new and understood concepts only appear similar, the learner may make a poor match between concepts. When this occurs, a negative transfer of prior knowledge hinders knowledge acquisition and makes learning new concepts more difficult than if no prior knowledge existed (Chidambaram, 1999). Previous researchers refer to this negative effect on learning as proactive interference (Armstrong, 2001). The concept of proactive interference stems from negative transfer theory and occurs when prior learning or presented information blocks an individual’s ability to learn, recall or use new knowledge (e.g. Melton & Irwin, 1940; Underwood, 1957; Nairne et al., 1997; Lustig & Hasher, 2001). Negative transfer theory further suggests that learners must unlearn their previous knowledge to overcome proactive interference and understand the new concepts (Nelson et al., 2002).

Previous studies of physiological and cognitive skills acquisition provide evidence of proactive interference because of negative transfer. For example, tennis students who first learned the forehand stroke had difficulty learning the backhand stroke (Eason et al., 1989) and experienced pilots had difficulties adapting to new, reversed-direction control-sticks (Hendrick, 1983). Learners have experienced proactive interference when transitioning between text editors (Singley & Anderson, 1989), programming techniques (Kessler & Anderson, 1986) and problem-solving tasks (Luchins, 1942). In these studies, subjects attempted to learn new concepts that appeared similar to known concepts, but were actually quite different. As a result, subjects made poor matches between novel and known concepts and experienced proactive interference. Similarly, proactive interference may occur during the transition from SD to OD because the two methods share some similarities yet are fundamentally different approaches (Armstrong and Hardgrave, 2007).

Both development approaches provide developers the means to break large, complex problems into smaller, more manageable pieces. However, conceptualization, decomposition and data handling differ between approaches. For example, SD uses a top-down approach, starting with the overall problem and focusing on data flows to create logical requirements that meet user specifications independent of implementation issues (DeMarco, 1979). In OD, analysts categorize aspects of the system specification into classes or objects, which possess behaviours and states. The objects interact with other objects within the system (Lee & Pennington, 1994).

SD and OD manage complexity differently. In SD approaches, developers decompose the system into processes that transform data within the system. This allows developers to design modular processes with levels of cohesion and coupling to facilitate coding and implementation (DeMarco, 1979; Yourdon, 1989). OD abstracts out the details and models the system in terms of the essential objects. These different approaches to managing complexity require structured developers transitioning to OD to stop thinking about ‘blocks of code that do things’ and conceptualize ‘objects that are things and have certain behavior’ (Nelson et al., 2002, p. 132).

With regard to data, SD developers think of functions and data as separate. Using OD approaches, data and behaviour are integrated (Détienne, 1995). The combination of similarities and differences may cause SD developers to make improper analogies between SD and OD concepts. The resulting proactive interference may be a root cause of the difficulty in the transition from SD to OD (Nelson et al., 2002).

Prior research has identified numerous examples of improper analogies between SD and OD concepts, suggesting that the transfer of SD knowledge produces errors in OD solutions (e.g. Détienne, 1995; 1997). In the following quote, we see an example of proactive interference where a programmer’s SD understanding of a string prevents him from understanding a string in the OD sense:

It really hit me last night when [the TA] was talking about a string. She was talking about sending a message to a string, and I was thinking, well, OK, send a message to a string, that makes sense. But part of me . . . was thinking that makes no sense at all because I know what a string is, it’s on page 30 of Kerninhan and Ritchie, and it’s a series of alpha characters terminated by a null. Sending a message to that makes no sense at all. So I had to redefine, overload, or whatever my understanding of the word ‘string’ to mean a Smalltalk string, an instance of string, that has some intelligence that would understand the ‘do’ message. But my brain was stuck in the old concept of string. When I realized that I was stuck I was able to get out of that and reburn that memory trace or whatever (Nelson et al., 1997, p. 69).

Despite this student’s efforts to change his mental processes from SD to OD, at the end of the course he found himself falling back on his SD knowledge to ‘get the job done’ because of the difficulties he experienced in applying his newly acquired OD knowledge to completing a Smalltalk programming assignment (Nelson et al., 1997).

In summary, prior knowledge can help or hinder learning. As depicted in Figure 1, if an appropriate mapping between SD and OD concepts occurs, existing SD knowledge facilitates learning of the OD concepts: positive transfer. If a poor mapping between SD and OD concepts occurs, existing SD knowledge hinders learning of the new OD concepts relative to if no analogical mapping had taken place. In this situation, negative transfer occurs, resulting in proactive interference. Prior research indicates that experienced structured developers make improper analogies between SD and OD concepts. Such negative transfer results in proactive interference and contributes to the challenges faced by experienced developers making the transition to OD.

Overcoming proactive interference requires unlearning the prior knowledge that hinders efforts to understand new concepts. This occurs in the previous example when the student feels he has to ‘reburn that memory trace’ or mentally rewrite his understanding of a string. If SD knowledge causes proactive interference with OD knowledge acquisition, SD knowledge should decline as developers unlearn it while acquiring OD knowledge. Hence, we anticipate an inverse relationship between any changes in SD and OD knowledge through the course of an OD project. We state the following hypothesis:

H1: There is a negative association between changes in a developer’s OD knowledge and SD knowledge.

Hypothesis 1 investigates the relationship between changes in knowledge of OD and SD concepts. To investigate developers’ changes in SD and OD knowledge during the transition to OD, we consider how developers’ understanding of both SD and OD change independently. To overcome proactive interference, developers may have ‘unlearned’ SD knowledge to acquire OD knowledge. Therefore, we anticipate that developers’ SD knowledge will diminish. Further, even if proactive interference is not a major influence, it seems reasonable that immersion in an OD project will make it more difficult for developers to recall SD knowledge because of lack of use.

H2a: At the end of the project, a software developer’s SD knowledge will have diminished compared to his or her SD knowledge at the beginning of the project.

We also investigate changes in OD knowledge. We anticipate that developers will demonstrate increased knowledge of OD at the end of a project compared to the beginning. While this expectation is a bit intuitive, we include this hypothesis to interpret fully any results of H1 and H2a. If no relationship between changes in SD and OD knowledge is detected, such a finding could be attributed to the independence of these knowledge categories but would also be consistent with no changes in OD knowledge. Further, if no decline in SD knowledge is detected (i.e. H2a is not supported), one explanation is that it is unnecessary to ‘unlearn’ SD knowledge to gain OD knowledge. Alternatively, if software developers failed to acquire OD knowledge, then they would have had no reason to ‘unlearn’ SD knowledge. Therefore, to establish whether developers acquired OD knowledge, we state the following hypothesis:

H2b: At the end of the project, a software developer’s OD knowledge will have increased compared to his or her OD knowledge at the beginning of the project.

In addition to changes in knowledge, Lee & Truex (2000) suggest that investigating developers’ cognitive structures, or mental models, and the evolution of these structures may provide a deeper understanding of the system development process. Therefore, we also consider the consistency of developers' mental models with an experienced OD developer. Mental models refer to the ‘general class of cognitive constructs that have been invoked to explain how knowledge and information are represented in the mind’ (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994, p. 405). Our interest in the consistency of the developers’ mental models stems from work on shared cognition, which investigates the antecedents and implications of the consistency and divergence of team members’ mental structures. Investigations into shared cognition have considered many types of knowledge: task knowledge (both procedural and declarative), contextual (organizational) knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, etc. (Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001). Slightly different terms have evolved to reflect the specific properties of shared cognition under investigation: team mental model, shared mental model, team situation model, etc. (Cooke et al., 2000). Regardless of the specific form of shared cognition, a consistent motivation for such studies is that team performance improves when team members possess similar menta structures (Klimoski & Mohammed, 1994). For instance, consistency of team members’ menta models aids coordination in large-scale software development teams (Espinosa et al., 2002).

Similarity of mental models, however, is not necessarily meaningful if team members share similar but inaccurate knowledge (Smith-Jentsch et al., 2001). Thus, mental model similarity does not indicate mental model accuracy. However, for less experienced teammates to attain mental models similar to that of an experienced teammate indicates beneficial development of their mental models (Smith-Jentsch et al., 2001). Hence, it would be desirable for those making the transition to OD to develop mental models of OD consistent with those of an experienced OD developer. Therefore, we compare the consistency of the transitioning developers’ mental models with that of an experienced OD developer at different points in time. Training and experience influence knowledge, and thus mental models. We do not anticipate that formal training alone allows developers to create mental models of OD concepts consistent with that of an OD expert. However, antecedents of mental model consistency include a similar education (Rentsch & Klimoski, 2001). As the transitioning developers participated in the same formal OD training, it is likely that their mental models with regard to OD concepts will be consistent. Therefore:

H3a: At the end of a training period but before acquiring project experience, software developers making the transition to OD will have mental models of OD concepts that are more consistent among themselves than an OD expert.

Over time, developers making the transition acquire additional knowledge of OD concepts Through this process, we expect that their mental models will become more consistent with those of an OD expert. Although most empirical evidence indicates that team members’ mental models become more consistent over time, research has not always supported this expectation. For example, a team of software developers’ roles became more specialized, resulting in a decrease in interaction over time and in less consistency among their mental models (Levesque et al., 2001). Because we cannot assume that team mental models become more consistent over time, we consider whether the transitioning developers’ mental models became more or less consistent with that of an OD expert. If the transitioning developers mental models became less consistent with that of an OD expert, the developers may have failed to utilize their OD knowledge and instead used their SD knowledge during the project (Nelson et al., 1997). As most empirical evidence indicates that team mental models become more consistent over time, we state the following:

H3b: After completing a project, software developers making the transition to OD will have mental models of OD concepts as consistent with an OD expert as with one another

We anticipate that a negative association between these two constructs could manifest because of either SD knowledge interfering with the acquisition of OD knowledge (consistent with earlier examples of proactive interference) or the acquisition of OD knowledge resulting in the ‘unlearning’ of SD knowledge to overcome proactive interference.

## RESEARCH METHOD

We studied an IS development team employed by an international petroleum company and engaged in the firm’s pilot OD project. The system was a Web-enabled, real-time reporting and transaction system appropriate for OD (Sircar et al., 2001). The project was a redeployment o a business-to-business extranet. The earlier system enabled customers to view some stored information, such as invoices. Unfortunately, the earlier system relied upon multiple sources of data, which were not always consistent, creating problems for users and requiring effort to reconcile. The new system pulled data from a single source, eliminating such problems. Further, it provided new functionality, allowing customers to access their relevant transaction information, including the ability to view their credit settlements and lift information (when a customer extracts petroleum products from a terminal location). Further, customers could create reports including an invoice report, which provided customers with summary level data of their invoices and an electronic funds transfer notifications report, which allowed users to see the specific debits and credits on their account. Finally, by interfacing with the existing SAP system, the new system supported placing orders, including updating and cancelling orders.

Prior to the project, all developers attended a series of training courses on OD concepts taugh on-site by a SUN-certified trainer. Courses included JAVA for structured programmers, OD Application Analysis and Design for JAVA, Developing JAVA Servelets, JAVA server pages, Developing J2EE Compliant Enterprise Applications, Database Application Programming with JAVA, Architecting and Designing J2EE Applications, and J2EE Patterns. The developers, bu not the project manager, attended 5 weeks of training full-time. Although the training was an official part of the project, the content was generic to OD rather than specific to the new system.

In the courses, the developers learned and subsequently used the Unified Modelling Language (UML). The UML contains guidelines regarding proper notation for creating graphic representations of the static and dynamic aspects of a computer system. The UML is applicable for developers using OD. By design, the UML does not contain guidelines for developers regarding a process to employ when using the UML during OD projects (Booch et al., 1999; Rumbaugh et al., 1999; Fowler & Scott, 2000). The Rational Unified Process (RUP), as a software engineering process, gives developers an approach to follow when applying the UML in an OD project (Fowler & Scott, 2000; Kruchten, 2000; Kroll & Kruchten, 2003). Created by the Rational Software Company. RUP captures many of the best practices in modern software development suitable for a wide range of projects and organizations (Kruchten, 2000, p. 18). RUP includes (1) inception phase for systems planning, building the business case and setting the scope for the proposed system; (2) elaboration phase for analyzing and designing system specifications; (3) construction phase for developing, coding, testing and integrating system features; and (4) transition phase for moving the software to the user community. Iteration plays a key role in RUP. One systems development project should have multiple iterations among the four phases. Developers reduce and manage complexity by selecting a subset of functionality from the inception phase for a single iteration through the other RUP phases. Thus, system delivery occurs in a series of small version releases with a controlled set of functionality (Kruchten, 2000; Kroll & Kruchten, 2003).

Based on advice from OD consultants, the team applied a modified version of RUP for the project. They first created a use case diagram to define the scope of the proposed system. The project team defined several project iterations by identifying a subset of use cases to include in each version of the system. During elaboration, team members identified software classes for later coding by developing class diagrams. In addition, the team employed a combination of activity diagrams and/or sequence diagrams to model a more complex logic for the use cases. The team used the class, activity and sequence diagrams to direct their activities during the construction phase. As a significant modification from RUP, the team engaged in iteration with several elaboration and construction phases; however, the team employed a more traditional approach by releasing the software to the users as a complete system.

Participants in our study included three women and five men with 8.3 years average (range: 1–25 years) professional software development experience (including the OD expert and the project manager). Excluding the OD expert and the project manager, the average professional experience is 7.6 years (range: 1–25 years). The OD expert (hired specifically for this project because of his expertise in OD) had a graduate degree in a computer science and 11 years of professional experience. The project manager and five developers had undergraduate degrees in Management Information Systems (MIS). The developer with the most experience (25 years) did not possess a bachelor’s degree. One developer completed her Masters in Business Administration (MBA) during the course of the project. One developer reported limited exposure to OD concepts in a C++ course as a student. Otherwise, the developers and the project manager reported no meaningful exposure to OD prior to this project. Except the OD expert, our research participants were professional, structured developers with limited exposure to OD prio to this project. Thus, the team is appropriate for this study. That is, a less experienced group of developers might face fewer challenges because of proactive interference (Nelson et al., 1997). Management assigned developers exclusively to the OD project for its duration.

We conducted phone interviews with the business sponsor and the project manager during an exploratory phase to determine if the project would be appropriate to study. Based on those interviews, we concluded that the project was appropriate for the following reasons: (1) It was the pilot OD project for the firm. As such, with the exception of one newly hired OD expert, it was the first OD project for the developers working on the project. (2) The firm had made a commitment to OD. Specifically: full-time, on-site OD training for the developers, as described earlier; an investment in new software and hardware systems (operating system, programming language, UNIX servers, etc.); and hiring an OD expert and an OD consulting firm. This commitment convinced us that the developers would have the support needed to make the transition and that the firm was unlikely to cancel the project. Given the commitment to OD made by the firm and the development team’s experience with SD and inexperience with OD, this setting provided us with a relatively unique opportunity to study professional IS developers making the transition from SD to OD.

During the phone interviews, we developed a research protocol that allowed a robust investigation of the phenomena and was agreeable to the firm. We utilized a multi-method approach that incorporated in-person group interviews, email logs and responses to a survey instrument. A multi-method approach allowed us to investigate the phenomena in a more robust fashion than a single method allows. By considering multiple sources of data, a researcher can better ascertain if findings are an artefact of the method rather than descriptive of the phenomena (Campbell & Fiske, 1959).

Following the phone interviews, we mailed the project team a survey to assess their understanding of software development (both SD and OD) constructs, which we discuss in detail below. We also asked the project team to submit email logs biweekly and conducted on-site group interviews at approximately monthly intervals. We requested the email logs electronically at 2-week intervals through the initial period of system development. If developers did not respond to the first request, we sent the same request a second time. We asked developers to record their thoughts and experiences regarding the transition process, and specifically their learning process. Because of the sensitive nature of the material, only the researchers had access to the developers’ responses. We assured developers regarding the confidentiality of their logs. In a span of 8 months, 87 logs were submitted: 74 from the seven developers making the transition (a range of 5–15, mean of 11), 11 from the Project Manager (PM) (who was away for 2 months of the development process for maternity leave) and two from the OD expert. On average, we received five logs per time period; however, there was a noticeable drop-off in responses as the go-live date approached (we received only three logs in each of the last three time periods). There appeared to be two reasons for the drop-off. First, developers were busy and did not wish to take time away from their work to complete a log. Second, it appeared that there were now fewer issues to report.

In general, the email logs were short, a paragraph or less, although on occasion developers provided one to two pages. Prior to each group interview, we reviewed the contents of the logs received since the last interview to generate discussion questions for the upcom ing interview.

We conducted the on-site group interviews with as many members of the development team as available that day. In total, we conducted nine interviews: seven at approximately monthly intervals during development, one approximately 2 months after the system had ‘gone live’ and a final interview 1 year after the previous interview. Hence, the interviews spanned nearly 2 years. The first author attended and facilitated all interviews. Throughout the interviews and logs, the developers maintained that they did not find the transition to OD more difficult than other technology transitions, such as to a new SD language or development environment. The developers saw issues other than the transition to OD as bigger challenges. Three issues noted consistently were that developing the application and framework concurrently created rework for the developers, uncertainty in how to interface the existing SAP systems with the new Web-based system, and awareness that the deadlines were unrealistic. The developers maintained that they did not need to ‘unlearn’ their SD knowledge to adopt OD, which was inconsistent with the arguments and findings of earlier researchers. However, the interview and email log data were qualitative and self-reported by the developers. Therefore, it was possible that developers were unaware or unable to report on their process accurately (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). In contrast, the survey responses provide a more objective assessment of software developers’ knowledge structures. Hence, we focus on these data in the remainder of this paper.

To assess developers’ understanding of software development constructs, we used the Software Development and Maintenance Approach (SDMA) instrument (Armstrong, 2001). Because of the short time between our notification about the project and its start date, rather than develop our own instrument, we searched for an existing, validated instrument to measure software developers’ understanding of SD and OD concepts. The advantage of using an existing instrument include:

1 the opportunity to test the instrument ‘across heterogeneous settings and times . . .’ (Straub, 1989, p. 148);

2 improving our understanding of independent and dependent variables by measuring ‘. . . the same constructs in the same way . . .’ across studies (Straub, 1989, p. 148); and 3 gathering data where there is insufficient time to validate a new instrument.

Before using an existing instrument, researchers should (1) ensure that the instrument was properly validated and (2) not make significant changes to the instrument without revalidating it (Straub, 1989). (In the Appendix, we summarize the steps taken to validate this instrumen (Armstrong, 2001). We made no changes to the SMDA instrument because it measured a construct space quite similar to our study (i.e. developers’ understanding of OD and SD constructs). The SDMA was developed to study an ‘individual’s object-oriented mindset’ in the context of an investigation of proactive interference between OD and SD knowledge (Armstrong, 2001). Therefore, consistent with our study, it assesses developers’ mental structures related to SD and OD. However, Armstrong (2001) applied the instrument in a cross-sectional fashion and contrasted across developers with varying levels of OD experience rather than studying the changes over time. Thus, there is a significant overlap in research questions and construct space, which allows us to use the SDMA without modifying it. However, the studies contributions are distinct.

In addition to a similar construct space, the participants in Armstrong’s (2001) study and in the current study share similar characteristics, as all were software development professionals. As discussed in the Appendix, the SMDA instrument was developed, validated, and tested with OD experts, SD experts and those making the transition from SD to OD. Our participants included one OD expert and seven developers making the transition from SD to OD. To understand how developers’ knowledge of SD and OD constructs changed, we administered the survey twice: Time 1 – at the beginning of the project (shortly after the development team completed 5 weeks of OD training) and Time 2 – approximately 1 year later (3 months after the initial go-live date). From the ‘go live’ to Time 2, the development team was engaged in system maintenance and revision. We compared developers’ responses to the two administrations of the survey to assess the development of their OD and SD mental structures over the course of the project. Given the 1-year interval between Time 1 and Time 2 data collection, it is unlikely that developers’ memories of Time 1 responses biased their Time 2 responses.

## ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

To analyze the survey responses from each developer, we developed scores for the five OD and two SD concepts identified via the factor analysis described in the Appendix. We use the Armstrong (2001) factors because our sample size precludes us from confirming the factor structure. Because of the sample size, a is set to 0.10 for all analyses.

## Hypothesis 1

The first hypothesis concerns the relationship between developers’ knowledge of SD and OD concepts. Specifically, if proactive interference occurs and developers need to ‘unlearn’ SD to learn OD, we expect an inverse relationship between changes in SD and OD knowledge. We created change scores by subtracting a developer’s score for each concept at Time 1 from the score at Time 2. ## Hypothesis 2

Hypothesis 2 relates to changes in a developer’s knowledge of the SD and OD concepts. We conducted paired t-tests using developers’ responses to the Time 1 and Time 2 administrations of the survey (Table 6). A positive mean indicates that developers’ understanding of the concept increased.

We argued that developer’s knowledge of SD concepts would diminish over the course of the project (H2a) in the process of acquiring OD knowledge. However, H2a is not supported. Developers did not experience any significant changes (increases or decreases) in SD knowl edge. With regard to H2b, we expected developers to demonstrate development of OD concepts; and this hypothesis receives mixed support. Only the ‘nesting’ and ‘system level concepts’ demonstrated a significant development. Inspection of the data revealed that developers’ knowledge of ‘nesting’ and ‘system level concepts’ at Time 1 was lower than the other three OD concepts. However, by Time 2, developers increased their knowledge of these concepts to levels similar to the other three. This finding suggests that developers gainec sufficient knowledge of three OD concepts (creating objects, object characteristics and interaction) via classroom training to complete the project. This finding seems inconsistent with the argument that OD is an extremely difficult transition for experienced developers.

## Hypothesis 3

Hypotheses 3a and 3b concern the consistency between IS developers’ mental models with regard to OD concepts. Because of differences in background and training, we compare the mental models of the transitioning developers with that of an OD expert at Time 1 and Time 2. To investigate these hypotheses, we created $\Upsilon _ { w g }$ scores (James et al., 1993) to assess the consistency of developers’ mental models using the procedure recommended by Espinosa (2001). The $\Gamma _ { w g }$ score measures within-group interrater agreement. We are unaware of a statistic to test differences between these scores. Higher scores (closer to 1) indicate greater agreement (or consistency) in responses to the survey. To investigate if team members are more consistent with one another or with the OD expert, we computed $r _ { w g }$ statistics for each pair of developers. We averaged each pair that included the OD expert, and then averaged each pair excluding the OD expert (the left two columns of data). Because the project manager attended only one training course, we made the same computations with and without her responses. This helped us to assess the role of training (the two right-most columns of data). The results for Time 1 data appear in Table 7. As Hypotheses 3a and 3b consider OD knowledge, we report only the values for OD concepts. However, we conducted an analysis of the consistency of developers’ mental models with regard to the SD concepts. Agreement was consistently quite high $( \mathfrak { r } _ { \mathrm { w g } } = 0 . 9 7 )$ for all types of SD knowledge on both administrations of the survey.

We anticipated that developers’ mental models would be inconsistent with the mental mode of the OD expert at the outset of the project (H3a), as training alone should not allow the developers to create mental models consistent with that of an OD expert. Overall, the developers’ mental models were relatively consistent with that of the OD expert, even at the outse of the project. In fact, the OD non-experts were less consistent between themselves than with the OD expert. However, inspection of the data confirmed that the project manger’s understanding of many concepts was inconsistent with other team members. When the project manager is excluded from the analysis (see the two right-most columns), the consistency between the pairs including the OD expert and the developers who attended the training courses is uniformly high. Given the high levels of consistency between team members’ mental models and the OD expert after training, it appears that the focused training was effective. This result also seems inconsistent with the argument that it is extremely difficult for structured developers to acquire knowledge of OD concepts.

## DISCUSSION

To better understand the challenges faced by IS developers making the transition from SD to OD, this study investigated changes in professional developers’ knowledge of SD and OD concepts over a 1-year OD project. Based on earlier studies, we anticipated that structured developers would face serious challenges in transitioning to OD because of proactive interference. Further, we expected that developers would need to ‘unlearn’ SD knowledge to gain OD knowledge. However, our results do not support earlier arguments about the difficulty, even incompatibility, of acquiring SD and OD knowledge. Instead, our results suggest that gains in OD knowledge are compatible with existing SD knowledge. Specifically, changes in understanding of functionality, an SD concept, were positively and significantly associated with four of five OD concepts. We also investigated whether individual developers’ understanding of OD concepts changed. Only two concepts reflect significant changes in understanding. It appears that developers acquired an understanding of the three other OD concepts via forma instruction, and this understanding developed little through a 1-year project. This finding calls into question the argument that OD is an extremely difficult transition for experienced structured developers.

We also assessed the consistency between developers’ mental models. Overall, developers’ mental models were quite consistent with one another and with that of an OD expert at the beginning and end of the project. This finding provides additional evidence of the benefits of formal training in development approaches (Lee & Truex, 2000), as the developers who attended formal training possessed mental models of OD concepts that were highly consistent with an OD expert. This finding highlights an important potential benefit of formal training. Consistency among team members’ mental models leads to higher levels of performance (Mathieu et al., 2000). Therefore, formal training process may contribute to project performance not only by increased levels of individual knowledge, but also through team members consistency of knowledge.

We note two limitations of this study: the small sample size and the lack of a pre-test (i.e. we were unable to assess developers’ knowledge prior to formal training). Our sample size was constrained by the number of developers assigned to the project. However, the longitudina design allowed us to assess developers’ understanding of SD and OD concepts before and after a 1-vear project. Further, all participants were experienced. professional developers whd worked exclusively on this project throughout the study. Additionally, none of the participants, except the OD expert, had practical experience with OD prior to this project. Hence, this group possessed the characteristics one would expect to be associated with proactive interference. Therefore, this project provided a relatively unique opportunity to study experienced SD developers as they made the transition to OD. Despite the small sample (eight participants), differences were detected, implying adequate power. However, we might have detected additional differences with a larger sample. Although these participants and the project provided an excellent opportunity to observe the SD to OD transition, because of the previously mentioned limitations, opportunity exists for additional studies of the SD to OD transition to yield important insights.

## CONCLUSION

Previous research suggests that SD experience can interfere with IS developers’ ability to learn OD, leading some to argue that developers must ‘unlearn’ SD concepts to ‘learn’ OD concepts (Nelson et al., 2002). Such a cognitive block between approaches may explain the difficulties in transitioning to OD (Lee & Pennington, 1994) and why OD has not spread as quickly as anticipated (Sircar et al., 2001) despite widespread interest in OD. Our longitudina study did not detect proactive interference between SD and OD knowledge. The implication for firms that wish to adopt OD is they may benefit by transitioning their structured developers.

However, the developers in this study received formal OD training at their job site and worked exclusively on this project. These actions likely enabled the transition process. It seems reasonable that a lack of formal training or forcing developers to switch between SD and OD applications during the transition period would hinder the development of OD knowledge.

Further, we found that knowledge of one SD concept increased over the life of the OD project, and this increase was significantly associated with the development of multiple OD concepts. That these categories of knowledge seem to coincide and, in fact, develop concur rently may highlight the potential usefulness of hybrid methods that integrate structured and object-oriented approaches (cf. Shoval & Kabeli, 2001).

Our findings suggest that prior research may have overemphasized the difficulties of learning OD and the issues of proactive interference while ignoring the potential for positive transfer between SD and OD. We encourage future research to examine more specifically the different types of software development knowledge. For example, one SD concept was better devel oped after the OD project, suggesting that some types of software development knowledge may be fundamental to both approaches, consistent with arguments made by Goulielmos (2004). Future researchers may wish to disentangle which types of knowledge benefit from positive transfer and which are more susceptible to proactive interference. A better understanding of how the software development concepts relate would have implications for pedagogy as well as research.

## Content Validity

Armstrong (2001) followed a four-step process designed to create an instrument with a high-level of content validity.

1) To create a representative pool of items for the SMDA, the researcher interviewed software developers (identified as experts by their peers) from 15 organizations. Transcripts of these interviews yielded 1265 statements relating to structured and object-oriented development.

2) Two researchers working independently examined the statements and identified 74 OD and SD concepts (reliability between the coders was acceptable; fisher exact two-sided $\mathsf { p } { \mathsf { - v } } { \mathsf { a l u e } } = 0 . 6 4 3 , \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 1 9 , \mathsf { d f } = 1 , \mathsf { p } = \mathsf { n s } )$ ). Three OD experts and one SD expert evaluated the coding scheme used by the researchers and agreed it was valid (reliability between the experts was acceptable; fisher exact two-sided p-value = 0.629, $\chi ^ { 2 } = 1 1 . 0 7 1$ , df = 5, p = ns).

3) The list of 74 concepts was refined by combining or deleting concepts, when appropriate. For instance, concepts mentioned by only one developer were deleted. The refinemen process yielded 41 distinct concepts.

4) To make sure the instrument contained items from representative questions drawn from a universal ‘expert identified’ pool (Straub 1989, p. 150), the researcher selected five items from the original pool of 1265 statements to represent each of the 41 concepts. Items were selected based on the content and clarity of the original statement to create an initial instrument with 195 statements.

## Construct Validity

To assess the internal construct validity of the SDMA, five IS experts (three OD and two SD) participated in a sort of the 195 statements included in the initial instrument. The experts were provided with a list of all 195 statements and a separate list of the 41 OD and SD concepts and were asked to sort statements by concepts. A high match provides evidence of the construct validity of the earlier mapping of statements to concepts conducted by the researchers. Armstrong and two research colleagues examined the results of the sort and through joint discussion decided to reword some statements and delete five statements. The remaining 190 statements were included in the pilot instrument.

## Pilot Test

To further investigate construct validity, the pilot instrument was administered to 31 software development experts. Data from the pilot test was used to assess if responses to the instrument differed depending upon developers’ understanding of OD and SD concepts. The subjects were classified into one of three groups: SD experts, OD experts, and transitioning experts (SD developers with some exposure to OD methods). Histograms for each group of subjects revealed different patterns for each group of respondents. Responses from OD experts tended toward ‘strongly agree’ on the OD questions and ‘strongly disagree’ on the SD questions. Responses from SD experts trended toward ‘strongly disagree’ to the OD questions and ‘strongly agree’ to the SD questions. Responses from the transitioning experts were scattered, consistent with a mix of OD and SD knowledge. The researcher examined the set of transitioning experts more closely by examining three subsets.

1) Subgroup 1 – (seven participants with minimal OD experience having participated in only one OD project) – The histograms for these individuals trended toward SD expert responses. 2) Subgroup 2 – (six participants with 1-3 years of OD experience and participation in one OD project) – The histograms for these individuals trended toward SD expert responses, but not as strongly as subgroup 1.

3) Subgroup 3 – (four participants with 2-3 years of OD experience and participation in two OD projects) – The histograms for these individuals trended toward OD expert responses.

These findings suggest the SMDA instrument is capable of capturing differences in subjects SD and OD knowledge.

The second purpose of the pilot test was to discuss the instrument with the subjects to determine if the statements were clear and related to the concepts they were intended to measure in a meaningful way. Based on these discussions, and in consultation with two other researchers, Armstrong eliminated 7 concepts and 15 statements.

Lastly, Armstrong (2001) assessed scale reliabilities via Cronbach’s (1951) alpha and used that analysis to reduce the instrument to a more concise length. Based on the scale reliabilities, Armstrong selected items from the initial instrument to represent each of the 34 concepts. Two concepts demonstrated scale reliabilities below .60. Five concepts demonstrated scale reliabilities between .60 and .70. Scale reliabilities for the remaining 27 concepts were above .70. Two content experts and one survey construction expert reviewed each item and recommended question wording and grammatical changes. After these changes, the final instrument consisted of 90 questions covering 34 concepts (22 object-oriented and 12 structured).

## Convergent and Divergent Validity

In the final validation step, Armstrong (2001) administered the SDMA instrument to 143 developers, including SD experts with varying levels knowledge of OD concepts as well as OD developers with no SD experience. A factor analysis of their responses revealed that the 34 concepts mapped onto seven distinct overall constructs (five OD and two SD). Each of the seven constructs possessed scale reliabilities above .80. The square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) between the constructs and their measures was also calculated and found to be .80 or greater for all constructs. Further, in all instances the AVEs were larger than between construct correlations, suggesting sufficient divergent validity.
