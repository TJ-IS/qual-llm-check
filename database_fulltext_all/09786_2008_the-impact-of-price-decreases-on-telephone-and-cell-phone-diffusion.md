# The impact of price decreases on telephone and cell phone diffusion

Kallol Bagchi <sup>a,1</sup>, Peeter Kirs <sup>a,\*</sup>, Francisco Lo´pez <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information and Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Texas at El Paso, El Paso, TX 79968-0544, United States

<sup>b</sup> Division of Economics and Business, ITEC 222, Macon State College, Macon, GA 31206, United States

Received 10 July 2004; received in revised form 12 July 2005; accepted 22 December 2007 Available online 3 April 2008

## Abstract

The diffusion process (the degree of penetration within a region) of telephones and cell phones has proceeded rapidly in most regions, but the factors influencing diffusion rates are still unknown. Using data from six regions (which varied in terms of economic wealth, stage of diffusion, technological and social infrastructures, and institutional policies), and two different technologies (telephone and cell phone) we showed that price-adjusted diffusion models had better explanatory power than pure diffusion models, validating Gurbaxani and Mendelson’s model. We found that the impact of price decreases was positive and significant in all regions and for both technologies. Additionally the study showed that the impact of price decreases was stronger for less wealthy regions and for nations with higher developed social infrastructure (as measured by educational level) and greater political stability (as measured by Mauro’s composite index) after controlling for GDP. We also found that, in general, the price effect was stronger for telephones (an older technology) than for cell phones. <sup>#</sup> 2008 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Diffusion models; Information and telecommunication technology; Price-adjusted models; Telephone and cell phone diffusion; Diffusion stages

## 1. Introduction

The diffusion (measured as the degree of penetration or number of users per 1000) of Information Communication Technology (ICT) has been rapid, but the factors influencing diffusion rates are still generally unknown. For example, cell phone growth has been spectacular in nations in general and particularly in those such as India and China, where usage increased from 3.53 and 65.82 per 1000 in 2000 to 24.75 and 214.77 per 1000 in 2003, respectively. However, worldwide, the pattern of diffusion has varied widely, recently decreasing in a number of nations, whereas cell phone penetration has been universally increasing. Land-based telephone penetration per 1000 inhabitants is illustrated in Fig. 1, and cellular phone penetration in Fig. 2; the level and rate of diffusion (the speed at which penetration occurs), varies between regions as well as technologies.

While a number studies have investigated ICT adoption and diffusion [29], relatively little attention has been given to the impact of price changes on the diffusion of ICT products [12,32] especially on a national level. For example, the cost of PCs and peripheral equipment in the United States fell 63.5% from 1997 to the end of 2000. Similarly, the cost of information processing index in the United States fell from 100 in 1988 to 28.2 at the end of 1999 [30]. Studies investigating ICT spending curves in the US showed that ICT expenditures had increased. Nonetheless, the impact of price depended on the technology. Karine et al. [17] found no significant relationship between cellular phone price and cellular subscriptions and other studies [3] questioned the impact of price. At issue, therefore, was how price decreases had encouraged growth, or, if there was a decline in growth, had reduced the rate of decline.

The purpose of our study was to extend earlier diffusion research by considering six sets of nations (the US, The OECD nations, Latin America and Caribbean (LAC)

We applied the price-adjusted logistic model (PALM) of Gurbaxani and Mendelson (G&M) [14], as opposed to a pure logistic model, to evaluate the impact of price decreases on the rate of technology diffusion for each of the technologies in each of the national groupings. We also investigated some of the factors which might moderate the impact of the price decreases on technology diffusion, namely economy, technical and social infrastructure, institutional factors, and the stage of technological maturity.

## 2. Technology diffusion

Diffusion modeling studies have tried to explain and analyze patterns of diffusion of innovations, usually over time and across a population of potential adopters, and thus forecast diffusion of the innovation. The emphasis has been on predicting the ultimate level of penetration and the rate of approach to saturation. Observations of diffusion or percentages of diffusion were put in the form of a time series and fitted to some functional form, such as the logistic, Gompertz, or mixed models [21]. Technology diffusion is generally believed to follow an s-curve pattern, where diffusion starts slowly, grows exponentially, peaks, and then declines, as shown in Fig. 3 [28].

G&M investigated the relationship between diffusion and ICT spending in the US. They found that while IS expenditure growth initially followed a typical curve, later growth converged to an exponential pattern. They subsequently hypothesized that while early growth was dominated by the development of new applications, expenditures continued to grow, despite declining computing costs, because the decreases in cost-performance technology made it costeffective for organizations to increase their outlays on ICT.

The impact of price reductions on ICT product on a national level has not been considered. Whereas costeffectiveness may be a primary influence for increased expenditures in mature nations, it seemed reasonable to assume that price decreases would vary across nations depending on the technology’s stage of maturity, etc. Investigation of the factors mitigating price impacts, and how they influence diffusion within a nation, therefore seemed warranted.

It was also assumed that the impact of price decreases would be stronger in less wealthy nations and for older technologies. As a basis of comparison, we contrasted telephone usage across various regions, assuming that price played an important role in telecommunication diffusion, but was moderated by a nation’s stage of maturity and internal factors, we examined the role of economic, institutional, and technical and social infrastructures that might affect the impact of price decrease.

## 3. The conceptual price-adjusted diffusion model of ICT

ICT diffusion, as G&M found, generally shows no sign of slowing down over the years, seemingly in contradiction to diffusion theory. It may be that pure diffusion models that overlook the effect of price cannot appropriately capture the growth process of ICT products.

Price-adjusted models, which consider the impact of price on diffusion, show that price contributes to ICT diffusion. The impact of price decrease has been assumed to be moderated by a number of other indicators, such as social and institutional factors, that play a role in price reduction as well as directly affecting the ICT diffusion process [26]. Previous research relied on a number of price-adjusted diffusion models. Chow [9] used a price-adjusted model to explain the diffusion of US computer equipment, and found that the Gompertz difference equation, modified by a moving equilibrium, could better explain the rate of growth for new commodities than could the logistic difference equation.

While there are a number of other diffusion models [11], including the price-adjusted modified exponential model, we used the logistic model and its price-adjusted version to calculate incremental growth in each technology. The PALM was selected because it is the most commonly applied model in this type of research. It allows for a dampening of the diffusion process so that the differences between early and later adopters can continue to widen [2]. It also follows a simple s-curve that corresponds to the technology acceptance life cycle.

The PALM takes the formulation:

$$
\frac {1}{f (t)} = \left(K + c \times b ^ {t}\right) \exp (- \lambda \times t)\tag{1}
$$

## 4. Factors affecting price decreases

The term ‘impact of the price decrease’ will now be referred to as l.

## 4.1. Economy

l was expected to be less noticeable in wealthier nations since they can better afford to develop ICT infrastructures early, when costs are still relatively high. In contrast, developing nations benefit from reduced prices later. As a consequence, a ‘leapfrogging’ effect [10] has occurred that allows poorer nations to realize a growth rate greater than developed nations by applying an ‘investment-based growth strategy’.

## 4.2. Technical infrastructure

Technical Infrastructure refers to a number of elements such as the size and maturity of the pool of technical talent. The existing bases of ICT products are also indicators of ICT infrastructure. l may depend, among other factors, on existing technology infrastructures: the lower the infrastructure, the greater the l.

New technology diffusion is directly related to the existence of a complimentary infrastructure. While technical infrastructure requirements vary, the degree of ICT diffusion should be positively related to it [23,27].

## 4.3. Social infrastructure

A nation’s education level is a major component of social infrastructure. The findings on the impact of education on ICT diffusion are controversial. Caselli and Coleman [8] and Lee [20], as well as others found that secondary education plays a role in ICT diffusion, although other researchers failed to find any significant influence. Nonetheless, greater numbers of educated people should result in improved purchasing power, thus lowering ICT prices.

## 4.4. Institutional factors

Institutional indicators have an impact on ICT diffusion, and government attitudes toward specific technologies have been shown to promote diffusion. Included here are such factors as political stability and bureaucratic efficiency [16].

## 4.4.1. Political stability

A nation’s form of government, as well as its people’s belief that it, and its policies, will remain stable over time impacts consumer purchase of technology. Authoritarian governments may choose policies that suppress the spread of the Internet and other technologies (as was recently demonstrated by the Myanmar government). Market pressures may be more powerful now, but domestic political institutions can still shape how these pressures are felt [22].

A nation’s political environment may also impact ICT prices; political uncertainty can create an unstable situation that deters investment and slows economic growth. In contrast, a stable political environment may shape the process of mass communication, such as message content, media personnel and technology.

It has been argued that the effect of economic change on political power is a key factor in determining whether technological advances will be blocked. Acemoglu and Robinson [1] proposed a ‘political loser hypothesis, suggesting that groups whose political powers are eroded will block technological advance. In addition, if political groups fear losing power, they will tend to block progress. Mauro’s political stability indicators included

1. Political change—institutional. ‘‘The possibility that the institutional framework will be changed within the forecast period by elections or other means’’<sup>3</sup>

2. Political stability—social. ‘‘Conduct of political activity both organized and individual, and the degree to which the orderly political process tends to disintegrate or become violent’’

3. Probability of opposition group takeover. ‘‘Likelihood that the opposition will come to power during the forecast period’’

4. Stability of labor. ‘‘Degree to which labor represents possible disruption for manufacturing and other business activity’’

5. Relationship with neighboring countries. ‘‘This includes political, economic and commercial relationships with neighbors that may affect companies doing business in the country’’

6. Terrorism. ‘‘The degree to which individuals and business are subject to acts of terrorism’

## 4.4.2. Bureaucratic efficiency

Whereas political stability refers to a government’s consistency over time, bureaucratic efficiency refers to how well a government performs at a given time. Mauro’s bureaucratic efficiency indicators included

1. Legal system—judiciary. ‘‘Efficiency and integrity of the legal environment as it affects business, particularly foreign firms’

2. Bureaucracy or red tape. The regulatory environment foreign firms must face when seeking approvals and permits. The degree to which it represents an obstacle to business’’

3. Corruption. ‘‘The degree to which business transactions involve corruption or questionable payments’

It is generally recognized that telecommunication regulation has an impact on diffusion. Regulation often involves a complex combination of price issues and entry restrictions that lead to higher prices and less service. While the purpose of deregulation is to eliminate regulations, frequently the regulations are only partiall eliminated.

Nonetheless, it is generally assumed that increased competition following deregulation is followed by price reductions. It has been suggested that the transfer of technology from industrialized to developing countries depended on trade liberalization and internationalization of production. Brock [5] mentioned that decentralized telecommunications policymaking in the US had generated rational outcomes consistent with public preference, such as reduced price and increased availability.

Corruption is a major factor that inhibits long-term economic growth. It has been suggested that it reduces economic growth by lowering incentives to invest, acting as a tax on investments, and thus lowering the quality of public infrastructure and services, decreasing tax revenues, and causing talented people to engage in rent-seeking rather than productive activities. Government corruption may also lower the size of aggregate output, leading to policies that block the adoption of superior technologies [4].

## 4.5. Stage of diffusion

Adoption factors vary by stage [7,26]. Depending on a nation’s diffusion capacity, l should increase for all technologies, over the years. However, the stage of diffusion is important and price and ongoing costs play a role in the rate of diffusion at different stages [13]. We classified product adopters along the different stages of the product life cycle as

1. Innovators (the first 2.5% of the population). In this phase, price skimming will take place because it can be used to generate enough profit to recapture R&D and those costs associated with educating the marketplace. A penetration scheme is sometimes adopted—lowering prices to stimulate trial, establish market share, increase cumulative volume, and drive down future production costs [25]. The overall price is high compared with other stages.

2. Early adopters (the next 13.5% of the population). In this stage of the life cycle, prices tend to fall as economies of scale and learning curve effects occur; also competitors use price discounts to increase their market share.

3. Early majority (the next 34% of the population).

4. Late majority (the next 34% of the population).

5. Late adopters. Price competition is intense as competitors cut prices to attract and retain customers. In this stage, price competition decreases as some competitors exit the market and the decline in total sales results in higher unit costs [19]. Prices usually are relatively low.

6. Laggards (the last 16% of the population).

## 5. Hypothesis development

The hypotheses are based on the price-adjusted diffusion model and factors affecting l. The research model is shown in Fig. 4.

There are two groups of hypotheses. The first (H1 and H2) deal with the effect of l on the rate of technology diffusion for the two technologies (telephone and cell phone) in each of the regions considered. These assume that the price will have an effect on diffusion rates (if they did not, there would be no reason to pursue any of the other hypotheses). The remaining hypotheses (H3–H7) were tested only in one region (the OECD).

Since we conjectured that l would affect telephone and cell phone diffusion in nations where price drops have occurred, the PALM should be overall significant and l should be positive and significant. Thus

## H1. l will be significant and greater than zero.

There is enough evidence that in the US (as in the OECD) the prices for telephones and cell phones have declined considerably. Fig. 5 shows the CPI index value on city average for a number of services in the US. Except for local calls, all index values show a decrease over time.

For older technologies (e.g., telephone), once pure diffusion nears saturation, l may help in maintaining a steady growth rate. For newer technologies l will be less discernible.

## H2. l will be more pronounced on older technologies.

The role of income as a major determinant of ICT technology diffusion is well known: nations with greater per capita incomes have better purchasing power and can thus afford to spend more on technologies. Therefore, a price drop in lower income nations should have more impact on diffusion than in developed nations.

## H3. l will be greater on low-income nations.

We further postulated that the more developed the ICT technical infrastructure, the greater the value of l. Diffusion of a technology should occur at a faster rate in a nation endowed with a well-developed ICT infrastructure. l should therefore be readily discernable from the introduction of a technology.

H4. l will be greater on high-technical infrastructure nations.

Because economic factors are assumed to contribute to ICT diffusion, the remaining hypotheses (H4–H7) require control of economic conditions. We postulated that the effect of social infrastructure on l would be positive, consistent with the observation that education plays a significant and positive role on ICT diffusion and a nation with more educated people would be better able to use the technology, thus reducing the price (or increasing l) of a technology.

## H5. l will be greater on high-social infrastructure nations.

Similarly, since better institutional infrastructures and political systems are generally associated with higher income, nations with higher institutional infrastructural indicator values should be associated with higher values of l; e.g., a politically stable nation may be conducive to economic growth which in turn can bring down the price of a technology faster. Better institution structure can lead to faster diffusion of a technology which will allow a higher l value from the start.

H6. l will be greater for nations with higher institutional infrastructural indicator values.

Finally, we postulated that technologies in the later stages of diffusion would have a greater l value. As a technology approaches saturation, the rate of diffusion was expected to decline. A lower price could reduce the rate of decline.

H7. l will be greater in the later stages of diffusion.

## 6. Methodolgy

In order to test H1, it was first necessary to calculate l. For each region, we calculated the PALM parameters $K , c , b ,$ and l using a non-linear regression. The same procedure was used to calculate the l values for each of the 30 OECD nations to verify H3–H7. The risk in this approach is that non-linear models may fail to converge or may sometimes produce parameter values that are not realistic and/or statistically significant.

To test H2 we needed to calculate the constant annualized percentage (CAP) growth rate from the PALM. This growth rate formulation is given by

$$
\text { Growth   rate } = \log (b) \times [ K \times f (t) - 1 ] + \lambda\tag{2}
$$

This equation has two components: growth due to the pure logistic curve (denoted by f(t)) and growth due to the price effect (l). The CAP due to price was captured by the component exp(l - t) in Eq. (1).

To investigate this hypothesis, we considered the incremental growth rate of the price-adjusted logistic curve which captures the growth rate due to price by component exp(l - t) in Eq. (1). The contribution of l is calculated as: CAP/(CAP + pure growth rate without price). Telephone and cell phone diffusion rates over the period 1981–2003 were calculated for all sets of nations.

For H3–H7 we calculated l for each OECD nation by running separate non-linear regressions. The OECD nations were chosen because they had been used in previous research (thus having some support), and were known to have reliable data over a period of years. For these hypotheses, we considered only telephone growth rates (cell phone data was lacking).

The resultant l was used in an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression as the dependent variable, with each factor assumed to influence l, considered as an independent variable. Because there were only 30 nations in the OECD, we were forced to apply a step-wise regression.

We also tested whether the PALM was superior to the standard logistic diffusion model (results are not included here but are available from the authors). The analysis showed that the $R ^ { 2 }$ values for the PALM were better than the corresponding pure diffusion models. Secondly, the l values obtained in the PALM analysis were all statistically significant. Thirdly, we employed Davidson and MacKinnon’s p-test for non-linear models [15,18]. This showed the superiority of the price-adjusted models.

## 7. Data

Our primary data source was the World Bank database [31], although additional resources were used. The time series for telephone and cell phone penetration were cumulative over time. The technology penetration rate was measured as units adopted per 1000. Sample diffusion data for each technology from each set of nations are shown in Table 2. These correspond to the data shown in Figs. 1 and 2.

Economy was represented by GDP per capita at PPP (GDP). To measure ICT Infrastructure we used the average value of telephone, cell phone, PC and Internet diffusion of the nations in the year 1995. Even though this data included telephones and cell phones, it was appropriate because it was used only to calculate the price effect, not to measure the diffusion of the technologies.

Social infrastructure was measured as the net percentage of tertiary education enrollment in a nation, available from the UNDP. Institutional indices (political stability, and bureaucratic efficiency) were taken from Mauro’s work [24]. To test the impact of different stages of diffusion on l, we classified nations according to Rogers’s criteria [26] based on telephones per 1000 residents. For example, we selected the top 2.5% of nations as innovators and the bottom 16% as laggards, etc. The classifications remained fairly robust over time. The data, descriptions, and sources are summarized in Table 3.

## 8. Findings

Our results are summarized in Table 4, which shows the results obtained from the PALM for telephones and cell phones and includes values, $R ^ { 2 }$ values, t-values and significances. It should be noted that the average values of l may not capture its contribution. For example, when the diffusion level is sufficiently low, a low value of l may have a large influence on diffusion.

While we expected l to be positive and significant (H1), the degree of significance was somewhat surprising, especially given the sample size. The non-linear approach converged with acceptable parameter values and the values of l for each group of nations converged and were positive and significant at $p < 0 . 0 0 0$ . We did, however, encounter a problem when investigating cell phone growth rates in India. The PALM would not converge, so we applied the priceadjusted exponential model of G&M which converged and yielded a statistically significant value for l. H1 was thus supported.

It is not surprising that telephone markets are highly sensitive (H2). Fig. 6a shows results on telephone diffusion. The value of l on telephone growth during the period 1981– 2003 steadily increased from 25% to 49% in the US, from 26% to 50% in the OECD nations, from 28% to 51% in the LAC nations, and from 26% to 50% in theworld. However, for China and India, the results are somewhat different. After showing a high contribution of price in diffusion in the early 1980s and 1990s (>70%), the value of l on telephone diffusion gradually decreased and was on a par with other regions in 2003. This might be due to China’s and India’s very low level of diffusion in the earlier years (1.85% and 2.36% per 1000 residents, respectively, in 1975) and thus l had a relatively greater influence on diffusion. As the diffusion level increased (in 2003 the diffusion rates were 209.03 and 46.28 per 1000, respectively), l became less significant.

## 9. Conclusions

We investigated the impact of price decreases on ICT diffusion at a national level. Our results provide solid evidence that price reductions make it possible to maintain high-sales levels, even in saturated markets and for older technologies. Predictably, price reductions had a more pronounced effect on lower income nations. However, we also found that price reductions alone do not assure rapid diffusion; countries that have well-established social and institutional infrastructures are most likely to take advantage of price reductions.

The study has important implications for managers, who should note that the diffusion of an ICT product need not follow a pure s-shaped curve. Our study found that decreases in the rate of growth may be alleviated by price reductions. However, managers should also note that all innovations and nations cannot be expected to show the same diffusion characteristics.

The study also has implications for ICT vendors. Price reductions have their greatest impact in lower income nations with higher educational levels and that are politically stable. ICT vendors that target these countries may be able to offset price reductions by increased sales.

For researchers, the study provides support for Gurbaxani and Mendelson’s integrative model of IS spending growth and their assumption that pure diffusion models that overlook the effect of price and may not be able to appropriately capture the growth process of ICT products. The PALM is well suited for explaining diffusion processes, especially for older technologies. The study further extends the model by finding that such intervening factors as income, social infrastructure, and institutional infrastructure can affect the influence of price reductions.

Unfortunately, the study had some limitations. We could not verify H3–H7 on cell phones due to missing data. When we ran non-linear regressions for l, PALM converged for only 14 nations. Similar data shortages caused us to limit the technologies considered to only telephones and cell phones.
