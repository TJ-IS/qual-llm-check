Abstract: Modern consumers increasingly embrace the personalization of services. Whether to disclose private information to companies for the sake of receiving personalized service is largely contingent to relative valuations and the utility of private information. Unfortunately, there is a lack of balanced research that analyzes and reconciles the contradiction between privacy and personalization service. In this study, based on the multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT), we introduce a utility model of privacy in personalization. Our simulation results validate our white-box utility model by demonstrating significant distinctions of calculating benefits and costs among three groups of consumers.

Keywords: Privacy Calculus; Personalization Service; Multi-Attribute Utility Theory (MAUT); Trade-off; Consumer Preferences; Fuzzy Logic.

## 1 Introduction

In e-commerce, the collection of personal data from consumers is inevitable if transactions are to be completed and delivery is to be achieved (Hui et al., 2007). On the basis of the information collected, a company can render a precise and personalized service to individual consumers (Lee et al., 2011). Consumer information may be also provided to advertisers in order that they can tailor thei marketing information for a target audience (Zhang and Zhu, 2014). A good example is Amazon’s personalized recommendations. By using the detailed records of individual consumers and corresponding analysis about webpage browsing and consumption, Amazon can offer its customers specific and tailored promotions as incentives to induce purchases. The personalized service is commonly welcomed by consumers, considering that it can offer more precise alternatives for them to compare the price, quality, relevance, and other characteristics before they make a purchase decision, thus also reducing their searching costs.

However, not every consumer enjoys (or wants to enjoy) a personalized service. Consumers face a personalization-privacy predicament or paradox (PPP) with respect to their decision to reveal private information on the basis that they may receive more personalized products and services, based on the disclosed profile information, and recognizing the risks of having such private information held by a third party (Chellappa and Sin, 2005). In fact, the promise of personalization in exchange for the disclosure of private information may drive some customers away (Awad and Krishnan, 2006). For instance, imagine a consumer who is shopping for a smartphone at an online store where she is a member. When filling out the necessary information for the transaction, she is asked for additional information such as education level and salary in exchange for a 15% discount on the product price and also a couple of \$25 coupons that she can use for her next purchase at the online shop. This kind of promotion, which involves a demand for more personal information (or being profiled online), causes the consumer to experience the PPP. Ultimately, she may decide neither to provide the requested information nor to buy the smartphone.

Consistent with the above vignette, some location-based promotion service providers, such as Groupon.com, are increasingly losing certain customer segments who dislike the feeling that their online footprints are tracked and therefore decide to cancel their accounts (Tewari et al., 2003).

A slightly different case, but also related to another concern of being profiled online, was Amazon.com’s dynamic price experiment in September 2000. A registered consumer had purchased Julie Taymor’s Titus DVD for \$24.49 on Amazon.com. Several days later, he found the price had risen to \$26.24, but when he logged out and removed the cookies, the price fell to \$22.74 (Acquisti and Varian, 2005). The consumer felt discriminated based on his online profile at Amazon and sued the company. In fact, Amazon and Groupon are only two cases in a long list of companies who have attracted consumers’ negative attention because of data collection or processing practices deemed objectionable. For instance, ChoicePoint, LexisNexis, Bank of America, and other firms were also reported in the months after the Amazon case as having placed millions of identities at risk by the loss or theft of personal data<sup>1</sup>.

Indeed, this feeling of being tracked and monitored causes some consumers to experience serious privacy concerns and an increasing sense of antipathy (Li, 2014). Furthermore, some consumers may think that the collection of such information by Internet merchants constitutes a potential threat because they may face risks

associated with information misuse.

Nevertheless, a great number of consumers rationally decide to share personal information with merchants because they expect to receive some benefit. It is commonly agreed that a consumer’s decision on whether to disclose private information and to what extent largely depends on his/her perception of the trade-offs in the PPP with regard to the potential benefits and risks (Smith et al., 2011).

The existing literature provides extensive insights into the PPP from the technology acceptance (Kim et al., 2008), legal, ethical, and functional perspectives (Li et al., 2015), involving fair information practices (Sheng et al., 2008), privacy statements (Berendt et al., 2005), and privacy seals (Kim et al., 2005). However, there is a lack of balanced research that analyzes and reconciles the contradiction between privacy and personalization service for relative valuations and utility in decision-making (Buchanan et al., 2007). Hence, following the call (Buchanan et al., 2007) for the contextual emphasis of trade-off privacy calculus research, we attempt to classify consumers on the basis of their privacy concerns and analyze their behavioral differences. In this way, we intend to contribute to the both practice and the scholarly literature as our findings may significantly influence business models and privacy strategies connected with the collection and processing of customer information.

To achieve this objective, we propose and validate a privacy calculus model grounded on the multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT) (Edwards, 1977). Specifically, we refer to the cost–benefit analysis perspective used in privacy calculus models for e-commerce transactions (Dinev and Hart 2006; Kehr et al., 2015). In their study, the perceptions of privacy risk and other benefits (such as trust) were “calculated” through a structural equation model. According to the comparison, consumers can

Consistently, comprehensive assessments of costs and benefits related to information disclosure have been conceptualized and empirically tested in a range of settings (Xu, et al., 2009; Kehr et al., 2015). Equating their calculations of risk and benefit for information disclosure (in a scale of 1–7), we formulate our privacy calculus by (1) using a scale of 0–1 for the distribution of an attribute (as shown in Figure 1); and (2) using a discrete scale when assigning the random value of benefit and cost attributes in the multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT) about privacy and personalization. ## 2 Related Works

The attention on privacy from the consumer perspective focuses on privacy concerns and trade-offs (Dinev and Hart, 2006). Furthermore, the computer science literature emphasizes the data processing and analysis, considering how to better design and test algorithms or software processes in order to protect the data when mining the private data. In parallel, economists have been paying close attention to the impact of privacy in the economy. In the 1970s, Salop and Stiglitz (1977) suggested that “complete” information leads to economic efficiency, a topic of ongoing debate among privacy researchers. For instance, consumers may rationally

##

want certain information to be shared with other parties, such as they may want their vacation and budget preferences to be known by telemarketers so that they can receive offers and deals that might actually interest them (James et al., 2015). However, privacy can also be considered as a commodity and thus restricted to the economic principles of trade-off based on cost–benefit analysis (Smith et al., 2011). Specifically, privacy benefit refers to the degree to which an individual believes that the favorable outcomes are associated with the release of his/her personal information to a company (Hann et al., 2002). Privacy risk has been defined as the degree to which an individual believes that a high potential for loss and/or profit is associated with the release of personal information to a company (Carpineto and Romano, 2015). Therefore, whether private information is disclosed ultimately depends on the relative valuation and utility of privacy and personalization.

In a broader sense concerning the costs and benefits of information disclosure, various privacy calculus models have been conceptualized and empirically examined. Bearing in mind the classical paper on privacy calculus models for e-commerce transactions (Dinev and Hart, 2006), privacy concerns and risks were calculated and contrasted with trust and personal interest in a structural equation model. The cost–benefit perspective of Dineve and Hart (2006) is consistent with more recent works on privacy calculus in different settings such as in mobile commerce (Xu, et al., 2009; Kehr et al., 2015). In this study, we equate our utility model about privacy and personalization with the cost–benefit analysis of existing privacy calculus models (e.g., Dinev and Hart, 2006; Xu, et al., 2009; Kehr et al., 2015).

More specifically for the privacy-personalization paradox (PPP), some researchers have suggested the impact of privacy concerns may be reduced or ignored in return for the advantages they enjoy from personalization, such as monetary

##

incentive (Hui et al., 2007). The researchers proposed a solution, referred to as a personalized, privacy-safe application (Sutanto et al., 2013), a dynamic model to address the PPP in the location-aware market context (Xu et al., 2011) and lastly, multidimensional developmental theory to Internet privacy concerns (Hong and Thong, 2013). Moreover, other research has explored the interaction of consumers and personalization agents for recommendations and distrust (Liu et al., 2005). In addition, Awad and Krishnan (2006) examine the paradox that consumers meet in the online setting as they draw upon utility maximization theory. Along the same line of reasoning, Lee et al. (2011) explore the motivation of firms for privacy protection and its impact on competition and social welfare in the context of product and price personalization by adopting a game-theoretic approach. However, the measures currently proposed to address information privacy issues have not yet yielded satisfactory results, especially regarding the way how to quantify measures, and less to point out the influencing attributes for the privacy calculus.

In this study, we carefully analyze related work to distill the driving benefits and cost attributes for privacy calculus. Indeed, Laufer and Wolfe (1977) were among the pioneers who raised the foundational conception of behavioral calculus, a calculus of behavior “accounting for situational constraints such as institutional norms of appropriate behavior, anticipated benefits, and unpredictable consequences, which is an important predictor of when and whether individuals would disclose personal information” (Laufer and Wolfe, 1977). Following this tradition, in this section, we review the related literature of privacy calculus in decision-making.

In this subsection, we review three classical theories of privacy calculus from the perspective of economics, as shown in Table 1. When enjoying shopping online for personalized service, consumers need to trade-off between privacy and utility (Krause and Horvitz, 2008). To solve the contradiction of protecting consumer information and personalization, Lim and Dubinsky (2004) apply the theory of expectancy-value to evaluate subjective values of the benefit and cost in decision-making. Later, Hann et al. (2007) extended the expectancy theory of motivation to calculate a motivational score involving three key factors: expectancy, instrumentality, and valence to cater for the decision of sharing information. More recently, Krause and Horvitz (2014) employed a probabilistic model to predict the risk of personal attributes and utility of sharing attributes of private data through a utility-theoretic approach.

Compared with previous studies, we are primarily interested in the domain of privacy calculus for consumers’ decision-making embracing the theory of behavioral economics, viz. MAUT (Edwards, 1977) and utilize the fuzzy logic in the calculus. MAUT has been applied to the analysis of risks and benefits associated with behavioral decision-making and has proven to be effective. We introduce and explore the economics of privacy in personalization for consumers and focus on the costs and benefits trade-offs the data having been disclosed or protected. The main reason to choose MAUT as the theoretical ground is because of its approximation to the ideal value of collation, which is a basic method for measuring recommended object utility in personalization. Compared with the above theories, MAUT supports a more sophisticated measurement of both benefits and costs relying on a comprehensive matrix. It also affords an approach using a matrix to unify attributes from various kinds of measurement dimensions. Accordingly, we detail the corresponding MAUT and fuzzy logic in Section 3 and the application of MAUT in our analysis for privacy calculus in Section 4.

## 3.2 Determine the Weighting of Attributes

The weight of an attribute in this study refers to a consumer’s relative valuation of an attribute in his/her decision-making process. This means that the weighted value reflects a consumer’s preference for an attribute. In this study, we use a simple additive weighting approach (Atmojo et al., 2014; Chou et al., 2008) to decide the weighting of attributes. We use $W = ( w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , \dots , w _ { m } ) ^ { T }$ to present the normalized vector of attributes’ relative importance weighting under the condition of

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} w _ {j} = 1\tag{5}
$$

Finally, the overall weighted utility value of alternative $U _ { i }$ can be represented by

$$
U _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} y _ {i j} \cdot w _ {j}, (i = 1, 2, \dots , n)\tag{6}
$$

## 4 Privacy Calculus for Consumers

## 4.1 The Proposed Model

For a consumer, the privacy calculus depends on relative valuations and the utility of private information, which is restricted to the economic principle of trade-off based on the cost–benefit analysis. In this study, we assume that a consumer’s valuation process can be captured in two dimensions: the levels of personalization preference and privacy concern. Thus, the utility is the combined outcome of the cost–benefit analysis based on the personalization preference and privacy concern.

In this study, the benefit attributes can be represented as a personalized product, a money reward and/or a product discount. Consumers also have different levels of sensitivity to costs including privacy concerns, collectively referred to as the cost attributes in this study. Furthermore, each company can indicate different products to express varying preferences of its customers. Then, the consumer preferences of benefits and the sensitivity to the costs are randomly distributed in the set of (0, 1), which is reflected on the weighting $w _ { j }$ , where $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } w _ { j } = 1 } \end{array}$ . The random distribution of the weighting for the preference is based on the consideration that individuals’ preference of specific personalization benefits and dislike of privacy risk can vary.

According to the survey (Westin, 2003), the public could be loosely classified on the basis of their different attitude toward privacy: the privacy unconcerned constitutes 20% of the public, whereas privacy fundamentalists constitute about 25%. The remainders, at about 55%, are privacy pragmatists. In this study, we use Westin’s (2003) report to classify consumers into three segments: (1) the privacy unconcerned, who are willing to provide their personal information to companies in order to obtain some benefits in return and refuse to consider too much about their privacy, thus concentrating only on the benefits; (2) the privacy fundamentalists, who refuse to consider consumer benefits or societal protection claims for data usage and care only about the risk of privacy rather than the personalized products and other benefits, therefore focusing merely on the cost analysis; (3) finally, the privacy pragmatist, who examines the benefits of the data collection and usage and wants to know more about the privacy risks and how organizations propose to mitigate those risks. So they care about both the privacy and the personalization, and are willing to share their information to obtain the benefits even as they consider the potential risks. To compare these three types of consumer, we label them as A (privacy unconcerned), B (privacy fundamentalist), and C (privacy pragmatist). We now identify the most common benefit factors based on the literature by covering both tangible and intangible benefits/costs. Among them, store credits (DeLaCruz, 2004), free gift or a trial product sample (Scott, 1976), discount (Edelman et al., 2011), and voucher (Pearlman et al., 2002) have long been recognized as the most common promotion methods to attract customers pertaining to the tangible benefits. In the IS discipline, the accuracy of recommendation/personalization based on analyzed data (Bansal et al., 2015) is also considered to be as, if not more, critical in obtaining and maintaining customers. This factor, which is an instance of an intangible benefit, contributes to a consumer’s overall online shopping experience (Liang et al., 2007) and thus is ultimately relevant to this study as it focuses on the PPP. As a result, we also include recommendation/personalization accuracy as one of the benefit attributes, and we quantify its value as a percentage to reflect the accuracy level of the recommendations.

Similarly, it is also important to cover both tangible and intangible aspects of costs in the privacy calculus. We include three typical cost items in this study, viz. spam emails/calls, discriminatory prices, and time used in searching for products (in minutes). Regarding intangible items, we consider the risk of personal information being traded and risk of an account being attacked (calculated in chance and measured in percentage). Respectively, $f _ { B ( 1 ) }$ refers to store credits (or membership points in another term). That means if a consumer is willing to share his/her private information, s/he can join the store membership and can obtain store credits or membership points as a reward. In this case, the membership system records the consumer data. $f _ { B ( 2 ) }$ represents a free gift or a trial product sample, a reward such as when a consumer registers as a member of a store or is qualified for a promotion. This is especially common in the cosmetics industry. $f _ { B ( 3 ) }$ is a product discount provided to a consumer who is willing to share more of his/her private information (such as preference or shopping habit etc.). $f _ { B ( 4 ) }$ refers to the monetary reward, which is shown as a voucher that can be used in the next purchase. If the amount of information that a consumer shares reaches a certain threshold, the company can offer some vouchers to him/her for shopping. Unlike the previous four types of explicit benefits, $f _ { B ( 5 ) }$ is a proxy of the accuracy level of the recommended benefit. This benefit is relative upon consumer’s perception and reflects his/her experience in selecting products.

Similarly, on the cost side, Longe and Osofisan (2011) show that 86% of the surveyed respondents received advertising emails from companies after they offered their email address during online shopping. Among them, 56% of the respondents thought that the advertising messages were issued too frequently and consequently became harassment to their lives (Wattal, et al., 2012). In this study, we use $f _ { C ( 1 ) }$ to refer to the risk of receiving spam mails and crank calls, which can accompany promoting products to those people who share their personal information. $f _ { C ( 2 ) }$ is the risk of private information being traded to unaffiliated parties after they have shared it (Dourish and Anderson, 2006). $f _ { C ( 3 ) }$ represents the risk of an account being attacked. In online transactions, although the payment information is encrypted, there is still a chance for the offender to reversely derive the original payment account. $f _ { C \left( 4 \right) }$ refers to the time consumed in searching and selecting a product (Janiszewski, 1998), i.e. the time which a consumer uses to locate a satisfactory product. Given that online platforms often provide mass product items and information, consumers’ time in searching for and locating a satisfactory product counts for a significant proportion of efforts in searching for and reaching a purchase decision (c.f. Xu et al., 2014). A more difficult and more complex searching task can require more time for product selection. $f _ { C ( 5 ) }$ is the discriminatory price (Kumar et al, 2005), meaning that the same products are charged with different prices by analyzing the user behavior (such as based on cookie data). A well-known example of the application of discriminatory prices can be found in Amazon’s dynamic price experiment (Acquisti and Varian, 2005). The price-matching theory (Srivastava and Lurie, 2001) suggests that the more information the company knows about its consumers, the greater is the probability that it will charge them according to a discriminatory price strategy. In this study, compared to the average price, we regard the five alternatives as the five discriminatory prices.

The utility of consumer alternatives can be further expressed as

$$
U _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} y _ {i j} \cdot w _ {j}, (i = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\tag{8}
$$

In addition to the above consideration of cost and benefit, the extant literature also suggests that the reputation of service providers could affect users’ risks perceptions and specific privacy concerns in PPP (Awad and Krishnan, 2006; Lee and Cranage, 2011). These variables that affect users’ specific privacy concerns may be more important than users’ general privacy concerns in determining their decisions with respect to information disclosure. Therefore, in this study, we also include the company reputation as the weight φ into the utility function $U _ { i \cdot }$ ,

$$
U _ {i} ^ {\prime} = \Phi_ {i} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} y _ {i j} \cdot w _ {j}, (i = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\tag{9}
$$

## 5 Implications, Conclusion and Future Work

## 5.1 Implications

In this study, we address the PPP by introducing the privacy calculus model in personalization, where people can opt to share personal information in calculation of the utility according to the benefit and cost attributes. We explicitly include consumer preferences and the three kinds of privacy levels via privacy calculus and then compare an optimized utility to reach the best trade-off for different consumer groups. By considering the privacy calculus from the perspective of utility, our analysis provides significant insights and implications.

Our proposed privacy calculus suggests that personalization should not merely focus on the consumers’ preference of benefits, but companies also need to zoom into consumers’ privacy concerns and also incorporate such concerns into the analysis for tailoring the personalized services/products. The extant literature has seldom put these two perspectives into one single nomological model for a balanced view. In practice, the companies often overemphasize personalization but overlook consumers’ privacy concerns. Therefore, by filling out these research and practical gaps, the current model can be used to elicit intentions about consumers’ willingness to disclose their personal data in returns in order to better define the level of personalization.

As pointed out by other scholars (Buchanan et al., 2007; Miltgen and Smith, 2015), the process and the details of cost and benefit trade-off is still not explicit and the measured dimensions of attributes cannot be unified for the utility analysis. In order to address this issue, we apply multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT) (Edwards, 1977) to measure both the attributes of benefit and cost by an approach of matrix to unify the attributes from various kinds of measurement dimension. We demonstrate the utility-privacy trade-off in an efficient manner. Therefore, the model can be used in solving issues associated with privacy trade-offs and similar problems.

From data analytics perspective, our study demonstrates the need to investigate the privacy and personalization paradox, in which MAUT can be used as an overarching framework for the investigation. Our MAUT model shows that the value of utility is sensitive to the factors of costs and benefits, the level of privacy concern, and also company reputation.

#

Specifically, consumers often make a purchase decision by implicitly evaluating multiple criteria of product alternatives. It will be useful for merchants to apply MAUT to analyze consumers’ different perceptions of privacy costs and benefits as well as estimate their utility calculation using the data available. It is also important to highlight the contribution of fuzzy logic in this study. When processing the attribute data, we fuzzify the attribute data into a scale of interval. The use of this fuzzy logic provides us an effective mechanism to compute and quantify the costs and benefits of information disclosure. The analyses by integrating MAUT and fuzzy logic to measure human recognition will help merchants to approach their customers with more accurate personalization strategies and privacy management in the return of profit.

To do so, classifying the customers into different segments can be the first step to formulate a more accurate personalization strategy and privacy management. Our study sheds light on the maximum utility of the three kinds of consumers with different levels of privacy concerns that appear in the different alternatives. As demonstrated by Table 8 and Figure 3, we can see that these three customer segments have different purchase evaluation criteria that result in distinct purchase decisions when they face the PPP. However, the data used in the current study only involved simulated and demonstration cases. With these data, the MAUT model used in our study shows the sensitivity to various factors including cost, benefit, consumers privacy concerns, and company reputations. This indicates that it is necessary to select an accurate segment of the customers based on the actual data. Echoing our example in the Introduction section on the smartphone purchase and also the case about Groupon.com, the company may turn customers away if they treat their customers blindly with one single business model with respect to a personalization and privacy strategy. This leads to our discussion of the next implication.

As shown in Figure 2, the three customer segments deserve different levels of personalization and different strategies for privacy management. Consumers who are not concerned about privacy care little about the risks involved in disclosing their personal information. For this segment, the company can maximize the possibility to obtain personal data in order to optimize the benefit of personalization based on such data. It is highly possible that this group of customers can be served with the best personalization service because their personal data and digital footprint are accessible.

The privacy pragmatist consumers always balance the risk and benefit of disclosing data. For them, the company can first work on managing these customers’ perception about how the company collects and manages their data. At the same time, it is equally important for the company to emphasize the benefits for the customers to provide personal data for better promotion and accurate recommendation.

In contrast to the above two segments, the privacy fundamentalist consumers may want to be ignored by personalization as they object strongly to a company collecting and working on their personal data. For this group of consumers, the best way for the company may be just provide standardized products without any attempt to tailor the service for them.

To summarize, the study aims to demonstrate (through simulation data as examples) the importance of considering the three customer segments with respect to privacy concerns. Considering that the different types of customers could exhibit the same preference but the same type of customers could have different preference, the use of simulation data appears a suitable method to empirically demonstrate the results of using the proposed privacy calculus framework. When consumers fall into different segments, and with different preference weighting when counting the cost and benefit of information discourse, the empirical results of this privacy calculus framework will be different. Hence, it is necessary to quantify a consumer’s preference as much as possible (on the basis of past transaction records or at the point of registration) and to classify her/him into the corresponding customer segment. Otherwise, the personalization efforts would not be effective, or may even have opposite impacts on consumer shopping experience.

The above implications also provide very detailed practical insights from the design perspective. Specifically, consumers should be allowed to choose how much sensitive information they want to share bearing in mind the requirements for personalization (such as browsing and transaction records as well as individual shopping preference with or without automatic recommendations, etc.). This indicates that the company can differentiate the three consumer segments starting from meeting a consumer during his/her registration. The subsequent website design, product pages, and consumer data collection should be also tailored according to a consumer’s sensitivity toward data privacy.

As also indicated by the comparison of Table 8 and Table 10, we find out that utility for a privacy fundamentalist can significantly change when considering company reputation. However, for those who are privacy unconcerned and pragmatists, only moderate or minimum changes were observed. Theoretically, this means that company reputation can play a significant role in making decision of information disclosure for the privacy fundamentalist. In other words, company reputation can effectively reduce the perceived risk in the information disclosure for privacy fundamentalists. From this perspective, if companies would like to obtain additional market share from privacy fundamentalist customers, practically they can consider improving their corporate image, branding, and social influence through various channels. Nevertheless, such promotion strategies may be less effective for consumers in the categories of privacy unconcerned and pragmatist.

## 5.2 Future Work

In addition to the above contributions, this study opens up several research opportunities for future work. First, we only employed simulation data to test the model. Although it is demonstrated to be an effective approach, the results can be further validated with real data. This also highlights the ultimate importance of investigating the fuzzy logic in calculating the weighting of costs and benefit, privacy concern level, as well as company reputation as the next step to further quantify the privacy and personalization paradox.

Meanwhile, for the companies, Posner (1981) proposed that the collection of consumers’ private information creates inefficiencies in the marketplace. Our result also reveals that a company that provides a product with the maximum utility may not finally gain a consumer due to different privacy concerns. Although the risk–benefit analysis of privacy calculus model provides an effective mechanism to assess the outcomes the individuals would face in return for the information and respond accordingly, quantifying the costs and benefits of information disclosure is most challenging and the privacy calculus model is still subject to the contextual nature of privacy (Smith et al., 2011). Blindly attempting to collect a consumer’s private information and striving to provide the most personalized products may result in very high costs and even have the consequence that the product is not adopted by consumers, especially privacy pragmatist consumers. Therefore, we call for the investigation of privacy calculus from the company’s perspective in future work.
