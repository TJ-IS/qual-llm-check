Abstrac t: Many countries limit the influence of foreign entertainment products, such as music, film, and television programs, to protect their domestic cultural industry.

Commonly observed policy tools include quotas, tariffs, and subsidies. However, advances in digital technology enable consumers to access digital versions of foreign entertainment programs via the Internet, a leakage channel that bypasses government protection methods. This calls for a reexamination of the effectiveness of these traditional tools. We build a unified analytical framework to study the impact of digital technology on cultural protection policies. We find that in the presence of Internet leakage, imposing a quota is the least effective protection policy to maximize the total domestic social welfare, but using either a tariff or subsidy policy is optimal, depending on the quality difference between domestic and foreign entertainment programs via the traditional channel and the Internet. Using quotas remains the least effective policy when we extend the analyses to consider the presence of piracy. In addition to the quality difference between foreign and domestic entertainment, the proportion of unethical consumers and the cost of piracy determine whether using tariffs or subsidies is the optimal policy.

Key words and ph rases: cultural protection policy, digital entertainment, quotas, subsidies, tariffs.

Many c ountries perc eive th e c onsum ption of Hollywood movies and American pop music as a threat to their national cultural economy, a perception perhaps deriving from the fact that the United States exports more than \$80 billion a year worth of entertainment products such as books, video games, and movies.<sup>1</sup> Further, in 2008, approximately 78.5 percent of movie ticket sales worldwide were for Hollywood productions. Hollywood’s 2007 total European market share was 63.2 percent, with the median market share for western EU countries at 64.43 percent [15]. The European Union accounted for 61 percent of U.S. exported audiovisual services in the same year [23].

In 1995, the European Union implemented the “Television Without Frontiers” directive to limit the influence of U.S. culture. This directive limits the amount of non-European television programming broadcast on EU member countries’ television stations. France has further imposed quotas on television stations, radio stations, and movie theaters. For television, at least 40 percent of broadcast time must be dedicated to French programming and an additional 20 percent dedicated to European programming to adhere to the EU’s “Television Without Frontiers” directive. Radio stations are required to play a mandated percentage of French-language songs in addition to a percentage of French bands. Finally, a screen quota dictates the relative screen times of foreign and domestic films. The European Union is not alone in its implementation of cultural protection policies. Countries such as Brazil, South Korea, China, and Australia have adopted similar policies to protect their own culture and domestic cultural goods.<sup>2</sup>

The recent development of digital technologies, however, has made the Internet a new venue for delivering entertainment products, which makes a government’s attempt to protect its domestic cultural goods much more challenging. For example, products such as Apple T V, Microsoft’s Xbox 360, and Sony’s Playstation3 allow consumers to download music, movies, television programs, and software using broadband Internet. Advances in compression and streaming technologies have allowed Netflix, an Internet DVD rental company, to give consumers the ability to watch movies and television programs directly on their computer via the Internet. Major U.S. networks such as ABC, CBS, Fox, and NBC post a significant portion of their newly broadcasted shows on their Web sites. For example, as of April 2009, NBC broadcasted 18 scripted, reality, or news prime-time shows, among which 13 shows are available to be viewed in full length on its Web site, including the complete season of Heroes. In addition to the networks’ Web sites, Web sites such as Hulu (www.hulu.com) and Fancast (www.fancast.com) also allow full-length episodes to be viewed. These Web sites have contracts with a number of television networks to broadcast both current and older television shows. In other words, in contrast to the traditional channel, which is more amenable to government control, the Internet has become a “leakage” channel for consumers to access and consume legitimate digital versions of entertainment goods. Henceforth, the consumption of legitimate digital versions of entertainment goods distributed via the Internet is called leakage via the Internet or Internet leakage.

Because Internet leakage will render a government’s cultural protection policies less effective, some governments have attempted to regulate access to the foreign digital content distributed over the Internet. However, governments have not been effective in applying regulations on servers located outside their country. For instance, either virtual private networks or two-way satellite services can be used to bypass government monitoring and access content located outside the country. Because governments have been unable to effectively regulate the Internet, the problem of Internet leakage will be a long-lasting one.

In addition to the newly created Internet venue for consumers to access entertainment programming, digital technology has also made it easier to pirate entertainment goods. The piracy of entertainment products has become a huge concern for both content producers and governments. A U.S.–India Business Council study [24] estimates that piracy causes the Indian entertainment industry a loss of \$4 billion each year. The Motion Picture Association of America estimated in 2004 that there was a \$512 billion loss in revenue due to intellectual property theft.<sup>3</sup> The availability of pirated goods as well as the leakage via the new Internet channel call into question the effectiveness of traditional cultural protection policies in this digital era.

In essence, “electronic commerce on the technological foundation of the Web– Internet compound has entered extensive areas of organizational and social activity” [31, p. 7], especially with the rapid development of digital technologies and the Internet. Despite abundant literature studying the impact of digital technologies and the Internet on various issues at the individual, organizational, and societal levels [8, 20, 27], little information systems (IS) research focuses on the challenges facing governments stemming from new developments of digital technologies. One notable exception is Zhu et al. [30], who analyze the European Union’s database Directive 96/9/EC, introduced in 1996 to provide legal protection for database contents and to safeguard the investment of database makers. They identify socially beneficial policy choices under various conditions. Our paper has a similar objective: to examine the effectiveness of traditional cultural protection policy tools prescribed in the EU’s “Television Without Frontiers” directive in the new Internet environment.

Many economists have investigated the performance of various trade protection policies, most notably, subsidies (financial assistance from the government to encourage the production or purchase of a good), tariffs (taxes on imported goods), and quotas (limits on the quantity of a good that can be imported). Among these policies, the most visible measure is the import quota. Quotas limit the quantity of foreign films, music, and television programs accessible via the domestic channels, which include television, radio, and theaters. Bhagwati and Srinivasan [6] discover that the optimal tools to achieve the objective of import restrictions, industry protections, and consumption restrictions are tariffs, subsidies, and taxes, respectively. Comparisons of the effectiveness of a subsidy versus that of a tariff began about five decades ago [2, 5, 12, 29]. Various articles compare tariffs with quotas [10, 13, 16] and find that uncertainty and market structure are the two critical factors that affect the equivalence of these two tools [3, 4, 21, 22]. Most of the research on the performance of trade protection policies deals with general industries, but some, such as that by Corden [12], examine it in specific industries such as clothing and wheat.

Another stream of relevant literature relates to the digital distribution of products, such as the effect of broadband penetration on DVD sales [25], the effect of digital distribution on the market structure of the digital music industry [7], and the pricing of digital content distribution [20]. In addition, attention has been paid to the impact of piracy of digital goods [17, 18, 26, 28]. Chellappa and Shivendu [9] examine the effects of trial or “lite” versions of a digital good on piracy and the conditions under which these are optimal. Chen and Png [11] examine how government policies (subsidies, taxes, and fines) can be used to enforce copyrights and to produce optimal domestic social welfare.

None of the extant research, however, considers the impact of leakage created by the advancement of digital technologies. In this paper, we provide a unified analytical framework to study the impact of digital technology on the effectiveness of the three most common cultural protection policy tools—quotas, tariffs, and subsidies. We find that the relative performance of these protection tools is determined largely by the difference in quality between domestic digital entertainment and foreign digital entertainment programs (distributed via both the traditional channel and the Internet channel). In general, we find that the presence of leakage via the Internet reduces the effectiveness of quotas, while making subsidies a more useful tool. We further find that social welfare under piracy is affected by the proportion of unethical users among consumers.

Next, we introduce our analytical model. The third section studies the effectiveness of using a quota, tariff, or subsidy in the baseline case, in which no digital entertainment from the Internet is available. Then, we examine the impact of the leakage via the Internet on the effectiveness of traditional government cultural policies. In the fifth section, we extend our model to consider the joint effect of piracy and Internet leakage. Finally, we conclude our paper.

## The Model

We f ollow th e spatial c om petition f ram ework introduced in Dixit and Stiglitz [14] and Krugman [19], which is widely adopted in IS research (e.g., [1, 30]), to study two firms—foreign and domestic entertainment programming producers—competing against each other via traditional channels under government regulation. For example, Hollywood is considered the foreign firm in this context, as its cultural goods dominate the global market, and any country other than the United States is the focus of our study with an aim to maximize its total domestic social welfare. Consumers are uniformly distributed by their preferences for product quality $\theta \in \ [ 0 , 1 ]$ with a utility function $u _ { \theta } ( s , p ) = \theta \cdot s - p .$ , where s is the quality of the entertainment program and $p$ is its price. A rational consumer is concerned with maximizing his or her own utility function. The government’s objective is to select a policy tool to maximize domestic social welfare, which includes the domestic producer’s profit and all consumer surplus.

In this model, we assume that foreign (subscript f ) entertainment programs distributed via traditional channels have a higher quality than domestic (subscript d) entertainment programs—that is, $s _ { d } < s _ { \bar { f } }$ —because otherwise there is no need for government protection of domestic products. We later extend the analyses to the case in which consumers have access to the foreign program via the Internet, a new distribution channel not controlled by the domestic government. The foreign entertainment programs distributed on the Internet (subscript i ) have a lower quality than those distributed via traditional channels but higher quality than that of domestic entertainment—that is, $s _ { d } < s _ { i } < s _ { f } .$ The quality of entertainment and cultural goods is a complex construct that has at least three dimensions: production quality, determined by the level of budget, which affects access to well-known actors and the latest production technology; product quality in terms of the postproduction finishing of the goods consumers obtain; and artistic quality, represented by the originality and creativity of the goods.<sup>4</sup> A domestic firm may create an entertainment program with original artistic quality. However, the same program is later turned into a product with higher production and product quality by the Hollywood studio with a much larger budget. For example, True Lies, a movie reshot in Hollywood, outperformed its original French version, La Totale, making \$146,563,703 in the United States and \$219 million abroad. Both totals dwarf the box office earned by La Totale. In this paper, the term quality refers to the first two dimensions of the quality construct, and we focus on the economic aspect of cultural production and international trade.

We consider a two-stage game. In stage one, the government announces which cultural protection tool is to be implemented. In stage two, the two producers decide on optimal prices for their products based on the government’s implemented policy. Then consumers make their purchase decision based on the prices and quality of the products to maximize their utility. The government’s objective is to maximize overall domestic social welfare given the optimal prices selected by the producers. As consumers subscribe to broadband services mainly for Internet-related activities, the marginal cost to consumers of using the Internet for entertainment is thus assumed to be negligible. ## Baseline: Quota $\bar { q }$ with No Leakage

In this case, a quota $\bar { q }$ is strictly enforced on the traditional channels through which foreign entertainment is distributed. Let $\theta _ { d }$ represent the marginal consumer who is indifferent between consuming domestic entertainment and not consuming at all and $\mathsf { \Pi } _ { \theta _ { f } } ^ { }$ represent the marginal consumer who is indifferent between consuming domestic and foreign entertainment. Then, one has $\theta _ { d } s _ { d } - p _ { d } = 0$ and $\bar { q } s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle d } } - p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle d } } = \bar { q } s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle f } } - p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle f } }$ . We only consider the case in which $\bar { q } > \theta _ { { \dot { f } } } ;$ otherwise, the quota has no effect. As illustrated in Figure 1, the demand for foreign entertainment is $Q _ { f } = 1 - \bar { q } .$ , and the demand for domestic entertainment is $Q _ { d } = \bar { q } - \mathsf { \theta } _ { d } = \bar { q } - ( p _ { d } / s _ { d } )$

$$

The government determines the optimal quota to maximize domestic social welfare by solving

$$
\max _ {\overline {{q}}} \left[ \pi_ {d} ^ {*} + \int_ {\theta_ {d}} ^ {\overline {{q}}} \left(\theta s _ {d} - p _ {d} ^ {*}\right) d \theta + \int_ {\overline {{q}}} ^ {1} \left(\theta s _ {f} - p _ {f} ^ {*}\right) d \theta \right],
$$

which leads to the following theorem:

Theorem 1: When there is no leakage and quota $\bar { q }$ is imposed on foreign entertainment, the government’s socially optimal quota should be set at the free trade equilibrium value $\bar { q } ^ { * } = ( 2 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } ) / ( 4 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } ) .$

Note that even though the optimal quota on foreign entertainment is set at the free trade level, the prices for both domestic and foreign entertainment are higher than those in the free trade case, which reduces consumer welfare. This is consistent with traditional international trade theory. Intuitively, when a quota is in effect, the domestic firm is under less pressure to compete with a foreign firm because the foreign firm cannot exceed the prescribed quota, resulting in a higher price for domestic goods. This gives the foreign firm an incentive to increase its price as well, because a lower price will not increase demand, due to the quota.

Based on the optimal quota set by the government, the corresponding optimal domestic social welfare is

$$
S W ^ {*} \left(\overline {{q}} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {4 s _ {f} ^ {2} + 8 s _ {d} s _ {f} - 3 s _ {d} ^ {2}}{8 \left(4 s _ {f} - s _ {d}\right)}.
$$

## Comparison of Baseline Cases

In this section, we compare the performance of the three cultural protection tools to determine which tool generates the highest optimal domestic social welfare:

Theorem 4: When there is no leakage, the use of a tariff produces higher optimal domestic social welfare than a subsidy and the use of a subsidy will always produce greater optimal domestic social welfare than a quota—that is, $S W ^ { * } ( \tau ^ { * } ) >$ $S W ^ { * } ( \sigma ^ { * } ) > S W ^ { * } ( \bar { q } ^ { * } )$

Theorem 4 shows that there is a clear ranking among different cultural protection tools in the case of no leakage. A tariff produces the best domestic social welfare because of the increase in government revenue. A subsidy is also effective because it not only increases the profit of the domestic firm but also expands the consumption of domestic entertainment. However, a quota does not perform as well as the other two tools because it not only limits the consumption of foreign goods but also increases the prices of both foreign and domestic entertainment, which reduces social welfare.

It shows that the performance of the three different cultural protection tools is driven mainly by the quality difference between foreign and domestic entertainment. Optimal tariffs always produce the highest social welfare among the cultural protection tools. The performance of an optimal subsidy can be very close to that of an optimal tariff when the quality difference between the domestic and foreign firm is neither too small nor too big (in the 40–60 percent range). Imposing a quota, however, is always inferior to implementing the other two policies.

## Conclusion

Many governm ents perc eive th e dom inanc e of foreign entertainment products as a threat to their domestic cultural industry and combat this threat with traditional cultural protection tools, including quotas, tariffs, and subsidies. However, the recent advancement of digital technologies has made entertainment goods such as music, movies, and television programs readily available in domestic markets via the Internet. The ability of the foreign firm to deliver digital entertainment over the new Internet venue has created a leakage channel that challenges the effectiveness of these traditional cultural protection policy tools.

This paper analyzes the impact of leakage via the Internet on the effectiveness of quota, tariff, and subsidy policies in both the absence and presence of piracy of foreign entertainment products from the perspective of a domestic government attempting to maximize domestic social welfare. Our research provides useful findings and recommendations for those countries at a competitive disadvantage in competing with foreign cultural goods.

We find that the effectiveness of cultural protection tools is determined largely by the difference in quality between domestic and foreign entertainment (via traditional and Internet channels). When there is no leakage via the Internet, a tariff produces the greatest optimal domestic social welfare due to its ability to tax the consumption of foreign entertainment and generate government revenue. In the presence of leakage over the Internet, social welfare is higher when the difference in quality between foreign entertainment via the Internet and domestic entertainment decreases. Either a tariff or subsidy policy produces optimal domestic social welfare depending on the quality of the foreign entertainment products. As in the leakage case, quality competition from piracy and Internet leakage produces higher domestic social welfare, and either tariffs or subsidies produce the highest optimal domestic social welfare. The domestic social welfare under piracy is also affected by the proportion of unethical users among consumers. The more unethical users there are, the higher the domestic social welfare due to increased consumer surplus from the increased “free” access to foreign entertainment. Despite the popularity of the quota adopted as a cultural protection policy tool in reality, perhaps due to its low cost and ease of implementation, our research suggests that the quota policy is consistently the least effective for attaining higher domestic social welfare in all the cases we consider, especially in the presence of Internet leakage and piracy.

There are several extensions to this paper for future research.<sup>7</sup> In our stylized model, the domestic firm is competing against a foreign firm. One possible extension is to have many foreign firms competing against one another for access to the domestic market, resulting in de facto perfect competition among foreign providers. Our model can easily be adapted to examine this situation, which is a special case of our model because foreign firms are then price takers under perfect competition, as opposed to price makers in our model. The price of foreign entertainment in the perfect competition case, $p _ { f }$ , becomes an exogenous parameter rather than a decision variable to be optimized by the foreign firm. The solution procedure remains essentially the same.

In this paper, we do not differentiate between the piracy of foreign entertainment goods via the traditional channel and digital piracy. Future research can introduce this difference, and we believe that this addition will not change our underlying finding that quota is the least effective policy. In addition, we treat the quality levels of both foreign and domestic entertainment programs as exogenously determined. It will be interesting to endogenize them and explore the effect of leakage via the Internet on quality competition. Finally, while governments have not yet systematically extended their protection of cultural goods to the Internet, it may well be an option pursued by them in the future, an issue to be addressed in future research should it become a reality.

## Proof of Lemma 1

Let $\boldsymbol { \theta } _ { \boldsymbol { f } }$ be the marginal consumer who is indifferent between foreign and domestic entertainment, and $\theta _ { d }$ be the marginal consumer indifferent between domestic entertainment and doing without. When $\bar { q } < ( 2 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } ) / 4 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } )$ , where $( 2 s _ { _ { f } } - s _ { _ { d } } ) / ( 4 s _ { _ { f } } - s _ { _ { d } } )$ is the optimal $\theta _ { b }$ under free trade, the quota has no effect on competition. The optimal prices follow from the standard free trade results of two firms under vertical quality differentiation.

When $\bar { q } > ( 2 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } ) / ( 4 s _ { { } _ { f } } - s _ { { } _ { d } } )$ , the demand for the foreign firm is restricted by $1 - { \bar { q } } .$ Because its profit function is concave, it will serve all the demand $1 - { \bar { q } } .$ Thus, at the point ${ \bar { q } } ,$ Equation (1) holds that $p _ { _ f } { = } \bar { q } ( s _ { _ f } { - } s _ { _ d } ) + p _ { _ d } { . }$ The domestic firm solves the profit-maximizing function

$$
\max _ {p _ {d}} \pi_ {d} = p _ {d} Q _ {d} = p _ {d} \left(\overline {{q}} - \frac {p _ {d}}{s _ {d}}\right),
$$

which gives the best response function ${ p } _ { d } ^ { * } = \bar { q } s _ { d } / 2$ . The optimal $\boldsymbol { p } _ { f } ^ { * }$ results from combining this best-response function with Equation (1). Q.E.D.

## Proof of Theorem 1

The government decides the optimal quota to maximize domestic social welfare described as follows:

$$
\max _ {\overline {{q}}} \pi_ {d} ^ {*} + \int_ {\theta_ {d}} ^ {\overline {{q}}} \left(\theta s _ {d} - p _ {d} ^ {*}\right) d \theta + \int_ {\overline {{q}}} ^ {1} \left(\theta s _ {f} - p _ {f} ^ {*}\right) d \theta ,
$$

where

$$
\pi_ {d} ^ {*} + \int_ {\theta_ {d} ^ {*}} ^ {\overline {{q}}} \left(\theta s _ {d} - p _ {d} ^ {*}\right) d \theta = \frac {s _ {d}}{2} \left(\overline {{q}} ^ {2} - \frac {\overline {{q}} ^ {2}}{4}\right) = \frac {3 s _ {d}}{8} \overline {{q}} ^ {2}
$$

and

$$
\int_ {\overline {{q}}} ^ {1} \Bigl (\theta s _ {f} - p _ {f} ^ {*} \Bigr) d \theta = \frac {s _ {b}}{2} + \frac {(s _ {b} - s _ {a})}{2} \overline {{q}} ^ {2} - \frac {(2 s _ {b} - s _ {a})}{2} \overline {{q}}.
$$

It can be easily confirmed that the second-order condition is positive when $s _ { f } > s _ { d }$ Thus, the profit function is convex in ${ \bar { q } } ,$ and the corner solution is obtained. Comparing the two corner values $( 2 s _ { _ { f } } - s _ { _ { d } } ) / ( 4 s _ { _ { f } } - s _ { _ { d } } )$ and 1 with $\bar { q } _ { \mathrm { m i n } } = ( 4 s _ { f } - 2 s _ { d } ) / ( 4 s _ { f } - s _ { d } )$ , the profit-minimizing $\bar { q }$ value, 1, is closer to $\bar { q } _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ .
