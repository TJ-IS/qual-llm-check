# Trust dynamics in a large system implementation: six theoretical propositions

Bjarne Rerup Schlichter<sup>1</sup> and Jeremy Rose<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Administration, Aarhus University, Denmark; <sup>2</sup>Department of Computing Science, Aalborg University, Denmark

## Abstract

A large information systems implementation (such as Enterprise Resource Planning systems) relies on the trust of its stakeholders to succeed. Such projects impact diverse groups of stakeholders, each with their legitimate interests and expectations. Levels of stakeholder trust can be expected to vary in large-scale implementations (which can take several years), and cannot be taken for granted. Previous studies have largely focused on the taxonomic deconstruction of the trust construct, through point-in-time variance studies. They have identified the relationship between trust and project outcomes, but largely ignored the dynamics of trust relations. Giddens, as part of his study of modernity, theorises trust dynamics in relation to abstract social systems, though without focusing on information systems. We use Giddens’ concepts to investigate evolving trust relationships in a longitudinal case analysis of a large Integrated Hospital System implementation for the Faroe Islands. Trust relationships suffered a serious breakdown, but the project was able to recover and meet its goals. We develop six theoretical propositions theorising the relationship between trust and project outcomes, the maintenance, breakdown and restoration of trust, the role of the information system in restoring trust, and the emergent and reciprocal nature of trust and project outcomes. ## Introduction

Implementation projects for large information systems such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems and Integrated Hospital Information Systems (IHIS) are known to be problematic (Berg, 2001; Heeks, 2006; Chowdhury et al, 2007). One factor identified as important for the success of these projects is trust (Gefen, 2004; Nah & Delgado, 2006). In a largescale implementation of an IHIS on the Faroe Islands, trust was identified empirically as a significant factor contributing to the outcomes of the project. The levels of trust between the different stakeholders and stakeholder groups could be seen to vary considerably over the 5-year period of study (Schlichter, 2010). The causes of these variations were complex, but it became clear that lack of trust in the project had serious consequences: social relationships, commitment and cooperation suffered, project members became stressed and uncomfortable, employees resigned and their expertise was lost. At other times trust improved and the project moved forward more smoothly. Trust in the project could therefore be understood as dynamic – a series of causally related conditions, events and consequences evolving over time. However, neither researchers nor project managers had satisfactory explanations for these observations.

The information systems literature contains many examples of point-in-time trust studies, which are helpful in understanding the nature of trust and its relation to other factors such as e-Commerce success, but fewer longitudinal process studies, and hardly any examples of the kinds of emergent process theories (Markus & Robey, 1988) that could help to explain the observed phenomena.

Giddens’ theories have played an important role in the development of the IS field (Rose et al, 2005; Jones & Karsten, 2008). Important contributions include the duality of technology model (Orlikowski, 1992) and adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Structuration theory provides a ‘dynamic conceptualization of structure as being continuously produced and reproduced through situated practice which facilitates the study of change’ (Orlikowski, 2000; Jones & Karsten, 2008, p. 128) – in other words, an emergent process theory. Trust is not extensively theorised in structuration theory, but forms an integral part of Giddens’ later work on modernity (Giddens, 1990); this work is the theoretical foundation for our research. We adapt Giddens’ definition of trust (below ‘Giddens: relational trust constructs for abstract systems’) to our context of inquiry; trust is: confidence in the reliability of the implementation project regarding project events and outcomes, where that confidence expresses a faith in the probity and professionalism of the project staff and managers, the quality and appropriateness of the information system solution, and the project’s ability to achieve valued results for its stakeholders. The objective of the study is to explain the dynamics of trust in largescale system implementations. In doing this, we will also seek to understand the role of trust in the outcomes of an implementation project, the mechanisms behind the breakdown of trust, and how trust is recovered in situations where it is lost or threatened. We follow the breakdown and recovery of trust relationships through a longitudinal interpretative case study in the Faroe Islands – studying conditions, critical events and consequences through analysis of the trust perceptions of the principal actors. The Faroe Islands’ IHIS is now largely operational; however, the project went through various crises that extended its lifetime by several years, and its budget had to be revised in the Faroese parliament.

The paper is organised as follows. We present the study’s conceptual foundations followed by the research approach. The next section relates the breakdown and recovery of trust in the IHIS implementation on the Faeroe Islands. Analysis of the trust relations leads to the development of six theoretical propositions, and the final section concludes with implications for future research and practice.

## Trust constructs for large system implementations

Trust is an established field of interest for IS researchers. In e-Commerce. customer's trust in the vendor is understood to be an important factor in the decision to buy (McKnight et al, 2002; Lim et al, 2006; Vance et al, 2008; Kim et al, 2009). Trust is influential in the collaboration relationship in virtual organisations, teams and communities, and in the success of inter organisational systems (Scott & Kaindl, 2000; Gallivan, 2001; Jarvenpaa et al, 2004; Ibrahim & Ribbers, 2009). Several factors are important in building reliable trust constructs and these are considered next. Trust construct specify who the truster is and who, or what, is the object of trust. Trust can be interpersonal, for which a psychological theory is appropriate. Thus, for Gefen (2004), trust is ‘the belief that others upon whom one depends, ye has little control over, will not take advantage of the situation by behaving in an opportunistic manner but, rather, will fulfil their expected commitments by beha ving ethically, dependably and fairly, especially under conditions involving risk and potential loss’. However, trust can also occur between groups, communities and organisations, where social theories are more appropriate. In economic theory, an abstract system – a market – can also both be the object of trust, and display trust. Further more, an information system, for instance a recommen der system or an e-Commerce portal can be the objec of trust (Komiak & Benbasat, 2008), and system design can promote trust in IT artefacts (Leimeister et al, 2005). Trust is often conceptualised as a condition or state that exists between individuals or groups. Thus, trust is ‘important for ERP customisation clients in determining their assessment of the relationship with the vendor, because the customisation of such complex software typically entails vulnerability and dependence on the vendor’ (Gefen, 2004, p. 266). Vendor–client trust, or its absence. is here a condition that can be observed. The trust condition can be treated as a binary switch (present or not present), or a continuum (more trust; less trust); in the latter case it will be necessary to understand whether mistrust and distrust are separate constructs. oi are conceptualised as negative trust – part of the conti: nuum. Trust constructs normally imply outcomes: a causal relationship between the trust condition and some (usually desirable) consequence. Thus. trust in implemen: tation projects is shown to have positive consequences. It has an influence on co-operation and commitment between actors (Rajiv, 1999; Salam et al, 2001), reduces complexity (Lander et al, 2004) and is crucial to establish ing positive results during implementation (Scott & Kaindl, 2000; Somers & Nelson, 2001; Lander et al, 2004; Wang & Chen, 2006). Successful implementa tion of ERP systems ‘requires a corporate culture that emphasises the value of sharing common goals over individual pursuits and the value of trust between partners, employees, managers and corporations’ (Somers & Nelson, 2001). Trust as a state with outcomes implies a static construct model, but trust relations can also be conceptualised as dynamic, evolving over time, for which process theories are suitable. Thus, trust, an important component of implementation success. can be created. Various mechanisms for establishing trust are considered, including initial interactions, integrity, predictability, communication, sharing control, concern for others, joint identification, commitment, potential for success, and managerial decisions (Lander et al, 2004). Trust can be built up with intensive communication, coaching, delegation of responsibility, personal care and attention (Jarrar et al, 2000). Building trust quickly in temporary project situations is considered important (Lander et al, 2004), and customisation companies need to behave responsively and to act in accordance with their clients’ expectations (Gefen, 2002). The dynamics of trust relationships in implementation projects are little researched, but Sabherwal (1999) provides a dynamic (but un-theorised) account of vicious and virtuous trust cycles in development projects. The consequences of lack of trust can be severe; as problems and delays mount, trust relations become strained – leading to a circle of suspicion and disbelief that is both destructive and hard to break out of (Gefen, 2004; Nah & Delgado, 2006).

We studied the 22 articles (see Appendix A) in elite IS journals (the so-called basket of six) in which trust is explicitly theorised, in order to understand how this literature typically builds and uses trust constructs. They are most often built as a taxonomic scheme – trust is a multi-dimensional construct with different facets or categories. Trust may be institution-based, cognitivebased (Lim et al, 2006); knowledge-based, characteristicbased, institutional-based, justice-based, swift (Gallivan, 2001); cognitive or affective (Lowry et al, 2010); mutual, cognitive or swift (Scott & Kaindl, 2000); calculative, predictive, capability-based, intentionality-based, trans ference-based (Leimeister et al, 2005). This style of construct building is echoed in the implementation literature: trust is deterrence-based, knowledge-based or identification-based (Lander et al 2004); process-based, characteristic-based or institution-based (Gefen 2004). An extended example of taxonomic theorising is developed by McKnight et al (2002), where trust is decomposed into 29 factors related in four levels structured by the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). In these literatures a series of descriptive trust constructs are theorised, leading to a rather extensive deconstruction of trust as a phenomenon in IS, though there is little agreement or progressive construct development between contributors (the McKnight example excepted). Trust is also (much less commonly) understood relationally – juxtaposed with other factors or concepts in a nomological network. Trust is related to social capital (Dinev et al, 2006) or culture (Lowry et al, 2010); trust is in opposition to distrust (Komiak & Benbasat, 2008), control (Gallivan & Depledge, 2003) or opportunism (Kumar et al, 1998). As Komiak & Benbasat (2008) point out, the majority of studies depend on variance theories, typically factor models with dependent and independent variables displaying an ‘invariant [static] relationship between cause and effect’ (Markus & Robey, 1988, p. 591). IS trust studies, in summary, rely heavily on a taxonomic understanding of trust that can easily be converted to a factor model and investigated through survey or student experiment.

This style of research is, however, less well-suited to studying trust dynamics in large implementation projects. Trust between various groups of stakeholders and in the information system solution being implemented can be expected to vary over time, and to be both the outcome of, and a contributor to project success. Social theories, particularly emergent process theories, investigated through longitudinal case studies will be more suitable candidates: they often use relational rather than taxonomic trust constructs.

## Giddens: relational trust constructs for abstract systems

An implementation project is an assemblage of roles, skills, events and procedures existing over time, indepen dent of particular actors. The incrementally implemented information system (IHIS) in our case is also an integra part of the project. These factors make it relevant to speak of ‘trust in the project’, and we proceed by conceptualising an implementation project as an abstract system. Giddens’ account of the role of trust in sustaining abstract systems is suitable for theorising emergent process (Schlichter & Rose, 2009). Trust is not decomposed taxonomically, but rather placed in a relationship with other concepts, producing outcomes that are not predictive or determinant, but dynamic – contingent and emergent. Such a related system of interdependent concepts can be understood as a nomological network (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Giddens’ theories of moder nity represent an extension of his earlier work on structuration and many of the underlying concepts and the central idea of how social practices reproduce themselves are carried forward. Trust is a central element in modernity, according to Giddens. He defines it as ‘confidence in the reliability of a person or [social] system, regarding a given set of outcomes or events, where that confidence expresses a faith in the probity or love of another, or in the correctness of abstract principles or “technical knowledge” ’ (Giddens, 1990, p. 33). Trust exists in the context of socially created transfor mative human activity. Human activity creates intended and unintended consequences (contingencies) (Rose & Jones, 2005) and thus involves risk and danger, to which trust is a response mechanism. Trust is related to absence in time and space – the ability to have confidence even though the trusted person or social system is out of direct contact – which is also a fundamental precondition for the existence of social systems. Giddens refers to thi throughout his work as time–space distanciation. Trust involves the attribution of probity to a person or socia system to act in a reliable way in relation to contingent outcomes and situations with incomplete knowledge; therefore, the breakdown of trust is also a personal failure of attribution. Trust is thus implicated in social actors ontological security – the concept that Giddens uses to represent an actor’s confidence in their social identity, and in their situation and how to proceed with it. Giddens distinguishes between trust in persons and trust in abstract systems. Abstract systems, such as legal and banking systems, are combinations of technical means, procedures, professional expertise and other structures. Trust in abstract systems enables dynamism in modern societies, by allowing social actors to act with confidence in the absence of personal knowledge of, or contact with the structures, actors and actions embodied in the (abstract) system. Abstract systems are thus dis-embedding mechanisms – enabling time-space distanciation (the durability of social systems across time and space) and providing security and guarantees to their users. An abstract system is thus a means to stabilise relations across time and space – it is ‘something to trust in’ (Walsham, 1998).

Dis-embedding is the process of lifting social relations away from a local interaction context and reconstructing them across intervals of time and distance. Dis-embedding is dependent upon trust – the actors involved must believe that the social relation will endure and can be re-captured at a later time and different place. In this respect, mutual trust can be understood as a form of social capital that can facilitate an abstract system, facilitating dynamism by temporarily reducing or removing the need for constant interpersonal social contact. A dis-embedded social relation can be reembedded – that is, it can again become localised, personal and immediate, however, temporarily. Re-embedding is important for the maintenance and re-establishment of the trust relationship between lay person (those outside the abstract system who interact with it) and expert system. Places where lay actors meet and interact with the expert system are termed access points by Giddens. Access points are described in terms of two forms of commitment – facework commitment and faceless commitment. Facework refers to interactions in co-presence. Faceless commitment describes non-personal forms of interaction at access points, such as an interaction with a computer system representing the abstract system. Re-embedding is made necessary by our modern habit of chronic reflexion, where reflective evaluation of our situation, our actions and their consequences is a constant feature of our social practice. This implies that trust in persons or abstract systems can never be absolute or constant over time, but must be periodically re-confirmed.

Trust in abstract systems characterises modern societies (according to Giddens), producing dynamism in society by allowing actors to proceed in situations of uncertainty – freeing resources and enabling social interactions across time and space. The absence of such trust forces social actors to take many actions to reduce risk and uncertainty, to control situations through face-to-face interactions and confidence-building measures, and to set in place procedures and regulations to govern social interactions.

In summary, the principle analytical constructs used in this article are:

\- Trust – in persons and in abstract systems.

\- Time–space distanciation – the ability of a social system to function over time and space without the physical co-presence of its social actors, sustained by trust.

\- Abstract system – dis-embedded social system trusted despite lack of detailed understanding or personal trust relations.

\- Dis-embedding, re-embedding – processes where an abstract system is removed from immediate close contact, and temporarily made personal again.

\- Access point – a point where a lay person makes contact with the abstract system.

\- Chronic reflexion – constant evaluation of our social situation and actions (including the trustworthiness of people and abstract systems).

\- Ontological security – confidence in the robustness and sustainability of self-identity and belief in the continuity of social practice, sustained by trust in people and abstract systems.

A large implementation project can be understood as an abstract system. The project brings together experts with their own knowledge structures, tools and technologies (project management, development and implementation methodology, change management, hardware and programming competencies), thus a combination of technical means, procedures, professional expertise and other structures. These are shared (at least to some extent) in the project team but generally poorly understood by other stakeholders, who, of course, have their own specialised competences (doctors, nurses, administrators, secretaries, managers, accountants, politicians). The project must function over several years and across implementation sites (time–space distanciation), and it cannot negotiate every discrete project event face-to-face with every stakeholder (instead there is a project charter setting out expectations and procedures) – it is therefore dis-embedded to some degree. Stakeholders meet the project through its access points, meetings, configuration tasks, training sessions, documents and websites and importantly, through experiences with the new information system itself as it is incrementally rolled out and tailored (though Giddens does not theorise the information system artefact). Where they meet project staff face-to-face the project is temporarily re-embedded (located in personal relations). They cannot avoid reflecting on these experiences (chronic reflection) and their interpretations affect their both their confidence in the project, and their sense of well-being in their work lives in relation to it (ontological security). Trust is the project is accordingly confidence in the reliability of project events and outcomes, where that confidence expresses a faith in the probity and professionalism of the project staff and managers, the quality and appropriateness of the information system solution, and the project’s ability to achieve valued results for its stakeholders.

## Research methodology

Trust perceptions are subjective phenomena, dependent on historical and social contexts, for which qualitative and interpretive studies are well suited (Walsham, 1993). The study is conducted according to principles for interpretive research established by Klein & Myers (1999). The chosen project can be understood as a ‘unique or revelatory’ case (Yin, 1994) in that it involves the radical automation of an entire community’s health service. Longitudinal data collection in the field at multiple interview points facilitates the study of process and change: evolving patterns of action and consequence (Pettigrew, 1990). The research methods chosen (longitudinal interpretive case study) are thus consistent with the research objective. One of the authors was consultant to the Faroese Healthcare Minister on IS strategy from 1998 to 2004, and thus familiar with the project context. This researcher was a non-participant observer (did not participate in discussions nor decisions) of the system implementation (2005 and onwards), attending project meetings and other significant events. We adopted a multiple source data collection strategy consisting of participant observation, individual semi-structured interviews and document study. The interview protocol (Appendix C) was designed to monitor the progress of the project, and actor expectations and perceptions of outcomes, without imposing any particular theoretical framework that might influence the data collection – a grounded approach. Interviewees were selected to represent project stakeholders at different levels of the organisation; however, some left the turbulent project and suitable new interviewees were found to replace them. A total of 18 actors were interviewed. Interviews took place at six interview points between late 2005 and early 2009 (not all interviewees were available each time) – a total of 50. Three interviews could not be transcribed because of equipment failure (see Appendix B) and do not figure in the analysis.

The empirical analysis was conducted using content analysis, supplemented by critical incident analysis (Flanagan, 1954). The theory base (Giddens) for the analysis is identified retrospectively on the basis of best fit to the emerging empirical themes and issues. Content analysis was used to develop an understanding of the empirical materials through the theoretical lens provided by Giddens, and critical incident analysis to focus the study on the process (dynamic) issues. Table 1 relates the design of the analysis to Krippendorff’s stages of content analysis.

The transcribed interview texts were coded independently by the authors (using HyperResearch software) to a coding table (Appendix D) based on Giddens’ theoretical constructs. Open coding was also allowed to capture important elements of trust situations not covered by the coding table. Positive and negative statements of confidence in the project were coded as an indicator of trust levels. The coding was piloted, discussed with an external reviewer and a cross-section of the interviews was coded by both authors to ensure consistency. This process led to the identification of more than 800 empirical observations of trust-establishing, trust-maintaining and trust-disrupting events, with the perceptions and causal attributions of the protagonists. In the critical incident analysis, an exhaustive search of empirical observations was made to identify specific references to the incident, and to uncover the pattern of actions and consequences leading to trust breakdown and recovery. These trust aspects were then cross-referenced with the original coding.

Theory building is through development of the nomological network (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955); (see Figure 1). The purpose of a nomological network in this context is to make the nature of a theoretical construct clear through a series of related constructs, concepts or propositions, so that the relations governing the construct can be articulated. A nomological network may relate

\- observable properties or quantities to each other;

\- different theoretical constructs to each other;

\- theoretical constructs to observables.

Learning more about a theoretical construct (in this case trust in implementation projects) involves elaborating the nomological network in which it occurs, or increasing the precision of the network’s components. New constructs or relations are either directly observable, or reduce the number of nomologicals required to reliably interpret observable phenomena. Giddens’ concepts and propositions are used as the theoretical starting point and adapted for the implementation case.

The theory building strategy is grounded, derived from Corbin & Strauss (2008). Causal relationships between observable trust phenomena in the case are understood as conditions and consequences in sequences of evolving interactions – in particular, we study which kinds of events and circumstances in the implementation have positive or negative consequences for stakeholder trust. Statements about future expectations for the project are coded as positive or negative to give a rough measure of trust. Where an expression of confidence (or lack of confidence) is given. the reasons (conditions) are also analysed from the surrounding text (e.g., they got a chance to try the system out themselves [condition]yyyit was a real breakthrough [trust consequence] [#29, CIO]). The many detailed observations of dynamic trust relations elaborated in the descriptive analysis are scaled up (generalised) to form tables of trust conditions and consequences expressing repeated patterns in the observations (Tables 2 and 3). These remain, however, local to the project situation. In the next step, trust conditions and consequences are related to the coding of Giddens concepts (e.g., communication, information and dialogue [condition] all the way along, that’s the way forward [consequence] [#43, PM] [code ¼ re-embedding]). The reasoning style is abduction: if patterns in the case can be observed, and those patterns can be explained by suitably adapting Giddens’ constructs, then those adaptations are likely to be reasonable, and theoretical propositions can be derived that are potentially generalisable across implementation situations. The resulting theoretical propositions are reliable both because they are observable in the empirical data and they form part of a nomological network supporting the trust construct based on Giddens. ## Implementation of IHIS in the Faroe Islands

The Faroe Islands are a self-governing part of the Danish National Community. Three hospitals and 27 general practitioners (GPs) report to the ministry of health. GPs are self-employed, but work in clinics supplied by the local authorities. Feasibility studies and planning for an integrated healthcare information system took place in 2004 and the contract was signed on 3 November. The IHIS was primarily understood as an integration tool: patient information should be available throughout the health system, and the patient should meet an integrated health system where all staff share an up-to-date understanding of their case. The project was discussed in the Faroese parliament, and the involvement of local IT firms sought. The implementation project is one of the largest

Two pilot wards configured and installed the IHIS, and, under the guidance of the supplier’s project manager, the core system was finally configured during the Spring of 2006. A major roll-out was planned and the surgical ward succeeded in configuring parts of the system to their needs. In early 2007 the IHIS was implemented in the emergency ward at the National Hospital, where GP’s could experience it, and a few adopted it in their own practices. In September 2007, the core system was formally taken over by the Faroese Healthcare Authorities and a party was thrown to celebrate.

In late 2007, the project organisation began to collapse. The workload was heavy, resources were scarce and project staff had divided loyalties leading to internal conflicts. Supplier deliveries were delayed and system functionality limited as a consequence. The standardised IHIS was not especially well-suited to Faroese work practice resulting in continuing configuration difficulties. Some influential groups of users (e.g., the medical consultants) were opposed to the project and there were major unresolved issues with data protection and patient information security. There were usability difficulties and frequent service interruptions. A system upgrade was available but there was a conflict between ministry and supplier over who should pay for it. The surgical ward decided that the IHIS could not be used in its present configuration and the project manager resigned, blaming the high workload and the level of conflict.

Year 2008 began with massive rescheduling of the project. The CIO secured additional funding from parliament to be spent on extra consultancy and staff compensation. A full-time consultant from the supplier with a professional background as a hospital secretary was contracted to carry out the system configuration. New super users were recruited from the wards and placed with the project staff in the ministry. The new project manager improved interactions with the wards and staff began re-configuration of the IHIS. Much time was spent on information meetings with end users and local management, and a few small self-contained clinics took the IHIS into use. By the end of 2008 the surgical ward’s IHIS was re-configured, local staff were trained in systems administration and the ward accepted a roll-out plan. A help-desk was established and GPs, interested in the system’s support for laboratory tests, shared journals and medical records, began to request the system. Both wards and GP’s became impatient with the slow rollout.

The IHIS was fully implemented in the surgical ward in early 2009 and the medical ward (except for the drug-prescription module) later in the year. The majority of GP’s also adopted the system.

## Trust issues in the implementation: conditions and consequences

In the following analysis we investigate factors impacting trust in the implementation project. The first analysis focuses on the declining spiral of trust in the project in 2004–2005, and the second on gradual recovery of trust relations in 2006–2009. References to interviews are given as [interview#, role].

## Trust decline and breakdown

People issues The Faroese health system was still largely paper-based at the start of the implementation. Lack of IT experience among Faroese health professionals (some of the nurses were really scared to have anything to do with computers y y y y they’d been on a very short course and that was that [#21, coordinator]) had a tendency to foster suspicion, sometimes expressed as overt resistance. A lack of experienced local IT personnel meant the import of foreign experts from Denmark and the language of the system interface was also Danish (It’s not our language, it’s not Faroese, you lose the sense of ownership there [#11, nurse]). The main supplier was new to the Faroese health service; the configuration supplier was local but had no previous relationship with the main supplier, or experience with IHIS. The project objectives were strongly integrative – to standardise the whole Faroese health service around the IHIS. This implied a wideranging project covering all aspects of health care – that is, extremely diverse groups of stakeholders: radiographers, consultants, medical secretaries, administrators and ministry statisticians. Co-operation between different many personnel groups with different histories, expertise and cultural expectations was therefore necessary. but difficult.

IHIS issues The IHIS is a standard system designed in Sweden; it did not match Faroese work practices particularly well, or the Faroese language or cultural conditions.

GPs, for example, have a billing system particular to Faroe Islands that was not supported by the IHIS. They also had positive experiences with another system that was discarded in favour of the new IHIS. The introduction of the IHIS threatened work practice including re-distribution of tasks and possible redundancies or relocation (You get a work practice upgrade – you leave poor existing practices behind [#47, hospital director]). It also implied a greater degree of formalisation and standardisation of work practices. which was often interpreted as more control by management and less individual autonomy (We’ll be forced to work in the way the system dictates – in the way the management wants. It’s about registration, monitoring y y [#14, general practitioner]). The formalisation of the medical journal into a digitalised patient record, at the heart of the implementation, also implied many work practice changes (e.g., the introduction of standard diagnostic codes). ## Trust dynamics in large system implementations: theoretical propositions

Our study seeks to explain the role of trust in the outcomes of an implementation project, the mechanisms behind the breakdown of trust, and how trust is recovered in situations where it is lost or threatened – thus the dynamics of trust. Trust is understood as (fluctuating) confidence in the reliability of the implementation project regarding project events and outcomes, including faith in the probity and professionalism of the project staff and managers, in the quality and appropriateness of the information system solution, and in the project’s ability to achieve valued results for its stakeholders. In this section, we develop six theoretical propositions.

## Trust and project outcomes

Trust is positively related to various kinds of outcomes in many studies; it is also positively related to project outcomes in studies of large implementations. Giddens offers an explanation for this positive relationship: disembedded abstract systems distanciated across time and space dynamise modern society by reducing the need for social relations that are primary located in personal, face-to-face interactions. In the implementation context; a high level of trust in the project acts as a form of social capital, facilitating the progress of the project by allowing project staff to pursue project objectives with the support and cooperation of their stakeholders. Trust facilitates progress by reducing social transaction costs: the effort that otherwise goes into building and maintaining social relationships in a work situation is reduced, as is the need for justification, external support and visible results. Project members can get on with their work without using too much effort for documentation, demonstration, negotiation, persuasion, compulsion, detailed explanation or contractual debate. Stakeholders voluntarily engage with, and commit to the project. Thus:

## P1: A dis-embedded project, supported by high levels of trust, is effective.

Empirical support for this proposition lies in a comparison of Table 2 (negative trust conditions and consequences) with Table 3 (positive trust conditions and consequences). Lack of trust in the project incurs many overheads and disadvantages compared with the improved trust situation. Social relations are highlighted in NTC2, 3, 6, 7, 10 (Table 2) and PTC1, and 4 (Table 3); project progress in NTC8 and 9 and PTC6; and the relationship with project outcomes in NTC5 and 10, PTC3 and 7.

## Maintaining trust

An abstract system, according to Giddens, is experienced through its access points and these interactions are interpreted and reinterpreted (chronic reflection), both individually and in social discourse. Trust in the project is constantly assessed and reassessed, and affects the ontological security of actors. Ontological security is reinforced where positive experiences match trust perceptions (I/we have correctly assessed the project), but can also be weakened (my/our faith in this project was misplaced). It follows that trust in an implementation project cannot be absolute or permanent, but will vary dynamically over time and between stakeholders. Project members interact with their stakeholders in management and configuration meetings, training, small-scale implementations, help and advice, but stakeholders also meet the project indirectly, through websites, emails, reports and news bulletins, evaluations, assessments and gossip. IT projects have a reputation for being difficult, so no news is not necessarily good news; it may imply that the project is in trouble. However, frequent satisfactory encounters with the project and small success stories reinforce stakeholders’ personal and shared feelings of comfort with the proiect. and thus their confidence and trust in it. This leads to the proposition:

P2: Trust in the project is maintained through positive interactions at its access points; these reinforce actors’ ontological security.

We find empirical support for this proposition both in the difficulties caused by NTC 8-10 in Table 2 (project delays, resource shortages, user dissatisfaction) – in effect many small failures – and in the positive consequences of PTC1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 (Table 3). Here many good interactions between project and stakeholders are interlinked with many reported small benefits, with all parties expressing confidence in the future.

## The breakdown of trust relations

Giddens argues that a dis-embedded abstract system is sustained by trust, but that trust is conditional. Unsatisfactory experiences at an abstract system’s access points interpreted through chronic reflection (by individuals and groups) prompt the re-evaluation of trust. As trust disappears, the dynamism of the abstract system (the product of the social capital of trust) is reduced. The effort required to sustain social relationships with stakeholders increases. An implementation project’s starting trust situation is determined by experience and history with earlier IT projects, relations between participants during the chartering phase, management advocacy and a variety of other factors. However, delays, cost overruns, broken promises, systems with severe quality problems, and mismatch between systems and work practices weaken trust, confuse and demoralise both users and project staff. Joint commitment disappears; project members lose confidence, become doubtful and demoralised and may eventually resign. In a situation of weakened trust, participants may demand technical explanations that would not normally be necessary, instigate surveillance measures, create rule systems and monitoring regimes and insist on explicit documentation and strict adherence of contractual provisions. Users may be disinclined to use time for adjusting their work routines or for learning complex features. Problems with the system may be interpreted as structural and critical, rather than as inevitable teething problems that will soon be fixed. Visible association with the project may become damaging to participants’ credibility, and the project may not be prioritised in relation to competing time commitments. Time overruns. missed deadlines. late deliveries and budget compliance become serious issues, and the cause of political and power manoeuvres in the wider organisational context. In extreme cases the project may be curtailed or cancelled. A low level of trust in the project, or mistrust, hinders the progress of the project by requiring project staff to overcome stakeholder doubt, and to build common understandings, support and cooperation before they can further project outcomes. Therefore:

P3: When trust is low or turns to mistrust, ontological security is weakened, overheads are introduced, and the project abstract system is less dis-embedded and less effective.

This is the empirical story summarised in NTC1-10 (Table 2).

## Restoration of trust relations

The primary means of restoring trust, according to Giddens, is re-embedding. Re-embedding re-situates an abstract system in the local and the personal through facework encounters at access points. Re-embedding encounters facilitate dialogue or experience, which improves confidence in an abstract system through personal contact with the experts that embody it. Weakened trust or mistrust in the project must be restored by meetings, negotiations, explanations and rework, additional training, negotiated adjustments to programmes, tailoredsoftware adjustments, bug fixes and help desk activity. Project problems can be made tangible and discussed face-to-face, misunderstandings corrected, compromises adopted. Issues can be tackled and solved one by one with the stakeholder (groups) concerned. Trust can be restored by developing inter-personal relationships between project team and project stakeholders, preferably face-to-face, representing a considerable extra investment in personal communication from project staff.

## P4: Trust can be restored by re-embedding activities, often in face-to-face encounters (facework commitment).

Empirical support lies in PTC1 (personal contact with stakeholders) and its interrelation with perceived benefits and project successes (PTC3, 7).

The role of the information system in restoring trust The overall objective of the implementation is a functioning information system embedded in users’ work practice. Giddens does not theorise the information system, but it can be understood as an important access point to the project, representing the realisation of the expertise of project staff and suppliers and the culmination of the project. Giddens argues that re-embedding takes place through facework commitment, but we contend that the incrementally implemented information system can be an important factor in confidence in the project. Thus, the information system itself can become an access point for faceless re-embedding of the project. When users can experience that (parts of) the information system are in place, learn to use it, experiment with it and recognise potential work benefits, trust in the project is strengthened. Contact with a reasonably well-functioning information system, with the correct learning support, increases confidence both in the system itself and the project that is responsible for developing it, even though it does not necessarily involve personal contact with project staff.

## P5: The information system being implemented (provided that it functions well) is an effective access point where re-embedding can take place (faceless commitment).

The empirical basis is in NTC5 and PTC2, 3 (relating to contact and use of the system), and their relation to PTC3 (experienced small successes).

## Trust in the project: emergent, reciproca

Giddens theorises trust in abstract systems as an emergent and reciprocal process. Emergence means that the relationship between constructs changes over time, where the finishing conditions for one time period become the starting conditions for the next: thus an evolutionary process. Reciprocity means that the relationship between constructs is bidirectional: trust affects encounters at an abstract system’s access points; encounters affect trust. We can translate this into the language of project implementation by understanding that a project has many interrelated outcomes with many stakeholder groups, which unfold over a relatively long period of time. Trust, or lack of trust, affects the quality of encounters between the project and its stakeholders and thus the short-term outcome. This outcome and the resulting trust situation (positive or negative) affects the next encounter – thus the next outcome. The next encounter is easier because of the social capital effect of trust – and thus more productive, or more difficult. Moreover stakeholders communicate their impressions of their encounters with the project to other stakeholders. These factors provide the conditions for snowball effects. A project may experience a negative trust spiral, in which one poor encounter sets the scene for the next. Confidence in the project wanes, stakeholders lose commitment, deadlines are missed and budgets overrun; each minor problem further erodes trust and makes the next task more difficult. The negative trust spiral in the case we observed was more severe than a corresponding positive spiral: recovering seemed more like hard work. Thus:

## P6: The relationship between trust in an implementation project and project outcomes is reciprocal and emergent.

Empirical evidence is found in NTC1-2, 4–5, 8–10 (Table 2) and PTC3, 5 and 7 (Table 3).

## Conclusions

In this study, we investigated a substantial IHIS implementation on the Faroe Islands, through a longitudinal case study. The research objective was to develop explanations of the dynamics of trust in the context of large-scale implementations, using constructs derived from Giddens theory of modernity. Trust is, of course, only one of many factors affecting project outcomes, but as an essential part of the social fabric of how we constitute modern work relations, can have a significant impact. Because trust operates as a kind of social infrastructure (in Giddens’ account), it is often the negative consequences of lack of trust that are most apparent to the observer. Trust is maintained by positive reinforcement encounters with the project, but can just as easily be lost by a series of minor disappointing encounters – a major project catastrophe is not necessary. Negative consequences of lack of trust, such as loss of expertise and unwillingness to collaborate, are compounded twice: first by the overheads of running a project where everything must be explained, justified, regulated, controlled and documented, and second by the expense of regaining trust. Thus, repeated minor trust disappointments can contribute to a snowball effect. We point to two strategies for restoring trust: intensive face-to-face communication, and interaction with the information system itself. The first, a reversion to a pre-modern model where trust lies in personal relations in order to anchor trust in the project again, is expensive in staff time. The second, where the information system needs to be configured and deployed early and progressively, to allow the development of confidence in the technical artefact (which is the end result of the implementation endeavour) requires a change in traditional project organisation.

These insights are formulated as six theoretical propositions theorising the relationship between trust and project outcomes, the maintenance, breakdown and restoration of trust, the role of the information system in restoring trust, and the emergent and reciprocal nature of trust and project outcomes. The study, thus complements earlier research focused more on variance studies and taxonomic deconstruction of the trust construct. We acknowledge the limitations of the study. It is exploratory in nature and the resulting propositions need to be investigated and confirmed by further studies taking the propositions as starting hypotheses and using a variety of research methods. As a single case study its external validity is weak, and the generalisability of the results should be investigated, especially in other cultural situations. Trust is a culturally relative concept, and known to operate differently under different cultural conditions. The study also needs to be integrated with taxonomic theories of trust that give a better deconstruction of the concept than Giddens’ theories encourage.

## Interview protocol

1. Welcome, about the research project 1.1. Who are we?

2. The implementation project and you 2.1. How are you related to the implementation project? 2.2. What type of tasks does that give you?

3. The supplier

3.1. Who are supplying the IHIS and related services?

4. Goals

4.1. What is the purpose of the project? 4.2. What should be achieved? Seen from the point of view by: 4.2.1. You 4.2.2. Your ward/Organisational unit 4.2.3. The Department of Health 4.2.4. The Faroese Society

## 5. Expectations

5.1. The technology as such 5.1.1. What is ‘IHIS’ and how is it constructed? 5.1.2. Who are the users?

5.2. Strategy

5.2.1. What are the success criteria of the project? 5.2.2. What is the motivation? Why?

5.3. Use of technology 5.3.1. How will the IHIS change daily life?

5.3.2. What happens with the working environment? 5.3.2.1. Your daily work 5.3.2.2. Is that good or bad? 5.3.2.3. Will it influence your behaviour and contact with others? 5.3.2.4. Any reflections? Will it be a better place to work? 5.3.2.5. Will it change the division of labour between groups of employees?

5.4.1. Who is responsible for the IHIS? 5.4.2. Who ‘owns’ it?

9. Areas of concern 9.1. Where will problems occur?

10. Resistance

10.1. Have you seen any resistance against the implementation project? 10.2. By whom?
