# Effectiveness of top management support in enterprise systems success: a contingency perspective of fit between leadership style and system life-cycle

Zhen Shao<sup>1</sup>, Yuqiang Feng<sup>1</sup> and Qing Hu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Management Science and Engineering, School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China; <sup>2</sup>Supply Chain and Information Systems, College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Qing Hu, College of Business, Iowa State University, 2211 Gerdin Business Building, Ames, IA 50011, U.S.A. Tel: +1 515 294 8925; E-mail: qinghu@iastate.edu

## Abstract

Top management support has been identified as one of the most important factors in the success of enterprise systems (ES). However, few studies have addressed the issue of what type of leadership is most effective in which phase of the ES life-cycle. Given the different challenges to leadership in the different phases of an ES, a ‘one-style-fits-all’ approach is clearly inadequate. In this study, we analyze the contingent fit between the recognized leadership styles and the phases in the ES life-cycle. The evidence from a multi-case study provides support for our contingency propositions that transformational leadership fits best with the adoption phase, transactional leadership fits best with the implementation phase, and two variations of combined transformational and transactional leadership styles are most effective in the assimilation and extension phases. This study breaks new theoretical ground in information systems literature by highlighting the contingencies of leadership effectiveness in the success of ES at different phases. It also provides prescriptive insights for top executives in terms of who to put in charge and what type of leadership style to look for when considering adopting and implementing new ES, assimilating the implemented systems, or contemplating integrations with business partners. European Journal of Information Systems advance online publication, 21 July 2015; doi:10.1057/ejis.2015.6

Keywords: enterprise systems life-cycle; contingency theory; transactional leadership; transformational leadership; top management; ERP success; multi-case study

## Introduction

With globalization of the economy and an increasingly dynamic market environment, enterprise systems (ES), such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), supply chain management (SCM), and customer relationship management (CRM), have emerged as some of the most critical information technologies powering businesses since the 1990s (James & Wolf, 2000). ES usually comprise integrated modules across multiple business functions and even organizational boundaries, and can provide effective functionalities for building knowledge platforms through systematic acquisition, storage, processing, and dissemination of organizational data and information. Thus, ES are regarded as one of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant levers for organizations to derive competitive advantage (Purvis et al, 2001; Hendricks et al, 2007). However, because of the scale and complexity of ES, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amounts of resources and organizational changes are often required and different challenges often rise at various phases of an ES life-cycle (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Besson & Rowe, 2001), resulting in a higher failure rate than those of other types of information systems (Hong & Kim, 2002; Liang et al, 2007; Bernroider, 2013).

Improving the chances of success of ES has been a focus of research in the last three decades (Hong & Kim, 2002; Umble et al, 2003; Law & Ngai, 2007). Top management support has been recognized as one of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors (Ngai et al, 2008; Dong et al, 2009; Staehr, 2010; Elbanna, 2013). This is primarily because successful adoption, implementation, and use of complex information systems often requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cant adaptation and change of organizational context. Thus, actions by top management to unfreeze prevailing institutional routines, resolve interpersonal and management con<sup>fl</sup>icts, introduce complementary structures that facilitate the use of the systems, and reinforce norms that value the routine and innovative use of the systems are critical to the success of ES (Besson & Rowe, 2001; Somers & Nelson, 2004).

However, most of the extant studies focus on top management belief, top management participation, or top management commitment. Few have examined what speci<sup>fi</sup>c leadership style of top management is needed or most appropriate in each of the phases of an ES life-cycle (Staehr, 2010).To our knowledge, only a handful of studies have alluded to the issue of leadership style vis-à-vis the adoption and implementation phases (Neufeld et al, 2007; Ke & Wei, 2008), and few studies have speci<sup>fi</sup>ed how, where, and why ‘top management support’ contributes to ES success across its life-cycle (Staehr, 2010). Given the complexity of ES and different challenges in each phase of the system life-cycle, what has been missing is a systematic examination of the <sup>fi</sup>t between leadership style and each phase of the system life-cycle and its consequences in the system’s success in organizations.

On the other hand, leadership theory suggests that speci<sup>fi</sup>c support actions and behaviors of top management are dependent on their leadership styles (Bass, 1985). In a typical ES life-cycle (e.g., adoption, implementation, and assimilation), the focal organization faces different challenges in each of the phases, which demand different types of leadership styles. For example, in the adoption phase, presenting a vision for the organization and articulating how an ES might enable that vision are critical in mobilizing resources and getting stakeholders’ buy-in. The implementation phase is primarily about plan execution, con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution, and project management. In the assimilation phase, attention to details and innovation focus are both important to foster continuous learning and improvement of system use. There are glaring gaps in the extant literature regarding the different phases of the ES life-cycle and the appropriate leadership styles needed in each of the phases. Although contingency theory of leadership effectiveness appeared in the management literature in the 1950s (Fiedler,1964; Kerr et al, 1974), the issue of whether different types of leadership style are needed in different phases of an ES life-cycle in order to provide effective leadership has not been a focus in the IS literature until recently (e.g., Shao et al, 2012).

Another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap in the IS literature is the lack of a clear and consistent de<sup>fi</sup>nition of success in each of the ES life-cycle phases. While a normative de<sup>fi</sup>nition of success for the implementation phase is commonly referenced in the literature – a project is completed on time and within budget with most, if not all, of the designed features implemented – there are no clear de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of what constitute success in the adoption and assimilation phases. For example, Liang et al (2007) proposed measuring ERP assimilation success in terms of breadth (percentage of business processes supported by ERP) and depth (users’ perception of ERP’s impact on their business activities), while Liu et al (2010, 2011) argued for measuring ERP assimilation success at both the organizational and individual levels.

In order to understand the <sup>fi</sup>t between leadership style and the ES life-cycle, there must be clearly articulated models for both, and a clear set of criteria for measuring success in each phase of the life-cycle model. These gaps are not just theoretical in terms of research literature, but also very practical in terms of managing ES. Clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and delineated ES life-cycle models will help organizations to plan and execute complex and often resource-demanding ES projects, and understanding what type of leadership style <sup>fi</sup>ts best with which phase of the ES life-cycle will help organizations put the right leadership in place and increase the likelihood of ES success.

The primary objective of this study is thus to explore which speci<sup>fi</sup>c leadership style is most effective in which particular phase of the ES life-cycle. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we measure leadership effectiveness indirectly by the success of ES in each phase of the life-cycle. In order to accomplish this goal, we conducted a multi-case study of six organizations and 27 interviewees, guided by leadership theory of Bass (1985) and Ouinn (1984). Based on the findings of our case study, as well as prior studies on IS success and top management support, we established a contingency framework of leadership in the ES life-cycle, which provides a new theoretical perspective for ES research and practical guidance to executives for managing the different phases in the ES life-cycle.

## Theoretical background

This study was motivated primarily by our observation that many of the top management members we interviewed over the years exhibited signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different leadership styles, and the success of ES in their organizations also varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in different phases of the ES life-cycle. Yet, there is little discussion in the IS literature about how speci<sup>fi</sup>c leadership styles may affect the effectiveness of top management support for ES systems (Shao et al, 2012). Is there a link between leadership style and ES success? A survey of the literature revealed that there is extensive literature on leadership style, ES life-cycle, and

ES success, but little about the linkage among these three. In the following sections, we review selected studies in these three areas, which serve as an a priori theoretical foundation for this case study (Eisenhardt, 1989; Paré, 2004).

## Strategic leadership

Strategic leadership theory has evolved from upper echelons theory developed by Hambrick & Mason (1984) to social construction of top executives (Vera & Crossan, 2004). Different from leadership theory, which refers to leaders at any level in the organization, strategic leadership theory focuses on top executive work as a strategic activity. One branch of leadership theory that has been proven useful to the study of strategic leadership is Bass’s (1985) transactional/transformational leadership framework, which was developed within large organizational contexts and has been successfully applied to the study of top-level managers (Finkelstein et al, 2009).

The de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of transformational and transactional leadership styles build on prior leadership research, such as relation-oriented vs task-oriented leadership, directive vs participative leadership, and charismatic, inspirational, visionary and path-goal leadership (Yukl, 2012). Bass (1985) argued that transactional leaders ‘focus on what can clearly work, keeping time constraints in mind, doing what seems to be most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and free of risk’; therefore, they are mostly concerned with ‘how to best keep the system running for which they are responsible—reacting to problems generated by observed deviances, looking to modify conditions as needed and remaining ever mindful of the organizational constraints within which they must operate’ (p. 105). On the other hand, ‘transformational leaders attempt and succeed in raising colleagues, subordinates, followers, clients, or constituencies to a greater awareness about the issues of consequence’ (p. 17); therefore, ‘transformational leaders are more likely to be proactive than reactive in their thinking; more creative, novel, and innovative in their ideas; more radical or reactionary than reforming or conservative in ideology’ (p. 105).

Later, Bass & Avolio (1995) developed the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) to measure transformational and transactional leadership, and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the two leadership styles into six sub-dimensions. Over the last two decades, the validity of the MLQ has been examined and supported with large sample studies (Bass et al, 2003). It is important to note that in the view of Bass (1985), transformational and transactional leadership are not two ends of a spectrum but two separate dimensions of leadership, and there exists a high inter-correlation between transformational and transactional leadership (Avolio et al, 1999). Therefore, the best leaders are described as those who integrate both transformational and transactional leadership characteristics (Vera & Crossan, 2004; Jansen et al, 2009).

On the other hand, Quinn (1984, 1988) summarized eight leadership roles:broker, facilitator, innovator, mentor, director, producer, monitor, and coordinator. Vera & Crossan (2004) compared the eight roles with Bass’s (1985) leadership framework, and argued that transformational leadership behaviors re<sup>fl</sup>ect the roles of innovator, broker, facilitator, and mentor, while transactional leadership behaviors re<sup>fl</sup>ect the roles of coordinator, monitor, producer, and director, and an ideal executive must develop ‘behavioral complexity’ or the ability to play competing leadership roles simultaneously (Denison et al, 1995; Egri & Herman, 2000; Vera & Crossan, 2004). Drawing on the extant literature, we summarized the subdimensions of Bass (1985) leadership framework and the corresponding leadership roles de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Quinn (1984, 1988) in Table 1.

In this study, we adopt the transformational-transactional framework as a theoretical foundation, and focus on how top management leadership style in<sup>fl</sup>uences ES success in each speci<sup>fi</sup>c phase of an ES life-cycle. We use the term combined leadership style to describe top managers who are able to identify and exercise transformational and/or transactional leadership behaviors appropriately according to the circumstances, and this is also consistent with strategic leadership theory (Vera & Crossan, 2004).

## Enterprise systems life-cycle

ES are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as software that enables the integration of transaction-oriented data and business processes throughout an organization (Markus & Tanis, 2000). As integration software, ES represent a complete or near-complete rearchitecting of an organization’s portfolio of transactionprocessing applications and business processes to achieve integration of business processes, information systems, and information. Along with supporting computing platforms and value chain activities, ES promise a seamless information <sup>fl</sup>ow throughout an organization (Davenport, 1998; Markus & Tanis, 2000).

Because of the complexity of ES, they usually cost millions of dollars to implement and several years for the host organizations to assimilate the systems’ functionalities and capabilities (Ross & Vitale, 2000; Hendricks et al, 2007). Therefore, ES are usually adopted and implemented in multiple phases with different tasks and challenges. However, there is no consensus in the literature regarding the exact nature and milestones of the phases in the life-cycle. Kwon & Zmud (1987) divided general information systems’ life-cycle into six phases: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion. Ross & Vitale (2000) divided ES life-cycle into <sup>fi</sup>ve phases: design, implementation, stabilization, continuous improvement, and transformation. Markus & Tanis (2000) divided ES lifecycle into four phases: chartering, project, shake down, and onward and upward. Swanson & Ramiller (2004) divided information systems into four phases: comprehension, adoption, implementation, and assimilation. Wu & Chuang (2010) classi<sup>fi</sup>ed SCM systems into three phases: adoption, implementation, and assimilation. Bernroider (2013) further divided the chartering phase in Markus & Tanis (2000) model into two phases: project initiation, and system justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and funding. Interestingly, only Ross & Vitale (2000) envisioned that ES would eventually span organizational boundaries into inter-organizational supply chain, and integrate with the systems of customers and suppliers. However, in their model the important adoption phase is absent where critical organizational decisions and actions take place before the design and implementation can start. See Shao et al (2012) for a more detailed comparison of the different ES life-cycle models.

In this study, we adopt a four-phase model of the ES lifecycle proposed by Shao et al (2012), which we believe is more concise and better re<sup>fl</sup>ects the major characteristics of ES in their evolutionary cycles. This model is described in Table 2.

## Structural <sup>vs</sup> leadership <sup>fi</sup>t perspectives

Studies on ES have frequently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed ‘mis<sup>fi</sup>t’ between an ES and its host organization as a primary cause of implementation failure or ineffective system assimilation (Soh et al, 2000; Hong & Kim, 2002; Morton & Hu, 2008; Strong & Volkoff, 2010). Several ES-organization ‘<sup>fi</sup>t’ or ‘mis<sup>fi</sup>t’ frameworks have been proposed, most notably the system-based <sup>fi</sup>t framework by Soh et al (2000), Wei et al (2005), and Strong & Volkoff (2010) and the organizationbased <sup>fi</sup>t framework by Morton & Hu (2008). In systembased <sup>fi</sup>t frameworks, the focus is on the ES, primarily on its input (data), process (functionalities, roles, and embedded controls), and output (reports and presentation) (Soh et al, 2000; Wei et al, 2005; Strong & Volkoff, 2010). In contrast, in organization-based <sup>fi</sup>t frameworks, the focus is on the organizational structure and its implied cultural characteristics as typi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the ideal types of organizations (Mintzberg, 1979). Morton & Hu (2008) argued that among the four major organizational types (machine bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, divisionalized form, and adhocracy), only machine bureaucracy organizations have a natural <sup>fi</sup>t to the integrated and standardized features ES usually impose on the host organizations. Strong & Volkoff (2010) extended the systembased <sup>fi</sup>t frameworks by including an organizational culture dimension to capture mis<sup>fi</sup>t caused by the ES required ways of operating that con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the prevalent organizational norms, such as the need to ‘move from a culture of anything needed to get the product out of the door to a culture of doing things the way they were supposed to be done’ (p. 745). This extension creates a bridge between the two <sup>fi</sup>t frameworks.

While the two <sup>fi</sup>t frameworks provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights into where and why mis<sup>fi</sup>ts occur and how they might be mitigated and addressed during the implementation and assimilation phases, they lack attention to top management leadership, which has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a critical success factor in the enterprise literature as described above. Focusing on mis<sup>fi</sup>ts between the structural characteristics of ES (input, process, and output) and those of organizations (type and culture) diminishes the important role of top management in identifying, mitigating, and correcting the mis<sup>fi</sup>ts over time throughout the ES life-cycle, a dynamic process that cannot be well explained from a structural perspective. To better understand the issue of <sup>fi</sup>t between leadership and ES life-cycle, we use contingency theory as the analytical lens to propose a contingency-based ES-organization <sup>fi</sup>t framework.

Contingency theory originates from organization research that covers a broad spectrum of organizational issues, from strategy and performance to leadership (Hofer, 1975; Donaldson, 2001). The fundamental argument of contingency theory is that there is no one optimal way to structure an organization, lead the organization, or make decisions that will lead to the best organizational performance. Instead, the optimal course of action is contingent upon internal and external situations and contexts, and organizational effectiveness is achieved by matching organizational characteristics to contingent variables, which moderates the effect of an organizational characteristic on organizational performance (Morgan & London, 1998; Donaldson, 2001).The most common contingencies include organizational environment, organizational size, and organizational strategy, and the most adaptable organizational characteristic is organizational structure (Donaldson, 2001). Thus, structural contingency theory has been the most widely used contingency framework in organizational studies, which argues that organizational structure needs to <sup>fi</sup>t with the three organizational contingencies in order to achieve high performance (Donaldson, 2001).

The paradigm of contingency theory has been adapted in various organizational contexts that have moved beyond the traditional structure and performance relationship in organizational literature. IS scholars have used the contingency lens to explain a variety of organizational phenomena and IS success. Raymond (1990) developed a research model to examine the impact of organizational contextual factors on information systems success. Barki et al (2001) developed an integrative contingency model of software project risk management and found that the outcome of a software development project is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the <sup>fi</sup>t between the project’s risk and how project risk is managed. Sharma & Yetton (2003) proposed a contingent model in which task interdependence moderates the effect of management support on ES implementation success. More recently, Morton & Hu (2008) utilized contingency theory to identify the <sup>fi</sup>t between organizational structure and ERP system characteristics, and provided insights into successful ERP adoption and implementation in various organizations.

One of the most successful adaptions has been contingency theory of leadership (Fiedler, 1964; Kerr et al, 1974; Yukl, 2012). ‘Research has indicated that leadership is to a great extent situational, and that what is effective leadership in one situation may be ineffective in another’ (Fleishman 1953, p. 1). Hogan et al (1994) argued that researchers should not ignore the effectiveness of the leader when considering their typical behaviors, and leader effectiveness concerns judgments about a leader’s impact on an organization’s success. Fiedler (1964) observed that group performance is related to both leadership style and the degree to which a particular situation provides the leader with the opportunity to exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence with that type of style. He further argued that task-oriented leaders perform best in situations that are either highly favorable (e.g., in position of power, well-structured tasks) or relatively unfavorable (e.g., being disliked, unstructured tasks) to the leaders, while considerate, relationship-oriented leaders tend to perform better in situations where they have only moderate in<sup>fl</sup>uence (e.g., accepted leaders in nebulous and ambiguous tasks, or unaccepted leaders in well-structured tasks).

In this study, we apply the contingency perspective in the context of ES management to illustrate the <sup>fi</sup>t between top management leadership style and the four phases of the ES life-cycle. We argue that each of the phases in the ES life-cycle model faces different challenges and demand different types of leadership support, and one speci<sup>fi</sup>c leadership style may not <sup>fi</sup>t well with all of the phases with varying demands and challenges. In order to achieve success, the leadership style must <sup>fi</sup>t well with the challenges in each phase of the ES life-cycle. Following this logic, we adopt a contingency lens in analyzing the fundamental research questions of this study: Which leadership style <sup>fi</sup>ts best with which phase of the ES lifecycle and why?

## Research design and methodology

The objective of our study is to explore a contingency theory of the most appropriate top management leadership style in different phases of the ES life-cycle. Given the nascent nature of this research goal, we choose to use a multi-case study methodology based on the case study protocols and guidelines speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Yin (2003) and Paré (2004). We consider each case as a distinct analytical unit and build theory from the cases through recursive cycling among the case data, emerging theory, and extant literature following the replication logic (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).

## Multi-case study design

Our multi-case study design requires that the case <sup>fi</sup>rms are in different phases of the ES life-cycle and have experienced several particular phases (adoption, implementation, assimilation, and extension). Small and mediumsized enterprises (SMEs) are preferred to ensure that the top management leadership styles are recognized throughout an organization. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we selected individuals who have experienced most of if not the entire ES life-cycle in their <sup>fi</sup>rms as the key informants to ensure accurate and comprehensive recalls and descriptions of evidence and experience.

To ensure triangulation of the case evidence, informants with diverse organizational roles and background (includ ing top managers, middle managers, and employees) were recruited in each <sup>fi</sup>rm to enrich the viewpoints related to and increase the reliability of insights into the relationship between leadership style and each phase of the ES lifecycle. In addition to the face-to-face interviews, we also collected internal documents such as employee handbooks, company brochures, and presentations to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of each of the case <sup>fi</sup>rms and their systems.

Following the prescription of Paré (2004), a case study protocol was developed to specify the initial research questions, a priori theories, goals of the interviews, semi-structured interview questions, desirable interview candidates, and idea pro<sup>fi</sup>les of case <sup>fi</sup>rms. Three sets of semi-structured interview questions were designed in advance to guide the interviews with top management, middle management, and employees in order to ensure coverage and consistency. A number of open-ended questions were used in each set to triangulate among the three groups of interviewees about speci<sup>fi</sup>c facts and events related to leadership styles and ES life-cycle. The initial interview questions are presented in Appendix B.

Based on a contact list of top managers available to the research team provided by a local campus of a top research university in China, a set of medium-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms located on the east coast of China were initially contacted according to industry, ES use, and willingness to participate in the research. Six were eventually selected for the case study primarily based on the availability of their top management team. Table 5 lists the pro<sup>fi</sup>les of the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms. To ensure the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of the case participants, we use A, B, C, D, E, and F to label the six <sup>fi</sup>rms.

All six <sup>fi</sup>rms are manufacturing enterprises that have implemented an integrated ERP system, including sales, purchasing, inventory, production, and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules, and all of them have used ERP systems for more than 5 years. All but one of the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ implementation processes were successful in the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt, and were able to put the systems into daily use after one to two years. Firm B, however, failed to implement the <sup>fi</sup>nancial module on the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt; a second implementation attempt was initiated two years later with a new vice president in charge of the ES project and this was ultimately successful. ## Data collection

The <sup>fi</sup>rst data collection took place in late 2010. The research team, which consisted of the authors and several doctoral students, visited <sup>fi</sup>ve of the <sup>fi</sup>rms to conduct the face-to-face interviews. Each individual informant was interviewed in an hour-long session, all digitally recorded with the consent of the participants. We conducted a second data collection in mid-2014 and revisited two of the <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>rms (Firm B and Firm D) to follow up on their systems’ use in the last 3 years and to gather more evidence from the top-level managers with regard to leadership changes during the systems’ life-cycle. We also interviewed two informants in <sup>fi</sup>rm F, which has a long history of using ES. Interestingly, Firm B has extended its ES to the supply chain by integrating B2B (business to business) and SRM (supply relationship management) modules from its original Oracle system, while Firm D has upgraded its production module. All of the interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese.

After the completion of the interviews at each site, the team then toured the production facility and observed actual use of ES in various business processes. Relevant documents were collected whenever available, including PowerPoint presentations, company brochures, promotional CD/DVDs, and marketing material.

One of the approaches to create a coding scheme in order to organize the unstructured text data is to rely on prede<sup>fi</sup>ned themes based on theoretical considerations (Paré, 2004). These two sets of themes de<sup>fi</sup>ned our top-level coding scheme.

<sup>Adoption</sup> <sup>phase</sup> In the adoption phase, an organization is faced with the decision whether or not to use a particular ES according to its internal and external environments. Research shows that the adoption decision occurs at the organizational upper echelons level, and usually without much lower-level participation (Jasperson et al, 2005). The adoption decision requires that top executives focus on organizational vision, be sensitive to internal and external environments, and make timely decisions regarding ES (Tong & Yap, 1995; Damanpour & Schneider, 2006; Bernroider & Schmöllerl, 2013). In <sup>fi</sup>rm B, when asked who initiated the adoption of the Oracle ERP system, the manager of the information center stated:

In the year 2000, our <sup>fi</sup>rm experienced rapid expansion, and the original system could not support the business requirements at that time. The CFO directly asked us to select a new set of systems to replace the original old system to better support our business.

When asked how he would describe the leadership style of the top executive, the manager stated:

He is very insightful. When going for business visits, if he <sup>fi</sup>nds that other <sup>fi</sup>rms have adopted new IT systems, he will think if the same systems can be introduced into our <sup>fi</sup>rm. He will then make a proposal at meetings and ask for our suggestion on the applicability of the system for supporting our business requirements.

Adoption of ES will also inevitably require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes to organizational processes, routines, power distribution, and culture (Staehr, 2010). Change requires a strategic vision for ES to ensure its long-term success (Aladwani, 2001), and leadership was ranked as the number one facilitator of large transformation efforts (Colbert et al, 2008). The top executive who wants to initiate the change must be able to articulate a clear vision of ES for the organization and the objectives of adopting the system, and to communicate the vision and objectives to the entire organization, in order to manage task, political, and governance con<sup>fl</sup>icts caused by power redistribution and business process changes (Besson & Rowe, 2001; Damanpour & Schneider, 2006). When asked about how to convince the other top management team members to accept a new system, the CFO from <sup>fi</sup>rm B stated:

I must clearly articulate to them why our <sup>fi</sup>rm needs the new system, and what bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the system can bring to us at multiple levels, including business operations, risk control, and strategy. This is very important to secure the support from key top management members. If they can foresee the long term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the enterprise system, they will be more likely to devote resources to the ERP project.

## This sentiment is echoed by the CIO in <sup>fi</sup>rm F:

I agree with the argument that ERP system is a top leadership project…If I recognize the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of an enterprise system, I must be able to articulate the adoption decision to the top management clearly and communicate expectations for the system’s bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, so they can understand the importance of ERP system for organizational long-term development and accept the system psychologically.

During the adoption phase, the organization must also select the most appropriate systems (software and hardware) based on its business strategic goals and allocate resources for the subsequent implementation (Markus & Tanis, 2000). This requires the top executive to be decisive, insightful, and knowledgeable, and lead the organization to select the most appropriate systems and invest necessary resources based on a thorough evaluation of business operations and competitive environment (Damanpour & Schneider, 2006). This argument is echoed by the CIO in <sup>fi</sup>rm F, when talking about systems selection:

ERP system selection is like choosing an appropriate marriage partner. The adaption process will be very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult if there are value differences between each other, which may lead to a <sup>fi</sup>nal separation. Thus a match between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the selected system is very important…we <sup>fi</sup>nally selected DigiwinSoft since it is more professional in manufacturing management and is appropriate for our business requirements.

The assimilation phase is quite unique when compared with the adoption and implementation phases in terms of its leadership challenges. On one hand, leaders still need to manage the routine tasks of con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution in daily transactions and regular system upgrades. ## Discussion

In this study, we set out to understand the important issue of what type of top management support is most effective in each phase of the ES life-cycle as indicated by the success of the phases. We accomplished this objective by conducting a multi-case study using six <sup>fi</sup>rms that have successfully implemented ES, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally ERP systems, from various vendors and with different modules and functionalities.

While our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the contingencybased <sup>fi</sup>t framework between leadership styles and ES lifecycle success, as summarized in the four propositions, call for different leadership styles in managing ES throughout their life-cycles, we by no means suggest that different top management leaders should be in charge in different phases of the life-cycle. Bass et al (2003) have argued that some leaders are able to demonstrate both transactional and transformational leadership styles. Therefore, it is possible that one single top executive could provide effective leadership throughout the entire ES life-cycle by exercising different or combined leadership styles. Our case evidence provided strong support for this argument. For example, the CIO in <sup>fi</sup>rm F has been involved in the entire ES life-cycle at his <sup>fi</sup>rm, and he is a leader who possesses both leadership styles and can exhibit different leadership traits in different situations. When selecting an ES, he was very insightful about the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s future; when carrying out the speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementation tasks, he attended to details and monitored the schedule of implementation; after the system was in daily use, he was active in promoting system assimilation by setting up a reward mechanism and providing personal coaching. The ERP system in <sup>fi</sup>rm F has now diffused across organizational processes and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased organizational ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, and the CIO has been invited by many other <sup>fi</sup>rms to share his <sup>fi</sup>rm’s experience. In this case, the CIO is a good example of strategic leaders who develop ‘behavioral complexity’ and exercise different leadership styles at different times (Egri & Herman, 2000; Vera & Crossan, 2004).

However, our case evidence also shows that it is fairly common in organizations for different leaders to be in charge at different life-cycle phases. For example, in the other case <sup>fi</sup>rms, a CEO or an SVP with transformational quality usually initiated the ES adoption process, but then an SVP or CFO with strong transactional leadership skills was tasked for the implementation phase. While the same leaders usually stayed on to the assimilation phase, the extension phase was likely back to the domain of a top executive with a combination of transformational vision and transactional skills. Therefore, it is very important for the top management team to carefully consider the most appropriate candidates and grant authority to the right person in each speci<sup>fi</sup>c phase of the ES life-cycle based on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c leadership characteristics of the candidates.

## Theoretical contributions

This study makes at least two major theoretical contributions to the literature of information systems research. First, we re<sup>fi</sup>ned a new ES life-cycle model that consolidates and extends the extant life-cycle models and overcomes the inconsistencies among the extant models. Our case evidence suggests that traditional ES life-cycle models are not adequate in covering the activities in the extension phase in which <sup>fi</sup>rms integrate their ES with their business partners. By envisioning that ES often span organizational boundaries in the global economy, we re<sup>fi</sup>ned a four-phase ES life-cycle model (adoption, implementation, assimilation, and extension). The new model de<sup>fi</sup>nes the key activities and success criteria in each phase, and provides a more comprehensive and parsimonious description of the ES life-cycle than traditional models prevalent in the literature.

Second, our study integrated two distinct literature streams into ES success research: leadership theory and system life-cycle using a contingency-based framework. As summarized in Table 3, extant studies have examined the issue of top management support in ES extensively, but few have explicitly articulated a contingency-based view of this study that there is no ‘one-style-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all’ model for top management support. This approach to ES research is important in at least three respects. First, it articulates the contingency nature of the relationship between leadership and system life-cycle in the context of ES success. Second, it highlights the need for versatile leaders who can exhibit different leadership styles at different times or occasions in organizations that are planning to implement and extend ES. Third, it combines leadership style and leadership role in an integral framework and matches their applicability and appropriateness in the ES life-cycle. It is not always feasible, nor it is necessarily desirable, to change leaders as an ES progresses through different phases. The contingency-based framework <sup>fi</sup>lls a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap in the information systems literature on the relationship between top management leadership and systems success by clearly articulating what types of top management leadership styles and roles are most appropriate, and therefore likely to be most effective, in which phase of an ES life-cycle.

## Managerial implications

For practitioners, this study lays out a prescriptive map for managing ES throughout the life-cycle. It provides insights for the board of directors and top executives into who to put in charge and what type of leaders to look for when they are considering adopting ES or contemplating integration with their business partners. Our case evidence suggests that in the adoption phase, top executives usually play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in scanning the external environment, initiating the discussion about technology adoption, and seeking consensus among the management ranks. For this reason, the initiating top executive needs to exhibit strong transformational leadership qualities in terms of vision and inspiration to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the beliefs of other top management team members and motivate the organizational followers. In the implementation phase, a top-level executive (e.g., CFO or CIO) with transactional leadership qualities usually takes charge of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementation tasks, and is responsible for translating the vision into actual actions by managing projects and resolving task con<sup>fl</sup>icts arising from business process reengineering, power redistribution, and resource reallocations, and this person plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in providing a sense of continuity and stability during implementation. In the assimilation phase, after the system has gone live, the major challenge facing the <sup>fi</sup>rm is to foster the exploitative as well as exploratory learning of the system functionalities. During this time, a senior-level executive not only needs to carry out tactical actions by setting up assessment mechanisms and providing contingent rewards, typical qualities of a transactional leader, but also needs to play the role of innovator and mentor and exhibit transformational leadership to promote a learning and innovation climate within the <sup>fi</sup>rm to foster new and innovative use of the system. Firms may stay in the assimilation phase for a long time once the systems are in routine operation. It takes a visionary leader with acute market instinct and strong execution ability to move a <sup>fi</sup>rm into the next phase. In the extension phase, in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm carries out system integrations with customers and suppliers, a top executive such as the CEO needs to be in charge to focus on strategic mission and coordinate with internal and external executives. In this case, an executive who possesses both transformational and transactional qualities becomes critical to the success of the extension, especially the combination of innovative instinct and execution prowess.

## Limitations and future research

We must acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, while our focus was on the ES life-cycle, our interviews took place at one time instead of over the complete life-cycle of a system. Thus, we relied on the ability of the informants to recall events and individual characteristics in the past. This could have an impact on the accuracy and reliability of the case evidence. Although data from multiple case <sup>fi</sup>rms and informants provide some degree of triangulation, longitudinal studies are still desirable and may be necessary to validate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, in this study we put forward four propositions to describe the <sup>fi</sup>t relationship between senior leadership styles and ES lifecycle based on six case <sup>fi</sup>rms in China. While some of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in studies of other countries and regions (e.g., Besson & Rowe, 2001; Wei et al, 2005), others may be subject to the potential biases and limitations of size, industry, structure, culture, and nationality of these <sup>fi</sup>rms. Future research may further examine the validity of these propositions with large sample empirical studies in different cultures and countries. Third, our study focuses on the <sup>fi</sup>t between leadership styles of top executives and the phases of the ES life-cycle without considering the role of middle managers. While middle managers certainly play an important role in the ES life-cycle, our interviews indicate that they are more likely to focus on executing the top management’s vision and decisions. However, we must admit that this observation may be unique to Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rms due to cultural factors. Future research on the role of middle managers in <sup>fi</sup>rms outside China could provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights into the dynamics of top and middle management interactions and consequences in ES success. Fourth, the complexity of ES and their reach to and in<sup>fl</sup>uence on almost every aspect of a <sup>fi</sup>rm make ES success in each of the phases of their lifecycle multifaceted and complex socio-technical issues (Staehr, 2010; Sasidharan et al, 2012). Our focus on the <sup>fi</sup>t of leadership style with each phase of the ES life-cycle has limited us from considering other important factors, as well as detailed accounts of how leaders overcome individual challenges in each of the phases, such as user resistance to new technology in the adoption phase, con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution in the implementation phase, and organizational learning in the assimilation phase. Future case studies that focus on the <sup>fi</sup>t between leadership style and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c phase may yield additional insight into contingency theory of leadership effectiveness in the context of ES success. Lastly, our case samples are biased toward relatively successful companies, making it impossible to <sup>fi</sup>rmly conclude that if there is a mis<sup>fi</sup>t according to our framework, then the chances of ES success will be adversely affected in a particular phase. Future research with cases that consist of successful and unsuccessful <sup>fi</sup>rms in terms of ES adoption, implementation, assimilation, or extension would be extremely valuable in terms of a comprehensive understanding of the focal phenomenon – how and why leadership style in<sup>fl</sup>uences ES success in organizations.

## Conclusion

Top management support has been at the top of the list of critical success factors in many published academic studies, and scholars have offered different perspectives on how and why such support is essential to the success of ES in different life-cycle phases (e.g., Besson and Rowe, 2001; Staehr, 2010; Bernroider, 2013). This study extends the research stream and addresses two speci<sup>fi</sup>c de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the literature: the inconsistency in the proposed ES lifecycle models, and the lack of attention to leadership theories about different leadership styles and their impact on leadership effectiveness in the different phases of the ES life-cycle. We integrated the extant literature on ES lifecycle and adopted a concise yet comprehensive four-phase life-cycle model – adoption, implementation, assimilation, and extension, and articulated the key organizational activities and success criteria in each of the four phases. We then analyzed the characteristics of the two most recognized leadership styles – transformational and transactional – the eight roles related to the two leadership styles – broker, facilitator, innovator, mentor, coordinator, monitor, producer, and director – and mapped the critical leadership style and leadership roles to the life-cycle model based on the empirical evidence found in a multi-case study, resulting in a contingency-based framework of leadership and ES success. This framework serves as a theoretical foundation to study the <sup>fi</sup>t between the leadership styles and the phases of the ES life-cycle and a prescriptive guide for selecting executives in different phases of ES. We found strong evidence that transformational leadership style <sup>fi</sup>ts best with the adoption phase, transactional leadership style <sup>fi</sup>ts best with the implementation phase, and two different combined leadership styles <sup>fi</sup>t best with the assimilation phase and the extension phase, respectively. We hope that this contingency-based framework of leadership in ES will inspire more empirical studies in the future to validate the fundamental thesis and speci<sup>fi</sup>c relationships, and thus signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly improve the chances of ES success in the complex and dynamic organizational environments.

## Appendix A

## Background of the case <sup>fi</sup>rms

At the beginning of each interview, we asked the respondents to provide an overview of their company and talk about the progression of their ES as they know it. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we asked them when the enterprise began to use ES and how many different phases they had experienced so far. Most of the interviewees talked about the initial adoption, implementation and subsequent assimilation phases. Then we asked them whether they had any plans for the future of their systems. Of the six <sup>fi</sup>rms, two had integrated their ES with suppliers, and three had plans to integrate ES with their business partners in the future. We now provide a brief description of the six case <sup>fi</sup>rms based on our interviews and background material collected during our visit.

## Firm A

Firm A is one of the three largest shoe-manufacturing enterprises in China. It installed its <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>nancial information system in 1997 (bought from an outside vendor) and developed sales and inventory systems in collaboration with external software companies before 2002. Although the three systems were connected through interfaces, the orders could not be transferred in real time and a manual assistant was needed.

In 2002, the <sup>fi</sup>rm had an opportunity to visit Sida, Lenvo and Haier (three leading companies in China that had implemented ERP systems successfully) to learn from their experiences. After the visit, the CEO made the decision to use an ERP system to replace the old systems in the <sup>fi</sup>rm, thus realizing the delicate management of business. The system was developed with an external software corporation and a vice president (the CFO) was in charge of the implementation project. The ERP project was initiated at the beginning of 2003 and the implementation process lasted about seven months. The middle managers of business departments were not very cooperative at the beginning since the system is not easy to use. The CEO provided strong support for the implementation and communicated frequently with the CFO on critical decisions.

All the sales, purchasing, inventory, production, <sup>fi</sup>nance and distribution modules were implemented in the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and the system was of<sup>fi</sup>cially put into daily use in 2004. At the time of our visit, the distribution system module was integrated with sales, production and purchase modules and the customers could log into the systems at any location to submit the order. The production plans were generated according to the actual orders. The system had received positive feedback from both internal employees and external clients, and it led to an increase in sales of 50–60%.The top management team was planning to extend the system to the suppliers to implement logistics integration.

## Firm B

Firm B is one of the largest tyre manufacturing enterprises in Weihai and its average annual sales revenue is 14,000 million RMB. The <sup>fi</sup>rm had developed sales, purchase, inventory, and production modules in-house and bought its <sup>fi</sup>nance module from external software suppliers before 2000. However, the original systems could no longer support the business operations around 2000.The top management team proposed to adopt an integrated ERP system instead of the original multivendor systems. After thorough market research and comparison of different ERP systems from different vendors and in-house IT units, the top management team selected the Oracle ERP system because it was more <sup>fl</sup>exible for supporting future business growth.

The ERP implementation process began soon after the system was selected. The sales, purchase, inventory, and production modules were implemented successfully but the <sup>fi</sup>nance module failed since the <sup>fi</sup>nancial operational principles of the Oracle system were quite different from the <sup>fi</sup>nancial system in use. This had created signi<sup>fi</sup>cant issues in consistency and traceability of data throughout the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In 2003, the <sup>fi</sup>rm began its second implementation attempt, and the <sup>fi</sup>nance module was eventually integrated with the other modules of the Oracle system This time the CEO designated a vice president (CFO) to be completely in charge of the implementation. With the mutual effort of the CFO and ERP key users, the second implementation was successful and the integrated ERP system was put into daily use in 2004.

Since then the system has gone through several revisions and upgrades, and it is now playing a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in managing and controlling business processes, and has realized real-time information sharing by integrating sales, inventory, production, and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules. At the time of our second visit, the <sup>fi</sup>rm had integrated SRM and Sales B2B systems to manage its suppliers’ and clients’ information and transfer orders between the headquarters and the sales subsidiary in real-time, and this had greatly increased its procurement and sales order processing ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

## Firm C

Firm C is engaged in glass manufacturing and has achieved independent intellectual property. It has a long history of using information technology for managing its business operations. It implemented the materials management system, sales management system, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial management system in a step-by-step manner in 1989, 1994, and 1996. With the growth of the business, the original systems became inadequate, and a major problem was the disconnection between the <sup>fi</sup>nance, sales, and inventory systems. Then in 2003, the <sup>fi</sup>rm decided to adopt an integrated ERP system. The <sup>fi</sup>rm investigated both domestic and international ERP software vendors such as U<sup>fi</sup>da, Kingdee, and SAP. They found that U<sup>fi</sup>da and Kingdee could not satisfy the special requirements of their products, while SAP was more complicated and expensive for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In the end, the top management team decided to develop an ERP system in-house with the help of outside software suppliers.

The CEO had a strong background in <sup>fi</sup>nance and paid great attention to the implementation process. The company’s CFO was designated to take charge of the implementation project. The CEO and CFO frequently engaged in systems training processes to make the employees know the importance of ERP systems for their business. The implementation began at the end of 2003 and lasted about one year. Finally, an integrated ERP system including sales, purchasing, product, inventory, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial management modules was implemented and put into daily use in 2005. Small improvements were made based on the new requirement proposed by the business units, and in 2007 the <sup>fi</sup>rm added the function of bar code management. At the time of our visit, the <sup>fi</sup>rm had largely achieved the intended goals for the ERP system. In the future it also has plans to integrate the system with its suppliers and clients to realize real-time information sharing in the supply chain.

## Firm D

Firm D is a famous carpet manufacturing enterprise in China. It implemented its <sup>fi</sup>rst purchase-inventory-sales system in 1998. However, each subsidiary of the <sup>fi</sup>rm needed to make separate data entries using the system and then transfer the results to the headquarters. In 2004, the top management team demanded better information systems support for its growing business operations. With this mandate, the <sup>fi</sup>rm conducted a thorough evaluation and selection process. It was confronted with the choice between buying a commercial packaged ERP system and developing an integrated ERP system in-house. Eventually the top management team decided to develop an integrated ERP system in-house in collaboration with a local university since the commercial ERP systems required extensive reengineering of the business processes and were considered high risk.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm implemented modules of sales, purchasing, inventory, and production management step by step to avoid the high risk of all-in-one implementation, and the employees experienced a gradual learning process during the implementation. The system went live in 2005. At the time of our second visit, the production module had been upgraded to realize the function of material requirements and instant data transfer between headquarters and each branch plant. About 90% of the products could be scheduled based on the orders with the ERP system, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm realized the gradual improvement of its business process by learning and assimilation of the system functionalities over time.

## Firm E

Firm E is a foreign-owned enterprise that focuses on printed circuit manufacturing. Its products are mainly applied in computers and digital cameras, and Samsung is its biggest client. The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s headquarters in South Korea has its own in-house-developed ERP system to manage its production, inventory, and quality management, and the Chinese subsidiary was integrated into the system in 2001. There were many problems with the ERP system during the integration, such as issues related to code translation from Korean to Chinese. In 2006 a new CEO was hired at the headquarters and this person pushed for the implementation of an SAP ERP system within the headquarters and all of the subsidiaries, since SAP can realize the instant and accurate data transfer from subsidiaries to the headquarters. The implementation process was not smooth and encountered resistance from employees. The CEO was very insistent and invited professional external consultants to train the employees with regard to the logic of the SAP system and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that the system would bring to the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Because of the complexity of the SAP system, the implementation process lasted for about 2 years and some employees were dismissed because of the noncooperation with the consultants. The system <sup>fi</sup>nally went live in 2008. After 2 years learning and adaptation, at the time of our visit, the <sup>fi</sup>rm could use the SAP system to make production plans and schedules, to monitor and control the quality of the products, and to use the information for forecasting and decision support. The system had been indispensable for the <sup>fi</sup>rm ever since.

## Firm F

Firm F is one of the leading pump manufacturers in China and was founded in 1952, with more than 50 years experience. There are 1300 staff and workers, including 150 technicians. The <sup>fi</sup>rm developed a purchase-inventory-sales system in-house before 2002. However, the system could not support the business requirements of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ‘small batch and multiple variety’; thus, in 2003 the top management team decided to adopt an integrated commercial system package within the organization. After detailed selection from among suppliers of Oracle, SAP, UFIDA, and DigiwinSoft, the top management <sup>fi</sup>nally decided to invest in DigiwinSoft since it is more professional in the manufacturing industry and is suited to Firm F’s business requirements.

The system implementation lasted about one year and encountered strong resistance from the <sup>fi</sup>nance department. The top management team played a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in pushing forward the implementation process. After the system was put into daily use, the manager of the information center was promoted to the position of CIO to take charge of the system utilization and upgrade within the <sup>fi</sup>rm. At the time of our visit in 2014, the <sup>fi</sup>nancial module had been integrated well with production, sales, procurement, and warehouse modules, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm had also integrated the ERP system with PDM (Product Data Management) and OA (Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Automation) to build an integrated information platform.

## Interview questions

The following questions were developed to ensure coverage and consistency of the semi-structured interviews. However, the interviewers were encouraged to ask followup questions if new and interesting information emerged and to skip certain questions if the information solicited was already provided in prior conversations.

## For members of top management team

1) What is the vision and strategy of your company regarding competition and success in the marketplace?

2) What is the role that enterprise systems play in your company?

3) Do you have a clear vision of enterprise systems in support of your business? How do you communicate that vision to other members of the top management team and the employees?

4) In your view, what would be a full life-cycle of an enterprise system? From its birth to its maturity or death, what are the main phases you have to manage?

5) In discussion about adopting a new enterprise system, what was the dominant attitude toward the system among the top management team members? And how was the top management team convinced about the decision?

6) What do you see as the most critical leadership qualities a top manager must have in order to initiate successful adoption of enterprise systems? Can you provide a few examples?

7) When did you start to implement your <sup>fi</sup>rst enterprise system? How much did it cost you to complete the implementation projects and how long did it take?

8) How much were you and other top management team members involved in the implementation phase?

9) What were the main challenges and obstacles you encountered in the enterprise systems implementation phase, and how did you and other top management team members solve them?

10) What do you see as the most critical leadership qualities a top manager must have in order to promote the successful implementation of enterprise systems? Why?

11) What were the main challenges and obstacles you encountered in the enterprise systems assimilation phase, and how did you solve them?

12) What did you or other top management team members do to ensure a high level of assimilation? Can you give us some examples?

13) How did you encourage employees to better assimilate system functionalities? Please give us some examples.

14) What do you see as the most critical leadership qualities a top manager must have in order to foster the successful assimilation of enterprise systems? Why?

15) How much are your enterprise systems connected with your partners either upstream or downstream of the supply chain? In what ways? Can you give us some examples?

16) Who is the main driver behind system integration with partners? How much are you or your top management team involved in this effort?

17) What are the major challenges and obstacles you encountered in the extension phase? How did you or your top leadership team overcome these challenges?

18) What is your role in negotiating with customers and suppliers during the process of system integration? And how do you coordinate your company’s internal resources and processes?

19) What do you see as the most critical leadership qualities a top manager must have in order to lead a successful extension of enterprise systems? Can you provide a few examples?

20) How does the success of enterprise systems relate to the success of your overall business? By what measure?
