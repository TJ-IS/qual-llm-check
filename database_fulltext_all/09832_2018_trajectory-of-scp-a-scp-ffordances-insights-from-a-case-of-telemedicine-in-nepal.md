# Trajectory of Affordances: Insights from a case of telemedicine in Nepal

Devinder Thapa<sup>1</sup> | Maung K. Sein<sup>1,2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Department of Information Systems, University of Agder, P.O. Box 422, 4604 Kristiansand, Norway

<sup>2</sup> Information Systems, Luleå University of Technology, Luleå, Sweden

Correspondence Devinder Thapa, Department of Information Systems, University of Agder, P.O. Box 422, 4604 Kristiansand, Norway. Email: devinder.thapa@uia.no

## Abstract

Although Affordance Theory has become increasingly influential in the Information Systems (IS) literature, the exact process through which the affordances of IT are actualised is less studied. In this paper, we build on a realist ontology of affordance and an interpretive epistemology of how affordances are perceived and actualised to trace the process of actualisation. On the basis of insights drawn from a case study of a telemedicine project in a remote mountainous region of Nepal, we develop a concept, which we call the “Trajectory of Affordances.” Trajectory of Affordances captures the complex relations between affordances of IT and the role of goal‐oriented actors who perceive and then play a vital role in actualising them, using capabilities that are enabled by facilitating conditions to take the necessary action. Trajectory of Affordances shows that the affordances of IT can travel from perception to actualisation through multiple paths, sometimes clustering together, and in the process, often lead to the emergence of new affordances.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

We begin the paper with a confessional account. We did not set out to study affordances nor was this perspective a candidate interpretive lens that we had in mind when we began our study of a telemedicine project in Nepal. The project has no formal name, so we call it the Nepal Telemedicine Project or NTMP in short. An application built on the well‐known Nepal Wireless Networking Project (NWNP) which, against all odds, has brought the Internet and, thus, the outside world to a remote mountainous region, NTMP was reported to be one of the successes. Villagers who previously relied on shamans and local health workers, who had very basic medical knowledge, now had acces to the expertise of specialist doctors from urban areas.

Intrigued by its reported success, we decided to examine the project more thoroughly and attempt to understan why NTMP succeeded when many similar IT interventions have failed around the world. A discussion of the problems and failures of such initiatives abound in the literature (eg Heeks 2006: Kiwanuka Kimaro & Senvoni 2015 Tomlinson, Rotheram‐Borus, Swartz, & Tsai, 2013). The case of NTMP was different.

In our study, we interviewed several people involved in the project and those affected by it. While trying to make sense of the interview transcripts we discovered that the Affordance Theory was an appropriate lens to interpret the findings. The theory originated in ecological psychology (Gibson, 1979) and is increasingly becoming an influentia theoretical premise especially in facilitating the understandings of the relation between technology and the human actor in fostering the organisational change (Bygstad, Munkvold, & Volkoff, 2016; Leonardi, 2011; Zammuto, Griffith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). By using this theory, though, critical questions are being raised (Pozzi, Pigni, & Vitari, 2014; Volkoff & Strong, forthcoming). Succinctly summarised, these questions are as follows: Are affordances embedded in artefacts or do they emerge in use? Who perceives affordances? Is it even necessary for affordances to be perceived to be actualised? How are the perceived affordances actualised? Can there be multiple affordances? If so, how do the multiple affordances interact and generate new affordances?

While notable attempts have been made to address these questions, knowledge gaps remain (Bernhard, Recker, & Burton‐Jones, 2013; Pozzi et al., 2014; Volkoff & Strong, forthcoming). The most glaring knowledge gap identified by Pozzi et al. (2014) and Volkoff and Strong (forthcoming) was that, with few exceptions (eg, Strong et al., 2014), there is a lack of extant research on the process(es) through which the affordances of IT are actualised. It became evident to us that our case study could provide insights to help address these questions and contribute to this discourse. That led us to this paper. We analysed the data from an Affordance Theory perspective to answer an overarching researc question: How are affordances of an ICT artefact perceived and actualised to achieve a desired outcome?

Our analysis revealed that it was not simply affordances per se, but interrelations between a variety of affordances and the action of a group of actors who leveraged their capabilities to actualise the affordances of NTMP. Facilitating conditions are needed to support the actualisation process. In this case, these facilitating conditions were personal, social, and cultural arrangements such as social networks and social groups in the villages. From insight gained through this understanding, we traced the process by which IT affordances travel from perception to actualisation, and then cluster to form an ensemble of affordances. Additionally, we found that actualisation of affordances leads to the emergence of new affordances. We term this process as a “Trajectory of Affordances” (ToA), which is the main contribution of our paper.

In the rest of the paper, we tell the story of how our research unfolded. Having set the scene and given an account of our initial interest in this section, we next review the literature on affordances. Then, we narrate our cas before presenting an analysis that emphasises how we induced ToA. We then present and explainToA and situate ou contribution in the discourse on affordances before concluding the paper with some suggestions for future research and reflections on the discourse of affordances in the IS literature

## 2 | AFFORDANCES

## 2.1 | Backdrop of affordances

The concept of affordance is highly influential yet controversial (Costall & Morris, 2015). It originates from the writings of James J. Gibson and has been a source of inspiration in many fields of research (Şahin, Çakmak, Doğar Uğur, & Üçoluk, 2007). Gibson (1979: 127) proposed that

[t]he affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either fo good or ill. The verb to afford is found in the dictionary, but the noun affordance is not. I have made it up. mean by it something that refers to both the environment and the animal in a way that no existing term does. It implies the complementarity of the animal and the environment

He further argued that inherent values and meanings of things in the environment can be directly perceived, and this information can be linked to the action possibilities offered to the animal by the environment

Gibson developed his ideas throughout his career, but he never provided a complete definition of affordances For example, in his early work, he emphasised visual perception, whereas later, he moved on to focusing on th resources for agency (Costall, 2012). He stated explicitly that his work was under construction and “subject to revision” (Gibson, 1979: 67). If we trace the development of Gibson's ideas, we can see how his focus moved from the natural environment to artificial objects such as tools. He also insisted that artificial affordances are no different from naturally occurring affordances. His examples about the affordances of chairs bear testimony to this transitio (Lanamäki, Thapa, & Stendal, 2015). This focus on artificial tools deems the theory of affordance relevant fo Information Systems (IS) research.

## 2.2 | Affordances in the IS discourse

In the IS literature, the affordances lens has helped researchers understand the relation between technology and the human actor (Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008). The affordances concept has been used in, for example, studies of the relation between IS and organisations (Ingram, Teigland, & Vaast, 2014; Zammuto et al., 2007) and social media use (Stendal, Thapa, & Lanamäki, 2016; Treem & Leonardi, 2013). Several researchers maintain that affordances hold promise for a relational middle ground between technological determinism and social constructivism (Faraj & Azad 2012; Leonardi & Barley, 2010; Robey & Anderson, 2013). In this view, affordance does not determine how people will use a technology, whereas at the same time, technology's potential uses are not fully open‐ended due to materia limitations. Thus, Affordance Theory is “a lens that is particularly well suited to help IS scholars build theory about ICT use” (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016: 272). However, among many unresolved issues, one remains the subject of intense debate in the IS literature: whether affordances are embedded in the artefact or whether they emerge through the user‐artefact interaction (eg, Fayad & Weeks, 2014; Zheng & Yu, 2016). Principally, there are 2 perspectives on affordances in the IS literature.

One perspective is to view affordances as “functional,” intended and embedded by the designers in the object (Markus & Silver, 2008). This is the perspective that the human‐computer interaction field took as epitomised by Norman (1999), In the IS literature, Seidel, Recker, & Vom Brocke (2013: 1279) define affordances as “action possibilities allowed by material properties existent in information systems.” In the same vein, Strong et al. (2014) identified various affordances in the context of IT‐associated organisational change while implementing electronic health record systems. Included in the identified affordances are capturing and archiving digital data about patients, accessing and using patient information anytime from anywhere, and coordinating patient care across sites, facilities, and providers The second perspective is to portray affordances not as pre‐existing in technology, but rather as emergent phenomena (Zheng & Yu, 2016). This is vividly expressed by Leonardi (2013: 772)

Another facet this study brings into relief is that affordances are not out there waiting to be utilised, as some design science researchers claim (eg, Norman, 1999), but rather that affordances must be enacted in practice through particular patterns of feature use at the group level.

Explicit in this definition is the notion that affordances are not just something that can be designed for and be intended but are emerging through use and actions. This is echoed in Leonardi's work on imbrications where he argues that users' involvement with the system over time may affect the perception from what the system can do (ie, affordances) to what it cannot (Leonardi, 2011)

Clearly, these 2 perspectives point to a principle disagreement on the ontology of affordances. The first can be framed as “independent existence” of affordances, whereas the second frames affordances as “emergent.” To avoi any ontological ambiguity in this paper, we turn to the discourse in ecological psychology, where according to Michaels (2003: 136), whether affordances exist independent of perception, or whether they depend on perceptio

… is an easy question and should be obvious to all ecological psychologists. Affordances are not created in the act of perception; they exist independent of it. The theory of affordances is part of the ecologica ontology. It is a statement of what is available in the world to be perceived.

On the same page, she goes on to make an even stronger claim “Given the unanimity of opinion among ecologica psychologists on this point, it may seem hardly worth raising as a point of attention and debate.” Nevertheless Michaels goes on to discuss this question because according to her, Gibson's original conceptualisation of affordances has been misinterpreted when adopted in other fields. She concludes that the main reason for the spread of th concept of affordance is that researchers find it appealing that action possibilities offered by objects can be perceived by perceivers to achieve some objective. This according to her is one consequence of Gibson's theory of affordances but not its original meaning. However, this interpretation has generally influenced the research on affordances in IS Consider this definition by Strong et al. (2014: 69) “An affordance is the potential for behaviour associated wit achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relation between an artefact and a goal‐oriented acto or actors.” The area of agreement irrespective of ontological differences is that affordances are relational between an artefact and a user.

Situated in the IS context, we argue that every IT artefact offer action possibilities. It is hard to imagine an artefact that offers no possibility of action. Consequently, all artefacts have potential or latent affordances, as long as there is at least one user who has the ability and a goal to achieve by using that artefact. This latent affordance exists indepen dently, regardless of the user's perception. This position is captured in Figure 1. Following Gibson, affordances are neither the properties of the IT artefact nor the characteristics of an individual (Stoffregen, 2003). Furthermore, affordances are relative to not only the characteristics of individuals, such as their physical dimensions and abilities social needs, and personal intentions, but also the features of the environment (Chemero, 2003).

To summarise the discussion up to this point, affordances exist independent of a user's perception but remain latent and do not lead to actualisation without the interaction between the goal‐oriented actor with action capabilities and the IT artefact that affords possibilities of action (Michaels, 2003; Van Osch & Mendelson, 2011). How affordances, are perceived and actualised is also contextually, influenced by cultural, social, and technical factors The variability of the context can reveal different sets of affordances in a specific environment because affordances are always relative to an actor's goal (Volkoff & Strong, forthcoming). Let us illustrate this using an example from ou data. Urban doctors, who felt that they should repay society for providing them with medical education that hardly cost them anything, volunteered their services to NTMP on a pro bono basis. We termed this affordance “volunteerability.” Should the same doctor or similar ones not have had the goal of volunteering and wanted to earn money instead, this “volunteerability” affordance would not have emerged. Instead, another affordance such as “earnability” might have emerged. We discuss this distinction later in the paper.

If affordances exist independent of a user's perception, we then come to Gibson's query: “The central question for the theory of affordances is not whether they exist but whether information is available in ambient light for perceiving them” (Gibson (1979: 140). This is essentially an epistemological question. Affordances have meaning only in the historical and social narrative of the context in which the artefact and the actor reside. As Bloomfield Latham, and Vurdubakis (2010) argue, a chair does not afford “sitability” in a society where people sit cross‐legged on the floor, whereas a post box affords “communicability” only in societies where people communicate to other people by writing letters. A consequence of this view is that affordances can be of many types (Zeleke & Junshan, 2009). Although the dimensions along which these dichotomies vary, one important dimension stands out. Some affordances (eg, communicability) are functional and more basic because they are influenced heavily by the materia properties of the artefact. Hence, these affordances emerge through a discovery process. Other affordances are more related to the needs of the actors and their goals. These factors vary much more between individuals. Hence, such affordances arise in a specific socio‐cultural and historical context. The process of their emergence is more akin to constructing. The literature broadly terms such affordances as “social” (Bloomfield et al., 2010).

Affordances offer action possibilities and thus are just potentialities: To have some influence, the affordance need to be actualised (Bernhard et al., 2013). As Pentland, Recker, and Wyner (2015) demonstrated through their Network of Affordances (NOA), it is the actualised affordances that lead to outcomes. The question then is what are the conditions that facilitate this actualisation. This is the question that we seek to answer in this paper on the basis of the insights we gained from our case study of NTMP, which we describe next.

## 3 | THE CASE

## 3.1 | Background: The challenge of health care in Nepal

By all accounts, poor health care in underdeveloped countries is a developmental challenge (United Nations, n.d.). Add the context of a remote mountainous region, such as Nepal, where primary infrastructure such as roads and clean water are at a very basic level, and the complexity of the challenge multiplies. Around 68% of the land area is covere by mountains and hills. Of the population of approximately 30 million, 80% live in villages and depend on farming and remittances from expatriate workers mainly in the Middle East. Figure 2 shows the mountain regions where we conducted our research the district, Myagdi, and our main site, the village of Nangi.

At the heart of the problem of poor health care in Nepal's mountain regions is the fact that no doctors come to these remote regions. Consequently, the sick people either have to rely on local health workers known as “village health‐sisters” for basic care or go to shamans. We can only assume what might be the condition of a patient in emergency circumstances. Moreover, should the key income earner of a family fall ill, the whole family suffers Providing adequate health care to villagers living in these remote mountain villages is a huge challenge. It is shaped by dynamic and interdependent factors, namely, a scarcity of doctors who do not find it attractive to move to a region beset with poverty and poor infrastructure. There are no medical clinics in the region. The region is so remote that there was a time when villagers from one of our case sites, Nangi (see Figure 2), had to walk 8 hours to reach th medical centre in the nearest town. This remoteness has not changed appreciably in recent years. The project we studied, NTMP, was initiated to leverage IT to meet these challenges. The timeline of the NTMP is shown in Figure 3.

## 3.2 | The case narrative

As shown in Figure 3, the story of the NTMP began in 2007 when a US doctor visited Nangi village. Intent on improving the appalling health care situation, he decided to establish a clinic where he also intended to train the health workers who still serve as primary health care providers. The initiative received a boost when a chance encounter between a Nepalese doctor, Saroj Dhital, and the founder of NWNP, Mahabir Pun, led to a pilot telemedicine project. Dr Dhital was a social activist and expert surgeon affiliated to Kathmandu Model Hospital (KMH) and the Nepa Research and Education Network (NREN), whose main mission was to spread awareness of health care in Nepal's remote mountain villages. Dhital described his mission as “demystifying medicine.” He used to travel to these villages and organise free training camps especially for women with basic education. This goal of bringing improved medica services to the remote mountain region motivated him to contact Pun. NWNP was looking to add new applications to its platform, and Dhital was looking for an infrastructure framework to deliver health care to the mountainous villages to, at least partially, circumvent physical travel. The initiative materialised in 2008 when NWNP initiated a pilot project between the Nangi medical clinic and Om Hospital located in the tourist town of Pokhara. The pilot project tested telemedicine services in the form of audio‐videoconferencing between village health workers and doctors in Om Hospital. The pilot was a success and firmly established the foundation of a telemedicine network

Emboldened by the success of this pilot project, NWNP and KMH formally commenced NTMP in Nangi village in 2009. KMH became the hub for medical services, whereas NWNP provided infrastructural support, an arrangement that continues to this day. Gradually, NTMP was extended to include the regional hospital in Dolakha (another nearby remote mountain village) and brought its doctors into the project. NTMP further extended to Jomsom District Hospital, Lete Primary Health Care Center in the neighbouring Mustang district, and Aulo Sub‐Health Post in Myagdi. The core of NTMP centred on “bringing in” virtual medical expertise to these remote mountain villages. It did so through daily videoconferences between health workers in village medical clinics and doctors from urban hospitals. The outcome was essentially consultative health care based on remote diagnosis for villagers. Gradually, the reach of these consultations expanded to include specialist doctors from beyond Nepal. KMH also periodically contacts other international hospitals in USA, Indonesia, and Japan to consult on complex issues. At present, the consultancy services are provided with respect to surgery and general medicine. The next milestone came when health workers realised the need for more education to provide better health care services. NTMP started daily seminars in which doctors from KMH, Dolakha, and Om Hospital and health workers from Nangi took part. These seminars led to the formal commencement of tele‐education for village health workers who received training through regular classes on general medicine. They also learnt how to provide medical assistance in emergency circumstances, in addition to diagnosing and providing basic treatment to patients. This was a means to realise NTMP's strategy of producing trained and capable local health workers who would thus not be completely dependent on expert doctors from elsewhere for basic health care advice and assistance. At this juncture, many doctors from other urban hospitals also joined NTMP, some as consultants and others as facilitators in the training of health workers. They felt that they should give back something to the society that had provided them a medical education at a relatively low cost. The success of tele‐education classes led to advanced surgery classes for Nepalese interns. For example, KMH arranged a platform where interns can watch live surgery performed by doctors from other parts of the world (eg, Korea and Indonesia). The platform also provides interactive sessions between the interns and the experts. The government of Nepal has now listed telemedicine projects on its priority list of health care services

## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

Our research approach was interpretive, which is appropriate to understand how an information system influences a context and is in turn influenced by it (Walsham, 2006). The system in our case was NTMP, and the context was the region of mountain villages. Our study sites were Kathmandu, Hetauda, and Myagdi districts in Nepal. The mountain village of Nangi in the district of Myagdi was the primary site. We chose this site because it was here that NTMP as well as NWNP itself was initiated. We visited Hetauda once, Kathmandu 4 times, and Myagdi district 3 times. In each of these sites, we visited hospitals that were part of NTMP

## 4.1 | Data collection

Our primary method of data collection was interviews. Although some interviews were conducted in English, most of the interviews were in Nepali. In all, 30 interviews were conducted with key stakeholders and a further 30 interviews were conducted with members of the local communities (Table 1)

In addition, we also used complementary techniques such as observation, focus groups with 8 participants in Hetauda, group discussion, and document analysis. We conducted 60 semistructured interviews in Nangi (20), Tikot (10), Sikha (10), Hetauda (7), Poudar (6) and Dolakha villages (2), and in KMH and other participating hospitals in the project (5). Our interviewees were individuals directly or indirectly involved in the NTMP, and those who benefitted through it such as health workers, doctors and community people, personnel of the E‐Networking Research and Development (ENRD), and NWNP. The interviews were conducted in 3 phases between March 2010 and May 2011; each interview lasted from 30 to 90 minutes. In addition, we had regular interaction during the study period (through email and a number of Skype meetings) with the 2 key actors in the telemedicine initiative: Pun, the initiato of NWNP, and Dhital, the initiator of NTMP. All interviewees were told that we wanted to know what they thought o the telemedicine project and how IT is being used to bring health care to the villages. We confirmed participants consent on recording the interviews.

The interviews were semistructured. As is customary in the villages in Nepal, an interview with villagers would start with enguiring about their health and that of their families the weather and other such conversationa pleasantries. We would then proceed to the topic of the interview with the initial question, “What do you think of this centre where doctors from Kathmandu treat patients from your village?” How each interview proceeded henceforth depended on their responses in line with the semistructured approach. The interviews with the doctors were structurally similar but with less attention paid to introductory informalities and more direct references to NTMP.

Through these interviews, we tried to understand how technology helped improve health care in the remote mountain villages, and what role actors and social networks played in establishing the project and realising its goals All the interviews were tape‐recorded with the consent of the participants, and the recordings were later transcribed. The interviews were supplemented by note‐taking and direct observation of the medical clinics in the mountain villages. We observed daily telemedicine activities such as seminars, consultations and consultative diagnoses. For example, we sat through a seminar session between Dolakha, KMH and Nangi clinics (March 21. 2010). The doctors presented some cases of skin diseases through digital images. We also sat through a session where a health worker mediated between a patient at the clinic and a doctor at KMH The patient had mostly indirect contact with the doctor (whom he could see and hear on the video monitor), although he or she was sometimes asked questions directly by the doctor (March 27, 2010). These observations revealed some constraints of consultative diagnosis because of the slow transmission rate of the Internet.

We also conducted a focus group interview in a suburban hospital connected to the project, Hetauda District Hospital. The focus group was composed of 4 expert doctors, 1 telemedicine technician, 2 nurses, and other stakeholders such as a district development officer. In these discussions, doctors and other participants shared their reasons for participating in the project. In the focus group, one of the challenges revealed was lack of technological understanding among users in terms of, for instance, how technology could improve the quality of health care. We conducted several follow‐up interviews and consulted documents (mainly related to telemedicine activities) published by ENRD and NWNP. Regular email exchanges with Dhital and Pun and various stakeholders related to NWNP and NTMP also enriched the collected data. For example, we received regular updates on new developments and emerging challenges of NWNP and NTMP from Dhital and Pun. Over our data collection period we had approximately 30 email exchanges.

## 4.2 | Data analysis

As we engaged with the data, the relevance of affordances as an interpretive lens began to reveal itself. Consider this excerpt from an interview transcript: (some of the excerpts are translated to English from Nepalese)

The idea of Telemedicine got realized when I met Pun in a seminar in Kathmandu. He was looking fo someone who can provide medical services to the remote areas and I was looking for the infrastructure where I can use my expertise to serve underprivileged people. Finally, we agreed to start Telemedicine in Nangi and Tikot villages. (Dhital, 17/7/10)

Dhital was telling us about what he could do with the technology. It struck us that he was pointing towards th action possibilities of the technology, which is how we have earlier defined affordance. Other transcripts reinforced this insight. Subsequently, the lens of Affordance Theory guided our analysis. Another excerpt from an interview transcript points to a different aspect of affordance, which states that action is required to satisfy some need of a goal‐oriented actor. The perception and actualisation of the affordance of a technology allows the achievement of a goal with the technology as a means for reaching that goal:

There is no way the Nepali government is going to build hospitals and bring doctors in the rural areas like this in the near future because it costs so much money to do that. Therefore, it is difficult to get specialist doctors in remote places; in this situation, we are using this technology to access doctors from remote places. The people who have not seen doctors can see the doctors through this technology. (Pun, 21/ 03/2010)

Our philosophical premise is based on a realist ontology and an interpretive epistemology. We applied thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to analyse our interview transcripts because of its compatibility to our philosophica approach. The technique is also suitable to capture the theme that emerged from the data, and the conceptualisation of ToA in this context.

We strictly followed the phases of thematic analysis: familiarising ourselves with the data, generating initia codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, naming themes, and finally building the construct (Braun & Clarke, 2006). We started our analysis by looking at events such as videoconferencing, remote diagnosis, remote consultancy, and teleseminars between doctors and health workers. Then, we proceeded by identifying the affordances of NTMP that made the services available and actors who were involved in the process of making those events possible. Then, we started our interpretation of the transcribed interviews taken from the key actors identified in the analysis process The analysis went through several cycles of coding and categorising The categories that emerged in the first round of thematic analysis were different actors and their roles, various stakeholders (governmental and non‐governmental), the role of technology (eg, telemedicine), the role of NWNP (eg, as an infrastructure), the role of communities, the benefits of telemedicine, and the various educational and health care opportunities created by NTMP. In the second round of thematic analysis, we refined the categories into affordances that have been perceived and actualised to provide health care services in the mountain villages. In the final stage of the analysis, we abstracted the constructs and identified a ToA (see Appendix A for an example of our thematic analysis). To refine these categories, we made constant comparison between the data from various sources and the categories. Managing and organising the entirety of our qualitative data was facilitated by the software NVivo version 10.

## 5 | CASE ANALYSIS

Using the interpretive lens of affordances, our analysis revealed different affordances and the processes of how affordances travel from perceiving to actualising, and from actualising to emergence of new affordances. We describe these next. In each case, the essential characteristic of affordances is that they are relational in nature

## 5.1 | Actualisation of affordances

A temporal view of how affordances are actualised was suggested by Bernhard et al. (2013) and further elaborated by Pozzi et al. (2014). Our data revealed that such a process occurs. Affordances in a particular spatial and tempora context remain latent until perceived by an actor(s) who has a need (eg, a societal or developmental problem) in socio‐cultural context. Then, through the actions of this actor(s), they are actualised

The potential of NTMP to address the problem of poor and often non‐existent health care in the mountain regions was perceived by Mahabir Pun as early as 2007. He stated,

It was the idea of one US citizen (doctor), who used to visit every two years. He suggested providing basic medical training to the women [health workers] of these mountain villages, so that they can provide basic medical prescriptions to the patients in the mountain villages. The objective was to empower them. Therefore, we along with Om Hospital started to build the telemedicine project. (Pun, 21/03/2010)

Simply recognising affordances was not enough; Pun could not do it on his own. Help arrived when he met Dhita at a seminar in Kathmandu in 2008. Dhital was looking for ways to improve health care in the mountain regions of Nepal. He was aware of the affordance of IT for health care, specifically telemedicine, because a visiting US doctor had told him about it. He was at that time looking for ways to reduce his physical trips to these villages by using technology. The meeting with Pun brought the 2 sides of the equation together. Dhital recalls the meeting,

It is the effort of Mahabir to connect the whole of Nepal through wireless technology. There are 3‐4 othe institutions working along with him; we are one of them to provide health related services. There is anothe organization called NREN (Nepal Research and Education Network); in this institute, there are 3–4 friends including Mahabir and I. This organization is also helping us. Besides that there are other private companies who are working with us. They are also motivated to make this telemedicine project successful. (Dhital, 21/ 03/10)

By now, affordances had been perceived, and actors had played vital roles. It now remained to actualise this affordance. Here, Dhital took over the role of the pivotal actor. Providing improved health care requires the contributions of a variety of actors, chiefly doctors and local health workers as well as NWNP, the Internet platform provider. That no doctors would come to the villages was the root cause of poor health care. However, through the infrastructure platform provided by NWNP, they could be brought together virtually. We term this affordance virtual co‐localisability. Pun and Dhital perceived this affordance of NTMP but needed conditions that could facilitate the actualisation of this affordance. Our data revealed that the facilitating conditions were (1) the extensive socia networks of Pun and Dhital and (2) the presence of social groups in the villages, Pun's network consisted of all those who had helped him build and extend NWNP. Dhital's network was the Nepalese medical community of which he was a central figure mainly due to his pioneering work in improving health care in remote mountain villages. The socia groups in the village played vital roles. Let us take the case of the local health workers first. It turned out that all of them belonged to a local social organisation called “Aama Samoh” (Mothers' Society). Dhital was highly respected by this society because of his ongoing work in bringing health care to the villages. He approached the society and successfully recruited the members who were local health workers into NTMP, Building relations with other members of the community was also vital; they needed to trust NTMP to bring their sick children and adult relatives to these health centres, instead of going to a shaman. A doctor from Dolakha district hospital emphasised the trust factor:

Particularly in the villages, people are afraid of diseases. When they see a doctor in front of the camera prescribing them medicines, they feel confident, they feel psychologically confident. (Dr Vaidhya, 21/02/ 2011)

Doctors were also enrolled through Dhital's social network within the Nepalese medical community. Equall important, he enrolled hospitals. After all, telemedicine requires videoconferencing facilities to be physically located somewhere. Dhital's own KMH was the first location that provided telemedicine services. Gradually, Om Hospita and other hospitals were enrolled. Dhital's social networks extended beyond Nepal; he also had ties with doctors from other countries (eg, USA, Japan, India, and South Korea). A statement from the director of ENRD corroborates this:

It's not just one hospital; we are building a network of all the hospitals in the Kathmandu area, and the other thing is if we are in an emergency and if our doctors could not solve it, then we have a connection with a foreign hospital and doctors such as at the University of New Mexico. We can directly talk to the University of New Mexico. This link is not only with the US, it can be extended to Japan, Korea, and the Ministry of Health has connected to 5 or 6 places in India. Therefore, there are lots of opportunities to extend this [telemedicine] service. (Rajan Sharma, 26/03/2010)

We now have a clearer picture of how the latent affordance of virtual co‐localisability was perceived by goal‐oriented actors who used facilitating conditions to actualise affordances. The facilitating conditions were the actors strong social networks and the existing social groups in the villages. The actualised form of virtua co‐localisability was the daily videoconferencing between urban doctors and the village health workers, which helped achieve the goal of bringing improved health care to the remote mountain regions of Nepal. The actualisation of affordances is shown in Figure 4.

The involvement of urban doctors and doctors from foreign countries led Dhital to perceive another affordance. He told us about his dream of enhancing the medical services to include remote surgery. Virtua co‐localisability could enable surgeons from remote locations to bring in their surgical skills to operate on patients in the villages. They could not bring their hands. “We don't have robots who can do the surgery,” Dhital told us. We realised that an affordance, even after it was perceived, could not be actualised because facilitation condition (access to robots) were not present.

## 5.3 | Emergence of new affordances

The actualisation of an affordance can be a pathway to the emergence of new affordances (Pozzi et al., 2014; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). NTMP got underway in 2008. Dhital noted that participants in the daily videoconferences ofte lingered after the end of the diagnostics phase: The doctors explained some aspect of the diagnosis to the local health workers who in turn often asked questions to clarify some of the procedures and concepts that arose during the diagnosis and consultation. The health workers themselves expressed a need for education. As one of the health workers from Tikot told us:

… after attending training such as dental or other health related issues, Aama Samoh or other health‐sisters can keep a small number of medicines and can treat people in the village. For example there is a health‐post in Ghatpo [another neighbouring village to Tikot], but it is one hour walking distance. If people fall sick they cannot walk one hour to take medicine from there. Therefore, it is very effective for the Ghatpo people, if [NTMP] takes the initiative to also provide training to health‐sisters in that village. (Health worker, Ghatpo, 17/03/2011)

This opinion was not unique. Almost all the health workers stated that their medical knowledge had improved since the telemedicine project began. However, they felt that they were still lagging behind according to internationa standards, and the first thing they needed was to be educated. One of the health workers stated

Education can help us to better understand about health care problems. Education also helps us to develop our confidence. The villagers feel happy when they get treatment in the village from a knowledgeable health worker. (Health Worker 1, Nangi, 23/03/10)

The training started as daily seminars that were added to the daily videoconferencing and were well received. As another health worker added,

We realised that to help the patient, first we need to be more active and educated; therefore we want to get more healthcare training. We think that the internet [Telemedicine in this context] has played a vital role in the development of our confidence through daily seminars and training. (Health Worker 2, Nangi, 23/03/10)

We see that the actualisation of the cluster of affordances (virtual co‐localisability and volunteerability) in the form of consultative diagnosis and access to expert doctors led to a concrete outcome of tele‐education through the addition of daily seminars. It was at this point that Dhital and the doctors from other countries who regularly took part in the daily seminars saw the action possibility that tele‐education offered. If NTMP can be used to train the village health workers, it can also be used to train Nepalese doctors. Dhital told us, “tele‐education has opened new learning opportunities for Nepalese doctors.” A new affordance was thus discovered. We term this educability. The concrete outcome was in the form of the advanced surgery classes for Nepalese interns that we mentioned earlier. This happens in 2 ways. The first i passive, where the interns watch surgical operations being conducted in real time in countries such as Korea and Indonesia. The second form is interactive sessions between the interns and expert doctors in these countries. In contrast to the temporal view where latent affordances are first perceived and then actualised, here, an actualisatio of a previous affordance led to the perception and thus emergence of a new affordance.

## 5.4 | Summary of findings

As depicted in Figure 7, the spread of NTMP occurred through a combination of different affordances (virtua co‐localisability, volunteerability, and educability). There were goal‐oriented actors who understood the complex social problems and acted to solve the problems. For example, had there not been a pressing need to improve the quality of health care and had social activist Pun not been aware about the situation, the affordances of telemed icine might not have been discovered, at least in this context. We found that even when goal‐oriented actors perceive the affordances, facilitating conditions are required to actualise the perceived affordances. We saw it in the nonactualisation of remote surgery. The facilitating condition was the social networks of the actors and the presence of social groups in the villages. Our findings point out that affordances can be first perceived and then actualised. However, intriguingly, our findings also show that the actualisation of one affordance can lead to an outcome through which a new affordance can be perceived as was the case with educability.

## 6 | INDUCING A TRAJECTORY OF AFFORDANCES

On the basis of the insights from our findings, we propose that the emergence and actualisation of affordances are interdependent and connected processes. To describe these relations, we put forward a construct, which we term Trajectory of Affordances (TOA) (Figure 8). This construct is the key finding that emerged from insights that we gained from the case of NTMP.

The trajectory starts with goal‐oriented actors with action capabilities perceiving affordances. These affordances are relational between the artefact and the actors. These affordances can be latent or perceptible in an environment, but need some actor(s) to perceive them. The actor perceives the affordance(s) in a specific socio‐cultural an historical context and appropriates the affordance (if the material properties allow) to achieve an intended goal. To actualise the affordance(s), there must be facilitating conditions that can be a pool of resources (such as financial, social, and political). This is shown as path 2 in Figure 8. Perceived affordances cannot be actualised if facilitating conditions do not exist. This is indicated by path 2. An actor or multiple actors may perceive one or more affordances in different contexts. Affordances that are actualised often form a cluster which together lea to some effect(s). This is shown by path 3. The actualisation of single affordances (through path 4) or a cluster of affordances (through path 5) may generate new affordances. These new affordances may be perceived after an action or goal has been achieved

## 7 | DISCUSSION

We set out to examine a telemedicine project that to a great extent has succeeded in bringing improved health care to a remote mountainous region in a developing country. Since our data collection, NTMP has expanded its aspirations, some of which have been achieved. Through funding from an international NGO, it has appointed 2 interns in remote hospitals and 1 general practitioner. These doctors use telemedicine for direct seminars, online consultancy, and to provide advanced training to health workers in these remote villages. Along with Center for ICT4D, NTMP has established a research centre called the NREN (https://www.nren.net.np/). Telemedicine is a priority area. As the name suggests, the purpose of the research centre is to focus on ICT4D research and network building with experts from other countries. Among its most ambitious aspirations is using drones for delivering medicine to the remote mountain areas.

In analysing our data, we discovered that Affordance Theory was an appropriate lens to interpret the data. By virtue of this lens, we identified processes through which affordances were actualised, cluster together to form an ensemble, and new affordances were revealed from the actualisation of a precursor affordance. On the basis of the insights we gained from our findings, we proposed the concept of the ToA, which is our main contribution to the current discourse on the conceptualisation and operationalisation of affordances in IS (see Pozzi et al., 2014 Lanamäki, Thapa, & Stendal, 2016; Volkoff & Strong, forthcoming, for recent reflections on affordances in IS).

Through ToA, we show how the affordances of IT travel along a trajectory through an interrelated web. We clearly distinguish between affordances (eg, virtual co‐localisability) and their actualisation (daily videoconferences between remote doctors and local village health workers) resulting in an outcome (improved health care services). This resonates with Strong et al. (2014) who emphasise that an affordance should be distinguished from its actualisation. That affordances are related through their actualised form is echoed elsewhere, notably in the NOA model developed by Pentland et al. (2015). The nodes in NOA consist of actualised affordances. Trajectory of Affordances adds to this understanding by specifying the process and conditions through which affordances are perceived leading t actualisation of affordances.

ToA provides a new element to augment the traditional temporal view that affordances are first perceived and then actualised (Bernhard et al., 2013; Pozzi et al., 2014). This view has been challenged by Volkoff and Strong (forthcoming) who question whether affordances have to be perceived to be actualised. They give the exampl of Facebook users posting information about themselves to “communicate” to their friends, not realising that at the same time, they are also “broadcasting” to the whole Facebook community. This “broadcastability” (our term) affordance was perceived after it was actualised inadvertently. Our findings also provide evidence of this “reverse perception” where the affordance of “educability” was perceived after training of health workers was added to the daily videoconferencing schedule. Educability was actualised through surgery classes for Nepalese interns. This supports the view that sometimes affordances may emerge in practice (Zheng & Yu, 2016), often inadvertently. This phenomenon is termed unintentional actualisation by Volkoff and Strong (forthcoming).

Our paper adds further insights to temporal models of actualisation of affordance (eg. Pozzi et al., 2014 Strong et al, 2014: Bernhard et al., 2013), These models do not make explicit the role of actors, A framework where actors occupy an explicit and central role was proposed by Seidel et al. (2013). Their actors were managers and users within an organisation. However, they did not describe specific actualisation processes nor did they elaborate on the facilitating conditions that enabled the actors to actualise the perceived affordances or resources used by them. This dimension is also missing in NOA (Pentland et al., 2015). The influencing role of facilitating conditions was implicitly discussed by Strong et al. (2014) in their mid‐range theory of affordance actualisation.

Although they did not specifically use the term “facilitating conditions,” they captured it via an all‐encompassing term, “organizational context” (p. 68).

Trajectory of Affordances (ToA) explicitly integrates these concepts. In examining how IT can help solve developmental challenges, we extended these frameworks to the societal level and explicitly described the role of social actors in the diffusion of affordances and the role of social networks in actualising affordances. Trajectory of Affordances (ToA) reveals the interdependent, mutually influencing roles of goal‐oriented actors and facilitating conditions as mechanisms in the affordance actualisation process.

Our paper adds to understandings of the role played by actors' goals vis‐à‐vis how affordances are identified Consider “volunteerability.” The specific properties of the artefact are the remote interaction features such as video and audio connections. The specific capability of the actors (urban doctors) was their medical skills and their time Their goal was to give something back to the society that had educated them at a low cost. Hence, they coul volunteer their pro bono services. Now, another urban doctor with the same capabilities may perceive an opportunity to provide the same medical services, but charge for it. The goal is to earn money. The affordance arising from the same relationality is no longer “volunteerability” but something like “earnability.” We saw this perception earlier i the paper, in the quote from a doctor who mentioned that doctors could be “partly paid.”

Multiple actors bring multiple goals. As Strong et al. (2014) ask, whose goals are met through actualisation of affordances of the same ICT? They answered this question by not focusing on the overall goal of an initiative, but focusing on the immediate concrete actions taken. We took the same focus but extended it by showing how these multiple goals form an ensemble and together reach the ultimate goal. One may ask what is and whose is the ultimate goal. In our case, the ultimate goal was improved health care, and it was the goal of the founders of NTMP, Dhital and Pun. The 3 affordances we unearthed in our case resulted from different goals of different actors. Through ToA, they came together to achieve the goal of bringing improved health care services to remote villages. This is a plausible response to addressing Volkoff and Strong's (forthcoming) call to consider “bundles of affordances” and how they interact

This raises the issue of whether the 3 affordances we identified are of the same “level,” that is, do they have the same ontology? Consider “virtual co‐localisability.” This can be termed as a “functional affordance” since it is tied closely to the material properties of NWNP (eg, video communication, store, and forward technology). Dhital and Pun's goal of bringing improved health care (their term was “quality health care”) was instrumental in perceiving the affordance. Contrast this with “volunteerability.” Here, the materiality of NTMP, while essential, was not the prime factor; rather, it was the urban doctors' goal of “giving back to society” that led to perception of the affordance. The ontology of “virtual co‐localisability” and “volunteerability” appears to be the same. However, a deeper epistemic exploration reveals some insights. Volunteerability arose from the doctors' desire to pay something back to society because they had paid very little for their medical education. This social and historical context is unique in countries where education is either free or inexpensive. One can question whether in another country where medical educatio is expensive, would this feeling of “paying back to society” emerge? It is a striking illustration of Bloomfield et al. (2010) thesis about the socio‐cultural and historical underpinnings of the meaning of affordances. Moreover, to use the vocabulary of those authors, functional affordances reveal themselves through a “discovery” process, whereas socialised affordances emerge through a “construction” or “generation” process.

In laying out the fundamental challenges to using Affordance Theory in IS research, Volkoff and Strong (forthcoming) observe that IS scholars, started adapting Gibson's theory before some of the fundamental debates about the concept had been resolved in the originating field of ecological psychology. Consequently, they imported the same debates into the IS literature. Information Systems scholars have not subsequently revisited ecologica psychology, where many of the fundamental issues had been resolved. A case in point is the ontology of affordances: Do they exist independently, or, do they emerge? By revisiting ecological psychology, we showed that this question is moot. As Michaels (2003) emphasized, they solved the issue several years ago, whereas we in IS keep on debating. The ontology that we proffer in Figure 1 is an articulation of a possible resolution of this debate in IS There are compelling reasons, however, to have our own debates regarding affordances. We will elaborate further in the next section.

## 8 | FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Our findings have generated directions for future research. We have clustered two of the affordances we identified namely, “volunteerability” and “virtual co‐localisability” together into an ensemble. However, an epistemological issue remains. Is “volunteerability” derived from “virtual co‐localisability”? It is possible also to conceive generation/emergence/discovery of volunteerability from the actualisation of “virtual co‐localisability.” The timeline of our case indicates that doctors started offering free medical services after the pilot was implemented which indicates that “virtual co‐localisability” had already been actualised in the form of daily conferences. Seen in that light, a plausible interpretation is that “volunteerability” emerged from the actualisation of “virtual co‐localisability.” Although this indicates a dependency among affordances (Strong et al., 2014), it also hints at a possible hierarchy of affordances, which is an important avenue for future research.

Other avenues for further research emerge from our paper. For example, we identified the importance of actors in identifying and actualising the affordances. However, the process of identifying relevant actors, building networks and actualising affordances needs to be further explored. An appropriate theoretical premise for this could be Actor‐Network Theory (Latour, 2005). Finally, though, perceived affordances have to be actualised if the technology in question were to solve a complex social problem. Here, we come back to the role of practice and posit that the ti that binds perception to actualisation is action. More specifically, in the context of technology meeting complex social problems, the collective action is required. We found rich evidence about the enabling role of actors' social network in fostering collective action. How to enable collective action is an important avenue for future research. For this purpose, social capital (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Huysman & Wulf, 2004; Ostrom, 2000; Thapa, Sein, & Sæbø, 2012) could be a theoretical lens to understand the process of actualising affordances.

There is also a need to examine further the forms and roles of facilitating conditions. Our findings revealed socia networks as the facilitating conditions that helped actualise the affordances of NTMP. We also found how in th absence of technological resources such as robots, remote surgery could not be achieved although this affordanc was perceived by Dhital. Facilitating conditions can also be availability of technical resources and skills.

In our conceptualisation of affordances, we employed a realist ontology and an interpretive epistemology. The discerning reader will immediately recognise the philosophical remise of critical realism in our stance. Examining affordances through a critical realism lens is another important direction for research; indeed, this call has also bee made by Volkoff and Strong (forthcoming).

We now come to the need for further debates in IS on affordances. Notwithstanding the resolution of the debate on the ontology of affordances in ecological psychology, we have issues that are specific to the IS field that sustain ou debates. We must, however, first specify the dimensions or characteristics of IS that make it difficult to directl transfer the concept of affordances from ecological psychology. The unique characteristic that distinguishes IS from ecological psychology is that IS deals with what Simon (1996) calls “the science of the artificial.” It is in the design of systems that IS differs from the study of animals. The role of design is a vital part of the discourse on affordanc in IS and is worthy of future research. We reflect on this in the next section.

## 9 | CONCLUDING REFLECTIONS

In this paper, we told the story of NTMP that was developed on the infrastructure platform provided by NWNP. The material properties of NTMP were specific aspects of NWNP. Seen in this light, NTMP is the actualisation of an affordance of NWNP. There was a time when NWNP did not exist and had it not existed, NTMP would not hav materialised. NWNP is a designed artefact and thus, the discussion moves to the realm of the artificial

We submit that the discussion on affordances in IS is incomplete without a designer (or a group that formed a “design team”). In designing, the designers inscribed some form of affordance in the artefact with some user(s) in mind We know that the origin of NWNP lay in Pun's need to check his email without trekking several hours to the nearest

Internet access point (Thapa et al., 2012). We can infer that at its inception, NWNP afforded “communicability.” This is a functional affordance. We see that this functional affordance led to other affordances culminating in NTMP, which in turn was the starting point of a trajectory that we have attempted to capture as ToA in this paper. Technica properties are built into an IT artefact by the designer with a virtual user in mind, and in doing so, the artefact is embedded with properties that can afford action possibilities. These affordances can be obvious (perceptible) or hidden (latent) (Gaver, 1991). These hidden affordances need to be perceived by an actor (eg, a user) who has som pressing problem to solve. How an IT artefact is used and appropriated depends on what the actor sees as its action possibility and how she acts on it according to the facilitating conditions that exist for her. Equally important is the socio‐cultural and historical context surrounding the actor that plays a vital role on which affordances are “constructed." The embedded affordances only, provide the springboard. So perhaps the debate in IS about the ontology of affordances, ie, whether affordances are embedded or emerge, should be more appropriately stated as which affordances are embedded by design and which affordances emerge in practice?
