# Identifying the ideal fit between mobile work and mobile work support<sup>§</sup>

Yufei Yuan \*, Norm Archer, Catherine E. Connelly, Wuping Zheng

DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, 1280 Main St. W., Ontario, Canada L8S 4M4

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 3 November 2008 Received in revised form 21 August 2009 Accepted 3 December 2009 Available online 4 January 2010

Keywords: IT diffusion and adoption User acceptance of IT Mobile computing Mobile work support Task-technology fit theory

## A B S T R A C T

The rapid growth in the use of wireless communication and portable devices has created a potential for a variety of mobile work support. However, few studies have explored the nature of mobile work, examined the needs for its support, and identified the appropriate support for various kinds of work in mobile contexts. In our study, a mobile task model was proposed and tested, including three dimensions: mobility, location dependency, and time criticality. Four context-related mobile work support functions were examined: location tracking, navigation, notification, and online job dispatching. In the light of task-technology fit theory and attitude/behavioral theory, we proposed a research model for the fit between task characteristics and the four mobile work support functions. The ideal fit was analyzed on the basis of empirical data collected from a sample of mobile knowledge and field workers. Our study resulted in a theoretical contribution to task/technology fit theory in the context of mobile work support, and suggested guidelines for the development and use of mobile work support systems.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid proliferation of wireless communication and portable devices has created a potential for mobile work support. Here, we refer to mobile work as requiring physical movement from one place to another and/or temporary activity outside a fixed workplace. Mobile workers work away from their normal home base for more than 20% of their work time [7]. The implementation of mobile work support can provide a competitive advantage and improve work productivity for business. However, not all mobile tasks require or can benefit from the use of mobile work support, nor do all types of mobile work support guarantee that they will be of value. The utilization of mobile technologies may be problematic, because they may be used in unexpected ways. As an example, mobile devices were introduced to provide communication and collaboration support between foremen and field workers at a construction site, but the use of mobile devices actually did not facilitate work collaboration. Middleton and Cukier [15] investigated functional and dysfunctional mobile e-mail usage patterns and found that mobile devices are highly functional and allow workers to be efficient, to multitask without disruption to others, and to respond immediately to messages, as well as offering them the freedom to work anywhere. However, there are also dysfunctional patterns that are dangerous, distracting, anti-social and that infringe on work-life boundaries. Prasopoulou et al. [16] also found that, by answering work-related phone calls during their private time, mobile workers started to have a sense of long working hours even when they were away from the office; they allowed work issues to invade their private time, enabling the institutionalization of an ‘anytime, anywhere’ availability detrimental to the preservation of existing temporal boundaries, in favour of work.

Therefore, identifying the appropriate mobile technologies for various kinds of mobile work is critical for achieving value from mobile work support applications.

Attitude/behavior theory has been widely used as a theoretical background in studying the use of mobile applications (e.g. [10,11,20,21]). According to attitude/behavior models, characteristics of IT lead to user attitudes about the system; these, along with social norms and other situational factors, lead to system use. Among the attitude/behavior models, the TAM has been widely used, but it does not deal with task focus [12].

As an extension to TAM, Fang et al. [6] categorized the tasks performed on wireless handheld devices into three categories: general, gaming, and transaction tasks. They investigate how the type moderated the effect of four possible determinants (PU, perceived ease of use, perceived playfulness, and perceived security) of user intentions to perform the tasks.

Task-technology fit (TTF) theory argues that, for an IS to have a positive impact on performance, the technology must be a good fit with the task it supports. This theory provides a conceptual basis for explaining how the nature of the tasks impacts individual and organizational performance.

Recently, TTF theory was applied to the field of mobile technology adoption. Junglas and Watson conducted an experiment to identify the fit between the time dependence/location dependence and ubiquity/uniqueness characteristics of mobile technology. Their study provided a task model in a mobile context and examined the TTF fit at the general mobile technology characteristics (ubiquity and uniqueness) level, but not at the mobile application function level and not for mobile work support.

In contrast, Gebauer and Shaw’s [8] and Lee et al.’s [13] studies examined TTF at the application function level. However, in each case no theoretical and systematic task model was developed. Gebauer and Shaw conducted a case study on a procurement system; they found that users value two aspects most: notification and support for simple activities, as opposed to handling complex processes online. Lee et al. studied the fit between PDA mobile technology and insurance tasks. They found that PDA technology provided different degrees of assistance to different types of insurance tasks. Among these PDA mobile technology provided the highest level of assistance in post-contract customer services, followed, at lower levels, by recruiting new insurance contracts, and tax and providing legal information services.

Our study focused on three main research questions:

(1) How can the nature of mobile tasks be modeled?

(2) What are the typical mobile work support functions? and (3) What is the best fit between mobile tasks and mobile work support functions?

According to the TTF model, the value of mobile work support is contingent on the fit between mobile work and mobile work support. In order to identify the best fit, we need to understand the nature of mobile work and the nature of typical mobile work support functions.

## 2. Research model

We developed a research model that is intended to identify the fit between mobile tasks and mobile work support functions by drawing on TTF model and attitude/behavior theory. The mobile task was analyzed along three dimensions: mobility, location dependency, and time criticality. Its four unique mobile work support functions were: mobile notification, location tracking, navigation, and real-time mobile job dispatching. The research model is shown in Fig. 1. Note that, PU is ‘‘Perceived Usefulness’’ and IU is ‘‘Intention to Use’’.

## 2.1. Mobile task model

A task is an activity that workers perform in order to accomplish a goal [18]. Tasks are usually analyzed along the dimensions of task complexity i.e., non-routinization and non-repetitiveness of the task performed and task interdependence: i.e., the extent to which workers depend upon each other to accomplish their tasks.

However, for mobile work, these alone cannot capture all the task characteristics of mobile work; the context is very important, and is the key characteristic that distinguishes mobile from stationary work. Activities are inherently situated in a particular context that frames and is framed by performing the activities recursively. Time and location are the fundamental elements for context. Balasubramanian et al. [1] suggested that we can describe a specific activity in terms of the degree to which it is constrained (or flexible) spatially and temporally. Junglas and Watson proposed that the dimensions of time- and location dependence were needed to describe mobile tasks. Gebauer et al. [9] introduced the concept of time criticality. We used mobility and location dependency to describe the location dimensions and time criticality to describe the time dimension of mobile tasks.

Mobility is used here to distinguish mobile from stationary workers. It has been measured by the frequency that a person is away from his or her standard office, though some have considered it as the geographic range of location. In our study, we focused on the nature of the work, and not on the person. Thus, driving a bus has much less location variety than driving a taxi, although both drivers spend most of their time on the road. Similarly, a hospital nurse’s work has less location variety than a home visiting nurse.

Location dependency also describes the extent to which dynamic location-related information is required to perform the task. Location-related information, such as the route to a destination and the traffic conditions is important for some work, such as driving an ambulance. Similarly, a consultant may receive a request for advice when traveling. Thus the consulting service can be delivered through a mobile device. This consulting task is thus not location dependent until it is necessary to meet the customer face-to-face at a particular location.

Time is an essential feature of social and organizational life, and is consumed by activities. We used time criticality to capture the temporal nature of mobile tasks; it refers to the importance of time in task performance and can be viewed from two perspectives: time flexibility and urgency. The first defines to what extent the mobile tasks must be performed on time, while the second pertains to the degree of rigidity in time structuring and task completion [2]. The degree of urgency refers to the importance with which a task needs to be performed promptly.

## 2.2. Mobile work support functions

Several classifications of mobile business applications have been suggested. For example, Liang and Wei [14] classified mobile applications into six categories: Time-Critical Services, Location-Aware and Location-Sensitive Services, Identity-Enacted Services, Ubiquitous Communications and Content Delivery Services, Business Process Streamlining, and Mobile Offices. Balasubramanian et al. proposed that mobile applications be categorized along three dimensions: the extent to which the application is locationsensitive, the extent to which the application is time critical, and the extent to which the application is controlled by the information receiver or the provider. Other studies classified some mobile application functionalities, but their mobile applications were for general use and not for mobile work.

Some technical support functions are generally available for stationary workers. With the advances of mobile communication and mobile devices, they also became available to mobile workers, without any restriction due to fixed-line connections. Such functions include:

\- Mobile communications: the general communication functions of voice and text messaging;

\- Mobile information searching: using mobile devices through wireless connection to the Internet or a company’s ISs;

\- Mobile transaction processing: well structured activities in a series of actions that have clear inputs and outputs; capturing transaction data and wireless connections for real time transaction processing, such as order placement and inventory management, and

\- Mobile office functionality: including general office functions supported by mobile devices, such as word processing, spreadsheet, presentation software, and database applications, as well as calendar, address book, and calculator.

In our study, we focused on functions that are significantly related to the location and time dimensions of mobile work. There are four: mobile notification, location tracking, navigation, and real-time mobile job dispatching.

Notification involves brief messages about scheduled or urgent events that are delivered to a targeted audience. Some key uses are: alerting others to change; an action taken; new data entries; responses; change to some object in which the user is interested; meeting delays or cancellations; severe weather warnings; traffic accidents; and flight delays or cancellations. Notification can be automatically or manually generated and distributed through voice mails, short message service (SMS), etc. Commercial notification services are, of course, available; e.g., Wireless Notification Solutions provided by Visiplex (http://www.alertwave.com/), and Urgent Alert by VoiceShot (http://www.voiceshot.com/).

Location tracking is the ability to identify and locate a moving target [22]. Using object identification and location identification technology, tracking can be used to locate a cell phone user and an RFID or GPS receiver. The E911 emergency response system locates an emergency cell phone caller so that police or fire trucks can be dispatched quickly to aid a victim.

Navigation provides travel guidance, including a dynamically displayed road map and the optimal route to the targeted destination, possibly including important locations enroute, such as restaurants, hotels, or gas stations. GPS-based navigation systems can be found in vehicles, farming, and mining equipment, etc.

Real-time mobile job dispatching: Job scheduling typically involves the assignment of scarce resources to completing tasks to optimize some aspect of system performance, in real-time mobile job dispatching, the job is dispatched when requirements are generated or changed. For example, mobile workforce support systems are provided by Mobile Computing Corporation (http:// www.mobilecom.com/). Taxi dispatching also makes real-time job assignments from call centers, based on the time and the location of both taxis and passengers.

## 2.3. The concept of fit and its measures

Fit is central to contingency theory. It can be conceptualized and measured in a number of different ways, but it is important to be precise in specifying what kind of fit is proposed and how it is to be measured [19]. There are three main operational concepts:

Congruence, measured by a combination of the levels of contingency and structure that produce higher performance.

Interaction, measured by a multiplicative interaction term i.e., the contingency variable multiplied by the organizational structure variable.

Absolute difference, measured as the absolute difference between the values of technology and task characteristic variables.

There are problems in operationalizing fit as an interaction term [5] because it does not capture the relationship between fit and performance, and it assumes that fit is a straight line relationship. Fit as absolute difference has the same problems. Thus we used the concept of fit as congruence, defined as the congruence between task characteristics and the functionality of the technology that improves work performance.

There are two methods for measuring fit as congruence: subjective and objective. Taking a subjective stance means measuring fit from a user’s perspective. The objective stance is determined by the system builders. The subjective method can be further split into two types: facets-of-fit and predicted-outcome [17]. Facets-of-fit involves identifying important facets of the task requirements and assessing whether the tool meets each of them. The predicted-outcome method predicts the outcomes of tool use and whether they are as desired. We used predicted-outcomes to measure fit. This measurement is straightforward and the data are easy to collect; users can respond whether they have used and like to use each support function.

Specifically, we used perceived usefulness (PU) to measure fit. We note that this is seen as being identical to the concept of TTF. Dishaw and Strong contended that Goodhue’s TTF instrument (i.e., conceptualization of fit as facets-of-fit) and fitness for use (i.e., conceptualization of fit as predicted-outcomes) are just differently derived operationalizations of a single fit construct.

## 2.4. Hypothesis development

## 2.4.1. Support for mobility

For mobile workers, there are inherent uncertainties about place thatimpacttheirworkchoiceandthekindofactivitiestheypursue,so managingthe uncertaintyofplaceisofparticular concern[4]. When a worker has to work in a variety of locations, it may be necessary to deal with unexpected events, such as bad weather, traffic jams, flight delays, appointment changes, and cancellations. Notification, therefore, will be useful for mobile workers to make them aware of and able to deal with these issues. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1-1. The degree of mobility positively affects the PU (Perceived Usefulness) of mobile notification.

When workers are assigned to work at different but not fixed places, mobile job dispatch could be useful in making optimal arrangements or changing job assignments due to changing situations. Thus, we hypothesized:

H1-2. The degree of mobility positively affects the PU of mobile job dispatch.

## 2.4.2. Support for location dependency

To perform location dependent jobs, mobile workers often need to know not only their own location but also a customer’s location. For instance, police must know a victim’s or criminal’s location. It is also more efficient for a forklift operator to receive location information of items to be picked up in a warehouse. When mobile tasks are location-sensitive, dynamic location-related information is critical. Junglas and Watson pointed out that location dependence and uniqueness (the position of a mobile user) should be an ideal fit. Thus, we hypothesized:

H2-1. The degree of location dependency positively affects the PU of location tracking.

Mobile workers often need to travel. Searching for routes to their destinations takes time, so people often resort to mobile communications to find their way. However, interacting with other people to find a place takes time and, in most cases, it is not easy to find people who have the required information. A more efficient way would be to use GPS navigation systems to receive travel guidance. This leads to the hypothesis:

H2-2. The degree of location dependency positively affects the PU of navigation.

When jobs are location dependent, it is better to send mobile workers to perform those that are at a convenient time and location, such as visit a customer along the way or pick up a nearby passenger. Taxi or fleet dispatching is a good example of improving productivity and enhancing customer service in this manner. This leads to the hypothesis:

H2-3. The degree of location dependency positively affects the PU of real-time mobile job dispatch.

## 2.4.3. Support for time criticality

Mobile notification is usually used when an urgent task needs to be done immediately, or when unexpected events occur, or to remind someone of a task that must be performed immediately. If the degree of time criticality is high, then mobile notification is critical, so that mobile workers may respond quickly. Gebauer and Shaw’s study found that mobile notification could help address emergency situations. Junglas and Watson’s study found that time dependence and ubiquity was an ideal fit. These arguments lead to the hypothesis:

H3-1. The degree of time criticality positively affects the PU of notification.

It is especially critical for dispatchers to know the location of mobile workers, so that they can assign the nearest ones to perform work quickly. It is therefore important to track the locations of mobile workers through tracking systems. Evidence of a relationship between time criticality and location tracking was shown in Basole and Chao’s [3] study of M-DSS (Mobile Decision Support Systems). They contended that information timeliness was an important criterion to their PU and suggested that timeliness was positively related to their PU. These lead to the hypothesis:

H3-2. The degree of time criticality positively affects the PU of location tracking.

Time considerations are important in job scheduling and dispatching, and mobile workers must perform mobile tasks when required. Real-time mobile job dispatching is usually used to deal with situations where the occurrence of mobile tasks cannot be predicted, such as taxi services and vehicle towing. In these situations, the employment of real-time mobile job dispatching would improve productivity. Thus:

H3-3. The degree of time criticality positively affects the PU of real-time mobile job dispatching.

## 3. Instrument development and validation

Instrument development and validation is a prior and primary process in confirmatory empirical research. Instrument develop ment in our study followed three steps:

(1) Specify the domain of the constructs and generate the instrument items. Based on the literature, the exact conceptual definitions of the constructs were specified. New items were then developed to measure them.

(2) Pilot test and technical validation. The objectives were to test the instruments for clarity and completion time and to revise the questionnaire as required, leading to an instrument that could be formally validated. Content validity and factor analysis were then conducted.

(3) Conduct a full scale validation. Factor analysis was again conducted using the full scale data. Construct validity and reliability tests were also made to validate the instrument. Construct validity included both convergence and discriminant validity. Convergence validity was tested by average variance extracted (AVE). Discriminant validity was tested by ensuring that the AVE from the construct was greater than variance shared between this construct and others in the model. Reliability was tested by consistency reliabilities (CR).

## 3.1. The specification of constructs and their measurement

Selection of an initial set of items from the literature for a draft instrument simplifies instrument development. When fully validated instruments are available, replication of a study in heterogeneous settings is also simpler.

In our study, the items to measure PU of mobile support functions were adapted from the original TAM’s PU scale: thus we measured PU in terms of ‘‘increasing productivity’’, ‘‘improving performance’’, and ‘‘being useful’’. The items for measuring IU of mobile support functions were also modified from Davis’ TAM study. We measured IU as ‘‘intend to use’’, ‘‘like to use’’, and ‘‘should be used’’. The Likert scales ranged from 1 (not at all) to 7 (a great extent).

For mobile tasks the literature had no well developed and verified measures, so we used three constructs: mobility, location dependency, and time criticality. For each construct, we defined the following measurements shown in Table 2.

Mobility was measured by the percentage of time spent outside the office to distinguish mobile workers from stationary workers.

However, in our study, mobility referred to the extent to which a task was being performed in different locations. We therefore measured it in terms of ‘‘location variety’’ (from always at the same locations to always at different locations), ‘‘location specificity’’ (from limited to a specific location to being at any place), and ‘‘location flexibility’’ (as freedom of choosing a place, varying from not at all to a great extent). The task related location can be a person’s location, a physical object’s location, or a work location. The object or its location may be unknown, or unfamiliar. For mobile workers, there are inherent uncertainties about places that impact their work choice and the kinds of activities they pursue. Managing the uncertainty of places and their kinds of activities is of particular concern to mobile workers. We measured location dependency in terms of the need for information on: self-location, other people’s location, equipment location, and travel directions.

Ballard and Seibold identified, measured, and verified ten dimensions of time in the workplace: flexibility, linearity, pace, punctuality, delay, scheduling, separation, urgency, scarcity, and future and present time perspectives. Among them, punctuality involves the exacting nature of timing and deadlines. Punctuality centers around measures of precise timing—at the macro-level, getting the job done on time, or at the micro-level, arriving at the appropriate place on time. Some tasks need to be performed punctually (attending a meeting or catching a flight). Urgency describes a person’s preoccupation with deadlines and task completion; it may reflect a temporary valuation of time based on an impending deadline. Emergency handling is often the case of high urgency.

Time and space are always interlinked—changes in space provoke changes in time, and vice versa. The time frame for mobile work is narrower than that for stationary work. On one hand, a narrower time frame constrains activities that mobile workers may perform. On the other hand, since activities must be completed in a certain time frame, it means that the activities are more urgent and time critical. Thus, we introduced the time window (time frame) as another measure of time criticality.

Measures of time criticality therefore included ‘‘the urgency of the task’’, ‘‘the punctuality of the task’’ and ‘‘the time window of the task’’.

## 3.2. Pilot test and technical validation

An initial draft of the questionnaire was distributed to a small group of twelve MBA and Ph.D. students in a University in Canada. Twelve questionnaires were collected from them, and feedback was also solicited from them. The participants responded that: (a) for some items the wording was not clear, (b) the definition of mobile tasks was not explicit, (c) the questionnaire was too long to finish (it took more than 20 min to fill out), and (d) some questions were hard to answer. The questionnaire was revised, based on these comments.

We then visited some companies that provided mobile work support solutions or were major users of mobile support tools, to learn how mobile support technologies were used in industry. We presented our research proposal to them and solicited their comments on the usefulness of our questionnaire. Based on their feedback, a further improvement was made to the questionnaire and it was distributed to off-campus personal contacts (mobile field workers and an e-business research center’s business manager e-mail distribution list). A total of 22 completed questionnaires were obtained in this manner.

Factor analysis (using Maximum Likelihood Extraction and Promax Rotation) was conducted on the 22 questionnaires for the task instrument items. This allowed us to determine which measures covary in explaining any high percentages in the variance of the dataset. As shown in Table 3, the 10-task instrument items loaded on three factors. We noticed that item TIME1 (time window for task completion) had a relatively high cross-loading (0.49). LOCVAR3 (location flexibility) also had a relatively high cross-loading (0.41). However, due to the small sample size of the pretest, we could not make a conclusive decision to remove these items. The questionnaire was further revised and improved, based on this feedback. Item TIME3 (start or complete on time) was extended to four items: TIME3 (start on time), TIME4 (complete on time), TIME5 (start as soon as possible) and TIME6 (complete as soon as possible).

## 3.3. Instrument validation in the full scale study

The mobile workers used as subjects in the full scale study were contacted through e-mail or in person. We searched for names and e-mail addresses on Internet public information Web pages to identify appropriate candidates, based on the nature of the companies for which they work, and their job positions. When we contacted mobile workers in person, we could generally identify them from the type of cars they drove (e.g., taxis). We also approached them in public places such as parking lots, when they were not busy.

550 mobile workers were contacted in person in one Canadian city, resulting in 100 responses, a response rate of 18.2%. A total of 810 e-mails were sent across Canada, resulting in a total of 96 responses for a response rate of 11.6%. 64 of the responses were returned by e-mail, 14 by fax, and 18 in our online survey. The overall response rate was 14.3%. By screening out incomplete data in the 196 collected questionnaires, 179 were found to be complete. The profile of respondents is summarized in Table 4. All subjects met our definition of mobile worker.

Table 6 lists the internal consistency reliabilities (CR) as well as AVE (average variance extracted). Based on these results, the items demonstrated satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity.

We found that the PU of mobile notification and real-time mobile job dispatching was positively related to mobility (H1-1, H2-2).

The results also confirmed that location tracking, navigation, and real-time mobile job dispatching were perceived to be useful when workers had a high degree of location dependency (H2-1, H2-2, H2-3). This was in accord with our expectations. In fact, some real-word mobile work support systems do combine these three functions.

We found that mobile notification and location tracking were perceived to be more useful in time critical situations (H3-1, H3-2). These results are consistent with Gebauer and Shaw’s study, which found that the use of notification was positively correlated with the perceived need to handle emergency situations. Real-time mobile job dispatching, however, was not shown to be significantly useful in time critical situations (H3-3). It should be noted that mobile workers, especially mobile knowledge workers, often decided what to do. Thus they did not need to use a job dispatching system to deal with time critical issues.

Hypothesis H4 was strongly supported. All related paths were significant at the p < 0.001 level. The coefficients of each of the four paths from the mobile work support functions ranged between 0.85 and 0.88. The R<sup>2</sup> for IU of each of the four functions ranged between 0.71 and 0.77. These findings indicated that the usefulness of mobile work support functions greatly influenced user intention to adopt the technologies.

## 5. Contributions and limitations

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

Our research made several important contributions. First, we identified the ideal fit between three dimensions of task characteristics and four typical mobile work support functions. Previous studies only examined specific mobile application, and did not have a mobile task model. We developed a more general model for TTF for mobile workers. Second, our work provided a comprehensive task model for mobile work. The traditional task model with only two dimensions of task complexity and task interdependence cannot capture specific task features in mobile contexts. We examined the specific dimensions of mobility, location dependency, and time criticality and also developed and empirically verified new constructs for these new dimensions. Our instruments had good reliability and validity. With this mobile task model, we have established a solid theoretical background through which it is possible to examine the nature of mobile work more thoroughly for different types of mobile work.

Third, our study identified context-related functions for mobile work support. We not only identified important mobile support functions but also explored their usefulness in supporting different needs for mobile workers.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Our study can help businesses wishing to select a suitable portfolio of mobile applications to fit the needs of their specific business. Companies should first assess the nature of their work by examining the relevant task characteristics. Based on the nature of the work, they will then be able to predict what kind of technology support is needed, or should be avoided. It can also help mobile technology providers understand their target markets, by checking the suitability of their products for particular situations. This will help technology providers develop suitable products to meet real mobile workplace needs. For instance, BlackBerry provides different solutions for business intelligence, field service, and sales force automation and has had great success in supporting mobile workers (http://na.blackberry.com/eng/solutions/needs/). Safe-Freight Technology has focused on providing a special GPS fleet management and vehicle tracking system to improve the productivity, safety, and security of a company’s trucks, heavy equipment, trailers, etc. and the workforce operating them (http:/ www.safefreight.com/). DAP Technologies has packaged mobile solutions into rugged handheld devices and tablet computers that can operate in harsh environments for utilities, emergency and security services, transportation and logistics (http://www.daptech.com/).

## 5.3. Limitations

Several limitations to our research must be acknowledged.

First, our study did not include all task characteristics for mobile work such as task complexity and interdependency. We also did not include general mobile information support functions that are needed in stationary office work, such as communication and information search. Mobile communication, although it was perceived as most useful by all mobile workers, did not correlate with any task characteristics. To make the paper more focused, we did not address these issues here.

Second, although we developed and verified new constructs for mobile work with good reliability and validity, the instrument development and validation steps were not as rigorous as they could have been; we could have used extensive formal interviews with mobile support experts for qualitative validity testing. The sample size was small, so instrument validation had to wait until the full scale study was completed. It would also be better to establish instrument validation first, and then conduct the full scale study.

Third, usability issues were not addressed. Usability testing can measure how well people use a device for its intended purpose. For mobile work support, usability may affect the value of mobile applications, even though the function may seem to be an ideal fit to support mobile work.

Fourth, our study did not examine the individual characteristics of mobile workers, such as education level and computer-related skills; these may also affect the PU of mobile applications.

Incorporating usability factors and individual characteristics in an expanded form of our research model could produce more comprehensive results.

## 6. Conclusions

We successfully identified important task characteristics of mobile work and provided constructs to measure them. We also identified important mobile support functions and successfully developed and validated instruments to test our mobile TTF model using a survey of real world mobile workers. Our study provided evidence that: (1) perceived usefulness of mobile notification and real-time mobile job dispatching are positively related to mobility; (2) location tracking and navigation are perceived to be useful in situations of location dependency; and (3) mobile notification and location tracking are perceived to be more useful in time critical situations. Our study also gave insight into how the fit between mobile tasks and mobile work support functions lead to PU.
