## Abstract

This study uses a unique cross-sectional dataset of the top 500 Internet retailers in North America and empirically investigates the moderating effects of keyword competition on the relationship between ad position and its determinants in the sponsored search market. The study draws on the literature in keyword auction design, search advertising performance, and consumer search behavior as the theoretical foundation. The study finds significant variation in the role of keyword competition for web-only versus multi-channel retailers. Specifically, this study finds that keyword competition has significant moderating effect only for multi-channel retailers. The empirical analysis also indicates that the position of ads for web-only retailers is dependent on bid values and ad relevancy factors, whereas multi-channel retailers are more reliant on their bid values. We discuss the implications of these results in light of the increased popularity of sponsored search advertising in recent years.

Keywords: Electronic Commerce; Search Engine Advertising; Sponsored Search; Internet Marketing; Internet Retailing; Ad Position; Keyword Competition, Online Keyword-based Advertising

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has enabled the distribution of information to be affordable, convenient, and remarkably fast. Search engines in particular have been shown to be the primary gateway for most Internet users seeking information (Jansen and Mullen 2008). The growth of users, along with simple, targeted, and customizable advertising, has allowed search engines to be the dominant online marketing channel on the Internet. Given the unique challenges advertisers face in this growing and dynamic marketplace (e.g., three-agent interaction $^{1}$ , mounting competition, and increasing budget allocation), it is evident that they must understand the competitive environment to develop effective campaign strategies and achieve their marketing goals. Not only is the display of an ad directly related to the marketing goal, but so is the position of the ad within the search engine result pages (SERPs). Although advertisers vary in their marketing goals (e.g., brand awareness, revenue maximization, lead generation, etc.), the conventional wisdom in the industry is that the top position is the most desirable position, leading to intense competition to secure the top positions in the SERPs (Agarwal et al. 2011).

The increasingly competitive keyword market requires advertisers to engage in several challenging activities, which include selecting relevant keywords, crafting compelling ad copies, setting appropriate ad budget, and formulating bidding strategies, among others. For example, firms with poor ad budgets may not attain their desired ad positions simply because their ads may not even be considered for the auctions. To effectively compete in this challenging environment, firms experiment with different strategies or use the services of third parties. However, what works in one competitive circle may not necessarily work in another. One feasible way for firms to improve their search advertising performance is to gain sufficient knowledge of the keyword competition and its impact on achieving their desired ad positions using different pay-per-click (PPC) strategies. Meanwhile, search engines have employed mechanisms that rank ads not only based on bid values, but also attributes related to advertisers' performance (Chen et al. 2010, Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006, Liu et al. 2010). For example, Google uses a measure called Quality Score (Google 2012) to determine whether an ad is eligible (relevant) to enter the auction as well as its position on the SERP $^{2}$ . Thus, the overall search advertising outcome for a firm can be influenced by the profiles as well as number of other firms competing for similar keywords.

Early research in sponsored search advertising has focused on the design of keyword auctions and the equilibrium outcome in these auctions (e.g., Chen et al. 2010, Edelman et al. 2007, Edelman and Ostrovsky 2007, Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006, Liu et al. 2010, Varian 2007). Another stream of research analyzes sponsored search ad performance and the relationship between different sponsored search metrics (Ghose and Yang 2008a,b, 2009, Rutz and Bucklin 2007, 2011, Skiera et al. 2010). Other related empirical studies have investigated consumer search behavior in sponsored search market and the classification of web queries (e.g., Jansen et al. 2008, Jansen and Resnick 2006, Jansen and Spink 2009). Despite the sheer number of firms competing in the keyword market, there is limited empirical work that examines how competition intensity impacts the sponsored search outcomes for firms. A few researchers have studied different aspects of competition in sponsored search auctions. For example, Jerath et al. (2011) studied a "position paradox" in a keyword auction market, where a superior firm may bid lower than an inferior firm and obtain a position below it, but still obtains more clicks than the inferior firm. Animesh et al. (2011) showed that the effect of a firm's ad rank on its ad's click-through rate(CTR) is moderated by the firm's ability to differentiate itself from its adjacent rivals in the SERP. Xu et al. (2011) illustrated that the value of search advertising slots should be determined endogenously in price competition. They show that a more prominent slot may or may not be more valuable, even if it is cost-free. Thus, firms in different competitive situations should tailor their advertising strategies accordingly (Xu et al. 2011).

In this study, we empirically investigate the impact of competition intensity on the relationship between a firm's key sponsored search variables (i.e., bid value, click-through rate, and ad quality) and its ad position in the keyword market. Using cross-sectional data on the search advertising practices of firms in the retailing industry, we analyze how the level of competition in the keyword market moderates the relationship between a firm's ad position and its key determinants. We draw on insights from the literature in keyword auction design, search advertising performance, and consumer search behavior to develop and test several empirical models that account for differences in merchant type, company size, advertising budget, and search engine optimization (SEO) performance.

The retailing industry is characterized by extensive search advertising practices and high-stakes battle for market share and profitability (InternetRetailer, 2011, 2012). Given that Internet retailers are at the very end of the supply chain, advances in web technologies have enabled them to develop new marketing strategies in their advertising campaigns. Utilizing this new form of advertising with its growing consumer exposure enables retailers to expand their market share. We believe that our unique, cross-sectional data and the analyses based on the top performing retailers in North America will be important additions to the empirical base of the sponsored search advertising literature and offer practical insights into the keyword market in general and the search advertising practices within the retailing industry in particular.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a review of the literature in search advertising. Section 3 presents the conceptual framework and the theoretical foundations. Section 4 outlines the research model and the hypotheses of this study. Section 5 describes the data collection procedure, the research methodology, and the empirical results. Section 6 presents the discussion of the results and the theoretical and practical implications of the study. Finally, Section 7 provides the conclusions, limitations, and future research directions.

Search Marketing, has received significant attention from both academia and industry. According to the “State of Search Marketing Report” by SEMPO (Econsultancy 2012), the North American search engine marketing industry has increased in value from \$16.6B in 2010 to \$19.3B in 2011, reaching \$22.9B by the end of 2012. The proportion of companies carrying out Search Engine Optimization (SEO) was reported to be about 87%, while the percentage of companies engaging in paid search marketing was almost 84% in 2012 (Econsultancy 2012). Prior research in advertising has shown that perceived goal impediment, perceived ad clutter, and prior negative experiences with online ads are major problems with conventional forms of Internet marketing, such as banner ads (Cho and Cheon 2004). However, perceived goal impediment (showing ads that are not aligned with the consumer’s goals), the strongest factor in ad avoidance on the Internet, is diminished in SEA. Matching the ads to a consumer’s query is one of the primary goals of search engine providers (Ghose and Yang 2008b, Google 2012). Evidently, all three agents of the sponsored search advertising (i.e., search engine, advertisers, and consumers) have impact on the marketplace (Yao and Mela 2011). The nature of the interaction and the benefits are threefold (see Figure 1): search engines provide relevant results to the user queries, resulting in user satisfaction and acquisition of more users. More users would attract more advertisers, leading to more reputation and revenue for search engines. As a result, search advertising is the most popular advertising channel today.

Search engine marketing is performed in two ways: search engine optimization (SEO) and pay-per-click (PPC) advertising. SEO is the enhancement of website content and visibility to be more search engine friendly. In other words, by optimizing website content and structure, webpages can appear at higher positions in natural (organic) search results. In PPC, advertisers pay a fee in order to appear on the sponsored sections of the SERPs. A Generalized Second Price (GSP)

auction determines where the ads (also called ad copies) should be positioned on SERP (Edelman et al. 2007). With the submission of each query, an online (real time) auction is conducted among advertisers bidding on that query. To enter the auction, each ad has to be relevant to the searcher's query and pass a certain minimum relevancy criteria (Google 2012). To position the ads on the result page, the search engine calculates the ad rank (related to the position of the ad on SERP) by multiplying an advertiser's maximum cost-per-click(CPC) and its Quality Score. CPC is the amount that each advertiser is willing to pay for each keyword and Quality Score is a dynamically calculated value that determines how relevant the ad is to a user's query. Quality Score is calculated every time a user submits a query, and it is dependent on a number of factors, including ad quality and the historical performance of ads. According to Google (2012), there is a huge emphasis on click-through rate (CTR) in the calculation of the Quality Score. CTR is the number of clicks on an ad, divided by the total number of times the ad is displayed (also known as impressions).

## 2.1. Mechanism Design in Search Engine Auctions

The search engine advertising model was first introduced by GoTo.com $^{3}$ , and used auctions to display ads on SERPs. In the GoTo.com model, the ads with the highest bid values showed up at the top positions on the SERP. Later, Google started incorporating the number of clicks that each ad receives. The total amount that the auctioneer charges the advertisers is calculated by multiplying the number of clicks by the bid value over a specific period.

A number of researchers have studied sponsored search auctions from different perspectives. Feng et al. (2007) compared several mechanisms (including those used by Overture and Google) for allocating sponsored slots and found that the performance of these mechanisms depends on the degree of correlation between advertisers' willingness to pay and their relevance to the search term. Weber and Zheng (2007) studied a model of an intermediated search market in which matching between consumers and firms takes place primarily via paid referrals. They found that the revenue-maximizing design bases rankings on a weighted average of product performance and bid amount. Edelman et al. (2007) and Varian (2007) studied equilibria of auctions with a GSP payment rule and under a complete-information setting. They showed that the auctioneer's equilibrium revenue under the GSP rule is at least as high as that under the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves(VCG) mechanism. Edelman et al. (2007) also proved that there is no dominant strategy equilibrium in GSP auctions under a complete-information setting, and advertisers will not bid their true valuation. Derived from the Nash equilibrium, Varian (2007) proposed a “symmetric” equilibrium for slot allocation in complete information auction settings. Similar to the findings in Edelman et al. (2007), he argues that optimal bids in sponsored search auction are dependent on the bids of the other agents in the marketplace.

Another stream of research examines the design of keyword auctions with advertisers' past performance information in various settings (Chen et al. 2010, Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006, Liu et al. 2010). Lahaie (2006) and Liu and Chen (2006) studied the equilibrium bidding under the ranking rules of unit price as well as the product of price and CTR. They showed that the latter rule is efficient, but neither of these rules is revenue-maximizing. Liu et al. (2010) extended Liu and Chen (2006) to a generalized multislot setting and allowed valuation-per-click to be correlated with CTR signals to study the use of differentiated minimum bids, together with the weighted ranking rule, as a way of exploiting ex ante infirmation on advertisers. Furthermore, Chen et al. (2010) examined a setting which considers the dynamic effects of bidder performance evolution (i.e., a situation in which advertisers may improve their performance for their own interest or in reaction to the performance-based ranking policy). This stream of research reinforces the recent changes in the ranking rules of major search providers and addresses many related issues that are relevant for both search engines and advertisers.

## 2.2. Empirical Research in Sponsored Search Ad Performance

A number of researchers have also conducted empirical studies to examine the relationships between various sponsored search variables in different settings. Gopal et al. (2011) empirically examined the interaction between the search and the content advertising channels and found significant cannibalization across the two channels as well as significant diminishing returns to impressions within each channel. Ganchev et al. (2007) proposed approaches that should be taken in order to model advertisers' bidding behavior in sponsored search auctions. They concluded that removing data related to certain strategies (e.g., jamming bids to deplete competitors' budgets) can contribute to finding a better fit for bidding data. Rutz and Bucklin (2007) proposed a method to calculate individual keyword's cost per sale, and consequently, a model to generate high performance keywords. Ghose and Yang (2009) used keyword level variables, ad position, and landing page quality to explain consumer search and purchase behavior, advertisers' cost-per-click, and search engine ranking mechanism. In another study, Ghose and Yang (2008a) proposed a model for calculating optimal bidding values and evaluated advertisers' overbidding and underbidding patterns based on keyword level attributes. Using search data from Yahoo!, Rey and Kannan (2010) also proposed a model to optimize the bidding value of keywords based on estimated conversion rate of each keyword. Incorporating information about terms relativity, quality, content, and scope of the ads,

Richardson et al. (2007) proposed a logistic regression model to estimate the CTR of newly created ads.

Furthermore, some studies have investigated the relationship between sponsored search advertising and organic search results. Using a game-theoretic model, Xu et al. (2012) analyzed how the presence of organic listing as a competing information source affects advertisers' sponsored bidding and the equilibrium outcomes in search advertising. They identify two aspects of a firm's sponsored bidding incentive (i.e., promotive and preventive) and show that the presence of organic listing alters firms' sponsored bidding incentives such that a stronger firm has primarily preventive incentive (i.e., preventing a competitor from improving exposure by occupying a better sponsored position), whereas a weaker firm has mainly promotive incentive (i.e., winning a better sponsored position to increase exposure, and subsequently, demand and profit in the product market). In addition, by comparing it with a case in which there is only a sponsored list, they showed that the differentiated exposure in the organic list gives the weaker advertiser chances to win a better sponsored position, thus improving the overall information structure the search engine provides. Yang and Ghose (2010) also showed that the presence of both sponsored and organic listings results in a synergy that increases the CTR on both listings. On the other hand, Jansen and Spink (2007) reported that combining organic listings together with sponsored search results decreases the CTR of sponsored search results.

A very nascent stream of research emerging in sponsored search is the effect of spillovers between different marketplace variables. For example, Ghose and Yang (2008b) have modeled the consumers' search-to-purchase behavior over different product categories. They show that there is a high probability that consumers searching for a product in a category might not only purchase a product in that category, but also tend to buy a product from a different category. They further elaborate their results based on keyword-level attributes such as retailer-specific keywords and brand-specific keywords. Rutz and Bucklin (2011) introduced the concept of “awareness of relevance” in keywords, and propose that there is a strong asymmetric spillover from generic search to branded search activity.

## 2.3. Consumer Search Behavior

Uncertainty about consumers' search behavior is one of the factors that make online advertising a challenging task. In general, consumer behavior has several aspects that can affect the purchase decision. The means-end model relating price, quality, and value, proposed by Zeithaml (1988), is a good example of this claim. The model illustrates the decision process that leads to a consumer purchase decision. Several intrinsic and extrinsic attributes, objective price, perceived satisfaction, and perceived quality, are coalesced to uniquely define the perceived value of consumers' objective. The perceived value of an item (in this context, the perceived value that each sponsored result can produce for consumers) determines if the consumer is going to take an action (e.g., clicking the sponsored ad). Therefore, in search advertising context, consumer search behavior drives the selection of keywords, the design of ad copies, and the overall campaign strategy.

From the search engine's perspective, relevant ads should be displayed at higher spots; the ads on the top spots receive more attention from users and generate more clicks. Users have different mindsets when searching on the Internet, and search engines can show more results that are relevant if they recognize users' intent during their search sessions. In this regard, Jansen et al. (2008) provide a hierarchical classification of user intent on search engines, derived from previous research in the classification of web queries. In their research, queries are categorized into informational, transactional, and navigational queries. Jansen and Resnick (2006) also reviewed the early research related to consumer behavior in search engines and concluded that consumers' preference towards organic results is stronger; however, consumers rate the non-sponsored results as relevant as the sponsored results. Furthermore, they found that integrating sponsored and non-sponsored search results does not increase user attention towards sponsored results (Jansen and Spink 2009).

Much of the work in consumer search behavior also overlaps with the research reviewed in Section 2.2. For example, several studies by Ghose and Yang (2008a,b, 2009) have direct implications to consumer search behavior. An important implication from previous research regarding consumer search behavior is how the user search behavior can be captured by CTR. The CTR value is one of the best indicators of the consumer search behavior, as it incorporates both impressions and actual user clicks. While impressions define how many consumers are searching for a particular product (much like reach and frequency in traditional advertising media), consumer clicks are indicators of ad relevancy based on consumer evaluation. More specifically, a click by the consumer indicates that the position, information content, and presentation of the ad have been reliable enough to generate a relevant search result. Interpreting CTR as a measure of consumer involvement also provides the rational for the current ranking mechanisms employed by most search engine providers.

## 3. Conceptual Model and Theoretical Foundation

The positioning strategy of a firm in sponsored search marketplace is often reflected by the position of its ads on SERPs. The ad position is an outcome of a strategic mixture of sponsored search auction determinants, which is observable in the form of bidding values and ad relevancy attributes. Furthermore, this relationship takes place in a competitive environment, where there is a high-stakes battle for keywords and ad slots. In the following sections, we explain why the outcome of positioning strategy can be reflected in ad position and how it can be compared to other performance metrics in previous studies. Then we discuss the key determinants of ad position based on keyword auction design literature and industry practice. We also provide an overview of the keyword market with a focus on keyword competition.

## 3.1. Positioning Strategy and Ad Performance

Positioning and differentiation strategies have been the focus of marketing in various contexts, such as service positioning (Shostack 1987), positioning in tourism industry (Ahmed 1991), and positioning in retail computer market (Brooksbank 1994). The following quote by Trout and Ries (1982, p. 3) provides an important perspective on positioning:

“... positioning is not what you do to a product. Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect. That is, you position the product in the mind of the prospect.”

A similar analogy can be drawn for the sponsored search marketplace by conceptualizing ad copies as products that advertisers are willing to sell to consumers (by drawing consumers' attention to them). The term “positioning” in sponsored search can be simply understood as the placement of the ads on a search page. However, at a more complex level, positioning strategy is a direct reflection of how a firm wants to appear in a consumer's mind. For example, consumers may perceive high priced items as having premium quality compared to their lower priced equivalents. Within the sponsored search context, higher positions of ads can suggest better product quality and higher reliability of the advertiser. In the end, it is the consumers' perceived value of an item that drives his/her decision process (Ahmed 1991, Zeithaml 1988), and advertisers always anticipate consumer behavior and strategize their marketing campaigns accordingly.

The directional nature of the sponsored search marketplace has enabled advertisers to strategize their campaigns based on consumers' perception of different ad positions (Animesh et al. 2011). By providing a ranked list of ads, search engines have enabled advertisers to target different consumer segments. This has been done by applying demographic filters as well as ad positioning strategies on the SERPs. For example, an advertiser might aim for top advertising slots to pursue brand awareness and recognition (Dou et al. 2010, Rutz and Bucklin 2011). Where as, another advertiser might be interested in revenue maximization by targeting middle positions (cheaper ads and higher conversion rates) (Ghose and Yang 2009). These examples demonstrate that advertisers can set goals based on implications from consumer search behavior (e.g., top position may lead to brand awareness, or lower position may imply lower product cost). In the sponsored search literature, consumer behavior metrics (e.g., CTR) (Agarwal et al. 2011, Animesh et al. 2011) as well as financial metrics (e.g., revenue per click) (Agarwal et al. 2011, Rutz and Bucklin 2007) have all been used as performance measures, often dependent on the ad position, informational content of the ad copies, and other sponsored search variables. However, the relationship between ad position and ad performance is not a one-way relationship. As such, the ad position is partly determined by the historical performance of the ad campaign (e.g., CTR).

Anticipating that the quality seeking consumers usually surf the ads in the premium positions, advertisers offering higher quality products are often interested in securing premium ad slots, leading to intense competition in the keyword market (Agarwal et al. 2011). While the ad ranking rules currently employed by major search providers indicate the relationships between ad position and its key determinants, how the level of competition in the keyword market impacts these relationships has not been examined extensively in the sponsored search literature. In a competitive setting, several important questions are pending. For example, does a firm that bids higher on keywords have a better ad position in its competitive circle? does a firm with a better profile in CTR and ad quality have a better ad position in its competitive circle? how does keyword competition intensity (high versus low competitive settings) moderate these relationships? In this research, we attempt to empirically test these relationships using competitive data from the retailing industry and controlling for variations in several important variables in the keyword market, such as the size of the firm, the size of the firm's advertising budget, the merchant category of the firm (i.e., multi-channel versus pure play firms), and the search engine optimization (SEO) performance of the firm.

## 3.2. Determinants of Ad Position

Prior research in keyword auction design has addressed several questions related to the inclusion of advertisers' past performance information in ranking ads (Chen et al. 2010, Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006, Liu et al. 2010). Other researchers also empirically explored factors that may affect ad position. For example, Rutz and Bucklin (2011) model the change in ad position as a function of awareness carryover, current CPC, historical CTR, and seasonality indicators. ## 3.3. Competition Intensity

Similar to competitions in other marketplaces, competition in online sponsored search markets has forced advertisers to adopt strategies (e.g., quality or price) to differentiate themselves from their rivals. Fortunately for advertisers, the pay-per-click mechanism employed by search engines allows them to enter the competition from diverse demographics. Even smaller companies with low advertising budgets and without well-known brands can enter the competition. However, with the increase in the number of advertisers, keyword competition is becoming increasingly intense. In addition to the bidding competition in the keyword market, there is also a product competition among firms that may affect how they value advertisement slots (Xu et al. 2011). Furthermore, from the users' perspective, not only does the order of the ads affect their click behavior, but also the likelihood of ads on the SERPs (Aggarwal et al. 2008). Therefore, it is important to consider the competition intensity when analyzing ad performance in the sponsored search market.

Previous empirical research has recognized the role of competition in the keyword market. Yet, most of the previous studies are based on data and analysis on either a single firm's marketing campaign or a small set of competitors. For example, in analyzing the ad position paradox in sponsored search auctions, Jerath et al. (2011) considered two advertisers in their model. Animesh et al. (2010) also focused on advertisers that appear immediately above and below a focal advertisement in examining competitors. The sponsored search marketplace is a dynamic environment with thousands of firms continuously entering the market, and an increase in competition intensity has been shown to have a direct effect on both sponsored search revenue (Edelman and Ostrovsky 2007) as well as advertisers' bidding strategies (Varian 2007). Thus, modeling the marketplace characteristics should account for the competition intensity among advertisers.

The lack of competition data has been also emphasized in previous literature (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Rutz and Bucklin 2011). Search engines also constantly change their ad ranking mechanisms. Therefore, some of the assumptions in previous studies may no longer hold, as the allocation mechanisms have evolved. An example is the variation and evolution of the ranking mechanisms (i.e., rank by bid, rank by the product of bid and CTR, rank by the product of bid and quality score) that may have different impacts on marketplace stakeholders' welfare (consumers, advertisers, and search engines) (Animesh et al. 2010). Although this dynamic nature of sponsored search might have challenged the ongoing research, it has led to a growing body of literature in the information systems and marketing fields. Yet, lack of publicly available data both from search engines and advertisers is one of the reasons for limited consideration of competition in empirical evaluations. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first empirical research that investigates the effect of keyword competition in a cross-sectional setting using competitive keyword data from a large number of firms.

## 4. Research Hypotheses

## 4.1. Main Effects

The variables that affect ad position in the sponsored search market have evolved along with changes in search provider' ranking rules. Following the lead by Google, major search engine providers have incorporated several variables in their ad ranking formula. Although our main research objective is to examine the impact of keyword competition on the relationships of these variables with ad position, these relationships (i.e., the main effects) have not been empirically tested in a cross-sectional setting before. Therefore, we first evaluate the main effects of these variables on ad position, before examining the moderating role of competition intensity on these relationships.

4.1.1. Bidding Strategy. A bid value is the maximum amount that an advertiser is willing to spend on each of its keywords. Most previous empirical research in sponsored search advertising uses the bid value as a key variable. For example, some researchers have proposed models for either predicting optimal bid values or controlling the determinants of bid values (Ghose and Yang 2008b, 2009). In general, following the ad ranking formula, it can be easily seen that a firm's bid value has a direct impact on its ad position. In the evolution of ad ranking rules, the bid value was also the only factor (e.g., Yahoo!'s original ranking rule) and is currently one of the key factors of ad ranking (in the current ranking rules of major search providers). The general expectation in auctions, including keyword auctions, is that the higher the bid value, the higher the likelihood of winning or the higher the ad position in the case of keyword auctions. 4.1.2. Ad Relevancy Factors. According to previous studies related to search behavior, consumers' clicks indicate how they find search results relevant to their queries. In fact, number of impressions and CTR are extensively used to understand consumer search behavior (Ghose and Yang 2009, Animesh et al. 2011, Jansen and Spink 2009). Furthermore, search engines and anecdotal evidence from practitioners indicate that CTR is an important factor in determining the Quality Score. The number of clicks an ad receives is an indicator of how relevant the ad is to consumer search goals. It is also the search engine's revenue strategy to value the ads with higher CTR (Aggarwal et al. 2006). A stream of research in the design of keyword auctions also examined the use of advertisers' past performance information, particularly in the form of CTR (Chen et al. 2010, Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006, Liu et al. 2010). For example, Lahaie (2006) and Liu and

Chen (2006) studied the equilibrium bidding under the ranking rules of unit price as well as the product of price and CTR and showed that the latter rule is efficient, but neither of these rules is revenue-maximizing. Liu et al. (2010) and Chen et al. (2010) also addressed several related research questions in extended settings. In the evolution of ad ranking rules, the CTR was also one of the factors in Google's previous ranking mechanism and it is the most important component of the quality score in its current ranking rule. Therefore:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): There is a positive relationship between a firm's click-through-rate (CTR) and its ad position on the SERP.

Research in advertising highly emphasize on the information-content of ads displayed to consumers (Abernethy and Franke 1996, Resnik and Stern 1977). The more customized the content of the ads to the consumer's needs, the higher the likelihood of dragging consumer's attention. Consumers are becoming information-seeking experts and the search process is relatively less expensive than before. Firms use their ad creative to differentiate themselves, and the nature of the message or unique selling proposition conveyed in an ad's creative impacts consumer behavior (Animesh et al. 2011). Thus, designing targeted ads would result in better relevancy metrics, leading to higher Quality Score. These academic findings also conform to the guidelines provided by search engines on the role of ad copies and their information contents (Google 2012). Therefore:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): There is a positive relationship between a firm's ad quality and its ad position on the SERP.

4.1.3. Competition Intensity. Consumers have different objectives when making a purchase. With a variety of products available, the consumer compares different products and decides which product is better based on her quality expectations. According to Zeithaml (1988), “evaluations of the quality take place in a comparison context”. Comparison context inevitably resembles competition context: while consumers are busy comparing ads from different advertisers $^{4}$ , advertisers are competing to capture their desired audience in the market place. Adapted from Edelman and Schwarz (2006), we use the term “market depth” to refer to the number of advertisers bidding on the same keyword. An increase in the “market depth” implies that the keyword is used by many competing advertisers; therefore, “market depth” can be an indicator of competition intensity in the sponsored search marketplace. Given the directional nature of the sponsored search market (i.e., the ordinal ranking of firms) (Animesh et al. 2011), the market depth may affect not only the likelihood of a firm’s ad to be noticed by consumers, but also the chance of appearing on the SERPs. For the average firm, an increase in the competition intensity of its keywords imply more competitive profiles entering the market which may affect its desired ad position negatively. Therefore:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): There is a negative relationship between the level of competition intensity in the marketplace and a firm's ad position on the SERP.

## 4.2. Interaction Effects

4.2.1. The Interaction between CTR and CPC. The current ad ranking mechanisms indicate that CTR and bid value simultaneously effect the ad position. Given that CTR is an important element of the ad's quality score, an increase in the quality score would increase the minimum relevancy threshold that an ad requires to enter the auction pool. Anecdotal evidence also indicates that in order to decrease the advertising costs in sponsored search advertising, it is important to increase the CTR of the ads. Bidding aggressively to create brand awareness in the beginning of the sponsored search marketing campaign would direct more customers to a firm's website. With an increase in impressions and customer clicks, the bid value is less likely to be the major determinant of the firm's ad position on the SERP. In other words, when ads with higher CTR exist in the competition, the impact of bid values on the ad position tends to be weaker. Therefore:

Hypothesis 5 (H5): The positive relationship between a firm's bid value and ad position is affected by CTR, such that with higher CTR, the impact of bid value on ad position will be diminished.

4.2.2. Moderating Effects of Competition Intensity. An increase in competition intensity increases the winning bids in auctions (Brannman et al. 1987). But, how would an increase in winning bid impact the ad position? Advertisers increase their bid values to secure a higher ad position. With an increase in the competition intensity, the winning bids will be higher than those in a less competitive environment. In other words, with an increase in competition intensity, the average winning bid among competitors will increase. Therefore, the increase in bid value will compensate for the increase in competition, diminishing its direct effect on the ad position (the main effect). The following hypothesis translates the above argument.

Hypothesis 6a (H6a): The positive relationship between a firm's bid value and ad position is affected by keyword competition, such that with higher competition intensity, the impact of bid value on ad position will be diminished.

An increase in the number of competitors decreases the role of CTR. Consider a situation where only one advertiser is bidding on a keyword. Clearly, regardless of how relevant the ad is to the searcher's query, the advertiser would bid minimally as he is the only one appearing on the SERP. An increase in the number of advertisers competing on similar keywords will more likely lead to more advertisers with competitive CTR values. However, the presence of many advertisers with competitive CTR values increases the minimum ad relevancy threshold to enter the auction pool for a given keyword. This increase in the minimum ad relevancy threshold will leave a pool of ads with mostly high CTR profiles, thus reducing the role of CTR. This situation can be formally translated into the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6b (H6b): The positive relationship between a firm's CTR value and ad position is affected by keyword competition, such that with higher competition intensity, the impact of CTR on ad position will be diminished.

Crafting customized ad content is an in-house strategy for each advertiser. The message that each ad copy contains can significantly define the positioning and differentiation strategy of a firm. The use of the search term in the ad copy content and using specific words such as “cheap” or “discount” can clearly convey the message of the advertisement. However, with competitors’ ads listed around the focal firm’s ad, the attention of consumers might be dragged away from the ad (Animesh et al. 2011). With few ads displayed on the SERP, each ad has better chance of differentiating itself from its rivals. An increase in the number of competitors with similar offerings may reduce the role of the focal ad’s unique message and its effectiveness. Therefore:

Hypothesis 6c (H6c): The positive relationship between a firm's ad quality and ad position is affected by keyword competition, such that with higher competition intensity, the impact of ad quality on ad position will be diminished.

## 4.3. Control Variables

Given the diversity of Internet retailers and the dynamic nature of the sponsored search market, there are several variables that need to be controlled in order to accurately evaluate the empirical model.

Advertising resources are always limited. Firms cannot allocate unlimited advertising budget to their campaigns. Operationalization of the advertising budget has been the focus of many previous studies (e.g., Mitchell 1993, Yang et al. 2014). In fact, setting the advertising budget is a critical decision in media planning when budgets are allocated to different media outlets (Little and Lodish 1969). The amount of the advertising budget also has an impact on the sponsored search market equilibrium (Auerbach et al. 2008, Lahaie 2006). If it is set too low, the firm runs out of the daily ad budget quickly. Consequently, even if other factors of ad rank were optimal, the search engine would not display the ads when the firm's daily ad budget is depleted.

The quality of the landing page is also an important consideration in sponsored search advertising. Landing page quality is highly correlated with SEO practices and incorporates, but is not limited to, the quality of the page header, website description, URL of the page and structure of the website. Impact of SEO strategy, a closely related Internet marketing strategy to SEA, has also been the center of attention in many studies before (Rutz and Bucklin 2011). Improving the landing page quality results in a better campaign performance, such as an increase in profits (Ghose and Yang 2009). Previously, spillover effects of organic and sponsored results have proved that SEO and paid ad rank are in direct correlation (Ghose and Yang 2008b). The presence of organic listing has been also shown to affect advertisers' sponsored bidding incentives and the equilibrium outcomes in search advertising (Xu et al. 2012). Also, practitioners often recommend SEO practices as a strategy in sponsored search campaigns to mitigate the risk of high bid values. Most companies invest both in SEO and sponsored search at the same time (Econsultancy 2012). Therefore, the impact of SEO quality should be controlled when examining the performance of sponsored search advertising.

Finally, we should control for the size and merchant category of the Internet retailers targeted in our cross-sectional study. The sponsored search environment has enabled companies from diverse demographics advertise their products and services online. Therefore, not only the performance of the companies should be measured in their own market sector (Ayanso et al. 2010), but also their differences in size should be considered (Ghose and Yang 2009). In summary, this study uses advertising budget, SEO quality, merchant category, and company size as control variables.

To measure the ad quality, we used the ratio of the total number of ad copies to the total number of paid keywords. This ratio is used to represent the level of the firm's “Ad Quality”. We assume that the firm’s effort in crafting and experimenting with multiple copies of ad contents lead to a better pool of relevant ads. The number of ad copies (i.e., different versions of an ad) indicates the degree of ad text customization and how advertisers appeal to different buyer needs. In order to effectively link keywords with the contents of an ad with limited text space, advertisers craft different copies of an ad with different wording and ad tone. They also add different ad copies from time to time to reflect seasonality, pricing strategies, and other marketing goals. While restricted on the number of characters, ad copies should highlight the unique selling proposition of a service or product, in addition to the benefits to customers. Although a copy of an ad may contain all these features, it may not be appealing or persuasive to all customers. Therefore, the common strategy available for advertisers is to craft and structure multiple copies of an ad with different tons or calls to action. The general expectation is that multiple versions of an ad are more likely to be appealing to a diverse group of customers. Therefore, while the ultimate judge of ad quality is the browser or the customer, we believe that the number of ad copies advertisers prepare with respect to the number of keywords reflects the time and effort they invest in crafting effective and compelling ad contents.

To operationalize the market depth, the number of companies whose ads appear along with each focal firm's ads is used. For example, if an ad appears on the third position on the SERP and there are 9 other ads displayed alongside the ad, the ad position will be 3 and the number of competitors would be 9 (the focal advertiser itself is excluded) for that keyword. The “Ad Position” and “Competition” variables are both averaged over a period of a month. Furthermore, the value of the ad position has been subtracted from the highest value to make the interpretation of the results easier. With this transformation, a higher number indicates a top ad position, while a lower one indicates a bottom position.

The operationalization of the remaining control variables is straightforward. To control for company size, annual sales figures are used. Sales figures have been extensively used in various disciplines such as economics and accounting to control for company size (Adams et al. 1998, Hart and Oulton 1996). Specifically, annual web sales instead of total sales are used for controlling the company size. This would lead to identification of online retailers with active search marketing campaigns instead of inclusion of retailers generating high sales via offline sales channels.

To control for SEO quality of the companies, the overall organic rank is used after log transformation. SEO represents processes to obtain a natural placement on natural (organic) or un-paid search engine results pages or the visibility of a firms website in the organic search results. Apart from conversion and revenue measures that are not often publicly available for comparisons, the organic ranking is the most commonly used measure of comparing SEO performances in industry (KeywordSpy 2012, SpyFu 2012). This is because the higher ranked on the natural search results page, and more frequently a site appears in the natural search results list, the more visitors it will receive from the search engine's users. In order to appear in the top organic results, companies need to use effective SEO strategies. Therefore, organic rank is logically a good variable to control for overall SEO efforts of the company. Finally, to control for the type of the merchants, the merchants are categorized into “web-only” firms, denoting firms without physical store, and “multi-channel” firms, denoting firms with both physical and online stores (clicks-and-mortar). The latter category includes retailers from catalog/call center, consumer brand manufacturer, and retail chain.

## 5.4. Research Methodology

To evaluate the research model, hierarchical multiple regression (West and Aiken 1991, Montgomery 2007) is used. To explain the relationships between the predictor variables and the ad position, eight different models were created (Table 4). In all models, $\alpha_{i}$ is the intercept, $\beta_{is}$ are the regression coefficients, and $\varepsilon_{i}$ captures the unexplained error term. Model 1 incorporates only control variables to account for the variations in our sample in terms of size, budget, SEO quality, and merchant type. All Control variables except the merchant type have been log transformed to derive a linear relationship. Model 2 and 3 test the main effects. To test the moderating effects, models 4 to 7 are constructed by entering interaction terms one at a time, and model 8 by entering all moderators simultaneously. The interaction terms in models 4 to 7 provide the estimates required to test the moderating effects individually.

The empirical analyses have been performed in two stages. First, all the February 2012 data were tested to fit the regression model. We observed that merchant type has a significant effect in all the models; therefore, the full data was divided into two sets representing each merchant type, and the analysis was performed for each merchant type separately.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

The literature in consumer search behavior indicates that ad position is an important indicator for a firm's positioning strategy. Given that consumers perceive different values depending on the position of the ads on the SERP (Animesh et al. 2011), the locations of the ad on the SERP have significant implications for advertisers in achieving their marketing goals. In addition, the ad position has significant effect on a firm's revenue (Agarwal et al. 2011). The significance of ad position for search engine advertisers conforms to similar studies in other advertising channels such as television, radio, and newspapers (Brunel and Nelson 2003, Pieters and Bijmolt 1997, Terry 2005). The primacy effect (ads appearing in premium ad positions) and recency effect (recalling the last seen ads) both have significant implications on the persuasiveness of ads.

Arguably, price is one of the main determinants of ad position across various advertising outlets. Clearly, it is the nature of any auction to favor the highest bidder. Our study indicates that search engine auctions are no different; they tend to allocate the better ad positions to the advertisers with the highest bids (i.e., CPC). Moreover, the impact of bid values on ad position is consistent in all the three datasets used in our analyses (i.e., full, web-only, and multi-channel retailers). The results are consistent with advertising via other channels as well. The newspapers and magazines charge premium prices for showing the ads on the front and back cover page; likewise, ads displayed at the beginning of the commercial breaks on television and radio have higher rates.

CTR is another determinant of ad position in sponsored search auctions related to the consumer search behavior. Search engines have incorporated measures to apply the consumer's click behavior into the ranking of ads. Previous studies in sponsored search advertising have also emphasized the importance of incorporating advertisers' past performance information in determining ad rank (Lahaie 2006, Liu and Chen 2006). Results of this study show that ad position is influenced by both bid value and CTR for web-only retailers. In contrast, CTR does not have a direct significant effect on the ad position of the multi-channel merchants. This might be due to the fact that multi-channel merchants are targeting sponsored ads like other conventional advertising channels (e.g., television, newspapers), where location of the ad has a price and remunerating that price will guarantee a spot.

Another observation in this study is the relationship between CPC and CTR in determining ad position. Ads with low CTR values indicate that consumers are less attracted to them. For example, uncommon keywords (e.g., “shop laptop sz340 in Canada, Ontario”) are rarely entered by searchers looking for information. Consequently, the chances of the corresponding ads getting clicked is lower compared to ads linked to more popular keywords (e.g., “shop laptop”). Therefore, at lower CTR values, the impact of CPC on ad position is more significant. The situation in a high CTR setting is quite different. Ads with high CTR values most likely have high quality score. Therefore, the quality score threshold for those ads entering the auction is much higher than the low CTR ads (search engines adjust the minimum quality score threshold automatically when the competition for a keyword increases and more firms are willing to advertise on a similar keyword). In addition, an increase in the minimum quality score threshold tends to eliminate the ads with high bid values and low CTR values. Therefore, at higher CTR values, the impact of CPC on ad position is less significant.

The results of this study also shed light on another characteristic of the sponsored search marketplace. On one hand, the negative impact of keyword competition on the ad position is probably the most expected outcome of this study. The number of merchants bidding on the same keywords certainly has a negative effect on the average ad position of the focal firm. This result can be related to a situation in conventional advertising channels (e.g., TV) called “advertising clutter” (all non-program material, e.g., commercials). The number of advertisements competing for space on a newspaper page or during a TV Commercial break impacts not only the likelihood of appearance, but also the performance of a particular ad (Brown and Rothschild 1993, Pieters and Bijmolt 1997, Webb et al. 1979). The number of competing advertisers has negative impact on consumers’ memory, and increases the advertising cost (Asdemir 2006, Brannman et al. 1987). On the other hand, keyword competition also has significant impact on other variables of the marketplace. Results of this study for multi-channel retailers indicate that competition has a significant negative moderating effect on the relationship between bid values and ad position. Existence of higher competition intensity would require higher bid values in order to maintain the ad position. This is also in line with previous results in auction design literature (Asdemir 2006, Brannman et al. 1987), where an increase in competition intensity increases the winning values. Contrary to multi-channel merchants, there is no such evidence for their web-only counterparts. This invites additional research to investigate the role of competition in different merchant as well as product categories.

The level of keyword competition also has significant effect on the relationship between CTR and ad position. The positive relationship between CTR and ad position is weakened at higher level of keyword competition for multi-channel merchants. This impact has both direct and indirect explanations. First, the direct impact of CTR on ad position is similar to the impact of CPC on ad position. Firms with higher CPC or/and CTR values have a better ad position in the market place. Therefore, the moderating effect of competition intensity on the CTR-Ad position relationship can be interpreted similar to its effect on the CPC-Ad position relationship. Second, to enter the auction pool, ads should pass certain minimum ad relevancy threshold. An increase in the number of competitors is more likely to increases the number of advertisers with high CTR profiles, resulting in an increase in the minimum ad relevancy threshold. This would lead to a new auction pool, in which ads have higher overall CTR values. Therefore, the impact of CTR on ad position is diminished in this setting.

## 7. Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Research

This study has drawn from the literature in keyword auction design, search advertising performance, and consumer search behavior to examine the effects of keyword competition on the relationship between a firm's sponsored search variables (i.e., bid value, CTR, and ad quality) and its ad position on the search engine result pages. The research model was tested on a unique cross-sectional dataset from the retailing industry. This study is also the first of its kind to investigate the determinants of ad position in a cross-sectional setting where the level of keyword competition is explicitly captured in terms of the number of advertisers competing for similar keywords.

The importance of the ad position on the SERP can be related to advertising in conventional marketing channels like television and radio (the order of appearance in the commercial break between programs) or newspapers and magazines (ads on the front and back covers). This study examined how a firms's sponsored search profile (CPC, CTR, and ad quality) are related to its ad position in the keyword competition. The study shows that these relationships are influenced by the level of the competition intensity in the marketplace. More specifically, the study reveals that the position of the ads from web-only retailers is more dependent on ad relevancy metrics; whereas, multi-channel retailers are more reliant on their bid values. This variation between web-only and multi-channel retailers is also seen in the moderating effects of keyword competition. Specifically, keyword competition has significant moderating effects only for multi-channel retailers. These results contribute to our understanding of keyword competition in sponsored search advertising and open several avenues for future research in this area.

This study has several limitations which can be described in two categories. The first category is related to the nature of the data used in this study. The empirical data used in this study were collected during the months of February and March. Although consistent results are obtained for the two periods, it may be important to run the analysis for other months of the year in order to see if the results are consistent. For example, during November and December the marketplace characteristics could change as the competition for the end of the year sales grows. Although this study assumed that analyzing data from top 500 retailers would not create bias in the results, inclusion of more Internet retailers would benefit the study, especially when analysis is performed on “web-only” and “multi-channel” merchants separately. Dividing the data into these two merchant categories produced smaller samples of each group and therefore collection of more data might change some of the findings.

Another limitation of this study is that it uses proxies to operationalize some of the research variables. For example, to capture the retailers' bid values, this study uses CPC values. Although CPC values have been extensively used in previous literature (Ghose and Yang 2009, Rutz and Bucklin 2007, Yao and Mela 2011), they may not capture the actual bid values. Another proxy defined in this study was ad quality, captured by number of ad copies per keyword for each advertiser. However, estimating the quality of ad copy itself can be a topic of future research.

The other category of limitations is related to the methodology. Although this study also accounted for the possibility of endogeneity for CTR (see Appendix B), incorporating time-series into the current data can provide additional insights. For example, the recursive effect of the ad position and CTR can be modeled taking endogeneity of the CTR values into account. Moreover, the endogeneity test can be performed using alternative instrument variables if data becomes available.

Nevertheless, the limitations of this study provide several avenues for future research. First, a better measurement for ad copy quality can be created to examine the relationship between ad (content) quality and ad position more accurately. The quality of the ad copies can be facilitated using techniques like text mining to extract marketing cues in ads related to a specific keyword. Another roadmap closely related to the ad content quality is the availability of competitive data on ad Quality Score which can be easily used to investigate its relationship with ad position in SERP. Quality Score is currently only accessible by advertisers through their AdWords account.

Another extension to the results of this study can be the use of simultaneous regression equations (e.g., two-stage least squares - 2SLS) and longitudinal data to model the relationship between ad position and its determinants (both endogenous and exogenous variables). Finally, a closely related area of research which has recently emerged in this area is the investigation of competition between search engines as marketplace auctioneers (Chang et al. 2010). A possible future research problem could be how search engine market share dominance can affect advertisers in developing their advertising strategies in the sponsored search market.

B.2.2. Robustness. The research model was also tested to see if it can yield similar results for data in a different period. In studying the robustness of the model, it is also important to check for endogeneity. The regression model is usually used to predict values for a dependent variable and endogeneity becomes critical if there is a recursive relationship between the dependent variable and any of the predictor variables. Endogeneity has been tested in previous research in sponsored search advertising (e.g., Animesh et al. 2007, Ghose and Yang 2009). Although the research setting in this study is different compared to keyword level, panel data setting in previous studies, existence of endogeneity can affect the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimation in above analyses. Consequently, the following test was performed to validate the above results.

Specifically, the results of the OLS regression can be biased if the problem of endogeneity exists between dependent and independent variables. If in a regression model, independent variable X causes dependent variable Y, and vice versa (Y causes X), the model suffers from simultaneous causality bias. The simultaneity bias arises in many econometric contexts and can be detected by using instrument variables and two stage least-squares (2SLS) estimation. For example, Animesh et al. (2007) use “age” of the website as an instrument variable for “traffic rank” in order to examine the simultaneous relationship between “traffic rank” and “position” of the seller on sponsored listings.
