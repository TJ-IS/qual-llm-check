# Determinants of e-repurchase intentions: An integrative model of quadruple retention drivers

Hsien-Tung Tsai <sup>a,\*</sup>, Heng-Chiang Huang b

<sup>a</sup> Department of International Business, Yuan Ze University, Chung-Li, Taoyuan, Taiwan, ROC <sup>b</sup> Department of International Business, National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC

Received 26 August 2005; received in revised form 18 May 2006; accepted 5 November 2006 Available online 12 March 2007

## Abstract

Although researchers and practitioners in the field of e-commerce are now paying more attention to online customer loyalty, the focus previously was on overall customer satisfaction. Here, we drew on marketing and consumer behavior literature to formulate a conceptual framework that considered community-based, customization-based, desire-based, and constraint-based drivers of online customer retention. Furthermore, the potential interrelationships between these drivers were explored. We then empirically tested our hypotheses using data obtained from a large online retailing store in Taiwan. The results strongly supported most of the hypotheses. Theoretical and managerial implications of the findings were discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Community building; Customization; Overall satisfaction; Repurchase intention; Switching barriers

## 1. Introduction

Online customer retention has attracted considerable attention in recent years, partly because it is a means of gaining competitive advantage. Despite its importance, however, only limited attempts have been made to understand online customer loyalty and investigate its factors and interrelationships. We started this study in an attempt to fill some of the gaps left unexplained.

The first gap concerns the theoretical validity and empirical applicability of TAM in an online repeat purchase context. Although a website is a form of IT, there are discrepancies between the use of the Web and online purchasing, especially with respect to repeat purchasing. In studying online customer shopping behavior, researchers have suggested that a customerfocused consumption community [25,39], customization [15,38], and overall satisfaction [42] were important in maintaining long-term online buyer–seller relationships. Although perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness may partially explain customers’ initial motives for using the WWW, they offer limited explanations for why customers remain with a particular e-retailer. In fact, some researchers have suggested that additional factors should be incorporated into TAM, or that it should be integrated with other theories to improve its specificity and explanatory power [37].

The second gap consists of the lack of knowledge about the interrelationships between the determinants of repurchase intentions. Several studies in both marketing theory and practice have attempted to investigate some of the relationships between repurchase intention and the antecedents in an offline context (e.g., [13,22,26]), but the complex interrelationships between the determinants are still not well understood, particularly in an online environment.

Against this backdrop, we focused specifically on customer perspectives in an attempt to contribute to ecommerce literature by examining the above gaps. First, we considered why online customers remain with a particular e-retailer. A key argument in the marketing literature is that customers are motivated to remain with a provider due to one or more of four attachments [8,9,11,24,43]: constraint-based (they ‘‘have to’’), desire-based (they ‘‘want to’’), customization-based (they can ‘‘specify modifications’’), community-based (they ‘‘flock to’’). In our study, we proposed and empirically analyzed a conceptual framework that considered these customer retention drivers in the ecommerce environment.

Second, we drew on marketing literature to posit the relationships between the main drivers that could lead to the creation of a retention strategy. For example, there is evidence that the effects of community building and customization on repurchase intentions are mediated by switching barriers and overall satisfaction (e.g., [31]). Empirical investigation of these issues was thus important, as building and maintaining retention involves investment of time and money. Thus, knowledge of the interrelationships would provide input for managers when making resource allocation decisions.

## 2. Conceptual framework and hypothesis development

In developing our conceptual framework, we reviewed the literature on consumer behavior and marketing that was relevant to our research focus. Fig. 1 presents an overview of the framework and its constructs.

## 2.1. Determinants of e-repurchase intentions

## 2.1.1. Community building

Marketing and consumer behavior researchers (e.g., [28]) have developed a concept of commercial communities. We extended this concept by considering a consumption community consisting of various participants (customers, marketers, and other interested parties) and the set of ties associated with them (shopping in a particular online store). Some researchers have suggested that proactive cultivation of communities by service providers encourages the development of relationships that have a direct long-term influence on customer loyalty [16]. Winer [46] even argued that developing a strong consumption community could be a critical step in creating relationship marketing, which stresses maintaining and enhancing long-term customer relationships. In line with this view, Muniz and O’Guinn, in their study of brand community, suggested that community members feel connection to a brand and, more importantly, a stronger connection with one another, even if they have never met. Furthermore, by sharing interests and expertise within a community, customers feel comfortable and secure because there are many like-minded others that generates stronger repurchase intention. Such claims reflect the findings of McAlexander et al. that community-building efforts resulted in significantly increased repurchase rates among Jeep owners, even though they were offered superior economic terms by competing brands. Therefore, we proposed:

## 2.1.2. Customization

The Internet enables e-retailers to better understand each customer’s needs, which facilitates provision of specially tailored offers. From a customer perspective, customization creates a perception of increased choice and thus allows the e-retailer to focus on what the customer really wants. Furthermore, Dellaert and Stremersch [14] suggested that customers attach a higher utility to products, services, and the transactional environment when they feel it is personalized for them. Much research has suggested that customization is one of the key determinants of online customer retention [38]. For example, Ansari and Mela [5] found that highly customized offerings based on customer preference data lead to stronger customer loyalty. Consistent with the concept of one-to-one marketing relationships, Simonson suggested that customization could enhance the perceived fit of customers, and therefore directly strengthen repurchase intent. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 2. Customization has a positive influence on repurchase intentions.

## 2.1.3. Overall satisfaction

Online customer satisfaction has often been used to measure e-business success [30]. Overall satisfaction is generally defined as a positive affective state resulting from a global evaluation of performance based on past purchasing and consumption experience [2,27,41]. A number of studies in Internet-enabled contexts have shown the link between overall satisfaction and repurchase intentions. For example, Szymanski and Hise suggested that the overall satisfaction experienced by online customers reduced the perceived benefits of switching to other e-retailers. Such arguments were supported by the findings of Anderson and Srinivasan [4]. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 3. Overall satisfaction has a positive influence on repurchase intentions.

## 2.1.4. Switching barriers

Switching barriers are defined as the degree to which customers experience a sense of being ‘‘locked into’’ a relationship based on the economic, social, or psychological costs associated with leaving a particular service provider [1,35]. Gruen et al. [19] used the term ‘‘continuance commitment’’ as a measure of the extent to which a buyer was psychologically bound to a seller. For example, when switching providers online customers must invest time and effort in learning about a new interface and how to use new features. They must also allocate irrecoverable resources to develop a relationship with a new e-retailer. Burnham et al. suggested that switching barriers prevented switching when there was a negative situation, such as a temporary decline in service quality. The barriers allow time for the provider to rebuild to higher satisfaction levels [10].

Several conceptual and empirical studies have posited switching barriers as a key determinant of repurchase intentions. For example, Wathne et al., drawing on economic sociology literature, suggested that switching providers would mean sacrificing the utility of an existing relationship. Therefore, switching barriers would be a psychological loss that customers would rather not incur. Furthermore, a study of Jones et al. provided empirical support for the view that consumers who felt ‘‘locked in’’ were more likely to remain with a provider. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 4. Switching barriers have a positive influence on repurchase intentions.

## 2.2. Interrelationships between the determinants

## 2.2.1. Community building and customization

Communities encourage members to engage in a dialogue and share their experiences and expectations [40]. This effect is important, because the communities provide more information about customers’ past purchases, enabling providers to understand clients’ desires and needs. More importantly, they can offer nearcustomized products to similar customer segments: this is termed ‘‘customization-responsiveness squeeze’’ allowing differentiated products to be delivered in record time [29]. In addition, customers are no longer passive observers, but become active players in co-creating value [34]. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 5. Community building has a positive influence on customization.

## 2.2.2. Community building and satisfaction

As mentioned above, community building not only enables e-retailers to gain knowledge about customer preferences, but also offers a platform for communication. Through such platforms, customers need no longer wait for a (possibly slow) response from their e-retailer, but can communicate directly with other participants. Furthermore, by creating a situation in which customers feel valued and appreciated (resulting in satisfaction), they may become further committed to the particular e-retailer. Thus community building increased customer satisfaction, which in turn, positively affects repurchase intention. Muniz and O’Guinn, using ethnographic and computer-mediated environmental data, found that building brand communities exerted a positive influence on marketing outcomes such as customer satisfaction. Similarly, McAlexander et al. demonstrated that proactive cultivation of a community by marketers can significantly enhance participants’ post-purchase evaluation. Therefore, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 6. Community building has a positive influence on overall satisfaction.

## 2.2.3. Customization and switching barriers

Customization may also influence switching barriers (e.g., [44]). Theoretically, it can be considered a cognition-based construct, whereas a barrier is primarily an affective and evaluative response. Social science literature has suggested that cognitive thought processes can trigger affective responses [45] and thus that customization can affect feelings about switching barriers. On the other hand, Watson et al. postulated that customized offers created an impediment to switching, because customers may have to accept inferior service during the time that the new service provider is learning about the needs of the customer. Furthermore, Gwinner et al. [21] indicated that products or services tailored to meet customers’ needs offered customization benefits, including ‘‘the customer’s perception of preferential treatment, extra attention or personal recognition, and special service not available to other customers.’’ This notion is also reflected in Guiltinan’s [20] view that, as service providers offer additional types of special benefits (i.e., customized services), emotional switching barriers increase. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 7. Customization has a positive influence on switching barriers.

## 2.2.4. Satisfaction and switching barriers

Overall satisfaction reflects online customers cumulative impressions of an e-retailer’s core service or product performance. This is an important way to persuade and encourage more specific asset investment. However, it increases customer dependence on a particular e-retailer and thus serves as an exit barrier [23]. According to Expectations-Disconfirmation Theory, when customers are satisfied with an eretailer, they believe that the e-retailer will deliver what they expect [32,33]. If the relationship terminates, switching costs are generated. However, when a consumer is satisfied with a relationship, the customer lacks motivation to seek alternatives. Thus viable alternatives appear to be lacking and the perceived benefits of changing are relatively low. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 8. Satisfaction has a positive influence on switching barriers.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Measures

All the focal constructs of the model were measured using multiple items based on validated scales obtained from the literature, and all the items were assessed via a seven-point Likert scale ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree.’’ We measured community building with six items adapted from McAlexander et al. and Dholakia et al.; these reflected the degree to which the customers felt connected to other customers, marketers, and other parties in the community. Based on measures developed by various researchers (e.g., [22]), we evaluated overall customer satisfaction by a four-item scale, which assessed respondents’ general satisfaction, confirmation of expectations, and the divergence of the actual purchase from the hypothetically ideal product or service. Similarly, a switching barrier scale was constructed to estimate the extent to which customers experienced a sense of being locked-in. A five-item switching barrier scale was adapted from the continuance commitment scale of Bansal et al. Based on measures developed by Srinivasan et al., we measured customization with six items that tapped the extent to which customers received customized offerings. In addition, we adapted the four-item scale used by Burnham et al. and Bansal et al. to assess customers’ intentions to remain with their current service providers. We also controlled for perceived service quality in testing the hypotheses, because it could influence customers’ intentions to remain with a particular e-retailer. Perceived service quality was measured by six items (adapted from Harris and Goode), which reflected the degree to which customers perceived that e-commerce facilitated efficient and effective shopping, purchasing, and delivery. ## 3.2. Sampling and data collection procedures

We collected data through a survey of customers of a well-known online store in Taiwan. We used only one online store for two reasons:

1. There are significant differences between stores. These make it difficult to identify appropriate respondents and to design a questionnaire that has consistent and readily understood language across stores [17].

2. We needed to identify four types of customers. By using the customer base of only one store, they could be found easily and clearly.

We used a web-based survey for our study. This was more effective for qualifying potential participants as appropriate subjects. The online questionnaire was set up on a survey portal and sent to 1750 customers selected randomly from the mailing list of the online store. The participants were told that the survey was for both educational and managerial purposes. As an incentive to participate, they were also told that they would receive a specially tailored gift worth NTD200 (approximately US\$7).

After discarding incomplete responses, 463 responses were usable for further analysis (a usable response rate of approximately 26.4%). The useable surveys were divided between men and women in roughly equal proportions. The respondents were well-educated, with about 92% holding a college degree or a higher qualification.

## 4. Results

Following Anderson and Gerbing [3], we adopted a two-stage approach to test the models. First, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to examine the construct validity (the convergent and discriminant validity). Second, we tested the theoretical model using the partial least squares (PLS) method [12]. PLS is suitable when the focus of the research is on theory development, while LISREL would have been preferred for confirmatory testing of the fit of a theoretical model to observed data. To cross-check our results, we fitted the structural model using LISREL and obtained consistent path estimates in terms of both magnitude and significance, suggesting the robustness of our model.

## 4.1. Measurement model

To evaluate the quality of the measurement model for the full sample, we examined the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the constructs in our model. Reliability assessment was based on the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). As shown in Table 1, the CRs ranged from 0.87 to 0.96 and AVEs ranged from 0.57 to 0.85; these were all above the recommended cut-off levels of 0.70 and 0.50, respectively [7]. Furthermore, the item loadings ranged from 0.61 to 0.94; they were thus above the recommended cut-off level of 0.60, demonstrating adequate convergent validity. Robust evidence of discriminant validity was found through applying a chi-square difference test in which the correlations between all possible pairs of constructs were first freely estimated, and then made equal. To determine whether the two constructs were significantly different [18], we examined whether the constraint caused a significant degradation in fit. All chi-square differences were significant at the 0.05 level, suggesting that the constructs under analysis were distinct and discriminately valid.

## 4.2. Structural model

Fig. 1 shows the results of the hypothesized structural model test, including the sign and significance of the path coefficients, and the $\hat { R } ^ { 2 }$ values, which indicated how well the antecedents explained an endogenous variable. Both the path coefficients and the $\scriptstyle { \bar { R } } ^ { 2 }$ values were used to assess the model’s nomological validity [36]. An examination of the $R ^ { 2 }$ values demonstrated that the model explained a substantial amount of the variance in the outcome variables; for example, it explained 64% of the variance in repurchase intentions.

Hypothesis 1 posited that community building is positively and directly linked to repurchase intentions. From the structural modeling results and standardized $\gamma$ coefficients of 0.25 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , this contention was strongly supported. However, the coefficient of the impact of customization on repurchase intentions was not significant $( \gamma = 0 . 0 9 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , which showed lack of support for Hypothesis 2. Our analysis of the results also showed that overall satisfaction influenced repurchase intentions positively $( \gamma = 0 . 1 9 ,  p < 0 . 0 1 )$ providing some support for Hypothesis 3. Hypothesis 4 proposed a positive and direct relationship between switching barriers and repurchase intentions. The results also strongly supported this view $( \gamma = 0 . 1 9$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . As predicted in Hypotheses 5 and $^ { 6 , }$ community building was positively and significantly related to customization and overall satisfaction, with standardized $\gamma$ coefficients of 0.64 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and 0.63 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , respectively. Hypothesis 7 was also supported, as the path from customization to switching barriers was positive and significant, with a standardized g coefficient of 0.36 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Finally, consistent with Hypothesis 8, overall satisfaction appeared to affect switching barriers positively $( \gamma = 0 . 3 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Perceived service quality, i.e., the control variable, was also positively correlated to repurchase intentions.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

The main goal of our research was to enhance understanding of the factors that influence online customers’ repurchase intentions. Some marketing scholars have posited that service providers appear to be stuck in a ‘‘satisfaction trap,’’ a myopic belief that overall satisfaction and perceived quality are the only drivers needed to retain customers. Our study was one of the first attempts to conceptualize and test online customer loyalty empirically within a multi-dimensional framework. We focused specifically on customer perspectives to develop this framework that considered a set of customer retention determinants and their potential relationships. The overall pattern of our results has presented a more complex picture than that presented in current literature.

Although, in most online customer retention research, the focus has been on overall satisfaction, our study demonstrated that switching barriers and community building were also highly significant. From a theoretical perspective, the results suggested the need to extend existing theories of online customer retention by showing that constraint-based and community-based drivers were also critical to retaining online customers. Specifically, community building dominated the other factors, and exceeded the combined effect of overall satisfaction and switching barriers on repurchase intentions. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that switching barriers in online settings were positively related to a customer’s intention to remain with a particular eretailer, and even had a direct effect that was equivalent to overall satisfaction.

Surprisingly, our results showed that customization had no direct effect on repurchase intentions. A possible explanation was that the Internet and other informationprocessing technologies in Taiwan enabled all eretailers to understand each customer’s requirements easily without high overheads. Consequently, an eretailer offering customized services or products was not perceived as different or non-substitutable, since competitors could also provide customized offerings that could be more attractive.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

e-Retailers must build and manage high quality, long-term relationships with their customers. Therefore, our study has important implications for the management of online customer retention. It suggests that eretailers should pay more attention to cultivating a sustainable consumption community to participate in forums and present customers’ preferences in providing tailor-made offerings. Specifically, through community building, consumers need not wait but can communicate directly with other consumers and obtain information or solicit opinions.

Constraint-based drivers were also shown to be valuable tools to help service providers prevent switching. Although we found evidence of the significant effect of switching barriers on repurchase intentions, it was necessary to exercise caution because of the potential dangers associated with an overly aggressive use of such barriers.

In addition, though many studies have pointed out the benefits of customized products and services in online contexts (e.g., [6]), our results indicated that customization was a necessary, but not sufficient, condition of maintaining loyalty. In particular, the Internet enabled all e-retailers to provide online customers with tailor-made options or products customized to their tastes. Thus, customized offerings in online settings may attract customer attention, but they may not directly and effectively enhance customer loyalty.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our study had some limitations. One was the applicability of results to other contexts. Our results were in accordance with the theoretically specified causal sequence outlined in the hypotheses; however, cross-sectional data simply does not permit us to rule out some alternative models of, for example, the relationships between community participation and consumer attitudes or behavior. Ideally, our study would have benefited from a time lag between the measurement of the independent and dependent variables so that causal relationships could be determined. Finally, our sample had a potential bias because all participants belonged to one online store. Hence, our sample was not representative of all online customers in world-wide ecommerce businesses.

## 6. Conclusion

It is widely recognized that customer retention has a powerful impact on the performance of Internetbased businesses. We adopted a marketing perspective toward online consumer loyalty. Our study advanced knowledge in several ways. First, the results suggested that customers are motivated to remain with a particular e-retailer due to constraint-based, desirebased, customization-based, and community-based attachments. In particular, community-based attachment dominated the other factors. Second, we found that switching barriers in online settings were positively related to repurchase intentions. When customers perceived an e-retailer as unique or considered that the switching costs associated with changing was too high, they locked into the relationship and buffered themselves from information about alternative providers. Third, we showed that customization had no direct effect on repurchase intentions but had a significant indirect effect. Consequently, customized offerings in online settings may attract customer attention, but they may not directly and effectively enhance customer loyalty.
