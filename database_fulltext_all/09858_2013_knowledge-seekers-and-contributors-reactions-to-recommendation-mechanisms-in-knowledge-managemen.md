# Knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ reactions to recommendation mechanisms in knowledge management systems

## A B S T R A C T

We examined the behavior of knowledge seekers and contributors to an internal Knowledge Management System (KMS) in a multinational organization. The system has two selection mechanisms. based on semantic algorithms and user ratings. The first utilizes an algorithm to ‘measure’ the quality of knowledge contributions and ranks them accordingly. while the second averages the ratings that knowledge items receive from KMS users. Building on appraisal theory, we found that knowledge seekers and contributors reacted differently to the two mechanisms. The rating-based rankings positively influenced knowledge seekers’ tendency to access, comment on, and spread the knowledge shared in the KMS, while the algorithm-based ranking positively influenced knowledge contributors’ to continue sharing knowledge via the system. Moreover, shorter (or longer) time delay between the time that the knowledge was shared and the time when knowledge contributors received their first comments seemed to positively (or negatively) influence the contributors’ tendency to continue sharing knowledge via the KMS. Our study adds to the existing KMS literature by investigating knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ reactions to the two different knowledge recommendation mechanisms, and recommends that managers understand the importance of implementing algorithm-based rankings in their KMS as well as the simpler and more commonly adopted rating-based ranking.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge is one of the foundations of a firm’s competitive advantage and one of the primary drivers of a firm’s value [11]. To enable their workers to store the knowledge they carry in their minds, and make it available for wider use, many organizations have implemented knowledge management systems (KMS) to allow their employees to share their knowledge easily, and search for and retrieve the knowledge (provided by others) that they need to complete their tasks. Easy access to shared knowledge is highly desirable, but its large volume may overwhelm knowledge seekers [9]. KMS must be designed and managed in ways that enable knowledge workers to find high-quality knowledge.

Many organizations are implementing recommendation mechanisms into their KMS [1], which can be split into three types: content- or algorithm-based (which apply predefined algorithms to measure the quality of the knowledge available), collaborativeor rating-based (which compute the average ratings given by KMS users to the knowledge), or historical-based (which tracks users’ behavior to display knowledge that may be of interest). To avoid the possibility of KMS users missing relevant knowledge, as well as to allow them to find high-quality knowledge without feeling overwhelmed, the most popular KMS knowledge recommendation design combines algorithm- and rating-based mechanisms. However, the algorithm-based (semantic) score and average rating of a particular knowledge may differ, leading to the question: Which will have greater influence on knowledge seekers behaviors?

Poston and Speier [9] conducted experiments and found that KMS knowledge ratings had strong influence on knowledge seekers’ search and evaluation processes. Although this finding is valuable, their study was conducted in a non-organizational context, as have most previous studies, so its generalizability to organizational contexts requires further investigation. Another gap we identified was that researchers have mainly focused on the relationship between the perceived quality of the knowledge shared in the KMS and how often the KMS was used for seeking knowledge [6] – i.e., their focus was not on the knowledge, but rather on the KMS. We therefore decided to examine knowledge seekers’ reactions to the quality of the knowledge items they found via the KMS, as measured by their semantic scores and user ratings.

Thus we investigated the extent to which users access, comment on, and spread the knowledge they find in the KMS. Specifically, we decided to answer the question: Which knowledge quality measure – the algorithm-based (semantic) score or the average user rating – has the stronger influence on the likelihood that knowledge seekers will access, comment on, and spread particular knowledge?

Successful knowledge sharing depends on knowledge seekers accessing, commenting on, and spreading the knowledge in the KMS, and also on knowledge contributors populating the KMS with new, valuable material. Hence, we were also interested in evaluating knowledge contributors’ reactions to these scores and the ratings that other users gave to the knowledge shared via the KMS. According to appraisal theory, the same situation, object, or event can trigger disparate reactions from different individuals or groups because it adds to their wellbeing or is relevant to their goals. We therefore proposed that knowledge seekers and contributors may react differently to the same recommendation mechanism because it has different significance to them or their circumstances. Accordingly, even though a shared knowledge item has a high average user rating, that may not be enough to influence its contributor to continue sharing his or her knowledge, as that individual may perceive such ratings as being less significant to his or her wellbeing. This leads us to ask: Which knowledge quality measure has a stronger influence on knowledge contributors’ decision to continue adding knowledge to the KMS – the algorithm-based score of their previously contributed knowledge or the average rating it attracts from its users?

We analyze the log files of a multinational organization’s KMS, which uses both algorithm- and rating-based mechanisms, from its official launch in late October 2009 until the end of April 2010. We also monitored the knowledge contributors’ reactions to semantic scores and average ratings (through their posting of new knowledge), and those of knowledge seekers to these same measures (by counting how often they accessed particular knowledge items, the numbers of their comments, and the frequency with which they forward the items to others).

While Ma and Agarwal [8] have investigated the effect of such IT artifacts on personal information, reputation and the ranking of artifacts on knowledge contributions, the context of their study was two non-organizational virtual communities (i.e., one for emotional-support, the other a common-interest community), and they surveyed their members’ perceptions rather than analyzing their actual behavior in response to those artifacts.

Indeed, most KMS studies in organizations have employed survey methodology, and thus measured perceptions, not behavior.

## 3. Appraisal theory as the theoretical underpinning

A central tenet of appraisal theory is that emotions are elicited and differentiated according to a person’s subjective evaluation or appraisal of the significance of a situation, object, or event: the classic approach suggests that, in evaluating the significance, individuals use a fixed set of dimensions or criteria, including their needs or goal. So the aim of such research is to identify the features of emotion-eliciting events that are of major significance to an individual’s needs or goals. However, since participants may construct a rationale for their emotional response or make socially acceptable representations of emotional meaning, the causal relationship between appraisals and emotions can be ambiguous, with appraisals appearing as both consequences and as antecedents of emotions.

To avoid this problem, it has been argued that emotions involve states of readiness for an action elicited by events that individuals appraise as being emotionally relevant to them, with different appraisals eliciting different states of action readiness. Events are appraised as emotionally relevant when they appear to favor or harm the individual’s needs or goals. States of action readiness – the individual’s readiness to engage in interaction with the environment – may be revealed by the execution of such reactions.

We examined the behavior of knowledge seekers and contributors to the emotion-eliciting features of a KMS which, in our study, are its algorithm- and rating-based knowledge recommendation mechanisms. If these features are of great significance to the knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ goals, they may elicit positive reactions.

## 4. Knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ behaviors

Knowledge contributors must be willing to part with their knowledge and share it via the KMS – indeed, the success of a KMS depends on people contributing content to populate it [4,7]. Typically, knowledge contributors log into the system, fill out a form describing their contribution, and either attach a document or post content into a text box: this can be a one-time or a repeated action. Having invested funds in a KMS, organizations desire a continued inflow of knowledge.

However it is possible that a particular item of knowledge in a KMS may never be accessed or viewed, so another factor for success is that people seek its knowledge. Thus we had to investigate knowledge seekers’ attitudes toward the knowledge stored in the KMS. A knowledge seeker typically logs into the system, types keywords to search for the knowledge they require, and then retrieves and examines the results. The seeker can click on those results to access a particular knowledge item, or forward it to others who might also be interested in it. So the dependent

## 5. Research model

The organizational KMS under study has two embedded knowledge recommendation mechanisms. The first is an algorithm-based mechanism by which the quality of knowledge items residing in the KMS is measured by an engine with sophisticated algorithms that compute a score (and thus a ranking to their value). The second mechanism is rating-based; using this KMS users assign ratings to stored knowledge items to record how useful they find them. All KMS users can view these average ratings and scores.

Appraisal theory holds that one dimension that individuals use to measure a situation or event is its significance to their wellbeing or goal (its motivational relevance [10]) which thus influences their behavior, and is not determined by the objectivenature of the stimulus but bytheirevaluation of it. Inour study, weargue that thesignificance of a particular knowledge recommendation mechanism to knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ goals may determine their behavior.

The major goal for knowledge seekers is to obtain high quality knowledge that is current, relevant and accurate, either to allow them to accomplish their tasks more effectively or to broaden their knowledge. But knowledge seekers may find the process of retrieving high quality knowledge in a KMS tedious and complex [2]. The use of a recommendation mechanism such as semantic score or average rating can assist knowledge seekers in finding high quality knowledge; both can be significant in achieving their goals, and so influence the numbers of knowledge items they access, the numbers of comments they add, and the extent to which they spread the knowledge they retrieve from the KMS. It would seem likely that the higher the semantic score and average rating of a particular knowledge item, the more knowledge seekers will access, commenting on, and spread that knowledge, so we hypothesized:

Hypotheses 1a–c. The semantic scores of a shared knowledge item will positively influence its attractiveness, which is measured by the number of times it is accessed and commented on, and how frequently it is spread.

Hypotheses 2a–c. The average user ratings of a shared knowledge item will positively influence its attractiveness, which is measured by the number of times it is accessed and commented on and how frequently it is spread.

Reputation has recently gained in importance in most organizations, while the old social contracts between a firm and its workers based on length of service and loyalty are becoming relatively less important. Knowledge contributors may desire to increase their prestige by projecting an image of being knowledgeable and possessing invaluable expertise. Providing others with high quality knowledge can increase one’s prestige. Thus, the significance of the semantic score and average rating of knowledge individuals contribute could provide different outcomes. The semantic score could be seen as more significant than the average rating – while the latter reflects feedback from other KMS users; however, the semantic score, has been approved by the organization, and would seem to reflect the organization’s view of the value of the person concerned. Accordingly, we hypothesized

Hypothesis 3. The semantic scores gained by the knowledge contrib uted will positively influence the tendency to continue contributing knowledge.

Hypothesis 4. The average rating given to the knowledge contributed will have no influence on the tendency to continue contributing knowledge.

Both extrinsic and intrinsic goals are motivators of human behavior. Besides increasing their prestige in the workplace, knowledge contributors may wish to help others etc.: previous studies on altruism have shown that people enjoy and derive pleasure from such acts. We argue that the time interval between contributors’ sharing knowledge items and the first comment they receive can be a proxy of the level of interest others take in the knowledge they contribute (which may influence them in making further contributions). Thus, we hypothesized

Hypothesis 5. The time gap between knowledge being contributed and other users responding will influence contributors’ tendency to continue contributing knowledge – the shorter the response time, the more likely they are to contribute again.

## 6. Research methodology

To empirically test our research model, we analyzed the log files of a multinational firm’s KMS from its official launch in October 2009 to the end of April 2010. For the algorithm-based recommendation mechanism, the knowledge items contributed to the KMS were transferred to a semantic engine provided by another organization that had originally developed it. Prior to the official launch of the KMS there had been close consultation between the top level executives in the focal firm and its KMS developers about the algorithms used to measure knowledge quality, with a few focal firm members participating in a trial KMS version for six months while the semantic scores of the knowledge they contributed were closely monitored by top level executives. For the rating-based recommendation mechanism, the KMS users themselves assigned ratings to the knowledge they accessed using the KMS: the average user ratings and semantic rankings were available to all KMS users. In Fig. 1, the number in the right panel reflected the semantic engine scoring, and the stars below the knowledge item title indicated its average user rating: higher numerical scores imply more valuable knowledge while more stars showed knowledge rated more valuable by users.

The target KMS users are managerial-level employees, who were categorized into three levels: Level 1: market research managers, innovation concept managers, and area key account managers, etc.; Level 2: area head of marketing, area relationship manager, and area managing director, etc.; Level 3: area president, and area vice president of sales. These labels were given by the organization under study. Over the 6-month study period, there were 37 knowledge seekers, of which 24 were also knowledge contributors. Table 2 shows their levels of knowledge seeking and contributing.

In all, 160 knowledge items were contributed and 181 comments posted to the KMS during the study period. Table 3 summarizes all the variables involved in our research, Fig. 1 shows how they were reflected in the KMS and collected from the logs, and Table 4 presents their descriptive statistics.

To test Hypotheses 1 and 2, we utilized generalized linear models with maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) nested by the levels of the KMS users. Since the dependent variable for Hypotheses 3–5 (Contribution Continuation (CC)) is non-metric, we tested it with logit regression models, which again were nested by the levels of the KMS users. ## 8. Implications

Our findings must be interpreted in the light of the study’s major limitation: that we did not incorporate survey methodology to complement the explanations of our findings from the objective data we gained. By using only objective data, we attempted to minimize bias in our findings, but adding data about users’ perceptions might have added richness to our findings. Nevertheless, the study’s longitudinal findings were based on the objective data logs from an organizational KMS. Moreover, our study’s findings, have important implications for practitioners.

## 8.1. Implications for researchers

To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet investigated how knowledge seekers and contributors react to knowledge recommendation mechanisms in an organizational KMS. Our study adds to the extant KMS studies by investigating their actual behavior when using a KMS which incorporated two recommendation mechanisms. Depending on their goals, the knowledge seekers may appraise and react similarly or differently to the two different knowledge recommendation mechanisms. Our study examined the knowledge sharing reactions of knowledge contributors, as well as knowledge seekers behaviors in accessing, commenting on, and spreading the knowledge held in an organizational KMS.

Our findings revealed that knowledge contributors reacted positively to the algorithm-based knowledge recommendation mechanism: they were more likely to share new knowledge items via the KMS when the knowledge they had already contributed gained high semantic scores. The average user ratings their contributions attracted were of no great significance to them. Knowledge contributors may have regarded the semantic score as more important because it better reflects the feedback from the organization.

We found that knowledge seekers reacted positively to the rating-based knowledge recommendation mechanism, showing a greater tendency to access, comment on, and spread knowledge items which had gained high average ratings from previous seekers: but the semantic score of shared knowledge items had little influence on knowledge seekers’ actions. It seems that, while knowledge contributors were very concerned about the consequences of their contributions to their career advancement, knowledge seekers were more eager to gain new knowledge, and relied more on other KMS users’ ratings to guide their new knowledge searches.

## 8.2. Implications for practitioners

We suggest that, for optimal outcomes, organizations should embed both types of recommendation mechanisms in their KMS. In terms of simplicity, the rating-based recommendation mechanism is simpler to set up and run – indeed, our focal company had to hire an outside specialist firm to help implement its algorithm-based mechanism, a process which involved close consultations and several trials with top level focal-firm executives before the semantic knowledge-measuring algorithm could be finalized.

It is important to note that the response time to the contributed knowledge (i.e., the time interval between the contributed knowledge being lodged in the KMS and the first user comment being received) affects whether knowledge contributors go on contributing. This suggests that it is important to have a dedicated KMS administrator who can help encourage new knowledge contributions by encouraging faster responses. This will be especially pertinent if the organization only implements a userrating-based recommendation mechanism.

A KMS administrator can also prove to be useful in situations where organizations embed both types of mechanism in their KMS, and knowledge contributions only gain either high semantic scores or high average ratings, but not both. In our study of organization’s KMS, we found no correlation between the two types of recommendation. The success of a KMS depends on people contributing content to populate it as well as people seeking knowledge from it, so such situations are not optimal. KMS administrators can try to avoid them by promoting knowledge items with high semantic scores, and by commenting on how they benefit the organization. On the other hand, such situations might be avoided by ensuring that the design of the algorithm-based recommendation mechanism involved both top management and several target KMS users.

Finally, while we found that the algorithm-based recommendation mechanism positively influenced the likelihood of continued knowledge contribution, this effect may not hold in the long term if the knowledge contributors encounter difficulty in connecting their activities in the KMS with their performance evaluations. For a long term effect, organizations should include the semantic scores of the knowledge employees contribute to the firm’s KMS in their performance measurement systems, to convey the message that the company recognizes and values their effort in contributing knowledge to the firm.

## 9. Conclusion

Many organizations have begun embracing and promoting KMS, but have found that their employees have been overwhelmed by the amount of knowledge thus made available. Longitudinally analyzing the database of an organizational KMS, we showed that, while the user-rating-based recommendation mechanism was the priority for knowledge seekers, an algorithm-based recommendation mechanism was more influential for knowledge contributors. If organizations decide to adopt only the latter mechanism (the easier to implement), they should invest in KMS administrators whose task is to monitor and encourage the attachment of comments to each posted knowledge item, since, unlike user ratings (which are insignificant in this regard), the response time to each knowledge post directly affects knowledge contributors’ continued appetite for making contributions to the KMS. The sooner their knowledge posts gains comments, the more enthusiastic they will be to continue contributing knowledge items to the KMS – which is vital if the system is to grow and become an increasingly comprehensive and useful resource for the firm’s knowledge seekers.
