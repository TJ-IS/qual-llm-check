## A B S T R A C T

This paper discusses the process of combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units into a hybrid organization. While most previous studies focus on public–private hybridization, this study focuses on the online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine type and discusses the combining process from the perspective of boundary permeability. By using Suning as a case study, we explore how a large retailer combines its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine units. The process model demonstrates that an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization is hybridized through three multiple and con<sup>fl</sup>icting boundary permeability paths, namely complete, partial, and preventive permeability paths. Each path includes a capacity-seeking process in which the organization improves its capabilities after developing strategies. Corresponding to the three paths, the organization develops absorptive, symbiotic, and preservative strategies. It then enhances its capabilities accordingly, including its assimilative, ambidextrous, and autonomous capabilities, to implement the necessary boundary permeability behaviors and form a hybrid organization.

## 1. Introduction

As an increasing number of online business units are operated by traditional companies [17], many companies are running online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units simultaneously. Consequently, many of them face the problem of combining these two con<sup>fl</sup>icting business units into one to operate them synergistically. Combining two units with different institutional logics leads to the formation of a hybrid organization [1,5]. Online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units have different institutional logics because the organizational behavior and consumer purchase behavior in these two units are different [9,31,32]; hence, a hybrid organization will be generated by combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. Although this combination leads to a new type of hybrid organization, only a few studies have discussed its formation process; thus, how such online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations originate remains unknown.

Studying online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations is of paramount importance for two reasons. First, online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations are becoming increasingly prevalent. As the Internet becomes the main channel for selling products, many companies must operate online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units simultaneously [17]. However, online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units have different institutional logics, and because they sometimes share the same resources such as customers, con<sup>fl</sup>icts between the two units become inevitable [10]. To resolve these con<sup>fl</sup>icts, many companies have adopted the strategy of combining the two units. For example, 92% of retailers are combining their online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units to enhance operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [3], and a brick-and-mortar (B&M) model is now a key pro<sup>fi</sup>t growth point for retailers [10]. This increased combination of business units in companies has led to a greater prevalence of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations. Second, the mechanisms driving online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations need to be thoroughly explored. Previous studies on hybrid organizations have primarily discussed the combination of public and private units such as social enterprises incorporating social welfare and commercial logics [4,5], state-owned enterprises incorporating markets and hierarchy [7], and medical schools incorporating health care and academic logics [13]. Enterprises after acquisition incorporate short- and long-term orientations [47]. However, the institutional logics of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations are different from those of the public–private type. When combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, organizations need to incorporate decentralization and centralization logics [43]. However, few studies have discussed this type of hybrid organization, despite its prevalence and differentiation.

Combining two different business units is related to boundary managerial behaviors because the combination process involves breaking up the original units’ boundaries so that resources can enter or exit the units [2]. Thus, Borys and Jemison [6] suggest that boundary permeability could play an important role in the formation of a hybrid organization. However, previous studies on hybrid organizations have largely focused on multiplicity, disparity, and con<sup>fl</sup>ict among the combined units [5], and few studies have discussed the role of boundary managerial behaviors in the formation of a hybrid organization. Thus, our knowledge of boundary managerial behaviors during the combination process remains limited.

In summary, there are two important gaps in the existing literature. First, most studies on hybrid organizations have focused on public–private hybridization [4–6], while few studies have focused on online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations despite their prevalence. Second, although scholars have suggested that there is a close relationship between boundary management and the formation of hybrid organizations [5], few of them have conducted empirical studies on the formation of hybrid organizations from the perspective of boundary theory.

This paper aims to explore the actual formation process of an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization from the perspective of boundary permeability. We conducted an in-depth case study using the Suning’s case. Suning engaged in the largest combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units in China, involving 1696 of<sup>fl</sup>ine large-scale retail stores and >2 million SKU online retail stores. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the relevant theoretical studies. In Section 3, on the basis of interview data, we describe the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs involved in the combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. In Section 4, we discuss how online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units develop into hybrid organizations through boundary permeability. In Section 5, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and propose a theoretical model of hybrid organization formation. In Section 6, we analyze the theoretical contributions, practical contributions, and limitations.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Hybrid organization

“Hybrid organizations” are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as organizations that incorporate elements (such as activities, structures, processes, and identities) from different institutional logics [4,40]. Thus, the main issue in forming a hybrid organization is how to integrate the problems and potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts between different institutional logics [5]. Previous studies on the contradictions and con<sup>fl</sup>icts involved in the formation of a hybrid organization have largely focused on the perspectives of public versus private institutional logics. Public logic concerns the social welfare, whereas private logic focuses on the market and pro<sup>fi</sup>t [40]. Previous studies that consider social enterprises [5,40], public tasks, and market hybridization [27] among nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t and business organizations [26] involve public–private hybrid organizations. In this paper, we discuss a new process of hybridization: the combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine logics.

A hybrid organization is a paradox that has multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icting and competitive institutional logics [5,23]. The organization not only maintains the features of the original business units but also acquires new organizational features through hybridization [40]. Therefore, a hybrid organization must develop con<sup>fl</sup>icting strategies for hybridizing and implement two or more such strategies simultaneously [40,47]. In addition, previous studies have found that the formation of hybrid organizations mainly involves two mechanisms, namely, strategic responses and managerial responses [26]. An organization adopts strategic responses when it faces con<sup>fl</sup>icting external demands, while it adopts managerial responses when it faces con<sup>fl</sup>icting internal demands and identity claims.

## 2.2. Online and offline business unit

Although the institutional logics of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units differ greatly from one another in organizational operations, these differences are re<sup>fl</sup>ected internally through organizational behavior and externally through consumer behavior [9,31,32]. Thus, the combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units leads to a hybrid organization.

With regard to internal organizational behavior, the organizational structures of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units differ. Online business units are decentralized [9]. Their organizational functions, power, and staff transform from a centralized to a dispersed organizational structure, and the central decision-making authority does not make all of the decisions. Thus, online business units are open and transparent, have minimum control, and experience a high level of staff participation [32]. This decentralization feature is more favorable for organizations to adopt new technologies and thus enhance their novelty [9,36]. As compared with online business units, of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units tend to have a centralized organizational structure that emphasizes the effects of the authority. The functions, power, and staff are centralized, and the central decision-making authority makes the decisions. Moreover, of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units often communicate face to face with less openness and transparency than online business units [32]. Thus, of<sup>fl</sup>ine units are less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and innovative [36].

With regard to external consumer behavior, online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units also have different institutional logics that lie in the following two aspects. First, online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units have different market advantages. The advantages of online business units include more product information, a larger quantity of products, easier price comparisons, and fewer limitations regarding location and shopping time. For of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, the advantages are that consumers can feel, touch, and test products before purchase, and they can obtain in-store experiences and human interaction. In addition, consumers can pick up the goods at the same time they purchase them [31,43]. Second, the perceptions of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping are different. Price sensitivity [8], price elasticity, [20] and risk perception (such as privacy and security) [49] in online shopping are higher than in of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping. Thus, online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units tend to use different external marketing activities.

## 2.3. Boundary permeability and boundary permeability strategies

## 2.3.1. Boundary permeabilit

“Permeability” is the degree to which the focal organizational unit is open to in<sup>fl</sup>uence from its environment [34]. The main role of boundary permeability is to build good cooperative relationships between organizations [48]. Boundary permeability has a close relationship with the formation of a hybrid organization. Borys and Jemison [6] suggest that boundary permeability is an essential factor in the formation of a hybrid organization. Which resources permeate business units directly dictates the formation of a hybrid organization. Minkoff [37] argues that to form a hybrid organization, the business units must coordinate and communicate across the boundaries, and coordination across organizational boundaries is a major factor in the choice of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c mode of governance and in the design of the mechanism to create the hybrid arrangement. Thus, permeability of resources between business units is fundamental to the formation of a hybrid organization.

## 2.3.2. Boundary-permeability strategies

Organizations should implement different boundary permeability strategies simultaneously when combining business units [40,47]. Boundary permeability strategies can be divided into different categories based on the extent of permeability. Duxbury et al. [14] propose that there are two types of boundary permeability strategies: one is a segmented strategy, which strengthens boundaries to clearly distinguish the boundaries and identities among different business units, while the other is an integrated strategy, which blurs boundaries to make business units <sup>fl</sup>exibly permeate one another. Haspeslagh and Jemison [24] divide postacquisition integration approaches into three types, namely absorptive strategies, symbiotic strategies, and preservative strategies. In an absorptive strategy, one element adsorbs another element directly and assimilates it into its culture. A symbiotic strategy re<sup>fl</sup>ects a strategy with high interdependence while also requiring a high degree of organizational autonomy. A preservative strategy re<sup>fl</sup>ects a strategy requiring low interdependence and high autonomy; such that the hybridizing business units are integrated only to a modest degree and the units continue to conduct business in the same manner as before the integration. These three strategies are related to boundary permeability because more interdependent business units in a combination increase the need for boundary permeability [6].

In addition, different permeability strategies lead to different results. For example, Leifer and Delbecq [34] suggest that a manufacturing unit has rigid boundaries, whereas the boundaries of a sales unit blur. Schweizer [47] believes that in a hybrid organization, the boundaries are rigid for units with long-term motives, such as a research and development (R&D) unit, whereas the boundaries blur for those with short-term motives, such as marketing units.

## 2.4. Boundary permeability capabilities

When a company prepares to implement a certain strategy, it needs corresponding capabilities before it can succeed [11,53]. For different degrees of boundary permeability, a company must have assimilative, ambidextrous, and autonomous capabilities.

## 2.4.1. Assimilative capability

“Assimilative capability” refers to an organization’s capacity to reconstruct managerial and technological capabilities by absorbing knowledge, resources, or cultures obtained from external sources through open boundaries [56]. When an organization completes such an assimilation, the absorbed external resources are integrated into the organization as a whole, thus reaching a state of deployment or utilization. However, when the organization wants certain parts of the business units to be completely integrated, the boundaries between these business units must increasingly blur to allow each business unit to assimilate the other units’ resources [5]. Once the resources of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units assimilate, the boundaries blur and open accordingly; thus, the business units operate as one unit [47].

## 2.4.2. Ambidextrous capability

“Ambidextrous capability” refers to the behavioral capacity to simultaneously pursue con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands such as the ability to demonstrate alignment and adaptability across a business unit [21,25,45]. Ambidextrous capability is the capacity to balance different business units: it thus allows for the development of the units in a balanced manner [46]. When combining two business units, an organization needs cross-boundary ambidextrous capabilities to use the resources from both units effectively, and the units must partly open their boundaries, thus facilitating resource permeability and balancing the units’ operations [39,46]. To use ambidextrous capabilities, business units must maintain their independence and open parts of their boundaries to increase crossboundary activity [6,48].

## 2.4.3. Autonomous capability

“Autonomous capability” refers to a capability in which an organization preserves the business units’ operations for continued exploration through structural separation [44,47]. Generally, when the structures of business units are integrated, the units exploit capabilities through enhanced coordination, but coordination and autonomy do not always con<sup>fl</sup>ict – they can coexist in one organization [44]. When an organization combines business units, autonomous capabilities help each unit concentrate on its own specialty, thus enhancing operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Schweizer [47] <sup>fi</sup>nds that when a company establishes a hybrid organization through a merger or acquisition, the companies adopt an autonomous approach to manage departments that require innovation or independence, such as R&D departments.

Overall, a hybrid organization is an organization with multiple, con<sup>fl</sup>icting, and competitive institutional logics. These disparate logics refer to not only the operations but also the processes of a hybrid organization. Because boundary permeability involves a degree of boundary openness, different degrees of boundary permeability may lead to different paths of hybridization. In this paper, we explore the actual formation process of an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization from the perspective of boundary permeability by using a case study.

## 3. Methodology

This paper uses the case study method for the following reasons. First, this method is applicable to research about process [18] and “how” questions [41,55]. This paper discusses a “how” question that analyzes the process of how online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units combine into a hybrid organization. Second, a case study is suitable for revealing complex multidimensional relationships and phenomena [55]. The question discussed in this paper concerning the combination process of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units involves many dimensions, including commerce, products, and knowledge; as a result, the question is complex. Therefore, using the case study method is appropriate.

We chose this case for two reasons. First, we want to investigate an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization, and a combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units is such a case. Second, we want to analyze the formation of a hybrid organization from the perspective of boundary permeability, and a study on the hybridization of business units provides a suitable setting. Suning is the largest and most successful case of combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units in China; therefore, a study on this company achieves these purposes.

## 3.1. Data collection

We collected data in two stages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage (preparation) lasted from March to April 2014. We contacted the senior management of Suning through phone calls and e-mails and established interview times. Before the interviews, we learned about Suning by collecting data from newspapers, magazines, journals, and the Internet, based on which we selected a theoretical lens [41]. In the second stage, in April 2014, a research group consisting of seven people went to Suning’s headquarters for interviews with the senior management team and visited company stores. Nine senior management staff from of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores, online departments, the logistics department, human resources (HR), and management (see Appendix A) were interviewed, including <sup>fi</sup>ve members of the board of directors (e.g., the CEO) and four middle

managers. We also interviewed three clerks at a retail store. The research group interviewed and communicated many times with these key management staff to ensure the reliability and validity of the data. We also communicated with the staff in the stores and headquarters. During the interviews, we veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the data about the company’s integration of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units from various perspectives and <sup>fi</sup>nally obtained all the data required for this paper [29]. From May 2014 to May 2015, we continued our communications with Suning through phone calls, e-mails, and WeChat to ensure that our theory conformed to the progress of events [41].

All interviews and discussions occurred at Suning’s headquarters and its stores. We adopted the method of a semistructured interview, and each interview lasted between 120 and 180 min. We encouraged the interviewees to speak freely about the processes that occurred throughout the combination process and how the <sup>fi</sup>rm hybridized the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine units. One group member translated the data, and another member proofread and checked the translations [29].

## 3.2. Data analysis

We analyzed the data soon after we <sup>fi</sup>nished the data collection process [15]. First, we analyzed Suning’s current development and background and the driving forces behind combining its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. This analysis is helpful for explaining the unique characteristics of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybridization. Then, we analyzed information related to the formation of a hybrid organization. In a hybrid organization, there are different institutional logics and different strategies for implementing combinations [47]; thus, at this stage, we focused on the institutional logics and corresponding strategies used during the process of combining the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. For example, during the analysis, we found that an organization adopts absorptive, symbiotic, and preservative strategies simultaneously to build a hybrid organization.

Because the formation of hybrid organizations is highly related to boundary management [6], in the third stage, we focused on the methods of boundary permeability under different institutional logics and strategies. For instance, we ascertained that organizations adopt different modes of boundary management according to different logics, such as blurring, crossing, and rigidifying boundaries. In the fourth stage, we extracted information about activities under different modes of boundary management and the necessary organizational capabilities. Throughout the analysis, we observed conceptual and structural differences among various strategies, boundary management modes, and boundary activities under different institutional logics. We engaged in two activities throughout the data analysis process – abstraction of process and re<sup>fi</sup>nement of tentative explanations – until we accounted for all tentative explanations and agreed among each other [35].

Throughout the analysis, we used multiple coding techniques to code, organize the interview data, and extend the theoretical lens [40,51]. The coding techniques [50] that we adopted include open, axial, and selective coding. Open coding was used to identify the theoretical dimensions. For instance, it was used to analyze different types of boundary permeability. Axial coding was used to identify second-order themes. For example, it was used to identify and analyze permeability capabilities. Selective coding was used to link the existing theoretical systems with our new <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, it was used to analyze hybridization strategies. While analyzing the data, we repeated the process described previously to uncover and further extract the core elements of the mechanisms to ensure alignment among the data, theory, and <sup>fi</sup>ndings [29] until we <sup>fi</sup>nalized the results. We also adopted the rule of triangulation to ensure that the interviewees’ interpretations converged [12]; that is, we used multiple data sources (interviews, news reports, and archival data) and data from various interviewees to ensure that the data were consistent.

Overall, we adopted the case study method to perform an empirical analysis that addressed the research issues. We selected a theoretical lens and collected data through face-to-face interviews and analyses of various second-hand materials. Reliability and validity were emphasized during the data collection process. In the data analysis process, we used multiple coding techniques to code the data and organize the information related to the formation of hybrid organizations, and we adopted appropriate approaches to ensure consistency of the data. Throughout the data collection and analysis process, we applied Klein and Myers’ [29] principles to conduct the interpretive work.

## 4. Case description

## 4.1. Organizational background and environment

Suning is one of the largest retailers in China, with annual sales revenue of more than RMB 108.925 billion (approximately USD 17.57 billion) and 180,000 employees. The company ranked <sup>fi</sup>rst in the top chain retailers list in China several times between 2000 and 2014. Since its establishment in 1990, Suning has expanded its of<sup>fl</sup>ine electronics stores, and by 2010, it had become the largest chain retailer for domestic appliances. By 2014, Suning had 1696 stores in 23 cities in China.

After 2010, Suning began to build an online retailing business platform with separate operations and accounting. The company has expanded its sales scale and market share through its online operations and mergers and acquisitions. At the same time, Suning built an IT-related team of 5000 people and established two IT R&D centers – one in Beijing and one overseas. By the end of 2013, Suning had sold >2 million SKUs on its online sales platform. In 2004, Suning’s transaction volume of online sales was RMB 22.599 billion (USD 3.65 billion), which ranked third in China’s ecommerce retailing industry, behind only Alibaba and Jingdong. Before 2013, Suning’s online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units operated separately; each unit had its own management system, staff, logistics, delivery system, and product categories. In 2013, the company combined its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units so that they became one overall operating system. This combination represents the largest in terms of such combination of online and offline business units in China.

Combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units had great signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for Suning, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in three aspects. First, the company’s internal needs encouraged the combination. Suning intended to create synergistic effects by integrating its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, and then improving the operating ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency

## 4.2. Reasons why suning combined its online and offline business units

There are three reasons why Suning combined its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units: internal needs, consumer demand, and competitive pressures.

First, from the viewpoint of the company’s internal needs, Suning’s of<sup>fl</sup>ine chain store is the largest retail store in China, and its sales volume of online products ranks third nationwide. Suning had a sound sales system in which the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units operated separately. However, the units would create more opportunities for development through cooperation. From an internal perspective, making the best use of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine resources means fully realizing their advantages while avoiding their disadvantages to achieve cooperative development between the units. The CEO explained,

Suning had established a leading sales system both online and offline. We had to revitalize these two advantageous resources to build an advantage for cooperative development, so I proposed the development strategy of integrating O2O (online to offline or offline to online) to realize a unified operation of online and offline business units.

From the perspective of consumer demand, shopping channels in China have changed tremendously. Online shopping, which is becoming accepted by an increasing number of consumers, is a clear shopping trend. Consumers can compare and select products online and obtain the shopping experience and service of<sup>fl</sup>ine. Suning’s online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units used to operate separately, which led to different prices and services for the same product. Many consumers complained and even demanded refunds. Therefore, Suning started to think about how to coordinate the development of the units. The vice president (VP) of operations explained the change in consumer demand as follows:

We divide shopping channels into four kinds according to the terminal, which include the store terminal, PC terminal, mobile terminal, and family Internet terminal. However, to consumers, these four terminals have no boundaries, so they could switch and compare among the four.

In terms of pressure from competitors, the rapid rise of online retailers has brought considerable pressure to bear on traditional retail stores. In the age of personal computers and the Internet, some online retailers have advantages in China. Therefore, traditional retailers must consider how to exploit their advantages to counteract the effect of online sales. The CEO and a senior management member said,

The rapid development of Alibaba, Jingdong, and other ecommerce companies brought us great pressure, so we had to bring the advantages of our stores into play and build an O2O ecommerce system. This is what urged us to venture again (CEO).

Online businesses have their own weakness – they do not have physical stores. Since physical stores are the main way for consumers to buy, to experience and to receive service, Suning had to consider how to revitalize the assets of its offline stores. In the personal computer age, Suning’s development of online sales was weaker than that of its competitors, but through a combination of online and offline business units, we thought we would be able to take advantage of offline retailing stores to develop our online business and take a leap forward in the mobile Internet age of the future (Suning VP of Overall Management).

## 4.3. Developing strategies and improving capabilities

Suning created a “one body, two wings” development strategy before the company combined its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. “One body” means that the company focused on the retail industry, whereas “two wings” refers to building an open platform that integrates the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units into one unit. At the same time, the company proposed centering its retail business on three core resources, “products, information, and funds,” combining the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine retail business units. With regard to how to execute this strategy, the VP of overall management explained,

From strategy to organization, from brand to human resources, we have finished the overall layout. What we take is the strategy-oriented way of thinking. The strategy has been made beforehand, after which we are only allowed to talk about how to implement it, such as how to do O2O, how to expand product categories, how to build the platform, and how to specify the operations to turn the strategy into reality.

The company’s management knew that after developing the relevant strategies, it still had to enhance its capabilities before it could achieve its strategic goal. Therefore, Suning improved its capabilities <sup>fi</sup>rst. Suning’s combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units represents a process of creating a strategy initially and then improving the company’s capabilities accordingly. The VP of Information Systems explained,

To carry out the transformation strategy, Suning had to figure out what its core ability was in facing a new round of transformation and how to develop this ability. Once the direction is decided, be firmly devoted. As long as we possess these abilities, the accumulated resources and capabilities will transform into performance, which will bring Suning into a new growth period

## 4.4. Strategies of combination

## 4.4.1. Strategy of integrating functions to meet customers’ demands

Suning thought about how to combine the two units from the perspective of consumer demand. The company believed that the core concept behind retail industry management is to please consumers, who make purchases both in physical stores and on their PC, cell phone, or the Internet, who use these different channels simultaneously, and who compare one channel with the others. Thus, Suning intended to create a strategy that would provide consumers with uni<sup>fi</sup>ed and consistent services on the four platforms. This strategy would require Suning to integrate the sales services of the four platforms into a whole, which is the key to the combination of the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. The VP of Operations said,

We adjusted our operations according to the changes in consumers shopping demands. Because consumers could make their purchases on four different platforms, we have to ensure that wherever they access us, the products and services are unified. The purpose is to satisfy them.

Suning’s strategy was based on the consumers’ perspective, primarily the demands of the market and consumers. The company

## 5. Discussion

In Section 4, we illustrate how Suning combined the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. In this section, on the basis of Suning’s case, we analyze how to combine online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units to form a hybrid organization. As shown in Fig. 1, we summarize the case and create a model for forming a hybrid organization.

## 5.1. Formation process of an online–Offline hybrid organization

## 5.1.1. Capacity-seeking process of hybridization

Fig. 1 reveals that the formation process of an online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization is a capacity-seeking process that begins with triggers, followed by the development of strategies, and <sup>fi</sup>nally leads to the building of capabilities in accordance with the strategy’s requirements [11]. In this process, the company does not consider its own capability limitations. Senior managers believe that they can gain those necessary capabilities; therefore, they create strategies according to the company’s development orientation [38]. Thereafter, the company successfully cultivates the corresponding capabilities that meet the requirements of the strategies [54] and forms a hybrid organization. Therefore, the formation process of a hybrid organization is a mechanism of strategic responses [26]. When executing the capacity-seeking process, three con<sup>fl</sup>icting paths are adopted simultaneously, namely, a complete permeability path, a partial permeability path, and a preventive permeability path. The fact that a hybrid organization is formed through a capacity-seeking process is a <sup>fi</sup>nding that differs from previous research.

## 5.1.2. Three permeability paths of hybridization

Under the complete permeability path (see Fig. 1), Suning <sup>fi</sup>rst established an absorptive strategy, and the company then achieved assimilation by letting the resources permeate by blurring the boundaries between the two business units [24]. Suning’s intention was to integrate some resources from different business units into one unit. To achieve this goal, Suning enhanced its assimilative capability, blurred the boundaries between the two units, and completely integrated some resources from the different units into a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed one. Under the partial permeability path (see Fig. 1), Suning developed a symbiotic strategy, namely a strategy with high interdependence as well as a great need for organizational autonomy [24]. The purpose of the symbiotic strategy was to allow the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units to enjoy complementary advantages while preserving their independent operations. To achieve this purpose, Suning improved its ambidextrous capability to enable the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units to use crossboundary resources that are both highly interdependent and highly independent. Under the preventive permeability path (see Fig. 1), Suning developed and implemented a preservative strategy that recognized the need for a low degree of interdependence and a high level of autonomy, in which the hybridizing business units are integrated only to a modest degree and are managed using a preservative approach [24]. To achieve this strategy, Suning improved its autonomous capability and maintained the two units as independent with rigid boundaries to enable the two business units to focus on their respective expertise.

In the following sections, we will explain the role of boundary permeability in the capacity-seeking process. We discuss this process based on the paths of “triggers of hybridization boundary permeability strategies boundary permeability capability” (see the lower part of Fig. 1).

## 5.2 Triggers of hybridization

The current case reveals that three factors trigger the formation of a hybrid organization in which a company combines online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units: the company’s internal needs, consumer demand, and competitor pressure. These drivers involve internal and external factors and reveal two different mechanisms for forming a hybrid organization. One mechanism comes from con<sup>fl</sup>icting and complementary internal demands (the company must improve its internal resource ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency), whereas the other mechanism is derived from con<sup>fl</sup>icting external demands that require the company to respond to changes in consumer demands and competitor pressure simultaneously [26].

## 5.2.1. Company needs

This case reveals that Suning faced both con<sup>fl</sup>icting internal demands [26] and complementary internal demands [17], which drove the company to combine the two units to reduce the con<sup>fl</sup>icts and obtain synergistic effects. There are three types of demands. The <sup>fi</sup>rst involves a need to integrate different institutional logics. Because online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units have different institutional logics [32], a company that operates both generates con<sup>fl</sup>icts and problems; thus, it would want to combine the two into one to reduce the problems [5]. The second is a need to unify management systems. Suning’s online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units had different products, personnel, and organizational structures that had to be bridged. Unifying the management systems improves the organizational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the entire company. The third is to address complementary internal demands. Because online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units meet different demands, Suning could combine the two to make them complementary [43]. Therefore, reducing tensions and improving cooperation between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units are triggers for a combination.

## 5.2.2. Customer demands

This case reveals that customers demand the opportunity to obtain all of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units because the two units have their own advantages and disadvantages. Online shopping has advantages in product quantity, product comparisons, shopping time, and limitations on physical location, whereas the merits of of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping include the shopping experience and shopping services [31,43]. However, consumers often hope to obtain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping, which is a hope that re<sup>fl</sup>ects con<sup>fl</sup>icting external demands [26]. Therefore, Suning had to provide online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine services by combining the two business units. This online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization is different from a public–private one because of its interesting multiple and con<sup>fl</sup>icting customer demands.

## 5.2.3. Competitor pressure

In terms of external competitive pressure, Suning faced considerable external competition from online retailers. However, compared with these companies, Suning had unique advantages in operating online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units simultaneously that could meet consumers’ dual demands for online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine shopping [16]. Moreover, these two business units could develop synergistically [10,17]. Therefore, Suning wanted to combine the two business units to alleviate the competitive pressure from ecommerce companies. External competitive pressure is also a key trigger. As compared with public–private hybrid organizations, external demands are a unique feature of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations; moreover, the fact that the two business units can operate separately to service customers, which is different from public–private hybrid organizations.

Overall, combining online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units into an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization solves several unique issues. First, the two business units have different institutional logics, and it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to operate them simultaneously. This internal con<sup>fl</sup>ict is a driving force for combining the units. Second, the two business units cannot meet all consumer demands individually. Each unit has disadvantages, and combining the two is a solution. Finally, the two business units can adopt <sup>fl</sup>exible approaches such as substitution, integration, complementation, and separation [47]; therefore, combining these two business units in a special relationship provides multiple operation alternatives to managers. A public–private hybrid organization is often triggered by internal needs rather than customers’ demands and competitors’ pressures [40], which is one of the key differences between online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine and public–private hybrid organizations.

## 5.3. Boundary permeability strategies

## 5.3.1. Absorptive strategy

Our analysis shows that Suning adopted an absorptive strategy to integrate the resources of its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. When the boundaries of units are blurred and opened, resources such as products and information from different units can be permeated and absorbed mutually; therefore, some elements from different units can come together as one [19]. In the current case, the reason why the absorptive strategy was adopted was that customers often purchase products from different online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, and they compare one channel with the others. Hence, Suning had to blur the boundaries between its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units to permeate the resources mutually to make the services of the different units consistent and improve customer satisfaction and loyalty. Therefore, the purpose of an absorptive strategy is to make the resources from different units permeate completely to unify customers’ perceptions of different purchase channels. The absorptive strategy, in which elements from different units are integrated, is often related to short-term motives and customers [34,47]. Under the capacity-seeking logic, an organization must seek and improve its corresponding capabilities to achieve a proposed strategy. Under the absorptive strategy, the capability that Suning needed to improve was an assimilative capability (see Fig. 1).

## 5.3.2. Symbiotic strategy

Our analysis shows that Suning adopted a symbiotic strategy to enhance cooperation between the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. Suning believed that having more interdependent business units in a combination would result in a greater need for boundary permeability[6]. In the current case, Suning intended for the resources of one business unit to be shared with another to enhance resource ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Therefore, under a symbiotic strategy, Suning let part of one unit’s resources across boundaries to permeate the other unit, which allows one type of institutional logic to complement and cooperate with another. In the current case, the symbiotic strategy was adopted because Suning intended to operate its business units both separately and cooperatively, which is a strategy that is related to partial permeability. Under the capacity-seeking logic, the organization must seek to improve the corresponding capability to achieve a symbiotic strategy. Under a symbiotic strategy, the capability that Suning had to improve was its ambidextrous capability (see Fig. 1).

## 5.3.3. Preservative strategy

Our process model suggests that Suning not only adopted absorptive and symbiotic strategies but also developed a preservative strategy. Under the preservative strategy, Suning strengthened the boundary between business units to prevent resources from permeating mutually, and it maintained the gap between the different institutional logics [2]. Thus, the preservative strategy is a preventive permeability strategy in which some boundaries between the two business units remain rigid, and at certain times, the units will build new boundaries [30]. In the current case, a preservative strategy was adopted because Suning intended to promote its specialization through building rigid boundaries. Under the capacity-seeking logic, an organization must seek to improve its corresponding capability to achieve a preservative strategy. The capability that Suning needed to improve was its autonomous capability under the preservative strategy (see Fig. 1).

## 5.4. Boundary permeability capability

## 5.4.1. Assimilative capability

After proposing an absorptive strategy, our analysis shows that Suning improved its assimilative capability to blur the units’ boundaries, and some resources of the two business units were completely integrated. Assimilative capability is one type of permeability capability that refers to an organization’s capacity to accept, use, and institutionalize external resources through open boundaries [56]. In the current case, Suning <sup>fi</sup>rst blurred the product boundary of the two units so that customers could receive uni<sup>fi</sup>ed services from the different units and obtain consistent recognition of products from both the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine units. The “product boundary” refers to product-related boundaries in the sales process, including product categories, sales promotions, and prices among different business units. Suning also blurred the data boundary between the two units to use both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine data to satisfy customers. The “data boundary” refers to the boundary between the operations and the consumer data of the two business units. Overall, the purpose of improving the assimilative capability is to enable resources from different units to permeate completely and mutually, thereby combining them as a whole. The purpose of this combination is to maintain customers’ consistent perceptions of services regardless of which units provide those services.

## 5.4.2. Ambidextrous capability

After proposing a symbiotic strategy, our analysis shows that Suning made relevant improvements in terms of its ambidextrous capability. The two business units can cross one another’s culture, knowledge, service, and logistics boundaries. A culture boundary is primarily derived from the different cultural logics of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units [25,32]. After combining the units, Suning had to adapt to two types of cultures simultaneously. A knowledge boundary is derived from the different knowledge systems of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units [33,52]. After the company combined the units, employees learned and used their knowledge about the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units simultaneously. A service boundary appears because different business units provide different services. Suning’s employees now provide online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine services to customers simultaneously. For instance, employees help consumers make online purchases in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores. A logistics boundary is derived from different business units adopting different logistics systems. Suning now operates and coordinates the logistics for its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units simultaneously. Suning obtained the ambidextrous capability of logistics through a combination of its online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine logistics systems. For instance, of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores often deliver goods from either the online or the of<sup>fl</sup>ine business unit. Overall, improving the ambidextrous capability enables resources from one unit to be used in the entire organization. The purpose is to establish cooperation between business units.

## 5.4.3. Autonomous capability

Our model suggests that Suning improved its autonomous capability to preserve and build regulatory and functional boundaries. As a result, the two business units operate more independently and more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. For a regulatory boundary, Suning reinforced the system construction of the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, and the more rigid boundary maintains the independence and specialization of the two units [22]. For the functional boundary, Suning built new boundaries by starting business divisions and new companies and by using other decoupling approaches to strengthen the creativity and adaptability of each business unit. Thus, the organization improved its autonomous capability to prevent resources from one unit permeating to another unit by building regulatory and functional boundaries.

## 5.5. Features of an online–offline hybrid organization

Similar to former studies, online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations also need to incorporate con<sup>fl</sup>icting and competitive institutional logics [5,23] and to implement con<sup>fl</sup>icting strategies simultaneously [40,47]. However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that there are some distinct features of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations that are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the following aspects.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst distinct feature lies in con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional logics. Public–private hybrid organizations are designed to achieve the two purposes of social welfare and commercial logic, which relate to resource allocation [5]. Conversely, online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations involve combining two types of operational modes and functions that relate to resource recon<sup>fi</sup>guration. Thus, online– of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations are more concerned about combining operations and speci<sup>fi</sup>c functions, and boundary management therefore becomes more important. Few previous studies have discussed hybrid organizations from the boundary management perspective.

The second distinct feature of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations lies in the capacity-seeking process. In contrast to previous studies, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the formation of an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization is a capacity-seeking process, which means that during the hybridization process, organizations often develop combining strategies without determining their corresponding capacities. The online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units are already operating separately, and hence combining them requires blurring and crossing boundaries, which means that new capacities are needed. Thus, the capacity-seeking process is different from the previous hybridization of strategic responses and managerial responses [26].

The third distinct feature of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations lies in con<sup>fl</sup>icting boundary permeability paths. In contrast to previous studies, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that boundary permeability plays a critical role in driving the online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybridization process. Previous studies propose that boundary permeability might affect the formation of hybrid organizations [6,37], but no studies explore the actual formation process of hybrid organizations from the perspective of boundary permeability. We further <sup>fi</sup>nd that there are three con<sup>fl</sup>icting boundary permeability paths during online– of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybridization. The complete permeability path refers to the process of developing an absorptive strategy and seeking an assimilative capability. The partial permeability path refers to the process of developing a symbiotic strategy and seeking an assimilative capability, while the preventive permeability path describes the process of developing a preservative strategy and improving an autonomous capability.

The fourth distinct feature lies in speci<sup>fi</sup>c boundary behaviors. We <sup>fi</sup>nd different demands for boundary permeability capabilities corresponding to different boundary behaviors, all of which re<sup>fl</sup>ect critical features of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations. Under the demands for an assimilative capability, Suning blurred the lines of the product boundary and the data boundary. Under the demands for an ambidextrous capability, Suning blurred the lines of the cultural boundary, the knowledge boundary, the service boundary, and the logistics boundary between the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units. Under the demands for an autonomous capability, Suning strengthened the regulatory boundary and the functional boundary.

Overall, the differences between online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organizations and public–private hybrid organizations lie in their con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional logics, their strategy and capacity orientations, the role of boundary permeability, and their boundary management behaviors.

## 6. Theoretical and practical contributions

This study makes two important theoretical contributions. First, the study explores the actual formation process of an online– of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization. Previous studies show that a hybrid organization has multiple and contradictory institutional logics [5,40], but few studies discuss the actual formation process. This study extends previous knowledge about the formation of a hybrid organization and offers a capacity-seeking process that includes both strategies and capabilities [11]. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, the organization creates three contradictory strategies: absorptive, symbiotic, and preservative. These strategies involve three different operational directions: integrating the two units completely, constructing an interdependent or independent system, and maintaining autonomy. Under these three strategies, the company improves its assimilative, ambidextrous, and autonomous capabilities and forms a hybrid organization. In contrast to most previous studies that discuss public–private hybrid organizations, this paper focuses on an online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine hybrid organization, which extends the knowledge about hybrid organizations.

Second, this paper analyzes the role of boundary permeability in the formation of a hybrid organization. The current case <sup>fi</sup>nds that resources permeate from one unit to another. The three types of permeating paths, namely, complete, partial, and preventive permeability paths, operate simultaneously. Under the complete permeability path, an absorptive strategy is proposed and the assimilative capability is improved to enable products and data to permeate between units completely to form a whole. Under the partial permeability path, a symbiotic strategy and ambidextrous capability are used to enable culture, knowledge, services, and logistics to permeate between the two units to increase cooperation. Under the preventive permeability path, a preservative strategy is proposed and the autonomous capability is improved to prevent resource boundary permeability between the two units. This paper shows that different boundary permeability capabilities apply to different organizational boundaries in the online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine context. Moreover, multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icting and competitive boundary permeability paths can explain why boundary permeability is closely linked to hybrid organizations.

This study also makes several practical contributions. First, creating strategies for combining units is important for many companies. Because there are many problems and contradictions between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, companies often face the troublesome question of what strategies to adopt to combine the two. This paper offers a direction for companies. In addition, it is also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for organizations to determine how to combine the various resources and functions of two units in a practical manner when the cultures, organizational structures, personnel, and operational processes of the two units differ. This study provides a road map whereby companies can follow three routes to hybridize their business units. The <sup>fi</sup>rst route is to let some functions of the two business units completely combine into a whole through mutual absorption. The second route is to keep the two units independent but strengthen their cross-boundary cooperation. The third route is to separate the two units’ functions and maintain their independence and specialization to improve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The three routes seem contradictory, but they can be actualized under the operational logic of a hybrid organization. This case also suggests that companies can completely integrate functions that involve customers and markets by unifying product categories, prices, and sales promotions. Specialized organizational functions, such as data analysis and information system construction, can be operated by companies separately. For organizational functions that connect internal management to external markets, such as customer service and logistics, organizations can use cross-boundary cooperation.

## 7. Limitations and future studies

This study also has limitations. We focus on the combination process of one retailer’s online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units, which represents one form of online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine business. We do not discuss other online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine businesses, such as cloud manufacturing, crowd sourcing, and digital–traditional media. As compared with other online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine businesses, online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine retailing is unique because the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine units can operate independently, and they may contradict or complement one another [43]. In the future, we plan to study other types of online– of<sup>fl</sup>ine businesses and further explore the mechanisms behind online–of<sup>fl</sup>ine combinations.

Other factors that affect the combination of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine business units should also be studied further, such as CEO characteristics and national cultures. CEO characteristics may exert certain effects on a combination; for example, if both the of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online business units have strong leaders, the combination might be affected by the behaviors of the two CEOs. National culture is another factor that may affect a combination because different national cultures may yield different organizational behaviors. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the effects of national culture on combinations by comparing different cases in different cultures, such as comparing US and Asian cases.
