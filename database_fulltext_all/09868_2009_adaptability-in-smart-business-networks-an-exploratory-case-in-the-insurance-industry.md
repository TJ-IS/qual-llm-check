# Adaptability in smart business networks: An exploratory case in the insurance industry

Javier Busquets ⁎, Juan Rodon, Jonathan Wareham

Department of Information Systems, ESADE, Universitat Ramon LLull, Barcelona, Spain

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 19 May 2009

Keywords: Adaptability Smart Business Networks Theory building

## a b s t r a c t

A focal characteristic of Smart Business Networks (SBN) is their ability to adapt to the environment. We think that the capacity to adapt, or adaptability of Smart Business Networks, requires more attention and formalization in the managerial discourse since we consider that it is one core capability of SBN to be considered “Smart”. The purpose of this paper is to employ theories of learning from the educational and organizational literature to develop a framework for modes of network. These modes, which are a function of the organizational awareness and the resources employed, are: 1) automatic responses, 2) assimilation, 3) accommodation, and 4) environmental enactment. We show the applicability of the framework with a case study about a SBN in the home insurance claims and repairs industry. The paper highlights the need for SBN management to design process and technology infrastructures that appropriately allocate limited organizational awareness and resources to optimize the process of adaptation in Smart Business Networks. We consider that the theoretical propositions may serve to guide future research in Smart Business Networks.

## 1. Introduction

In this paper, we use the concept Smart Business Networks (SBN) as an arrangement of organizations coordinating their business processes in a manner that exhibits adaptive, agile and robust behavior in order to assure the value creation, survival and growth of the arrangement of organizations [38]. Although research and practitioner literature [11,14] recognizes that adaptability is required for the success of business networks, we consider that the concept of adaptability of SBN has received little scrutiny. We contend that greater insight into adaptability is a requisite towards understanding the determinants of value creation, survival and growth of SBNs. This paper aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap in the literature by developing a conceptual framework for adaptability in SBNs. We further substantiate the framework with a case study in the home insurance claims and repairs sector, and formulate propositions that describe the process of adaptability in SBNs. This paper recognizes the multifaceted nature of adaptability; thus we do not seek to generalize it beyond the scope of business networks. Rather we focus exclusively on SBN adaptability and explicate its various modes to create and maintain value in business networks under environmental and internal change [20]. The central argument of this paper is that adaptability is one of several key requisites if a business network is to be considered “Smart”.

The paper is structured as follows. We begin by surveying the literature to develop de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of adaptability that may be relevant to

SBNs. We then present a case study of a European SBN whose focal <sup>fi</sup>rm is Multiasistencia, a Spanish company that has operations in Spain, Portugal, France and the United Kingdom. The company manages a service called “Comprehensive Claims Management Service” (CCMS) serving over 100 corporate clients such as banks, insurers and utilities that service some 9 million retail and corporate customers. Multiasistencia coordinates a network of some 11,000 trade professionals (plumbers, painters, roofers, etc.) organized in small <sup>fi</sup>rms that provide <sup>fi</sup>nal repair services to individual claimants. We describe the behavior of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, Multiasistencia, and illustrate four modes of adaptability. Next, we augment this discussion by describing the socio-technical infrastructures that support and enable adaptability in our focal SBN. Next, based on the evidence from the empirical data we formulate several propositions that may guide further research. The paper concludes with implications for the theory and management of SBNs, as well as limitations of our study and prospects for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Adaptability

The idea of adaptability is central in economics, biology, sociology, psychology, cognitive sciences, learning theory, as well as managerial and organizational sciences [9,13,31]. As the central concept of this analysis, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne adaptability as the ability of an agent to consciously or unconsciously change to <sup>fi</sup>t different circumstances — for instance, changes in the environment. Likewise, we assume that agents can be both active and passive; they can accept the environment as is, or they can seek to in<sup>fl</sup>uence or affect environmental change.

As an example, consider natural selection, where an environment can be dynamic and endogenously change, thereby selecting passive species/organizations to survive or die based on the chance <sup>fi</sup>t of random mutations. This kind of metaphor has been severely criticized in the managerial discourse [25]. First, where an organizational environment in many respects is given, in many others it is socially constructed [3,9]. Second, organizations are products of boundedly rational agency, conscious intent and creativity, which in<sup>fl</sup>uence, affect, or enact environments [25,33]. Third, organizations adapt as a formal intent of organizing change both endogenously and exogenously [4,31,36].

These conceptualizations represent two positions; in one the environment is exogenous, immune to any in<sup>fl</sup>uence by social action. In the other, it is a product of agency and intention. We choose a position that lies in the middle of this continuum, one that acknowledges the changing, exogenous nature of the environment, but also accepts the ability of agents to not only actively learn, but also in<sup>fl</sup>uence and enact environments.

Existing literature has analyzed (1) adaptation between <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same network, both in processes and information systems, emphasizing organizational ecology issues; (2) rules of interaction and inter-organizational complexity, and (3) the adaptation paradox: the more two <sup>fi</sup>rms are adapted to each other, the less adaptability, that is the less capacity to engage new types of relationship [14]. In our approach, we consider a SBN as one type of organization that shows as a distinctive organizational skill its capacity to adapt to the new circumstances, that is, adaptability.

In this paper, we borrow the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of adaptation from Jean Piaget (1896–1980) and apply it to the organizational context to mean an ongoing process tending towards equilibrium between an active agent and a dynamic environment [12,26].

## 2.2. The influence of Jean Piaget in organizational theory

Piaget is well known for his work in children's evolutionary processes [27,28] and his work on human learning and development. Piaget's theories have inspired organizational learning scholars extending his theories into organizational change and evolution [31]. Piaget, who was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by open systems theories, considers adaptation as an activity, product of intelligence [26] or “smartness”. Hence, adaptation can be described by a metaphor of a dance; an ongoing process between an intelligent agent and an environment that is both dynamic and exogenous, yet in<sup>fl</sup>uenced or enacted by the agent. In organizational language, adaptation is the result of an ongoing negotiation between the organization and its environment [6]. Piaget conceptualizes adaptation as a combination of two basic modes (or functions): assimilation and accommodation [26]. We posit this two modes are central to the understanding of adaptability of SBNs. The basic assumptions are: (1) organizations are receptive towards their environments; (2) environments “send” signals that may need different responses; and (3) organizations “sense” environmental signals and actively search for the right response. We recognize however that some of the signals coming from the environment may result from previous actions taken by the agent.

On one hand, assimilation takes place when an agent accepts the actual circumstances (environment) as given, and chooses a response based on an existing portfolio of actions. That is, the agent situates the actual experience within the existing limits of behavior [12]. Assimilation always requires either an explicit or implicit decision since the agent accepts the signal from the environment and decides to act within a certain learned limits of action. On the other hand, accommodation is required when the agent's current repertoire of action is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Then the agent must change mental models and set new behavior to <sup>fi</sup>nd an appropriate response to the situation, or change the parameters or limits of the problem, which in turn requires acquiring new skills or knowledge, that is, a learning activity.

Some authors have drawn upon Piaget [12] to develop organizational learning theories [8,37]. As much of this literature embraces concepts closely related to adaptability, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review them in order to expand and deepen our conceptualization of adaptability. The core of Piagetian theories refers to understanding living beings as Open Systems that may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced and may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their environment in a dynamic interaction towards equilibrium. The capacity to interact and learn is central for any intelligent or smart agent and related to its self-organization and self-control. The result of this intelligent behavior is the capacity to adapt, that is to say, adaptability. Since Piaget focuses his studies in human evolutionary process, he emphasized the aspects of action and behavior driven by the interactive and social process of learning, not driven only by instinctual mechanisms and natural re<sup>fl</sup>exes. He also recognized that learning requires energy and in any smart action there is an economic component to use the right amount of energy required. For doing so, management has to know where to invest resources. We consider that this approach based on Open System, learning and energy has inspired different organizational learning theorists, as we show in the following paragraphs.

Cyert and March viewed the organization as an open and adaptive learning system [6], where organizations act and react to environmental changes by adapting to them. Theses actions can be successful or not, thus necessitating feed-back mechanisms to adjust behavior [32]. Organizations interact with the environment by producing outputs over which they exercise considerable control. However, outcomes represent the joint product of organizational outputs and environmental response, thereby introducing a dialectic element to the concept of organizational performance [32]. According to the literature we have reviewed, Piaget's in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the theory of organizational adaptation is premised upon: (1) organizational decisions and behavior; (2) organizational learning; (3) organizational change and (4) governance.

The emphasis of organizational decisions and behavior is best represented by the work of March and Simon [17] who argued that “If an organization has a repertoire of programs [rules of action], then it is adaptive in the short run insofar as it has procedures for selecting from its repertoire a program appropriate to each speci<sup>fi</sup>c situation that arises. Short run adaptiveness corresponds to what we ordinarily call problem solving, where long-run adaptiveness corresponds to learning” [17]:192. March and Simon tied these two concepts, problem solving and learning, to the capacity to process information and organizational memory (where repertoires of action reside).

The emphasis on learning and knowledge generation can also be represented by Nonaka and Takeuchi [21] who re-interpret March and Simon's approach of information processing by incorporating the idea that any organization is a knowledge system [25]; that is, describing an organization that creates knowledge (or learns) by achieving problem-resolution routines (no premises changed) and actions where changes of premises are needed. Learning is also the critical aspect of Argyris and Schön's theory of single-loop and double-loop learning [1]. Here adaptation is considered a learning process where in the <sup>fi</sup>rst case, authors suggest that single-loop learning is designing new actions while errors occur. Double loop-learning describes governing organizational values. In single-loop learning, we learn to maintain the <sup>fi</sup>eld of constancy by learning to design actions that satisfy existing governing variables. “In double-loop learning, we learn to change the <sup>fi</sup>eld of constancy itself” [1]:19. March had a similar line of logic in his concepts of organizational exploitation and exploration more linked with the use of organizational resources [16].

The bias of change can be represented by Orton and Weick who explicitly claim that “adaptability implies assimilation and accommodation to change” [23]. The authors also regard adaptability as an outcome of loose coupling systems [23]. Finally, Vickers [39], cited by Van der Heijden and Eden [37], makes a similar approach, linking adaptation with organizational governance: the executive function (effectiveness in current governing function) and the political function (modify the governing function).

## 2.3. Adaptability and Smart Business Networks

In the present research, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne a Business Networks (BN) as a set of inter-organizational relationships between the focal actor and interdependent external actors closely linked and working cooperatively together to create value for the customer [7]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition recognizes the existence of a central node and the need for the governance and coordination of activities and actors in the BN. Coordination begins with the creation of the BN and has to be perpetual throughout all value creation activities. The effective operation of a business network is premised on three requirements: (1) the effective management and implementation of key business processes, (2) the effective communication and coordination between <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, and (3) the creation of an effective interface between the network and its environment [38]. Accordingly, these actions are realized through an infrastructure of business processes and information and communication technologies.

Although the discourse concerning Smart Business Networks (SBN) is nascent, authors have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed salient characteristics of successful SBNs, such as: (1) each participant increases its own value, (2) self-renewal behavior or sustainable over time as a network, (3) they generate more pro<sup>fi</sup>table outcomes other than other forms of competitive, or cooperative, strategy, (4) interacting in novel ways, and (5) establishing a common understanding of ethics, meanings and symbols [38]. According to Nohria and Goshal [20] business networks are expected to enhance the capacity to change and innovate organizations. Similarly, Burns and Stalker [4] recognized the relationship between innovativeness and organizational structure. All these characteristics of SBN implicitly recognize the need for adaptability. However, agents have to invest resources in developing adaptability. Therefore, another critical factor of SBN adaptability is the amount of resources required. It is sub-optimal to approach well de<sup>fi</sup>ned problems that require assimilation with excessive resources that would normally be used in a process of accommodation. Likewise, approaching problems of accommodation with insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient effort will be insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient and probably lead to failure. Therefore, the challenge is to recognize the difference between assimilation and accommodation, and respond with the appropriate and the right amount of resources.

## 3. An adaptability framework

In previous section we have relied on Piaget to review the use of concept of adaptability in the organizational literature. As we have seen, adaptability is composed of two main modes, assimilation and accommodation, with biases on learning, decision-making, change and governance. Moreover, we extend this logic by suggesting a third mode, environmental enactment [32,41], in which organizations exert substantial resources to affect or change environmental conditions. Finally, we also think that there is a need for one more mode of adaptability: automatic responses in processes and systems programmed between the organization and environment [43]. Here we consider organizational interaction with the environment as described by the cybernetic approach [19,35], or Simon's theory of recipes [32].

Accordingly, we propose a framework (see Fig. 1) that conceptualizes adaptability as compromising four modes:

(1) Automatic responses may be carried out by people or embedded in information systems. We may <sup>fi</sup>nd automatic responses in living organisms. For instance, our iris adapts to changes in light intensity, or increases in pulse rates when we do exercise. This metaphor also applies to information systems and organizations. Like a software application acting according to programmed limits of action. For example, a request for service or information that triggers an expected programmed response of the system or organization. In this case the program of action is de<sup>fi</sup>ned ex-ante and executed by either a human or a machine.

(2) Assimilation may be carried out by the combination of people and information systems to carry on with the “business as usual” actions and behavior. In contrast to automatic responses, assimilation embraces the possibility of human actors to take decisions within pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned processes and boundaries. Hence, outside of long term design issues, human agency has no role in automatic responses, but does exhibit a constrained presence in assimilation.

(3) Accommodation may be carried out by informationed people, that is, people using information to monitor the processes and to use abstract reasoning to come up with (1) new solutions for problems or exceptions of a particular business environment and, (2) incremental innovation driven by customer's needs. Here, human agency exercises much higher degrees of freedom than in assimilation.

(4) Environment enactment. People and technology that create new conditions or new domains of action [36]. For instance, opening new markets and innovating services driven by the creativity of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This concept is also developed by Weick [41], “managers construct, rearrange, single-out and demolish many ’objective’ features of the environment” [32, p.141].

From our perspective, adaptability is a key characteristic that steers SBNs and is a function of the agents' awareness and the resources invested. First, awareness refers the consciousness and knowledgeability of agents in performing an adaptive action. Low awareness may suggest automatic responses and tacit activities [29] performed in planned or “business a usual” modes, while high awareness is required in un-planned and novel activities [22]. From knowledge theory, awareness is more suitable that theories of choice. Choice is an almost non-delimitative concept, while awareness stresses the importance of attention to any new problem or issue managers need to cope with. Therefore, increased awareness is linked with learning processes based on explicit knowledge [29,30].

Second, in adapting agents have to devote resources. The scale of adaptation or organizational change may be enabled or constrained by the amount of resources [15,22,25]. We stress that in any process where decisions and political in<sup>fl</sup>uences should be taken into account, management needs to concentrate on “knowing what they are doing” [41]. Planned and un-planned actions for large scale adaptation or change require more resources or greater organizational “energy” — paraphrasing Piaget.

As depicted in Fig.1, the primary antecedents of adaptability modes are organizational awareness and resources invested. By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, automatic responses require fewer resources and less awareness of the agent or organization. However, assimilation and accommodation require greater levels of awareness and resources. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst case, assimilation, as organizational responses range from “business as usual”; in the second case, accommodation stands for organizational responses to problems, exceptions, response re-de<sup>fi</sup>nition, and incremental innovation. Finally, environmental enactment consumes the maximum resources and awareness, as the organization not only rede<sup>fi</sup>nes the problem scope, but also in<sup>fl</sup>uences and de<sup>fi</sup>nes the problem context or domain. Implicit in this framework is an idea of economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. One mode of adaptability is not superior to another. Rather, each maintains its speci<sup>fi</sup>c function, its own relative costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which are dependant on context and need.

The next section illustrates the four modes of adaptability with a case study about a SBN, whose focal <sup>fi</sup>rm is Multiasistencia, in the home insurance claims and repairs. The SBN operates in Spain, France, UK and Portugal serving some 9,000,000 end customers.

## 4. Research method

Given the exploratory nature of our research problem, we have opted for a qualitative research method that combines ethnography and case study research [10]. This research method is well aligned to the nascent understanding and complex nature of the phenomenon with a variety of entwined levels of analysis [10]. Likewise, the case study method is suitable as we wanted to investigate a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, where boundaries between the phenomenon and the context were not clearly evident, and in which multiple sources of evidence (e.g. interviews, documents, observations) were used [42].

The data were mainly gathered through interviews and recorded in paper notes, tape and videotape. We conducted interviews with: 1) <sup>fi</sup>ve executives of Multiasistencia over three days in June 2004, three days in February 2005; and two days in March 2006; and 2) managers from three of Multiasistencia's main corporate clients, and from two of the franchised repair companies (trade professionals) in February 2005 and March 2006. The average duration of each interview was between 2 and 3 h.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst author also employed an ethnographic approach by spending several weeks in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, doing <sup>fi</sup>eld observation of the Control Center and conducting interviews with customer service representatives and supervisors in June 2004 and February 2005. In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author also participated in corporate client meetings, where different client executives and trade professional perspectives were explored. We prepared and conducted annual focus groups with Multiasistencia's senior management and board of directors from 2004 to 2006, allowing them to validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case data. We also have used direct information as internal documentation and <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements of the Multiasistencia, as well as press articles from 1998 to 2004 in Spanish, French and English media.

Data collection focused on the events, decisions, changes and roles in the SBN during the period 1999–2005. Data collection and analysis occurred iteratively, thus guiding subsequent data gathering.

The levels of analysis in the case are: the organization–environment interaction, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, and the relationship between the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and the other actors of the business network.

## 5. Case study: Multiasistencia

Multiasistencia was founded in Spain in the early 1980s with the intent of creating a “Comprehensive Claims Management Service” (CCMS). The service standardized the highly fragmented and heterogeneous household repairs <sup>fi</sup>eld, applying pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned prices, transparent conditions, establishing service performance and quality guarantees, ensuring timely response (24 h and 3 h for emergencies) and warranting the work for 6 months. The CCMS was offered either as part of a fully comprehensive household insurance policy or as a customer loyalty service in Spain, Portugal the UK and France. The group attends to its users from an International Control and Coordination Center located in the outskirts of Madrid. The Customers Service Representatives (CSR) received customer's calls and deployed jobs to a Network of Trade Professionals. Service quality was a key feature of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's corporate culture. Multiasistencia's commitment was re<sup>fl</sup>ected in its slogan: “Yes, we can”.

The Customer Service Representatives (CSRs) were coordinators of the repair management process who engage in the reception of home repair job requests and deploy repair jobs to the appropriate af<sup>fi</sup>liated trade professional. Multiasistencia was very successful in managing collectives and attracting corporate clients like banks and insurers in the 90s. In the year 2000, the <sup>fi</sup>rm experienced a problem of declining quality as a symptom of a non-scalable operating model. A new executive who joined the <sup>fi</sup>rm radically changed the <sup>fi</sup>rm's operating model, previously based on telephone calls and placing Multiasistencia on the Internet. The <sup>fi</sup>rm applied re-engineering concepts and used ICT to build up a ubiquitous application based on Contact Centers, the Internet, Web Services and Mobile Systems [5]. The reengineering process took three years (2000–2003). As a result of the process, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's productivity rose by 49.5% and a new SBN structure was designed. Since 2000, the Control Center manages the process, monitors the jobs in process and executes quality assurance tasks. The Control Center had, in 2005, 375 CSRs who manage over 25,000 incoming and outgoing calls a day from the different countries in which Multiasistencia offers its services. When the Control Center receives a call (or e-mail or fax), a CSR assesses the request for repairing. The Control Center also has a Computer–Telephone Integration Application (CTI). This interconnection allows the automatic identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the end customer and the information related to his or her policy which is subsequently presented to the CSR. ## 5.2. Assimilation

The new Control Center integrates communication media like email, mobile communications, SMS messages and Internet access. What is important about the assimilation mode is that every time the Control Center receives the call (or e-mail or fax), a CSR assesses the repair according to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's schema built on previous knowledge and categorizes the claim in order to trigger the CCMS process. In other words, Multiasistencia assimilates the claim within (1) the de<sup>fi</sup>ned framework or pattern of behavior and (2) with the committed Service Level Agreement (SLA's). One very important issue about assimilation is that Multiasistencia accepts service under the established limits of behavior. As an example the <sup>fi</sup>rm received in 2006 some 9,000,000 phone calls and carried out 1,200,000 repairs. According to one executive “Sometimes we need to inform about the exact coverage of insurance policies, or if we can take care of the claim and repair.”

Using the information system, once a repair order is open, no one needs to intervene unless an exception occurs. One executive explained that, “With the automation process, the number of errors and incidents in the process has dropped drastically. The number of telephone calls made from Multiasistencia has dropped from 10 to 1 and the back-of<sup>fi</sup>ce costs have gone down 66%”.

The results of automation have been: (1) to have more control and continuity of the process, (2) setting a framework or pattern of expected behavior, (3) have clean information <sup>fl</sup>ows between Multiasistencia and the other organizations in the network, and (4) have more information. Corporate clients can exploit information [16] by having access to the same information as any Multiasistencia CSR for any direct management with their client or to complement their own corporate systems.

Corporate clients can likewise make enquiries in real time through their personalized Internet Portal and cross-reference information from its contact channels, its corporate claim management system, and from external sources with the aim of providing benchmarking against averages for the sector. In particular, comparative information can be offered on the number of incidents open and their state, type of opening indices by type of clients, causes of repudiations by geographic area, invoicing details by claims, average costs or invoicing amounts. As one Multiasistencia executive added,

“Information management is a key element in our business and in the relations that we maintain with our clients has provided us with great credibility in the face of our clients. It is, however, all the more important to ensure that the data that we handle are correct and that we can have them when we need them. This is one of our priorities when making information technology investments.”

## 5.3. Accommodation

Within the case, accommodation has taken place in: (1) the international expansion of the <sup>fi</sup>rm; (2) organizational network change process affecting to the claim management process and the trade professional network; (3) the new structure of the trade professional network; and, since (4) the existing processes of automatic responses and assimilation provide additional information, thereby releasing resources to accommodate new requirements, exceptions and facilitating innovation with much more agility. Furthermore, customer-centric innovation [40] is part of the new identity of Multiasistencia.

First, Multiasistencia opened its United Kingdom headquarters in London's Docklands with 100 employees initially, including a small call center. Operations started in January, 1997. In the beginning, as one executive explained “We tried to impose our model in the UK, but we needed to adapt it to the particular market needs. They weren't at all interested in the CCMS. They were much more interested in getting a bolt-on claims management service. As a matter of fact, we offered repair services through our trade professional network.” Critical mass was another big issue since in UK as Multiasistencia started its operations from scratch.

Multiasistencia learned from this experience and redeployed its international strategy in France in late 1996. The <sup>fi</sup>rm developed a join-venture with a French leading construction and public works insurer which had immediate credibility in the French insurance market, opening doors to major insurers rapidly gaining the critical mass to carry on its operations. One year after operations started, Multiassistance France handled 60,000 repairs.

Second, the organizational network change process affected the CCMS (claim process) and also the functions and organization of the Control Center which had to accommodate to the capacity constraints imposed by the growth in demand. The organization of the Control Center is based on two teams: the “front of<sup>fi</sup>ce” is in charge of managing business as usual (our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of assimilate) and the “back of<sup>fi</sup>ce” is in charge of the whole repair process. The front of<sup>fi</sup>ce CSRs, by receiving incoming calls, proceed to open the repair process. The rest is automatic (see previous section): assigning the job to the appropriate trade professional and close the repair.

In short, “Our CSRs do not attend telephonic calls”, states one Multiasistencia executive, “they manage the repair process as owners, being responsible for predictive control of quality”. To that matter, CSRs manage more information as a result of the automation process since they have to understand the entire process [43] and have the ability to steer resources and information to track any deviation and work creatively digest this information based on their accumulated experience or knowledge [24], In this sense, Multiasistencia exhibits accommodation. The CSRs control a process as a combination of automatic responses, and business as usual processes (the system provides real time measurement of some 100 variables within the process), in addition to the active monitoring and response to deviations and rede<sup>fi</sup>nitions of standard operating practices.

Therefore, the processes now placed under automatic responses and assimilation generate additional informational resources that enable higher levels of organizational awareness, where the CSRs assume greater decision-making capacity and a higher level of responsibility. As an example, trade professionals have an agreed time from when the job request is assigned in the system. When the trade professional reaches the end customer's home, he reports this through a Mobile Portal or by sending an SMS. All this information is followed up in real time in Multiasistencia's Control Center. If Multiasistencia does not receive con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the appointment with the end customer in the preestablished period, an exception is generated, which will be dealt with by the CSR owner in charge of managing the claim until it is resolved.

Third, after 2000, the network of trade professionals had to accommodate to the new schema. Multiasistencia assured trade professionals of much higher volumes of work and asked them for more exclusive contracts and to increase their use of information technology. Indeed, in order to be able to manage the new volumes that Multiasistencia wished to spread among its members, the independent professional model had to be changed to small companies with a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of professionals to deal with this demand in the contractually determined times. Multiasistencia advised them on forming a business that could ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the requirements that they set. This change has resulted in most of the companies of trade professionals that work with Multiasistencia being franchised. These new franchises are connected in real time through the Internet, Web Services and mobile systems. The new organizational ties that conform the trade professional's network structure are more hierarchical than market-oriented.

Finally, Multiasistencia has boosted innovation in its original repair service by collaborating with its corporate clients creating a network for innovation. As one executive added “The 80% of our new software developments have been done in collaboration with our corporate clients”.

This collaboration assumed a variety of forms. First, the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers custom-designed services that include a complete range of features, such as comprehensive management of repairable or cash settlement claims, the provision of customer care help-lines, the management of networks of loss-adjusters and the management of recoveries (negotiated or through a judiciary process). Second, Multiasistencia has introduced innovations in the process: digitalized signature and electronic invoicing, which have increased savings up to 40%. Third, the creation of new services based on information like the management of claims adjuster reports on behalf of insurance companies. One manager added,

“When the repair assessment goes below an economic threshold, loss adjusters can carry out a “desk-top audit” on the Internet with the digital photographs our trade professionals take in customer houses. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst tests carried out with the new system, the inspection cost has been reduced by 60%.”

And <sup>fi</sup>nally, in 2005, one of the largest insurance companies in Europe and Multiasistencia collaborated to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a new service to inform end customers about the process whenever a claim was presented. The application sends Short Message Services (SMS) messages through cell phones to the end customers. As one of the insurer's executive explains,

“We inform our customers of every step we follow when he or she presents a claim and until the claim is closed. In order to ensure loyalty when the service is used, we not only have to satisfy quality expectations, but we also have to inform the customer. We have to add transparency to our service. Information is the key to increase loyalty.”

## 5.4. Environmental enactment

Environmental enactment refers to the creation of new domains of action [36], that is the creation of new markets, services delivered and customers. First, environmental enactment is the creation of the network itself. Second, environmental enactment is the design of a new model of relationships with corporate customers. The process of environmental enactment in Multiasistencia is best described as a movement from providing a cost-effective service to becoming the reference of innovative complex process-implementation company. According to one of the Multiasistencia's managers

“We have developed three key skills: front of<sup>fi</sup>ce capability, the management of very diverse networks and a powerful and seamless process based on the Internet. By taking advantage of these skills, we want to become the “processes factory” for our corporate clients, guaranteeing them speed and quality. We also have plans to develop new market opportunities.”

Multiasistencia is creating new domains of action beyond the repair services. Some actual corporate clients asked the <sup>fi</sup>rm to design and manage complex informational processes like mortgages, or changes of customer information from one bank branch to another. Multiasistencia also develops <sup>fi</sup>rm-driven innovation and offers new services (information services for mobile operators or the electronic secretary or assistant) to its actual base of customers or for <sup>fi</sup>nding new market opportunities.

## 6. Discussion

Multiasistencia's case shows how a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in a SBN has been able to develop new capabilities that are novel in the manner they combine people and technology to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll and adapt to new market needs and requirements. This SMB liberates people and informational resources from the day-to-day operations to create new knowledge, adapting to new scenarios to develop economies of growth [25] demonstrating a network operating system that is scalable, but also demonstrating self-renewing behavior [39].

From our theoretical framework and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the case study, we formulate a set of propositions that link and qualify the different modes of adaptability and may guide future research in the area.

First, Multiasistencia has developed a new network operating system that allows the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to manage adaptation among participants, structuring and integrating information among nodes and links, and allowing <sup>fi</sup>rms to establish compatible goals. In other terms, the new information system spread out in the network has helped to establish structured and institutionalized patterns of behavior [32]. This behavior is so highly embedded in the systems and processes that it is “dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify where the real smartness resides” [38], since the system is a combination of people and machines, that is a cybernetic system [19]. The system (1) tracks environmental variables [32], (2) shows selfregulating and self-organizing behavior adapting to environmental change [38], and (3) thereby creates an image of the environment where the action occurs.

One of the main enablers of this system is the substantial investments in ICT which have played a fundamental role in rede<sup>fi</sup>ning the relationships Multiasistencia manages in its professional network. The Franchise Internet Portal for trade professionals extends the same information as that available from the Control Center, enabling trade professionals to carry out 100% of the transactions de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Multiasistencia automatically. The system assures payments and has reduced the trade professionals' paperwork, which previously represented around 50% of their time. Moreover, Multiasistencia has been able to run operational tasks such as claim opening, automatic job assignment and claim closing, with full transparency to their service partners and customers [43]. This infrastructure not only allowed substantial reduction in administrative costs, it also increased the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (response times, satisfaction measures) of the service providers. Based on the evidence from the case, we formulate our <sup>fi</sup>rst proposition.

Proposition 1. The use of ICT for automatic responses and assimilation has a positive relationship with the operational performance of the business network.

It follows from Proposition 1 that the use of ICT has allowed the SBN to automate many processes and thus release limited resources. As one of the executives indicated,

“Our model is that of making the whole process transparent. The integration of processes on the Internet has allowed us to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a “virtual machine of <sup>fi</sup>nite states” with very precise expiry dates between states. The application has about 100 controlled steps linked to our client and professional applications. When an exception occurs, we must offer all the information available to the person directly responsible for its management to make the decision-making more effective.”

This exploitative use of ICT deals with the use of structured information, single-loop learning processes [2], and problem resolution [17]. Adding more experience to people running the business, and facilitating a learning process of the day-to-day activities that results in knowledge internalization and socialization [34]. On the other hand, as a result of more information, Multiasistencia has developed experimental uses of ICT, that have allowed the <sup>fi</sup>rm to develop new solutions and services – for instance, the Factory of Processes – based on informational exploration [16]. Propositions 2a and 2b follow.

Proposition 2a. The use of ICT for automatic responses and assimilation enables the generation of more operational and contextual information.

Proposition 2b. Additional operational and contextual information enables explorative processes such as accommodative actions and environmental enactment.

The case shows that accommodation is a form of incremental innovation that is performed in collaboration with corporate clients (e.g. tele-surveys, SMS customer information, etc.). On the other hand, more radical innovation can be found in the environmental enactment, that is, the generation of new services development (i.e. virtual/remote surveyor's inspection), process development (i.e. better task planning) and customer and market development. Hence, it follows that the increased levels of accommodation and environmental enactment lead to higher levels of incremental and radical innovation, respectively. Moreover, we can deduce that accommodation and environmental enactment are positively related to the ability to self-renew and appropriately adjust to environmental changes, thereby leading to greater sustainability of the SBN. Propositions 3a and 3b follow.

Proposition 3a. The degree of accommodation and environment enactment has a positive relationship with the degree of innovation of the business network

Proposition 3b. The degree of accommodation and environment enactment is positively related with the self-renewing behavior, and hence, sustainability of the business network.

However, it is important to recognize that adaptability in shortcycle learning modes (automatic responses and assimilation) require low awareness and low resources. In contrast, accommodation and environmental enactment (e.g. exception handling, agile responses to unde<sup>fi</sup>ned problems, or de<sup>fi</sup>ning new domains of action) [36] require higher levels of awareness and resources to develop explicit managerial knowledge [34]. Management is then an exercise of value generation by de<sup>fi</sup>ning limits for appropriate actions, commensurate with suitable expenditures of organizational resources. In other terms, a criterion for successful management is to know when to allocate limited resources on the more demanding long-cycle learning forms. Accordingly, Propositions 4a and 4b are de<sup>fi</sup>ned.

Proposition 4a. Long-cycle learning modes (accommodation and environmental enactment), require greater awareness and resources, and are therefore less frequent than short-cycle learning modes (automatic responses and assimilation).

Proposition 4b. Managerial awareness governs ex-ante decisions to choose the appropriate mix of organizational adaptability modes.

6.1. Contributions to theory

As mentioned previously, key characteristics of Smart Business Networks (SBN) are their ability to exhibit adaptive, agile and robust behavior [38]. However, adaptability in the context of business networks has received limited attention. Our analysis has synthesized diverse theories from learning to delineate a nascent theory of adaptability that consists of four distinct modes. Adaptability and its four modes are a function of awareness and resources. Automatic responses and assimilation require limited awareness and resources, where accommodation and environmental enactment demand signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater resources and managerial cognition. These observations are important for several reasons. First, there has been no shortage in the literature professing the merits of innovation, exploration and or other forms of long-cycle learning. Our analysis highlights that fact that while these capabilities are admirable, they are not always the most appropriate for the set of tasks or problems. Short cycle assimilation and automatic responses are equally important in operations of any organization and network. Moreover, our analysis also suggests that long-cycle learning modes have high costs in terms of organizational cognition and resources. The expenditure of limited resources on the wrong types of problems can lead to inef<sup>fi</sup>cient and ineffective and potentially destructive managerial decisions. Finally, we draw attention to the interdependent nature of the adaptability modes. The appropriate placement of processes in the automatic response and assimilation modes generates important bi-products; additional information and the release of constrained organizational resources that enable accommodation and environmental enactment.

## 6.2. Contributions to management

The challenge for management in a SBN is how to set a path for effectiveness, in other words, how to judge what are the appropriate responses and resources, every time management decides to develop new concepts, new services, new spaces. By managing information and knowledge to accommodate new situations, innovate products and services, and invent new domains of action, managers can ensure high levels of innovation as one of the core competences of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, it is important to carefully delineate between tasks and interventions requiring: 1) automatic responses, 2) assimilation, 3) accommodation, and 4) environmental enactment, such that managers can design processes and technology infrastructures that appropriately allocate limited organizational awareness and resources.

## 6.3. Limitations

As with all studies, this analysis has several limitations that warrant attention. First, all research on networks suffers from the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of delineating the unit of analysis, where in networks, many levels of analysis are entwined [13,18]. Secondly, we have appropriated theories with their genesis in individual learning and applied them towards organizations and networks with little consideration for how the theories become more or less adequate as we transgress levels of analysis. While we suggest that this contribution is novel, we also recognize that future research should consider the coordination and learning mechanisms that govern network behavior and their differences from other units of analysis. Namely, are models of individual or organizational cognition appropriate to study networks? If not, what modi<sup>fi</sup>cations or other theoretical foundations are more applicable. Moreover, we specify awareness and organizational resources as the main antecedents of adaptation modes. However, this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is fairly broad. Understanding the types of resources consumed at different levels of adaptation is also worthy of additional attention. Finally, the normal caveats of case study analysis also apply to this study. We have used this case in order to synthesize divergent theories of learning to explore a poorly understood, yet important characteristic of Smart Business Networks. Additional quali<sup>fi</sup>cation and validation across larger samples is obvious in a larger research agenda.

## 7. Conclusions

Whereas adaptability has been noted as a key characteristic of successful Smart Business Networks, the concept has received scant attention in the Business Networks and Information Systems literature. Accordingly, this paper draws upon theories of learning from the educational and organizational literature to develop a framework that de<sup>fi</sup>nes four distinct modes of adaptability in Smart Business Networks. The four modes of adaptability are: automatic responses, assimilation, accommodation, and environmental enactment. The modes of adaptability are a function of organizational awareness and the resources devoted. Where automatic responses and assimilation represent business as usual operations, accommodation and environment enactment place greater demands on organizational cognition and resources. We illustrate these modes in case study of Multiasistencia, the focal node of a Smart Business Network. The case highlights the need for Smart Business Networks to design information and process infrastructures that appropriately allocate limited organizational awareness and resources to the right kinds of tasks and processes to optimize the process of adaptation in Smart Business Networks. Finally, the theoretical propositions developed in this paper may serve to guide future research in Smart Business Networks.
