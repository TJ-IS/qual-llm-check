# INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY AND GOVERNMENT CORRUPTION IN DEVELOPING COUNTRIES: EVIDENCE FROM GHANA CUSTOMS<sup>1</sup>

Atta Addo Department of Digital Economy Entrepreneurship and Innovation Surrey Business School, University of Surrey, Guildford, U.K. {a.addo@surrey.ac.uk}

Chrisanthi Avgerou Department of Management, London School of Economics Houghton Street, London, U.K. {c.avgerou@lse.ac.uk}

The literature on information technology (IT) and government corruption in developing countries indicates contradictory evidence about the realization of anti-corruption effects. So far, there is no theoretical explanation of why the anti-corruption potential of IT demonstrated in some countries is not realized in many other countries. Drawing evidence from a case study of information systems interventions at Ghana customs over 35 years, we investigate how and why IT’s anti-corruption potential may be curtailed in the context of developing countries’ governments and societies. We focus on ITmediated petty corruption practices of street-level officers, which we consider to be socially embedded and institutionally conditioned phenomena. We find that conditions of possibility for the IT-mediated petty corruption practices are created during the implementation of information systems. The configuration of IT and organizational processes of a government agency are constrained by the broader government administration system and influenced by the vested interests of government officers, politicians, and businesses. Subsequently, the co-optation of IT for petty corruption practices is enabled by networks of relationships and institutions of patronage that extend across government, business, and society. We thus explain the often limited effects of IT on petty corruption as the inability of localized information systems implementations to change modes of government administration that are embedded in the enduring neopatrimonial institutions and politics of many developing countries.

Keywords: Government corruption, developing countries, anti-corruption, street-level bureaucracy, social embeddedness, IS user responses, IT co-optation, IT implementation, neopatrimonialism

## Introduction

Corruption in government occurs globally but is more widespread in developing countries, where it is decried as an inhibitor of “good governance” and national development (Bardhan 1997; Olken and Pande 2012). Views about the role of information technology (IT) in curbing corruption in government administrations of developing countries vary widely. Indicative of the extremes, IT is portrayed as a tool for stamping out corruption (Srivastava et al. 2016) or as an amplifier of corrupt practices (Toyama 2015). In the information systems (IS) literature, evidence of the effects of IT on corruption in developing countries is inconsistent. Several country-level studies have associated IT in government with the reduction of corruption (Andersen 2009; Mistry and Jalal 2012; Srivastava et al. 2016), whereas case studies of IT implementation in specific government agencies have revealed unfulfilled corruptioncurbing objectives (Davis 2004; Heeks 1999; Masiero and Prakash 2015).

Moreover, there is evidence that similar IT systems yield different anti-corruption effects in different countries. Under the influence of international development organizations that promote the global spread of good governance, generic software systems and best practice approaches have been deployed in developing countries to support administration reforms that reduce corruption (Hanna and Schware 1990; Kim 2014; World Bank 2016). However, effects have been idiosyncratic (World Bank 2011).

The implementation of the Tradenet system in customs clearance across countries is a case in point. The TradeNet system was first developed by Singapore in the 1980s to reduce the turnaround time for administrative procedures and to confer strategic benefits that would make the country a leading player in port operations. TradeNet’s launch in 1989 followed Singapore’s fight throughout the 1970s and ’80s against widespread corruption inherited from its colonial past (Klitgaard 1991), and the system quickly achieved effects that surpassed expectations (King and Konsynski 1995; Neo 1994; Teo et al. 1997). By the 1990s, Singapore was recognized as the least corrupt country in Asia and one of the least corrupt in the world. Analyses of its anti-corruption strategies highlight the reform of customs and other agencies as a contributor (Hin 2013; Teo et al. 1997). Customs administration, which is generally considered to be prone to corruption, was particularly corrupt in Singapore before becoming with TradeNet, an exemplar of the country’s ITenabled government transformation.

The TradeNet software was subsequently trademarked and became available around the world as a best practice “single window” system, enabling regulatory and other administrative processes to be provided by a single online entry point. However, TradeNet has not replicated Singapore’s remarkable transformation of international trade administration and corruption curtailment in any country. In this paper, we study this empirical puzzle by investigating the implementation of TradeNet and its effects on customs corruption in Ghana. From this case study, we draw insights to explain why IT implementation and concomitant reorganization in government agencies of developing countries may not bring about intended anti-corruption effects.

We focus our research on petty corruption, which we define as the opportunistic self-serving abuse of public office by street-level government officers as they engage in the routines of administration. Street-level officers are “public service workers who interact directly with citizens in the course of their jobs” (Lipsky 1980, p. 3). We consider the persistence of petty corruption with TradeNet as a case of unrealized intended outcomes of IT-enabled organizational change and seek to identify its contextual enablers.

Our research delves into the context of IT-mediated corruption practice with two lines of investigation. The first is a view of street-level officers’ IT-mediated petty corruption practices as embedded in networks of social relationships and local institutions. The second is a diachronic view that considers the anti-petty corruption effects of IT use in relation to IT implementation interventions according to the punctuated theory of IS and organizational change (Lyytinen and Newman 2008). From this two-dimensional perspective, we observe that street-level officers’ IT-mediated practices of petty corruption are supported by networks of relationships that extend beyond a government agency to the whole system of government administration, political systems, and society. We also observe that the conditions of possibility for practices of petty corruption with IT are created by actors involved in the decisions regarding systems implementations. This includes influential actors external to the agency that hosts a new information system, who convey concerns and interests from the broader government administration as well as the domains of politics and business. The networks of relationships that we found implicated in the IT implementations and subsequent IT-mediated corruption practices in the case study of Ghana customs adhere to neopatrimonial institutional logic, which is prevalent in many developing countries.

Our analysis therefore suggests that IT implementations that introduce anti-petty corruption managerial logics into government agencies, such as Ghana customs, are too limited in scope to confer enduring deep change. We explain the limited corruption-curtailing effects of IT in government agencies as the result of constraints to organizational change exerted by relationships and institutions of patronage that extend across the administration and into the domains of politics, business, and the everyday life of the citizens of many developing countries.

## Theoretical Foundations

## IT and Petty Corruption in Government Administration of Developing Countries

Organizational interventions to fight petty corruption aim to change the conduct of street-level officers. The IS literature suggests that IT enables such change by removing corruption opportunities and by providing transparency, managerial control, and accountability (Choudrie et al. 2017; Heeks 1999; Neupane et al. 2012).

Opportunities for petty corruption arise primarily because of the discretion available to street-level officers within government organizations (Brehm and Gates 1999; Lipsky 1980). Discretion is understood as “the power or the right of deciding according to one’s judgment” (Langbein 2004, p. 377). Contrary to a common view about government bureaucracy that officers strictly implement policies emanating from the top of the government structure, street-level officers exercise discretion in the course of their everyday tasks and, in so doing, administration practice may differ significantly from high-level formal policies (Lipsky 1980, p. 13).

Discretion is considered vital for the functioning of government administrations, enabling public service delivery in situations of high ambiguity and imperfect information (Hupe and Hill 2007; Tummers & Bekkers 2014). It allows flexibility in the application of rigid rules to specific situations and achieves efficient allocation of government resources, increased effectiveness, and relevance (Brodkin 1997; de Witte et al. 2016).

Studies of officers’ discretion in developing countries show that they negotiate multiple and often competing interests from the complex web of relationships that they are embedded in (Blundo and Olivier de Sardan 2006; Chalfin 2010). Such relationships involve upward ties to actors in higher-level government organizations or political leadership, horizontal ties to other public agencies and officers, as well as ties to citizens, businesses, or other service-seekers. Aside from the official, top-down policies that guide street-level officers, the network of relationships within which they are embedded provides important and frequently more immediate influences on their behavior (Hupe and Hill 2007). The challenge for anticorruption reforms that aim to ensure compliance with topdown “rules of the game” is to allow officers the flexibility to act without being able to “game the rules” (De Herdt and Olivier de Sardan 2015).

There is limited understanding of the effects of IT on streetlevel discretion that games the rules. IT systems like TradeNet provide routine task standardization, extensive automation, and “best practice” principles, but their local implementations vary substantially. Furthermore, we know little about the responses of street-level officers and their managers to discretionconstraining IT functions. Research in advanced economies suggests that IT can enhance or constrain street-level discretion, depending on the institutional context of administration in which officers develop their strategies of compliance or defiance (André et al. 2018).

Following this literature on discretion, we consider IT-mediated petty corruption to be socially embedded action. Thus, our approach differs from others that assume the anti-corruption effects of IT to be the aggregate outcome of rationally acting individuals—for example, research informed by principal-agent models of corruption (Cho and Choi 2004; Kim et al. 2009; Klitgaard 1991; Neupane et al. 2012; Rose-Ackerman 1999). Instead, we adopt a view of street-level officers as social actors (Avgerou and Li 2013; Lamb and Kling 2003; Rai et al. 2009), which is supported by anthropologists’ studies of public administration practices in developing countries (Blundo and Olivier de Sardan 2006; Bersselaar and Decker 2011; Hellmann 2017). We draw from and contribute to the theoretical stream that considers IT-mediated practice to be influenced by networks of social relationships and subject to social institutions.

Two empirical questions emerge from this theoretical framing: How does the implementation of IT systems intended to curtail corruption affect the discretionary petty corruption practices of street-level government officers in developing countries? What social conditions enable IT-mediated discretionary petty corruption practices in such contexts?

## A Process View of IT-Enabled Organizational Change

To address the first empirical question, we adopt the process view of punctuated change theory, which provides a longitudinal perspective of IT and organizational change (Besson and Rowe 2012; Lyytinen and Newman 2008). Lyytinen and Newman (2008) follow Pettigrew’s (1985) layered contextual process analysis and propose a sociotechnical punctuated change view of IS and organizational change as a diachronic interaction between an IS “building system,” a work system, its organizational context, and the broader environment of the organization. According to this view, critical incidents trigger relatively brief periods of “system upheaval,” that is, action to work out radical change in technology and organizational structures and processes. The triggers may be the emergence of misalignments between an organization’s information system and its work system or events external to an organization. Radical change periods are then followed by “evolutionary” periods of relatively stable practices. Radical change concerns the destabilization and transformation of deep structures— “the set of fundamental choices a system has made of the basic parts into which the units will be organized and the basic activity patterns that will maintain its existence” (Gersick 1991, p. 14). After implementation, change may continue incrementally in the evolutionary period, as users adjust their work practices and the use of technology. The two periods involve different actions and actors. In the radical change period, teams of internal and external IT and management professionals and user representatives engage primarily in design-oriented action (Jenkin et al. 2019; Silva and Hirschheim 2007). We use the term implementation to refer to the whole range of radical change tasks, which typically comprise the configuration of the IT system, concomitant design of the work process of an organization, and their introduction in organizational practice. In the evolutionary period, non-IT experts exercise their discretion and use IT to shape their routine organizational tasks (Orlikowski 2000).

Scholars acknowledge that deep-structure changes are not necessarily produced as intended during the radical stage of IS implementation. IS implementation may alter work processes but not affect deep structures (Lyytinen and Newman 2008; Silva and Hirschheim 2007). Many studies have contributed explanations of diversions from intended outcomes during implementation, including studies of misalignments of the new system with existing organizational processes and structures, and studies of contestations and conflicts surrounding IS projects (Lapointe and Rivard 2005; Lyytinen 1988; Mähring et al. 2004; Markus 1983).

After implementation, the realization of intended deep structure changes is affected by users’ responses to the technology artifacts and work processes (Berente et al. 2019; Ciborra 2000; Orlikowski 1996). Resistance to change (Lapointe and Rivard 2005) and unfaithful appropriations of the designed IS may allow drift away from the intended deep structure change (Ciborra 2000).

The adjustments that individuals improvise in their use of IT systems (Orlikowski 2000) were studied in relation to groups (Lapointe and Rivard 2005), networks (Schultze and Orlikowski 2004), and institutions (Avgerou and Li 2013; Berente et al. 2019; Lamb and Kling 2003). Two kinds of embeddedness are suggested by such research: relational and institutional. Relational embeddedness refers to interpersonal ties that regulate an individual’s behavior (Granovetter 1985; Uzzi 1997). Institutional embeddedness refers to the regulating effects on the behavior of individuals by material and symbolic elements of social entities, such as organizations, the government sector, or the nation-state (Friedland and Alford 1991; Thornton et al. 2012).

## IT and Organizational Change in Government Organizations of Developing Countries

Research seeking to explain the high frequency of IS failure in developing countries has shown that the deep structure change entailed by an IS project is often unsupported by local infrastructural, cultural, or political conditions, or is undesirable to powerful local stakeholders. Assumptions about the context of use built into technology systems are often different from local organizational conditions, resulting in “design/actuality gaps” (Heeks 2002). Such extraordinary conditions are not adequately addressed by the techniques that IS development professionals follow to design and implement information systems. IS implementation, therefore, involves “design improvisation” to bridge the gap between the inscribed work system in IT applications transferred from their original design context and the “actuality” of organizational and broader life conditions in developing countries (Heeks 2002).

The implementation of systems with anti-corruption goals in government organizations in developing countries introduces managerial rationalities that are often radically different from and incompatible with the principles that underpin historically developed arrangements for the delivery of public services. IS implementations such as TradeNet and the reorganization they entail embody principles of good governance and New Public Management (NPM). This mode of administration has been championed by international development organizations as part of donor programs to reengineer public sector structures and processes. It involves the privatization of functions and the introduction of business-like practices to improve effectiveness and service quality (Brinkerhoff and Brinkerhoff 2015; McCourt 2001).

Improvisations to bridge the design/actuality gap confronting IS projects in government organizations in developing countries may erode the managerial rationality of the intended reorganization and lessen its anti-corruption effects. IS projects are initiated at the “top” by government officials or development organizations and involve IT vendors, consultants, and other experts (Common 1998; Korac-Kakabadse et al. 2000; Saint-Martin 2000). They tend to follow generic technical-rational approaches, like the International Standards Organization (ISO) criteria, to design processes for corruption-free administration. Interest-laden conflicts that interfere with such technical-rational approaches and design improvisations that adjust the generic software and best practice principles are poorly understood.

In the following case study, we consider the TradeNet implementation that aimed to curtail revenue losses resulting from petty corruption at Ghana customs as an attempt of deep structure organizational change. We investigate the opportunities for street-level officers’ corruption-enabling discretion that were created by the configuration of the TradeNet software and the processes of import clearance. We then trace various acts of petty corruption improvised by streetlevel officers and their managers and highlight the relational and institutional conditions in which the administration officers’ abuses of discretion were embedded.

## Empirical Strategy

Given our theoretical interest in IT and changes in petty corruption over time, we opted for a longitudinal case study. We studied the import clearance process at Ghana’s customs administration, where a succession of IT systems had been deployed between the 1980s and 2019. Our analysis focuses on the implementation and use of TradeNet import clearance practices, and the associated managerial controls over the latter fifteen years (2004-2019).

## Study Setting

Customs administration is known globally to be particularly prone to corruption (Hors 2001; Mclinden and Durrani 2013; Widdowson 2013). The widespread corruption in customs had been a worrying concern for the World Customs Organization (WCO), the global body that represents over 180 national customs agencies (World Customs Organization 2012). A former secretary-general of the WCO observed that “there are few public agencies in which the classic pre-conditions for institutional corruption are so conveniently presented as in a customs administration. The potent mixture of administrative monopoly coupled with the exercise of wide discretion, particularly in a work environment that may lack proper systems of control and accountability” (Mclinden and Durrani 2013, p. 4).

Corruption persisted at Ghana customs despite IT-supported reorganizations that began in the 1980s (Abbey 2015). Although TradeNet reorganization was heralded for yielding significant improvements (Adaba and Rusu 2017; De Wulf 2005), it remained unclear how the system affected corruption (Addo 2016; Addo and Senyo 2020). Our primary research site was the customs field office at Ghana’s largest seaport, the Tema Harbor, where 80% of goods were cleared. At this point, the harbor was a bustling hub of activities that were underpinned by customs processes and TradeNet. We gained access to senior and junior personnel from customs, other government organizations, and the private sector (Appendix).

## Data Collection

We collected data during six research trips to Ghana between 2014-2019. The trips lasted 12 months in total and differed in scope. We explored the organizational setting of Ghana customs on our first trip to become familiar with our key informants and the case. A year after the first visit, we conducted the main data collection over five months in 2015. During the second trip, we collected data on clearance processes before and during TradeNet as well as data on the implementation of TradeNet and its precursor system. We conducted subsequent trips in 2016, 2018, and 2019 to follow up on the rapid pace of change involving the introduction of new IT systems. These trips also allowed us to validate themes from earlier data collection and update informants who were keen to learn about the progress of the study. Table 1 summarizes the data collection process.

Collecting data on IT implementation and organizational processes was straightforward. However, collecting data on corruption was challenging because corruption is selfconcealing, meaning that those involved were reluctant to disclose their involvement or to reveal incriminating details of the practice. Given the limited scope to directly observe a full range of corruption practices at customs, we relied on a mix of evidence sources including (1) interviews with informants to identify perceived corruption within customs and across stakeholder groups, as well as users’ experiences of corruption during TradeNet’s implementation, (2) documented accounts of corruption, e.g. by whistleblowers and bribe payers, state auditors, investigative and news journalists, and (3) participant observation of customs clearance activities. The breadth of evidence sources gave us reliable insights as we were able to triangulate and overcome retrospective biases of interviewees as well as their self-serving narratives. Table 2 summarizes our data corpus, with further methodological details given in the Appendix.

## Data Analysis

We analyzed the data in iterative steps that included organizing and classifying with qualitative analysis software (Atlas.ti), coding of detailed descriptions to identify emerging themes, and writing explanatory narratives that were vetted by informants. Because we were interested in understanding IT and petty corruption from the perspective of local actors and their practices, we adopted an interpretive stance (Klein and Myers 1999; Walsham 2006). The analysis addressed the questions emerging from our literature review of petty corruption—namely, what discretion was afforded to streetlevel officers by the TradeNet system and what social conditions enabled their abuse of discretion for practices of petty corruption. We traced the process of TradeNet implementation along the trajectory of IT implementations at Ghana customs, petty corruption practices in customs import clearance with TradeNet, and the relationships and institutions within which the street-level officers’ petty corruption practices were embedded.

## Data Coding

To identify the subprocesses and activities of trade clearance, we used the process flow charts published in the official customs manual (Ghana Revenue Authority 2016) and we supplemented these with accounts of declarants’ actual experiences. We then associated the subprocesses and activities with corruption instantiations derived from our data using inductive coding following the grounded theory method (Strauss and Corbin 1998).

Open coding derived 20 unique instantiations of petty corruption that were linked to interactions among 19 sets of actors involved in about 42 activities in the 5 subprocesses of import clearance.

## Ghana Customs IT Implementations

Customs administration is vital in developing countries because the proportion of government revenues from duties and trade-related taxes tends to be higher than in economies that have more diversified revenue sources (Cantens 2012). For example, customs provided 40% of Ghana’s revenues in 2011, roughly equivalent to all direct domestic taxes (Bank of Ghana 2012, p. 3). Customs has therefore been pivotal to the development of Ghana, and IT implementations have been historically carried out to improve capacity in that regard. Figure 1 presents a timeline of key events related to IT implementations.

## Customs Clearance before TradeNet (1980s-2004)

The paper-based customs practices involved at least 30 manual steps prior to the introduction of IT in the 1980s. Oversight was weak and practices idiosyncratic. Paperwork was bulky, error-prone, slow, and required serial interactions with street-level officers from desk to desk, station to station, and one agency to another. With over 20 government agencies involved in trade clearance depending on the type of goods, there were severe backlogs, high error rates, and expensive delays. Importantly, manual processing involved abundant face-to-face interactions between officers and declarants, and with this, plentiful opportunities for abusing bureaucratic discretion and pursuing petty corruption.

The Automated System for Customs Declaration (ASYCUDA), a data management system developed by the UN for developing countries, was introduced at Ghana customs in the mid-1980s. It supported a single administrative document (SAD) that declarants used to assess duties and taxes consistently via harmonized codes. Entries could be checked against a database to determine taxes payable; however, declarants still had to submit multiple paper copies to various agencies.

At customs, document processing occurred at a row of windows in the “long room”—a hallway where officers sat behind glass stalls to interact with declarants. Paper copies used at different steps were entered manually into ASYCUDA and all other steps were also done manually over several weeks or months.

## Practices of Petty Corruption Before TradeNet

ASYCUDA enabled changes that limited the discretion of officers in the long room. For example, it made it “less possible, and therefore less enticing, for cashiers to tamper with daily account receipts in their cash books because as payments were made, they were put into the computer, thereby making monitoring instantaneous” (Tettey 1997, p. 344). Nonetheless, malpractice still occurred. For example, a government audit in 1994 found that “cash payments made by importers which were duly supported with cash slips were recorded as cheque payments with cheque numbers quoted, strongly suggesting that the cash was embezzled” (Government of Ghana 1994, pp. 30-31).

Officers also falsified entries on the electronic system and faked receipts:

Two customs officials at Tema have been dismissed for conniving with an Accra-based trading company in an attempt to dupe the state of customs duties ... employees keyed the information on the transaction into the computer and gave out computer receipts without examining or demanding the necessary documents from the importer (Daily Graphic, November 4, 1994, qtd. in Tettey 1997, p. 351).

As a veteran customs officer commenting on corruption before TradeNet recalled:

The corruption was shocking! Some senior collectors had two large boxes in their office. They often said, “this box is for the government, and that is mine.” By the close of the day, their box overflowed while the government’s was not halfway. Some ran loan schemes from their gains (Assistant Commissioner [retired], July 2014).

## TradeNet Design and Implementation (2002- 2004)

TradeNet implementation followed international trade reforms that gained momentum in the 1990s when organizations such as the World Trade Organization (WTO), World Customs Organization (WCO), World Bank, and International Monetary Fund (IMF) promoted international trade as part of development programs. Developing countries like Ghana were pushed to prioritize foreign trade as a path toward socioeconomic development. By the early 2000s, trade facilitation became an international issue with developmental ramifications, and TradeNet emerged out of the government of Ghana and the World Bank program with the intent of making Ghana a middle-income country with competitive advantages in port operations and trade (World Bank 2010). Corruption in frontline agencies like customs was targeted because it not only curtailed government revenues but also disadvantaged businesses and stifled economic growth (World Bank, 2014).

Inspiration for Ghana’s TradeNet came from Singapore’s highly lauded experience from the 1990s. Ghana’s system was developed by Crimsonlogic, the company that developed the Singapore version. Like the successful version in Singapore, Ghana’s TradeNet was a “single window” system that could, in principle, allow a single electronic submission to fulfill all requirements, in contrast to the previous “multiwindow” regime. The system streamlined processes and interactions by linking TradeNet servers to a new customs management system, the Ghana Customs Management Systems (GCMS). With modular, configurable features and risk management functions, GCMS allowed customs to conduct document verification, compliance checks, the examination of goods, revenue reconciliation, and data reporting within the TradeNet environment. The TradeNet-GCMS system was managed by a public-private partnership company, the Ghana Community Network Services Ltd (GCNET), and was networked with shippers, declarants, banks, and over 20 government agencies involved in foreign trade. In this paper, we focus on the composite TradeNet-GCMS and refer to it simply as TradeNet.

As part of the reorganization that accompanied TradeNet implementation, functions of import/export valuation and classification were outsourced from customs to five private destination inspection companies (DICs). DICs work online, so declarants generally did not encounter customs officers until after valuation and classification and during the compliance and examination stages at the port. DICs’ profits were tied to revenues collected, and this created competition and incentive to maximize collections through superior managerial controls.

## Attempting Radical Change with TradeNet

TradeNet comprised commodity software and hardware artifacts that were deployed into various countries and configured to fit the context of each. Implementation in Ghana was accompanied by the reorganization of customs clearance processes according to New Public Management and good governance best practices that were expected to result in high service standards. Such reorganization was a precondition for the ongoing development assistance extended to Ghana by international development organizations and the country’s donor partners. Process reconfiguration and automation resulted in rapid service improvements such as the decrease in the average number of processing steps from 30 or more in the pre-TradeNet period down to less than 10. TradeNet enabled about 70% of documents to be processed under five days (GCNET 2015), a significant improvement in comparison to several weeks or months it previously took. Metrics such as the World Bank’s logistics performance index (LPI) showed Ghana moving up 36 ranks among 160 countries in the efficiency of customs and border management (from 129th in 2007 to 93rd by 2016) (World Bank 2016). Such process improvements yielded gains in customs financial metrics. Revenues grew by 31% year-on-year despite cargo volumes only growing by 6% over the first decade of TradeNet. Although the type of goods had not fundamentally changed, revenue growth outpaced cargo volume growth by a factor of five, suggesting significant efficiency gains and potential reduction in corruption-related losses (GPHA 2015; GRA 2015).

Nevertheless, the potential of TradeNet implementation to curtail corruption was dampened by choices made during the configuration of the software and the organizational redesign. TradeNet integrated with government agencies, but most of these agencies were unable to take full advantage of the system’s functionality. All had manual internal processes that required paper forms to be scanned and attached to the electronic declaration form. TradeNet’s single window configuration in Ghana, therefore, remained at the most rudimentary level of single window configurations possible (Level 1: paperless customs declaration form) (UNESCAP/UNECE 2012). In contrast, the Level 5 single window configuration in Singapore allowed fully electronic, that is, paperless integration across all regulatory agencies, entire port communities, logistics, and private companies, as well as multiple national single windows across borders (UNESCAP/UNECE 2012). The rudimentary configuration of Ghana’s TradeNet was not for lack of technology or knowhow. As a senior manager at GCNET Ltd explained, “the problem we have is not technology … we know how to do it … but how do you force powerful people [across the government] who may appear to want change but do not, because of vested interests?” (Manager 1, June 2015).

GCNET Ltd attempted to eliminate paper use by developing add-on modules for ministries and other agencies to digitize and automate their internal processes in line with TradeNet’s paperless environment. Nevertheless, there was resistance and the system maintained parts of the existing manual/paperbased setup. In a particular government agency, only one officer could approve paper permits and processes ground to a halt whenever he was absent. The task of approving paper permits was considered by his colleagues as “his turf” (Eye on the Ports 2015). The General Manager of the GCNET Ltd condemned the compromises by stakeholders: “there are still elements in the system (stakeholders, government officials) who insist on paper instead of automation, and prefer that ‘you (declarants) come and see me (the officer)’ … for obvious reasons [corruption]” (Eye on the Ports 2015).

As a result, the clearance process became a compromise between the vision for a fully integrated, “one-stop” online process (a Level 5 single window), and the pre-TradeNet multiwindow. A senior GCNET Ltd employee noted that “officers are used to face to face contact for whatever reasons. Even if you send your form online with no paper, they will still be in the ‘long room’ expecting hard copies. Without the paperwork, they refuse to work” (Senior Product Specialist, July 2015).

From the viewpoint of anti-corruption reorganization, such behavior could be seen as an attempt to recreate diminished face-to-face opportunities that facilitated petty corruption. Officers pointed to a “turf war” between customs and the DICs, insisting that the classification and valuation functions should be the remit of customs rather than private companies. As one officer complained, “it makes no sense that the DICs should be doing valuations and classification. This is our core function and even if you look at the bottom of the import declaration form [she points to the printed form] it says that customs are the statutory authority” (Officer C, July 2015).

## Withdrawal of TradeNet (2016-2020)

In the autumn of 2015, Ghana’s President, John Mahama, issued a sudden directive for the discontinuance of TradeNet. Another single window system was to be contracted to a littleknown IT company in violation of Ghana’s public procurement laws and processes (Kubi 2015). It later came to light that the company, West Blue Consulting, had no experience in Ghana and a problematic record from previous customs IT work in neighboring Nigeria (Maritime & Transport Digest 2015). The contract was awarded by the government of the political party (the NDC) that opposed the NPP political party that awarded the TradeNet and DIC contracts when it was in office in the early 2000s. Furthermore, the owner of West Blue was personally linked to the ruling NDC party and the president (Kubi 2015).

The West Blue contract took the trading community, including customs officers, by surprise, creating panic and uncertainty. Critics pointed to the high risks, potential backlogs, and costs involved in abruptly transitioning to different IT systems rather than improving existing ones. Notwithstanding such protestations, and with little preparation or training for customs, the five destination inspection companies were dissolved. Under the new arrangement, the classification and valuations work returned to customs, and a new unit, the Customs Technical Services Bureau (CTSB), was launched.

Although the initial plan was to replace TradeNet completely, it quickly became apparent that such fundamental change was unrealizable without collapsing the entire clearance process. An eventual compromise was a “mashup” system that combined TradeNet with West Blue’s Pre-Arrival Assessment Reporting System (PAARS), Risk Management System (RMS), and a Joint Inspection Management Information System (JIMIS). The resulting “system of systems,” as West

Blue described it, was to advance Ghana’s single window toward higher levels of integration and automation. In practice, however, the new configuration did not improve much upon TradeNet. Revenue improvements were reported (Awiah 2018) but petty corruption persisted.

After a change in government in 2016, the new NPP government similarly announced the abrogation of the TradeNet and West Blue systems, and the procurement of Universal Pass (UNIPASS), a new system developed by the Customs UNIPASS International Agency (CUPIA), a South Korean company (Daily Guide 2018). There was no convincing justification as to why a new system was needed, and the UNIPASS contract was awarded without a competitive procurement process (Suleiman 2017). Ghana’s cabinet approved UNIPASS despite a lawsuit by West Blue alleging that UNIPASS had stolen and cloned its software (Acquah-Hayford 2019). In January 2020, the government ordered both West Blue and GCNET Ltd to hand over their systems to UNIPASS. However, months after UNIPASS went live, it failed to meet technical targets (Bokpe 2020), creating backlogs in import clearance (Amoah 2020; Ghana Web 2020a) and resulting in the decline of port revenues (Business & Financial Times 2020; Ghana Web 2020b).

## Corruption in TradeNet Practices

A decade after TradeNet operations, according to a baseline survey commissioned by Ghana customs in 2015, the business community and the Ghanaian public still regarded customs as one of the least trusted government agencies. 94% of freighters, 60% of traders, and 25% of customs officers claimed to have experienced or witnessed corruption in the past year (Abbey 2015).

Import clearance occurred by declarants first making a selfdeclaration to customs. Customs then verified the validity and accuracy of declarations to assess payable duties and taxes and the fulfillment of all regulatory conditions before goods could be released from the port. According to Ghana customs’ regulations, importers were not permitted to process their own clearance but could only do so through a customs house agent (clearing agent). Other intermediaries included officers from over 20 government agencies and private companies like shippers and banks (Soputamit 2019).

The clearance process comprised five subprocesses: the submission of manifests, the submission of the Import Declaration Form (IDF) and issuing of the Final Classification and Valuation Report (FCVR) (hereafter “declaration form” and “valuation report”), compliance, physical examinations, and preventive measures. Customs clearance is complex, and several other contingent subprocesses could be activated depending on the type of goods being cleared, e.g., inspections, special permits and licensing, customs lab testing, scanning examinations, and exemptions. A full list of Ghana’s customs processes can be found in a 582-page customs process manual (Ghana Revenue Authority 2016). All customs subprocesses are susceptible to corruption but, for simplicity, we illustrate corruption findings from the five core clearance subprocesses that implicate TradeNet. Each of the five subprocesses further consisted of a set of interdependent activities carried out by technology and the various traderelated actors (Table 3).

## Subprocess 1: Manifest Submission

The manifest was an official document that listed the nature and quantity of items to be cleared, as well as details such as the shipment carrier, consignor, consignee, origination, and value. It was submitted by the shipping line/shipping agent to customs through a set of prescribed activities. On first inspection, there appeared to be limited scope for corruption in manifest submissions because the declarant/shipping agent interfaced directly with TradeNet when making submissions. Nonetheless, a hard copy of the manifest was still required to validate the electronic submission to ensure a match. The endto-end subprocess remained corruption-prone because manifest entries often concealed or misrepresented cargo, and the mix of hard copy and electronic version requirements for validation provided an opportunity for physical mediation and negotiation by officers, who could be bribed to overlook dodgy manifests or to skip requests for supplementary information when prior relationships existed with a declarant. Such collusion promoted underdeclaration and the evasion of duties and taxes. Furthermore, the screening of paper manifests in addition to electronic versions made activities more cumbersome and inefficient, thereby allowing delays and holdups that induced “speed money” payments.

## Subprocess 2: Declaration Form Submission / Issuing of a Valuation Report

Submission of the import declaration form and the subsequent issuance of a valuation report were critical steps of interest to a range of actors because how goods were classified and valued determined their treatment throughout the clearance process, as well as how much duty and taxes were paid. An assessment of high duties and taxes and the subsequent failure to pay could lead to the lawful seizure and ultimate forfeiture of goods to the state.

This subprocess retained several nondigitalized steps and was the locus of pervasive petty corruption, given the high stakes. The following petty corruption practices were common: Misdescription—where a declarant willfully entered the wrong item description or attributes in the system. Misclassification—where a classification officer (from a DIC or customs) willfully assigned wrong classification codes, e.g. by assigning a high-value product with the code for a cheaper alternative or different item. Misinvoicing—where falsified or inaccurate invoices were attached to support a claim. Underdeclaration—where dutiable goods were deliberately not declared or concealed with false information such as the wrong quantity, which was common with importers from the private sector. Overdeclaration—where the values of dutiable goods were inflated to allow corrupt importers to embezzle the surplus value through kickbacks, which was common with public officials and procurement officers from other government agencies. Data theft—where officers took declarants’ contact information from the system and contacted them secretly to make personal demands for cash or favors. Abuse of exemption codes—where exemption protocols for nondutiable goods were violated and exemption codes were wrongly applied to dutiable goods. Override of automatic assignment features—where officers overrode the randomization feature meant to prevent collusion by automatically assigning declarations. Even where this was not done manually in the system, officers still coordinated informally to contravene formal processes. Such corruption instantiations occurred through collusion between declarants seeking to evade duties and taxes and confederates in customs (or the DICs) who contravened rules for bribes.

## Subprocess 3: Compliance

Compliance checked all declarations and supporting documents to ensure they were valid (true) and accurate (consistent with their supporting documents). Whereas the clearance data was captured entirely electronically by this stage, compliance remained a manual process that reverted to paper copies and required face-to-face interactions. Like in Subprocess 2, customs officers exercised significant discretion. Corruption at the compliance stage involved declarants bribing officers to deliberately override the automated risk profiling functionality to send unqualified consignments from the red lane to the green lane where they would not undergo a physical examination. It also involved officers abusing the query function to create face-to-face opportunities with declarants so that they could solicit or negotiate bribe payments. Officers sometimes abused the resubmission function to frustrate declarants into paying bribes, falsified remarks to allow or justify favorable treatment, deliberately held-up paperwork to induce the payment of bribes, and delayed declarants with redundant activities, thus inducing the payment of speed money.

## Subprocess 4: Physical Examination

Physical examination involved opening containers of cargo to search and examine their content manually. Previously, this subprocess was carried out by all the government regulatory agencies with an interest in a consignment. With reorganization, examinations were streamlined into one joint examination. Physical examination brought street-level officers into direct contact with the goods of anxious declarants for the first time in the clearance process. Most declarants reported dreading the physical examination process, not because they had anything to hide but because of the unpleasantness of having their goods or personal belongings rummaged through and intrusively searched by groups of meddlesome and sometimes hostile examination officers. Common corruption practices during physical examination involved the pilfering and theft of goods, the unlawful seizure of goods, extortion and solicitation for cash or goods, and deliberate hold-ups of the process, e.g., when officers deliberately refused to show up for joint examinations.

## Subprocess 5: Preventive

The customs preventive unit acted as the last line before goods exited the port. Their functions were redundant and overlapping with the compliance and examination functions, as well as the work of security agencies at the ports. Nonetheless, their role remained entrenched even after reorganization at the port. Preventive officers were located at the customs yard, and visitors could see them freely soliciting bribes and holding up declarants. Along with other security actors at the ports, such as the police, port security, Ghana Ports and Harbors Authority (GPHA) security, and national security, among others, they were frequently accused of egregious corruption. Since they made limited use of the TradeNet system, corruption was largely “offline” and involved activities such as soliciting and receiving bribes, extortion, unlawfully seizing goods, and holding up activities, e.g., by deliberately misplacing a service seeker’s documents.

## Street-Level Discretion and TradeNet Petty Corruption (2004-2015)

Despite TradeNet reorganizations, multiple instantiations of petty corruption emerged along the subprocesses of import clearance. Some of them involved the use of the TradeNet system, others were practiced in the manual tasks of the IS configuration. While some instantiations of corruption were practiced by individual street-level officers (who exercised their isolated discretion), others involved fellow officers from customs or other government agencies, as well as political actors such as politicians and their associates. Crucially, several instantiations of petty corruption involved service seekers (declarants and their agents such as freight forwarders, shipping, and clearing agents) who either initiated them or were willing to accept them. ## Enabling Conditions of Petty Corruption with TradeNet

Customs had a hierarchical structure as part of the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA) which was formed in 2009 to combine revenue organizations such as the internal revenue service (IRS) responsible for income tax, and the value-added tax (VAT) division, responsible for sales and service taxes. The governing board of the GRA consisted of nine members appointed by the president (Daily Guide 2017a). Customs was headed by a commissioner who was similarly appointed by the president. Even though formal processes existed for public service recruitments, customs staff selection and promotions were highly political and nepotistic. Junior staff members were typically hired through “protocol appointment,” whereby powerful politicians or senior officers handpicked a candidate for an appointment—for example, a relative, a protégé, or a partisan. Senior ranks were only attained with influence from the presidency or other members of the government in office. As a result, the independence, fairness, and objectivity of customs were fundamentally challenged.

Like other organizations, customs had three operational layers—a senior managerial layer responsible for oversight and policy, a managerial layer responsible for controls, and an implementation layer responsible for day-to-day activities at the street level. Senior managers were at the rank of senior collector and above, whereas managerial roles were played by principal collectors and collectors. Officers at the rank of assistant collector and below worked at the street level. Despite such a seemingly efficient bureaucratic structure, weak oversight, ineffective supervision, and process violations remained commonplace.

## Challenges to Effective Managerial Controls for Anti-Corruption

Senior officers were expected to balance facilitating trade and maximizing government revenue collections. The complexities and contingencies of customs procedures necessitated the use of discretion, although that frequently challenged effective managerial controls against corruption. From the perspective of the state, the measure of customs work was the extent to which officers met their revenue targets.

Revenue targets were critical for the national budget and represented a commitment between the government and its development partners or aid donors. It was also important as the means for a ruling political party to obtain the funds to pursue its election campaign promises and remain in power.

Control of corruption was therefore an objective that remained subsidiary to hitting revenue targets. A senior officer depicted this view when he observed: “the difference between a good customs officer and a bad one is that a good one can collect revenues for the government and himself. However, a bad one only collects for himself.”

Failure to hit revenue targets had serious professional consequences. For example, the customs commissioner, commissioner of internal revenue, and the commissionergeneral of the Ghana Revenue Authority were sacked for missing targets (Emmanuel 2019). Given the threat of job loss, senior officers had a singular focus on their revenue targets as a measure of success, rather than fighting corruption per se.

Various other conditions in the government administration and broader society contributed to the difficulties fighting corruption through IT. In our analysis of petty corruption instantiations, themes emerged that point to specific social challenges to effective managerial controls and anticorruption in developing countries. The three key themes are patronage within customs and the broader government, politicization of the public administration, and cultural norms. ## Patronage

Petty corruption involved officers across ranks because street-level officers were often beholden to the interests of senior officers who had recruited them or facilitated their promotion. Senior officers were similarly beholden to the interests of powerful actors in government who may have played a role in their appointments and promotions or could determine the course of their careers. Such patron-client relationships and solidarity networks could extend to include hundreds of officers. For example, over 200 officers of various ranks were involved in a corruption scandal linked to a politically connected importer (Daily Guide 2017b).

Patronage enabled coercive control, cover-up, quid pro quo, and impunity. Political actors in government wielded coercive control over senior customs officers who also controlled their subordinates for private gain. It was not uncommon for key decisions related to personnel or processes to be taken by “an order from above” rather than due process. Coercion, in particular, enabled TradeNet petty corruption that implicated senior officers or powerful political figures through, for example, manipulating the risk profiling functionality, abusing exemption codes, and overriding the system’s automatic/randomization functions.

Corruption within customs often happened with the cover-up and protection of senior officers, especially when those senior officers also had powerful patrons in government. Cover-up and protection underpinned the commission of corruption acts like abusing the query and resubmission functions, unapproved data sharing or data theft, falsified remarks, pilfering, extortion, and solicitation by street-level officers acting alone or with colleagues. It often took a change in power before such patronprotected corruption surfaced or was penalized (Daily Guide 2017b). This also underscores the political nature of the discipline and managerial controls at customs, where officers received protection from their political patrons or the political patrons of importers with whom they were complicit.

Senior officers also devised their own corruption such as conniving directly with companies to evade duties and taxes (Kubi 2017). Fifteen senior officers were interdicted for “conniving and permitting” importers to evade duties and taxes to the sum of GHS 1.2 billion (or about \$285M, assuming the average USD to GHS rate of 4.2 in 2017) (GhanaWeb 2017). Often, senior officers received “cuts” from the petty corruption of street-level officers, thereby establishing a quid pro quo. Such quid pro quo also existed among fellow street-level officers when they committed petty corruption like unapproved data sharing, unnecessary delays, deliberate holdups, and unlawful seizure of goods. Because of such quid pro quo, stringent controls were construed by the rank and file as a violation of an ethos of customs camaraderie. For example, supervisors failed to oversee the proper issuance of exemption permits, which was widely abused by government officials and companies, leading to a 40% reduction in customs revenue (Tiger Eye PI 2011).

Ultimately, the regulation stipulating an imprisonment term of up to five years for bribery (Ghana Customs 2015) was rarely enforced, and impunity persisted. Impunity remained the biggest enabler of petty corruption and the greatest challenge to anti-corruption at Ghana customs. The worst punitive sanction if an officer was caught in a corruption act was to get transferred to a less lucrative customs outpost or to be interdicted. Interdictions were a common way that politicians chose to “purge” customs of corruption but such measures lacked deterrent effect because they had come to be perceived as a political tool to get rid of officers with opposing political allegiances. Furthermore, interdicted officers sometimes got reinstated after interventions by their powerful patrons or influential individuals in society.

## Politicization

We observed that officers were openly or covertly affiliated with one or the other of the two main political parties and, depending on which government was in power, an officer’s fortunes could wax or wane. An officer’s chances of being sanctioned were low if their party was in office. Furthermore, because customs was controlled by the executive branch of government (the office of the president) through the finance and trade ministries, political influence was wielded over nearly all internal affairs of customs. Customs officers had limited security of tenure despite the due protections afforded their role as officers in the public service. They were victimized if they refused to connive with or provide preferential treatment to powerful politically connected importers. Political influence and associated preferential treatment enabled powerful service-seekers and political actors to cause revenue losses to the state through petty corruption instantiations like misclassification, misdescription, misinvoicing, over- or underdeclaration, manipulating and overriding the system, or abusing exemption codes.

In 2017, an audit of TradeNet transactions revealed fraud linked to political appointees and members of the government in office. The code “pending parliamentary approval” was for the exclusive use of certain officials under restrictive conditions but had been systematically abused to cover several nonapplicable cases and ineligible consignees, depriving the state of the equivalent of tens of millions of US dollars (Tiger Eye PI 2011). Politically connected importers also routinely used the name of the president or links to his office to clear goods.

## Cultural Norms

Cultural norms created ambiguity about gift giving and blurred the lines between legitimate and illegitimate exchange. Petty corruption implicating service-seekers and street-level officers—such as the solicitation or giving of bribes, favoritism, extortion, and abuse of TradeNet’s query functions—were often shaped by normalized expectations around gifts and gift giving. It is considered offensive to refuse a gift in Ghana and public sector work is generally characterized by the giving and taking of personal gifts. In a noteworthy incident, Ghana’s president became embroiled in a corruption scandal after it came to light that he had received a vehicle in exchange for a lucrative contract from a businessman. He was subsequently absolved by the national anti-corruption watchdog after claiming it was a gift (Rahman 2018).

We also observed the presence of public ambivalence toward corruption, driving the acceptability of all forms of petty corruption. While many Ghanaians publicly condemn corruption, they may privately embrace it. There is social recognition that corruption is harmful, yet it enjoys sympathies among a significant part of the population who believed it to be a way for public officers “to survive” in an otherwise underpaid and challenging work environment. Because corruption is widespread, it is often perceived as “a fact of life.” For example, over 180 judicial staff, magistrates, and high court judges were caught in a widely publicized corruption sting by an investigative journalist and anti-corruption crusader (Anas 2015; BBC 2015). Such incidents reinforce the public perception that corruption is wrong only if a perpetrator is caught. We observed that many Ghanaians also hold the belief that public resources can rightly be squandered by those with access because “what belongs to everyone belongs to no one.” Such sayings emphasize a lack of regard for the public interest and the primacy of private interests.

## Discussion

We develop an explanation of the limited effects that IT has on petty corruption in the government administration of many developing countries, even though the same IT systems have enabled significant anti-corruption effects in other countries— notably for our study of TradeNet, Singapore. Our analytical approach comprises (1) a systemic view of petty corruption that considers networks of social relationships and identifies institutions within which street-level officers’ routine interactions with IT are embedded, and (2) a diachronic view that traces the creation of conditions of possibility of the improvisation of petty corruption by street-level officers in the events that trigger IS implementations and the decisions made in the local IT system design.

## Relational Enabling of Street-Level Officers’ IT-Mediated Petty Corruption Practices

Street-level officers at Ghana customs used their discretion to creatively improvise ways of using the TradeNet functions and exploit the manual tasks of the redesigned import clearance process in multiple instantiations of petty corruption. They did so with the encouragement, collaboration, support, and protection of multiple other actors associated with the country’s government administration (Table 5). Consistent with the social actor perspective of the user in IS research (Lamb and Kling 2003), our search for an explanation of this phenomenon focuses on the relationships that enable street-level officers’ petty corruption IT practices in developing countries.

A web of direct and indirect relationships facilitated streetlevel officers’ practices of corruption with TradeNet at Ghana customs in various ways. Only some of the instantiations of corruption were perpetrated by streetstreet level officers alone while interacting with the TradeNet system. Even in such cases, the discretion of street-level officers was inclined towards “gaming the rules” of import clearance with the understanding that there would likely be support and cover-up if the corruption were exposed. In many instantiations of corruption, street-level officers colluded with a variety of other self-interested actors. Service seekers often initiated bribery by asking street-level officers to accept misdescribed declarations for a bribe. Other confederates included fellow officers from within customs, officers from other government agencies, officers from the private destination inspections companies, senior officers in customs, and politicians. Petty corruption practices of street-level officers often served instances of grand corruption by officers at the top administration hierarchy, politicians at the summit of the national political system, and powerful business owners.

The network of actors implicated in petty corruption was dense—involving multiple government officers across ranks—and extensive spreading far beyond the boundaries of the customs agency into other government agencies, the central government (executive, judiciary, parliament), businesses, and ordinary citizens. For example, while the abuse of exemption codes was an act of street-level officers, it was typically initiated as a demand by a politically connected importer and required support by politicians and cover-up by senior officers in customs and perhaps other agencies relevant to the goods to be cleared.

## Institutional Enabling of Street-Level Officers’ IT-Mediated Petty Corruption Practices

TradeNet at Ghana customs was implemented to support a mode of governance oriented toward the efficient use of resources for the effective pursuit of organizational goals. The reforms inspired by New Public Management, adopted under pressure from international aid donors, emphasized a managerial orientation to increase revenue for the state and service quality for traders. This implied radical institutional change of the organizational mission, as well as the values, rules, and norms guiding officers’ actions.

The mode of governance historically formed in Ghana and many other developing countries is a hybrid of two types of administration: the legal-rational mode of bureaucratic governance introduced during colonial rule and continued by postcolonial governments, and the traditional patrimonial governance where public officers are controlled by patronage and public and private life spheres are conflated (Bratton and Van de Walle 1994; Chabal and Daloz 1999; Eisenstadt 1973; Erdmann and Engel 2006; Van de Walle 2001). Known as neopatrimonialism, this hybrid mode of governance involves informal practices that differ substantially from the formal rules of behavior prescribed by regulation. It also blurs the distinction between public and private property: “officials hold positions in bureaucratic organizations with powers which are formally defined, but exercise those powers ... as a form ... of private property” (Clapham 1985, p. 48).

The incongruence of the managerial rationality of TradeNet and the incumbent neopatrimonial administration in Ghana’s government agencies are the dominant institutional conditions for street-level officers’ improvised practices of corruption. The incongruence of the rationality inscribed in a new information system and the rationality of an affected area of organizational practice has been studied in research about enterprise systems (Berente and Yoo 2012; Dobson and Nicholson 2017; Sahay et al. 2010). But the way that TradeNet use was adapted at Ghana customs and the manner in which it perpetuated features of the incumbent rationality differ from outcomes observed in such research. Berente et al.’s (2019) meta-analysis of user responses to enterprise systems is pertinent for understanding this difference. Berente et al. (2019) draw from the theory of institutional logics, which suggests that actors act based on cognition but that cognition draws upon historically formed collective rationalities (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, pp. 103- 104). These rationalities influence the mindful behavior of actors, which in turn shapes institutional logics. According to Berente et al. (2019), users whose practices are underpinned by a logic incongruent to the managerial rationality of enterprise systems respond either with loose coupling strategies by devising workarounds and bolt-on systems or resist and reject the new system—depending on the degree of exerted pressure for compliance. Loose coupling allows the new managerial logic to remain intact while users continue practices underpinned by incumbent logics, but resistance under pressure for compliance leads to system abandonment.

The response of officers at Ghana customs to the implementation of TradeNet represents neither loose coupling nor resistance. It is co-optation of the IT system for their benefit and to the detriment of organizational objectives. Unlike cooptation under congruent logics identified in Berente et al.’s (2019) analysis, at Ghana customs, the co-optation of TradeNet by its users significantly eroded the institutional logic that the system was intended to introduce.

Co-optation is recognized in Berente et al.’s (2019) metaanalysis—whereby users take control of a system and, under weak management, use it and steer its future direction—but the authors associate it with congruent logics. When the logic of the system does not fundamentally clash with that of the users, under weak central direction, users take control of the strategic role of the system, according to their views of how the system benefits the organization rather than being faithful to its developers’ purpose. Importantly, under such users’ control, the system retains its rationalizing logic. In contrast, the instantiations of corruption that we found performed with TradeNet by street-level officers amount to user co-optation for the continuation of illicit personal gain from public office. Pressure for compliance with the systems’ logic was not only weak but often nonexistent. We did not find evidence of senior officers championing the logic of managerial good governance. The balancing act of senior officers’ meeting customs revenue targets while colluding in multiple instantiations of corruption for their personal gain suggests their commitment to the continuation of the neopatrimonial rationality of administration rather than its transformation.

The incongruence between the institutional logics inscribed in TradeNet and Ghana’s established traditional mode of governance led to a digitalized variant of neopatrimonialism. The resulting administration practices achieved some effects of good governance, such as the efficiency of clearing goods and more effective revenue collection for the state, but the underpinning institutional logic of good governance was eroded.

## The Creation of Conditions of Possibility of IT Effects on Petty Corruption

Our diachronic analysis of IS and organizational change associates the IT-mediated petty corruption practices of streetlevel officers with the temporal context of a sequence of IS implementation decisions and actions. It shows that the conditions of possibility of petty corruption practices of streetlevel officers are created in the episodes of system upheaval of the punctuated IS change process, and involve decisions to terminate existing systems, the procurement of new technologies, and the design of IS and work processes (Lyytinen and Newman 2008). The case study of TradeNet at Ghana customs shows how bold initiatives for IT-enabled deep-structure organizational change may lose their transformative anti-corruption capacity. This happens primarily by adapting generic technologies and “best practices” to function in local conditions, allowing opportunities for improvising petty corruption with IT use. The scope for corruption-prone discretion is constructed by the decisions of IT and management experts in their efforts to fit the generic technologies and best practice principles to the local administrative context. One of the most striking findings of our study is the TradeNet system’s design compromise at Ghana customs that drastically reduced its anti-corruption potential.

A major constraint of the implementation of TradeNet in Ghana was the need for the system to integrate with information systems in other government agencies. Notwithstanding the constraints from limited technology infrastructures confronting the TradeNet configuration team, design for interorganizational integration opened possibilities for negotiations with officers who were reluctant to change prior work practices. Under the pressure of local politics and vested interests, systems developers configured a minimal version of TradeNet that idiosyncratically retained paper use and manual procedures. The improvised integration produced a system that involved corruption-prone manual tasks and face-to-face contact points with service-seekers. This allowed street-level officers ample scope to further improvise petty corruption routines through and around the IT functions.

Extending the analytical perspective beyond the implementation and subsequent use of an information system, the long-term perspective of the punctuated process of change exposes the precariousness of sustaining anti-petty corruption effects over time in developing countries’ government administrations. The petty corruption curtailment achieved by an IS implementation may be challenged by subsequent IS implementations marred by the “grand” corruption of political and industry actors. Looking at the succession of four IT systems—ASYCUDA, TradeNet, West Blue, and UNIPASS—we observe the influence of powerful actors external to the government administration. The politically motivated procurement of the TradeNet successor systems in Ghana and the failure of the UNIPASS system to deliver improvements over the prior West Blue and TradeNet systems show that even modest anti-corruption effects may be shortlived in developing countries. IS implementations with anticorruption objectives set by actors external to the administration, such as the international development agencies’ fostering of TradeNet, may be challenged and undone by future changes in national and international politics. The critical events that trigger the termination of IT systems and the launching of new IT implementations often stem from changes of government (Silva and Hirschheim 2007) and have motives other than the improvement of government administration (Lyytinen and Newman 2008). Such IT implementations may reverse the administration reforms of prior interventions without an alternative rationalizing logic for the containment of petty corruption.

The diachronic view of IS change suggests a link between street-level officers’ petty corruption and corruption by highranking government officers, politicians, and industry that unfolds in time. Petty corruption in existing street-level practices may provide a rhetorical justification for the termination of a system and a new implementation with lucrative opportunities for IT procurement and reorganization consultancy contracts, clientelistically favoring political party supporters. Evidence from the aftermath of the termination of TradeNet in Ghana suggests that organizational performance deteriorated with the resulting new systems and it is likely that TradeNet’s weak control of petty corruption has further declined. While follow-up empirical research is needed to study changes in street-level officers’ practices, our research suggests that in volatile political circumstances, petty corruption in IT use by street-level officers and grand corruption in IT implementation projects by political and industry actors may be mutually reinforcing.

## Confronting the Corruption Complex with IT

The co-optation of an information system by street-level officers for practices of corruption that we found at Ghana customs is—for the IS literature—an unusual type of user response to IT implementations intended to change an organization’s dominant institutional logic (Berente et al. 2019). Also, IS implementations initiated by actors external to an organization with motives other than addressing organizational performance improvement are poorly understood in the IS literature (Lyytinen and Newman 2008, Silva and Hirschheim 2007). To explain our empirical findings, we delve next into the specifics of neopatrimonialism as the broader context within which streetlevel officers’ practices are embedded, and contrast the pervasive and enduring nature of this institutional logic to the limited and localized scope of IS implementations.

The multiple instantiations of petty corruption that we found in the use of TradeNet are part of a “corruption complex” that is highlighted in anthropologists’ ethnographic research on endemic corruption (Blundo and Olivier de Sardan 2006). Such a corruption complex merges a variety of informal and illegitimate (according to regulation) practices of dense and extensive networks of actors. Our analysis shows that the corruption complex pervades not only government administration but also the political and cultural domains of society in developing countries such as Ghana. Neopatrimonial relationships of patronage exist not only in the hierarchical setting of government administration but also between the administrative and the political system and society at large. The events of the replacement of TradeNet reveal the coexistence of petty corruption practiced by street-level officers with grand corruption practiced by actors at the summit of the state, such as ministers and the president’s office.

Importantly, while corruption is widely stigmatized in public discourse, a variety of manifestations of the corruption complex are blurred with sociocultural norms such as the exchange of services and gifts. Many service seekers and business firms preferred personalized arrangements to reduce their taxes even if it involved paying bribes to streetlevel officers. Egregious cases of collusion that surfaced in the media involved powerful businesses and politicians. They were legitimized and actively supported by shared norms and behaviors across institutions of the modern state, including the judiciary and enforcement agencies, such as the police. They enjoyed moral justification and impunity within the national social system.

A localized IS implementation with the objective to reduce petty corruption is confronted by the pervasiveness of the neopatrimonial logic and the corruption complex it enables. The TradeNet implementation was too limited in organizational breadth and its intended anti-corruption effects had too weak political and public support. In an environment of social networks and institutions in which the logic of managerial government was alien and undermining of vested interests, the discretion afforded to street-level officers by the configuration of TradeNet creatively co-opted technology to continue corruption practices.

The inadequacy of information systems change in government agencies to produce significant anti-corruption effects can be demonstrated by the contrasting case of Singapore’s implementation of TradeNet amid a broader set of reforms. In Singapore, the development of TradeNet was part of comprehensive anti-corruption efforts across government and society. It was underpinned by broad-based public reforms, a strong institutional bedrock of strict legal enforcement, and an anti-corruption watchdog that bit hard. The reforms in Singapore included better salaries for public officers, recruitment on merit, and training for high standards of professionalism, and integrity (Civil Service College [Singapore] 2015; Ha 2013). This is consistent with studies of anti-corruption reforms that suggest the potency of multifaceted, “big bang” approaches to shake up the corruption system (Michael et al. 2010; Rothstein 2011).

In contrast to the volatile political context of Ghana’s customs and its TradeNet processes, Singapore’s deployment of TradeNet for the fight against corruption was characterized by single-minded political will and continuity over several decades (Quah 2001, pp. 32-33). In many developing countries like Ghana, developmental projects are abandoned or manipulated upon changes of government because of political differences, vested interests, or patronage (Lehne et al. 2017). Such effects are related to political business cycles where incumbents manipulate the economy or public resources for electoral advantage (Block 2002). In our case, a different government displaced TradeNet with West Blue, and subsequently, both TradeNet and West Blue were replaced by UNIPASS after another change of government.

## Research Contributions

The main contribution of this research is a theoretical explanation of the very limited anti-petty corruption effects that are often observed in developing countries’ governmental organizations. The midrange theory of IT and petty corruption that we propose foregrounds the relational and institutional conditions that are prevalent in many developing countries and may compromise the potential anti-corruption effects of IT at the launching of IT projects, during the design of information systems, and in subsequent practice.

Crucially important in the proposed theoretical explanation is the socially embedded reproduction and sustenance of corruption practices through the use of IT. Street-level officers’ discretion is a central mechanism for the continuation of corruption with IT, but street-level officers do not act in isolation to circumvent the corruption-constraining functionality of technology. They do so as social actors (Lamb and Kling 2003) immersed in the complex relationships of their organizational setting (Granovetter 1985) and in multiple institutions (Berente and Yoo 2012; Friedland and Alford 1991; Mignerat and Rivard 2009). Network relationships and the enabling and constraining role of institutions on individual agency vis-à-vis technology are well understood in IS. But in our case study of TradeNet, we found atypical interactions and outcomes. The managerial logic conveyed by the IT system is eroded by the incumbent logic of government administration; the IT system survives and even delivers some of its intended rationalizing outcomes but without substantial effect on corruption. Our study therefore exposes the occurrence of starkly different outcomes from those found in research on IS and organizational change that highlights individual and group user discretion as a mechanism for the survival of the logic conveyed by the incoming information system when confronted with incongruent incumbent institutional logics (Berente et al. 2016). In the context of IT use in government administration in developing countries, we found user discretion that complies with a pervasive incumbent logic and renders ineffective the logic conveyed by IS, thereby diminishing its rationalizing effects.

The two-dimensional perspective of the explanation we propose highlights the tightly woven system of interpersonal and institutional relationships of societal scale that enables the co-optation of organizational IT systems to retain widespread informal practices of corruption. It also traces the creation of conditions for the possibility of IT-mediated street-level officers’ petty corruption practices in the decisions and actions of system implementation interventions, thus calling attention to diachronic circuits of influence on petty corruption practices. In many developing countries, the anti-petty corruption effects of IT are severely constrained by systemic and enduring neopatrimonial conditions of the administration, politics, and culture within which the discretion of street-level officers is embedded.

This research also makes an empirical contribution in the form of a detailed description of the experience of a government organization, with IT systems bearing anti-corruption potential. By opening the black box of IT implementation in a context of widespread corruption, we respond to calls for qualitative insights on this poorly understood phenomenon (Srivastava et al. 2016). The case study documents the configuration of a minimal version of generic technology and a work system that continued to require extensive manual procedures and gave rise to multiple instantiations of corruption practice. It exposes contextual influences and constraints that led to the erosion of the system’s anticorruption capacity at the design stage, diminished its anticorruption effects in use, and led to system termination.

The findings of this research have implications for IS developers, government policymakers, and international aid agencies that sponsor IT projects such as the TradeNet. Our research shows that organizationally localized implementations of IT aiming at ambitious institutional changes encounter obstacles from the wider context of government and society that erode their anti-corruption effects. Planning for large-scale institutional change to create conditions conducive to the managerial rationality that most IT systems inscribe is a challenging endeavor. The “big bang approach,” as in Singapore’s example, demonstrates the importance of systemic change but does not provide a feasible route for resource-constrained or more populous countries with fledgling democratic regimes. An incremental approach of sustained improvements may be more feasible in many developing countries but modest anti-corruption gains and improvements such as efficiency in revenue collections require protection from political interference.

## Conclusion

Having grappled with the questions of why the intended anticorruption effects of IT often fail to materialize in the public administration of developing countries and why the same technology might have different anti-corruption outcomes in different countries, our explanation highlights the importance of the mode of administration. In neopatrimonial administrations, potential anti-corruption effects will likely be compromised at the launching of IT projects, at the design stage, and in subsequent practices of street-level officers and their managers. The theoretical perspective we propose is an analytical generalization that is applicable across many developing countries that have since decolonization developed forms of neopatrimonial bureaucracies (Beekers and van Gool 2012; Jackson 1987). The experience of Ghana customs with IT is representative of widespread IT implementation in conjunction with good governance reforms in African countries and elsewhere. Nonetheless, the dynamics of institutional change vary substantially even in countries of the same region with similar historical trajectories and comparable development indicators; therefore, the outcome of IT anti-corruption interventions could be different from our findings. The value of the context-specific explanation of limited anti-petty corruption effects of IT that we develop is analytical guidance for considering what may happen in countries with similar conditions, thus informing IS decisions and management action.

Although we have distinguished petty corruption instantiations and associated them with enabling social conditions such as patronage, politicization, and cultural norms, future studies might further examine the mechanism or combination of mechanisms by which these or other social conditions in developing countries produce or enable types of IT-mediated corruption practices. Such causal analysis could provide useful insights into both the regularities and variations in patterns of IT-mediated petty corruption in developing countries.

Our study does not address the full complexity of sociotechnical dynamics in developing country government contexts. It also has a limited view of IT, having considered only a “single window” technology for the intergovernmental domain of international trade. Other technologies with different functionalities and more localized scope might produce more significant anti-corruption effects. Our research is also limited to public sector reorganization associated with the New Public Management and good governance managerial rationality. IT implementations that convey different logics of government administration might have better or worse chances of producing anti-corruption effects. Moreover, our research is partial and incomplete regarding the societal change that impacts localized IT implementation and effects. We have not explored the links of government administration logics with the unfolding social and economic changes that affect neopatrimonial conditions in developing countries. Also, we have not studied the dilemmas and behaviors of IS professionals in implementations with dubious motives of political and business actors that were exposed in our case study. We know little about the encounter of professional IS practice with projects serving the interests of powerful sponsors. These are themes that could be explored in further research.

Interviews aimed to understand the organizational and broader context of customs in Ghana, the implementation of TradeNet and other IT systems, as well as clearance practices and the everyday interactions of declarants and officers with TradeNet. Attention was paid to capturing different voices while probing tensions, inconsistencies, and anomalies (Myers and Newman 2007). Informants were initially chosen purposively from across stakeholder groups and subsequently by snowballing from the initial cohort (Bryman 2001). For example, while interviewing a key informant at the Ghana Export Promotion Authority (GEPA) for background on Ghana’s exports and TradeNet, the informant invited the author, impromptu, to attend an ongoing workshop to educate exporters on new features of the system. The workshop devolved unexpectedly into a heated dispute between the exporters—most of whom were struggling business owners—and government officials, over what the exporters called “extortionate fees” and “illicit money” extracted through TradeNet. Through this serendipitous interaction, the tensions inherent in TradeNet’s managerial logic versus the realities of corrupt everyday life first emerged as an important puzzle. At this workshop, we met key informants who referred us to many others in the import sector.

## Participant Observation

Participant observation provided ethnographic details of the everyday practices of customs clearance and gave insights into t he activities of stakeholders such as customs officers, officers of the Destination Inspections Companies (DICs), and declarants. One of the authors spent 2-3 days per week between May and August 2015 in different locations at the customs office, offices of the DICs, and offices of clearing agents and freight forwarders in Tema and Accra. Informants allowed the author to interact freely and ask questions as interesting observations emerged.

The experience of being immersed in the “customs clearance world” provided thick descriptions (over 280 pages of field notes) of the clearance subprocesses from the vantage point of key actors involved and allowed the researcher to probe different themes in situ. For example, at the customs yard where the examination of goods and preventive work happens, the author witnessed first-hand some customs and other government officers asking for or receiving bribes. Such firsthand observations were in juxtaposition to the mythical “official narrative” presented in customs procedures and process maps. In general, clearance times and activities were rarely as stipulated and often took much longer. Furthermore, hurdles and “unofficial” payments that were routinely made in practice were not captured in the official procedures and guides. Three workshops and four meetings were additionally attended involving the GCNET officials, importers, exporters, and staff of the export promotion authority and other government agencies. During the question-and-answer sessions of these meetings, and in separate interviews, one of the co-authors obtained a firsthand understanding of the challenges faced by importers and exporters with TradeNet.

## Document Review

Customs procedures were governed by numerous technical arrangements that spanned across the global (e.g., WTO, WCO, UNCTAD), regional (ECOWAS, bilateral agreements), and national levels. There was a high level of documentation for about a dozen customs regimes that included temporary import, temporary export, export, re-export, import, re-import, transit, free zone, duty free, customs warehousing, processing in customs territory, processing outside customs territory, refusal in favor of the state, destruction/abolition, as well as processes for trade facilitation stipulated through the WTO and WCO. Historical and contemporary records existed of customs arrangements that had been implemented in Ghana over the years. The high level of documentation helped to establish a detailed picture of the official processes and was also useful for tracing changes at different points over time. We collected internal and external documents (related to work and processes within customs or affecting but not directly related to processes within customs). Over 200 soft and hard copy documents were also reviewed, in addition to hundreds of web pages from an extended set of actors involved in international trade facilitation.
