# The Impact of Sharing Mechanism Design on Content Sharing in Online Social Networks

Irina Heimbach,<sup>a</sup> Oliver Hinz<sup>b</sup>

a WHU–Otto Beisheim School of Management, 56179 Vallendar, Germany; b Goethe University Frankfurt, 60629 Frankfurt am Main, Germany <sub>Contact:</sub> irina.heimbach@whu.edu, http://orcid.org/0000-0003-2592-0367 (IH); ohinz@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de (OH)

<sub>Abstract.</sub> Research on online content difusion is vast but has rarely examined contextual factors, including the influence of online sharing mechanisms, such as social plugins (e.g., Facebook’s “Like” button), on online social networks (OSNs). While these mechanisms generally enable the content flow between senders and recipients, they vary in protecting users’ <sub>social</sub> and <sub>institutional</sub> <sub>privacy</sub> on OSNs. Additionally, sharing mechanisms might difer with respect to their <sub>labeling</sub> (e.g., positive versus neutral), which might interact with the sharable content. We examined the efects of these three design aspects on users’ sharing behavior in a controlled experiment and two analyses of observational data. The results show that two types of sharing mechanisms negatively afect content sharing in the domain of news sharing: those that allow greater information flow control over the sharing process and thus protect users’ <sub>social</sub> <sub>privacy</sub> and those that employ twoclick designs to preserve users’ <sub>institutional</sub> <sub>privacy</sub>. These negative efects mainly stem from higher frictional costs associated with these features. For the average user in this domain, the disutility and additional cognitive efort generated by one additional click often mitigates the utility of sharing itself. Moreover, we find that neutral button labeling is important for fostering content sharing as users might encounter schema incongruity when using a positively connoted label to share bad news. Overall, a wrong decision in terms of the sharing mechanism can easily decrease the number of shares by up to 86%. Therefore, content providers can easily and substantially increase content sharing by properly designing the sharing mechanism on their websites.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing trend toward digitization and Internetbased services has altered people’s habits in many respects (Aral et al. 2013). In particular, people spend more of their spare time visiting online social networks (OSNs) such as Facebook, Google+, or Twitter, as well as online newspapers or magazines. In fact, a recent Pew Research Center report indicates that nearly 44% of U.S. adults obtain their news from Facebook (Gottfried and Shearer 2016). Internet users often use email or OSNs to share links to interesting articles or funny videos with friends, colleagues, and relatives. The number of shares reflects the number of readers (a key metric for advertising) as well as the level of audience engagement, which can, in turn, attract greater awareness from other OSN users. Social media managers admit that about 20% of their website traffic comes through OSNs with Facebook being the most important channel.<sup>1</sup>

Users on OSNs often share digital content via <sub>so-</sub> <sub>cial</sub> <sub>plugins</sub>, or pieces of program code that can be integrated into content providers’ websites to facilitate users’ interactions, such as Facebook’s Like and Share buttons (https://developers.facebook.com/ docs/plugins). The <sub>social</sub> <sub>button</sub> is a typical example of such plugins. Figure 1 illustrates an example from <sub>The</sub> <sub>Guardian</sub> website. Content-providing websites like this one voluntarily customize and integrate social plugins to enhance their reach. We refer to this choice and implementation of customized social plugins as the <sub>sharing</sub> <sub>mechanism</sub> <sub>design</sub>. As Figure 1 depicts, numerous OSNs already ofer such social plugins, often in diferent variants. These buttons can difer with respect to their functional afordances or appearance (symbolic expression) (Markus and Silver 2008). Previous research did not examine systematically how the design of sharing mechanisms afects users’ content-sharing behavior. This paper therefore systematically investigates the efects of these sharing mechanism design aspects in a controlled experiment. We then test the validity of our findings with two observational data sets consisting of press articles that were published in 2012 and eventually shared on Facebook. We chose to study Facebook because it is currently the most popular OSN (Schiller et al. 2016), and it also provides social plugins that can be customized in various ways (https://developers.facebook.com/docs/ plugins/).

This research question is also of high practical relevance as content providers can only integrate a certain number of OSN plugins. First, integrating plugins usually entails technical costs. Social plugins typically use JavaScript to communicate between the website content and the respective OSN; thus, each additional button will slow down a website’s loading speed. Considering that Google uses page load speed as a ranking factor (Googleblog 2010), an increase in loading time could negatively impact a website’s search position and subsequent trafic. Third, the popularity of certain OSNs influences the sharing mechanism design. Content providers may be willing to integrate several variants of the leading OSN (like Facebook in 2017) but may only embed one plugin from a new or growing OSN. For this reason, the operators of new OSNs are compelled to develop and promote only the very best plugin to foster activity on their platforms. Finally, content providers are not beholden to premade plugins; many OSNs ofer an application programming interface (API) for the purpose of developing custom social plugins. Website owners may seek to craft a customized plugin that fosters an optimal difusion of content, but guidance for developers is scarce. Given the above concerns, this paper aims to delineate the design factors that promote the most successful sharing mechanisms, particularly in the domain of news sharing. Our analyses reveal the sharing mechanism that is, on average, the most appropriate in this examined application area.

## 2. Content Sharing as an Information Difusion Process

Human life depends greatly on social communication and interaction, which drive the dissemination of news, products/services, and behaviors as well as complement the promotional eforts of firms and institutions. We use Hovland’s (1948) schema of social communication to structure research in this area: as studies of the aggregated <sub>outcomes</sub> of word of mouth (WOM) show, WOM can lead to increased sales (Chevalier and

Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008), afect the adoption and discovery of new products and ideas (Garg et al. 2011, Susarla et al. 2012), and change individual and collective attitudes and opinions (Oh et al. 2015).

In addition, a <sub>sender’s</sub> willingness to difuse information is influenced by the sender’s personality traits and motives as well as the sender’s relationship with the <sub>recipient</sub>. Every time people encounter a viral message, piece of content, or some other information, they decide whether such content should be forwarded and to whom. Their decision is typically driven by one of two types of motives and characteristics: recipientrelated and sender-related. The former type may reflect altruism (Ho and Dempsey 2010) in the sense that people want to help others and therefore share content with high practical utility (Barasch and Berger 2014, Berger and Milkman 2012). Thus, people motivated by recipient factors often share useful articles to strengthen social ties (Choi et al. 2015, Zeng and Wei 2013) or invest in social capital (Burt 2005)—with the caveat that audience size might moderate the type of content shared (Aral and Walker 2011, Barasch and Berger 2014, Choi et al. 2015). By contrast, senderrelated motives pertain to self-enhancement, such as the need to appear knowledgeable and smart (Barasch and Berger 2014, Berger and Iyengar 2013). Hennig-Thurau et al. (2004) found that concern for others, extraversion, and social benefits lead to consumers’ engagement in WOM. Research in the area of social networks has examined the influence of the sender’s social position on information difusion (Dou et al. 2013, Fang et al. 2013, Hill et al. 2006, Hinz et al. 2011, Richardson and Domingos 2002) as well as how network structures afect social contagion (Bampo et al. 2008, Hinz and Spann 2008).

Content <sup>(</sup>message<sup>)</sup> characteristics <sup>also</sup> <sup>determine</sup> <sup>the</sup> outcomes of the content-sharing process. Content that is memorable, interesting, surprising, or emotionally loaded (Berger and Milkman 2012, Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan 2013) has a greater likelihood of difusion.

Finally, communication processes can be influenced by the <sub>communication</sub> <sub>context</sub>, such as temporal aspects (Chen et al. 2015, Szabo and Huberman 2010), attention competition factors (Dellarocas et al. 2015, Iyer and Katona 2015, Jones et al. 2004), firms’ product, promotion, and pricing strategies (Goh et al. 2013, Oh et al. 2016), and the characteristics of the sharing mechanism and communication channel (e.g., face to face, phone, email, OSN). Such contextual factors might directly influence information difusion or interact with key components (e.g., sender–recipient relationship).

Although research on the drivers and outcomes of information difusion through WOM is vast, scholars have devoted little attention to the efects deriving from sharing mechanism design. To our knowledge, only four studies have addressed sharing mechanism designs or examined diferent communication contexts that could alter content difusion: Berger and Iyengar (2013) found that asynchronous (email, text posts) communication channels give conversation partners an opportunity to select the most interesting topic or brand whereas synchronous (phone, face to face) connections incline people to discuss any topic that comes to mind. Barasch and Berger (2014) showed how the audience size (broadcasting versus narrowcasting) afects what people share. Aral and Walker (2011) distinguished between passive broadcast mechanisms and active personalized referrals, finding significant diferences with regard to how each generates peer influence on Facebook. Schulze et al. (2014) extended this description to several dimensions—solicited/unsolicited, incentivized/nonincentivized, and from friends/from strangers—as well as distinguished between hedonic and utilitarian products. According to their results, incentivized sharing mechanisms can generally foster product adoption whereas messages broadcast by strangers can hinder the difusion process. They also concluded that Facebook is poorly suited for broadcasting information about utilitarian products as users’ expectation of joyful and entertaining experiences on Facebook conflicts with the products’ utilitarian character.

In sum, Aral and Walker (2011) and Schulze et al. (2014) focused on the difusion of apps within Facebook while Berger and Iyengar (2013) and Barasch and Berger (2014) primarily investigated WOM conversations. The present study extends these findings by exploring how sharing mechanism design might afect content sharing on OSNs. To this end, we characterize sharing mechanisms as information technology (IT) artifacts in accordance with the framework developed by Markus and Silver (2008).

## 3. Conceptual Framework and Theory 3.1. Conceptual Framework

According to Markus and Silver (2008), IT artifacts comprise three concepts: technical objects, functional afordances, and symbolic expressions. The <sub>technical</sub> <sub>objects</sub> concept refers to the IT artifacts themselves regardless of the relationship between users and those technical objects (Markus and Silver 2008). <sub>Functional</sub> <sub>afordances</sub> reflect the potential usage of the technical objects, including “the possibilities for goal-oriented action aforded to specified user groups by technical objects” (Markus and Silver 2008, p. 622), such as when social plugins enable users to share content online. <sub>Symbolic</sub> <sub>expressions</sub> encompass how users interpret and react to an IT artifact, reflecting the “communicative possibilities of a technical object for a specified user group” (Markus and Silver 2008, p. 623). For example, in most Western cultures, a “thumbs up” gesture is a positive sign expressing approval or acceptance; in contrast, the gesture would ofend conversation partners in some Middle Eastern countries. Accordingly, Facebook abolished the “thumbs up” icon from its social plugins in 2013 so as to avoid any cultural misunderstandings (Crook 2013).

Because both functional afordances and symbolic expressions are user-related, they might invoke diferent user behaviors that constitute distinctive uses of such IT artifacts (Markus and Silver 2008). For this reason, we see the design of sharing mechanisms as a nontrivial task for social media practitioners, who have to consider not only users’ diferent interpretations of the symbolic expressions contained in various sharing mechanisms but also the efects arising from the different levels of functional afordance. In the following, we explain how this framework can be applied to sharing mechanisms, starting with a discussion of symbolic expressions.

## 3.2. Symbolic Expressions: The Importance of Sharing Mechanism Labeling

The act of labeling sharing mechanisms with diferent words or symbols is inherently associated with individuals’ schemas, which are “representations of experiences that guide action, perception and thought. Schemas (and the resulting expectations) are developed as a function of the frequency of encounters with relevant instantiations where new encounters are evaluated against existing schemas” (Mandler 1982, p. 3). Thus, schemas represent a kind of heuristic that helps people process incoming information or master repeating situations. A major theory associated with schemas is (in)congruity theory, which assumes that individuals strive for cognitive consistency between their values, feelings, behaviors, and experiences (i.e., their schemas). Whenever a state of cognitive dissonance appears (i.e., new information does not fit the individual’s schema), people strive to resolve it (Festinger 1962).

Originating in the study of children’s cognitive development (Piaget 1932) and the formation of memory and attitude (Mandler 1982), schema (in)congruity has a long research tradition in marketing areas such as branding (Haubl and Elrod 1999), product evaluation (Aggarwal and McGill 2007), advertising (e.g., Lange and Dahlén 2003), and sponsorship (e.g., Jagre et al. 2001). On one hand, relevant studies show that advertisements with a high level of incongruity lead to a higher recall probability: Such messages demand more attention and thus cognitive efort and are thereby more memorable (Lange and Dahlén 2003). On the other hand, the incongruity between the endorser and the brand schema can harm firms’ promotional activities (Jagre et al. 2001). In fact, the finding of Schulze et al. (2014)—that utilitarian apps advertised through broadcasting mechanisms achieve little success on Facebook—could be attributed to the schema incongruity between Facebook (hedonic) and utilitarian apps. Apart from Schulze et al. (2014), researchers have devoted little attention to how schema (in)congruity between sharing mechanisms and the components of social communication afects content difusion.

Given that language invites its own cognitive schema, the designers of sharing mechanisms have to consider whether particular words, icons, and symbols are congruent (i.e., expected and consistent) with users’ schemas. Whenever a situation of schema incongruity appears, the theory of cognitive dissonance (Festinger 1962) would presume that the communication act (i.e., content sharing) is less likely to occur.

Consider the two words “like” and “recommend,” which are the typical labels for Facebook’s sharing buttons. Whereas “recommend” is more neutral sounding, the word “like” is positively connoted and emotionally charged, implying some positive evaluation of the shared content and a self-focus on the part of the sender (“I like”).<sup>2</sup> Thus, achieving a situation that is “expected and consistent” would require that people use the positive or neutral label to share positive content. Sharing negative content (e.g., news about the death of a prominent person or a natural catastrophe) via a positively connoted label might produce incongruence and thereby mitigate content difusion.

Additionally, the word “like” might seem ambiguous. Sometimes, people share negative news because they like or support the idea behind the content or perhaps want to acknowledge the author’s outstanding work.<sup>3</sup> At the same time, previous research shows that people refrain from sending bad news when they want to avoid negative assessments of their personalities (Barasch and Berger 2014, Hansen et al. 2011). Social psychology refers to this phenomenon as metaperception (Laing et al. 1966), which posits that people are generally concerned about other people’s opinions of them and are thus less likely to adopt behaviors that could be misinterpreted or negatively evaluated. When people use the “like” button to share negative news, they either have to explain their stance to minimize misinterpretation, which entails additional costs, or else risk their intention being misunderstood. This issue may lead people to limit their sharing to situations that are “safe” and unambiguous.

Another potential source of incongruity stems from the mismatch between the content and the sender’s self-focus as implied by the word “like.” Consider two people: person A, who is uninterested in soccer, and person B, who is a known soccer fan. If A shares news about some soccer event with B using a sharing mechanism labeled with “like,” the latter person might perceive the communication act as nonauthentic (assuming A’s peers know that A has no afection for soccer) and a violation of B’s existing schema about A.

By contrast, A’s image would not be violated if A shares an article using a more neutrally labeled mechanism, such as “recommend” or “share.” However, a positively connoted button might be more suitable for person B because it more accurately expresses B’s feelings. Thus, a label’s efect will depend on the user population, the reasons for sharing, and the content itself.

Based on (in)congruity theory, one might predict that sharing mechanisms labeled with “like” would, on average, negatively afect sharing behavior because of a potential mismatch between the content and the user’s personal involvement. Logic would then suggest that words with a neutral connotation and a lack of implied self-focus (e.g., “recommend” and “share”) are more universal and thus viable labels. At the same time, previous research has shown that people like to share positive experiences with others (Berger and Milkman 2012, Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan 2013), and sharing mechanisms labeled with “like” (at least if used to share emotionally parallel content) could reinforce this positive experience (“a joy shared is a joy doubled”).

In summary, the expected efects of labeling are not straightforward. They seem to depend not only on the nature of the content but also on the size and nature of the user population.

## 3.3. Functional Afordance: Designing the Information Flow

In the semipublic spaces of OSNs, users interact with family and friends, gain access to and spread information, and cultivate their sense of belonging to a group. However, users may also want to broadcast their activities to a broader audience. Of course, this carries the risk of unexpected or regrettable outcomes as indicated by anecdotal media reports of what happens when information is shared inappropriately: “partners finding out about engagement rings before the oficial proposal, employers learning about deceitful sick leaves, tax authorities finding out about undeclared expensive purchases, and parents discovering their children’s sexual preferences” (Gurses and Diaz 2013, p. 3). Thus, users may desire that OSN plugins channel information to the desired audience (Choi et al. 2015, Jiang et al. 2013).

This difusion of information to and within OSNs also touches on questions of preserving users’ <sub>social</sub> and <sub>institutional</sub> <sub>privacy</sub> (Brandtzæg et al. 2010, Gurses and Diaz 2013, Raynes-Goldie 2012). <sub>Social</sub> <sub>privacy</sub> could be easily ensured by allowing users to specify a targeted subgroup of their friends and thereby maintain a high level of control over the information flow. However, many users are not aware that the standard social plugins from Facebook, Twitter, and Google+ send information to these social networks even if the user does not employ them, which is a breach of <sub>institutional</sub> <sub>privacy</sub> (Gurses and Diaz 2013, Raynes-Goldie 2012). So long as the plugins are integrated on a web page, OSNs can assemble fairly complete browser histories of their users by tracking the websites that users visit. Because this practice runs contrary to the expectations of many users and the desires of many website operators, its public reveal spurred outrage in the European media.

To mitigate some of these concerns, independent software developers invented so-called “privacyfriendly” or “privacy-preserving” social plugins. In general, these plugins feature two states: deactivated and activated. As a default, the plugin first displays a gray mockup image of, for example, a Like button. By clicking this button, the user loads the real plugin and efectively agrees to send information to the OSN. With a subsequent click, the user can recommend (like, Tweet, etc.) the web page as usual. Figure 2 displays the deactivated and activated states (after one click) of these plugins for Facebook, Twitter, and Google+.<sup>4</sup>

In 2012, about 40% of the leading German media decided to implement these two-click buttons. The feature recently gained additional traction in Europe when the courts ruled that these social plugins can be used in a way that violates European privacy laws (see, e.g., Woollaston 2016). In the following, we discuss the theoretical motivations for the diferent sharing mechanism designs in terms of functional afordances.

3.3.1. Controlling the Information Flow to the OSN. <sup>In</sup> practice, the efort to preserve users’ privacy via twoclick sharing buttons could exert diferent efects on users. First, websites that implement two-click sharing mechanisms ofer an explanation for the practice, which could foster privacy awareness (via priming) and make users reluctant to share content. Second, users’ willingness to share content on OSNs might be dampened by the extra efort of one additional click. Third, the herding efect might disappear. To elaborate on this point: A conventional one-click design ofers clues about an article’s popularity by allowing users to see how many other people have already shared the content. Because readers tend to share the most popular content, this design promotes a herding efect or positive feedback loop (Muchnik et al. 2013). Without this popularity information, users might be less likely to share. Within the area of news sharing, we expect that privacy-preserving features hinder content difusion, irrespective of the underlying user reaction. However, there could be a genuine need for such plugins in areas involving more sensitive topics, such as health.

3.3.2. Controlling the Information Flow Within the <sub>OSN.</sub> Several disciplines have widely discussed the trade-of between providing control over a decision or action on one hand and reducing the related efort on the other (Ariely 2000, Klayman 1988). In studies related to education and learning, researchers have found that participants tend to be more successful when they choose their own subjects compared with when the subjects are already fixed (Klayman 1988). Similarly, Ariely (2000) showed that decision makers prefer high control over information systems and ultimately express more confidence in their decisions when aforded such control.

In the area of WOM, previous research states that consumers are more likely to make referrals to strong ties than to weak ties (Frenzen and Nakamoto 1993). By implication, a sharing mechanism that allows users to choose the group of friends with whom they share information (i.e., with high information flow control) should generate a higher conversion rate. Moreover, Frenzen and Nakamoto (1993) found that people share all types of information with their close friends (strong ties). Therefore, sharing mechanisms with high information flow control would allow users to match different topics with particular peer groups, which could increase users’ likelihood of sharing articles via OSNs.

Although the functional afordance of increased information flow control may address the heterogeneity of users’ information needs, audiences, and interests, it is also associated with higher <sub>cognitive</sub> <sub>efort</sub> and <sub>processing</sub> <sub>costs</sub>. For these factors, we use the term <sub>frictional</sub> <sub>costs</sub>, which are defined as a disutility that emerges when conducting any online transaction; these consist of the costs of thinking (Shugan 1980) as well as the time and efort involved with interacting with a website and various user interfaces (Hann and Terwiesch 2003). As shown in the area of nameyour-own-price markets, frictional costs can be substantial (Hann and Terwiesch 2003) and should not be neglected when designing online transaction mechanisms, be those social plugins or shopping processes in online shops. Similarly, Ariely (2000) found that providing more information flow control could sometimes negatively afect information utilization as a result of increased frictional costs.

In sum, a higher level of control demands more of a user’s cognitive efort and time, which might engender a greater reluctance to share content. It is possible that this process parallels Berger and Iyengar’s (2013) arguments about the impact of the communication modality (synchronous versus asynchronous) when choosing conversation topics. Furthermore, high information control would limit content difusion to a smaller network and thereby hinder the accumulated efect. With low information control, by contrast, the broader spread might produce dynamics that ultimately result in a significantly higher number of shares.

To summarize, the aggregated efect of high information flow control over sharing behavior depends on how the average user perceives the net benefit of the functional afordance—a factor that remains unclear for both researchers and practitioners.

## 4. Overview of the Empirical Studies

To investigate the efects of diferent sharing mechanism designs, we conducted three empirical studies. First, in a controlled experiment, we examined the causal impact of diferent sharing mechanism designs on content sharing. Second, we tested the generalizability of our findings from the controlled experiment using two field studies. In the first field study, we tested the efects of diferent sharing mechanism characteristics across media while controlling for several other factors, such as content characteristics, time efects, and attention competition. In the second field study, we focused on the efect of two-click sharing mechanisms on content sharing, employing a natural experiment built on a diferences-in-diferences (DID) approach. Table 1 provides an overview of our empirical studies.

The chosen approaches have some advantages and limitations. The controlled experiment helps to reveal the causal mechanisms that drive the sharing process. With the experimental setting, we can bypass the endogeneity problems that commonly accompany observational field data while controlling for several factors. In this way, we can efectively relate the diferences in sharing behavior to the design of the particular sharing mechanisms. At the same time, controlled experiments may lack generalizability and external validity because of their rather artificial setup (e.g., the experimental design does not provide users with an unlimited number of articles to share). Moreover, the fact that people know that they are under observation can alter their behavior (the Hawthorne efect). Thus, by using a multi-method approach, we hope to overcome the limitations of each individual method and cross-validate our findings.

## 5. Study 1: Controlled Experiment 5.1. Pre-Study

In our experiment, we decided to test the labels suggested by Facebook, namely “like,” “share,” and “recommend.” In a pretest study, we asked 83 people (convenience sample) to rate their perceptions of these words. According to this pretest, the words “share” and “recommend” are quite similar with respect to positivity $( M = 5 . 6$ versus <sub>M</sub>  5<sub>.</sub>7, <sub>p</sub> <sub>></sub> 0<sub>.</sub>1), emotionality (<sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>2 versus <sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>4, $p > 0 . 1 )$ , self-focus (<sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>0 versus $M = 4 . 4 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ and other-focus (<sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>7 versus <sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>5, $p > 0 . 1 )$ . The word $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { l i k e } ^ { \prime \prime }$ is, by contrast, perceived as more positive $( M = 6 . 0$ versus <sub>M</sub>  5<sub>.</sub>7 (for “recommend”), $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and more emotional (<sub>M</sub>  5<sub>.</sub>8 versus <sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>4 (for “recommend”), $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , implying a higher degree of self-focus (<sub>M</sub>  6<sub>.</sub>3 versus <sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>4 (for “recommend”), $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and a lower degree of other-focus $( M = 2 . 7$ versus <sub>M</sub>  4<sub>.</sub>5 (for “recommend”), $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ .

To keep the experimental design parsimonious, we decided to compare the positively connoted label “like” with only one neutral label. We settled on the label “recommend” after a further analysis of our evaluated media channels, which revealed that more of them use “recommend” (62.5% of the leading media outlets) than “share” (37.5%).

## 5.2. Experimental Design

In the first study, we used a 2 (one-click versus twoclick design) × 2 (high versus low information flow control) $\times 2 ~ ( ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { l i k e ^ { \prime \prime } }$ versus “recommend” label) experimental, between-subjects design to test the impact of (1) the diferent labels, (2) increased information flow control over the sharing process, and (3) two-click designs on sharing behavior.<sup>5</sup>

Each participant was randomly assigned to one of the eight conditions that reflected the diferent sharing mechanisms. Across all conditions, each participant read nine press articles (the order of articles was random to avoid any order-related biases) and had the option to share each article hypothetically on Facebook through the provided button (see the online appendix for experimental instructions). Under the condition “high information flow control,” participants could choose to share the content publicly, with close friends, with acquaintances, with oneself, or with a customized peer group; under the condition “low information flow control,” participants simply broadcasted the content. In each condition, the employed sharing mechanism was explained via both text and illustration (see Figure 1 in the online appendix).

We varied the design of the diferent sharing mechanisms between subjects (i.e., the mechanism stayed constant for each user but varied across users) while the content characteristics varied within subjects (i.e., each user had to read every article but in a randomized order). For the experiment, we chose nine diverse articles published by the leading German news outlet Spiegel Online. Thematically, the articles pertained to the science, lifestyle, culture, and society categories. We removed any information about the author and magazine as well as any pictures to avoid the influence of these factors.

After the subjects read each article and made the decision to share or not share, we asked the subjects about their subjective feelings evoked by said article and had them rate the congruity between the provided sharing mechanism and the article (seven-point scale: “The provided sharing mechanism was appropriate for sharing the article” anchored with “1  strongly disagree” and “7  strongly agree”). Participants rated the articles on the dimensions “positivity,” “evoking anger,” “anxiety,” “awe,” “sadness,” “interest,” “surprise,” and “practical utility” (see Berger and Milkman 2012 for an explanation of these dimensions) using a seven-point scale anchored with “1  strongly disagree” and “7  strongly agree.”

In line with previous research, we followed the nine experimental rounds with surveys of several personal traits, such as participants’ motivation to be part of a group (need to belong), need to be diferent, altruism, and opinion leadership. We also considered demographics (gender, age, and education) and Facebook usage habits (number of friends and frequency of Facebook usage as a categorical variable). To measure the psychographic variables “need to belong,” “need to be diferent,” “altruism,” and “opinion leadership,” we used seven-point scales derived from previous research (see Table 2), anchored with “1  strongly disagree” and “7  strongly agree.” Although the random assignment to groups should help to neutralize any participant diferences (at least with a suficiently large number of subjects in each treatment group), we used this information to evaluate the success of the random assignment as well as the characteristics that drive the content-sharing process.

## 5.3. Participants, Summary Statistics, and Randomization Checks

At no point did we inform the participants about the actual research question; instead, we simply stated that the study sought to investigate the kinds of content generally shared on OSNs. We conducted the experiment online using a sample of representative Internet users (with respect to age and gender) provided by a market research company. Each session took approximately 20–30 minutes. We used the first question to screen out non-Facebook users to avoid biasing our results. In total, 411 participants took part in our experiment for a monetary compensation of about e6 (∼\$7 USD). After confirming that participants possessed a valid Facebook account, we randomly assigned them to the eight conditions. After removing the participants with incomplete answers or disabled cookies, we arrived at 3,222 observations from 358 users, resulting in a completion rate of 87%.

Table 1 in the online appendix details the sample’s characteristics (see Table 3 in the online appendix for the summary statistics of users’ article evaluations). Both genders were almost equally represented (55% male). In terms of education, the largest segment of participants possessed a vocational diploma (34%), followed by a high school degree (18%) and a diploma degree (14%). The mean age in the sample was 45 years old (the average age of a German Internet user is about 41 years old). The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests showed that users were indeed randomly assigned to the treatment groups on the basis of gender, personal traits, demographics, and Facebook usage habits (see Table 2 in the online appendix). Also, the order of the articles was perfectly randomized over the nine iterations (Pearson $\stackrel { \cdot } { \chi } ^ { 2 } ( 6 4 ) \stackrel { \cdot } { = }$ 54<sub>.</sub>52<sup>n.s.</sup>).

Because our data featured repeated observations on individuals, we estimated generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a logistic model to account for the binary outcome measure (share or not share). This method, also referred to as panel data analysis or crosssectional time-series analysis, allowed us to address the problem of ordinary models (such as logit models) that tend to produce underestimated standard errors when analyzing data with correlated observations. Our estimation equation thus reads

$$
\operatorname{logit} \left(\text {Share} _ {i j}\right) = \mathbf {x} ^ {\prime} \beta + \varepsilon_ {i j} \quad \text {Share} \sim \text {Bernoulli},
$$

where $S h a r e _ { i j }$ measures user $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ likelihood to share an article <sub>i</sub>. The vector <sub>x</sub> describes article <sub>i</sub> on several content-related and user <sub>j</sub>-related characteristics. We used robust standard errors and assumed an autoregressive within-subject correlation structure.

5.4.1. High Information Flow Control. <sup>As</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>manipula-</sup> tion check, we asked participants to rate their perceptions of the sharing mechanism’s information flow control. Using a seven-point scale, all users in the high information flow control groups evaluated the control over the sharing process $( M = 4 . 9 5 )$ as significantly higher than the users in the low information flow control group $( M = 4 . 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). The diferences in perceived control were even higher for the subsample of users who shared at least once $( M = 3 . 7 2$ versus <sub>M</sub>  5<sub>.</sub>52, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . We conclude that this manipulation worked as intended.

High information flow control exerted a negative efect on sharing $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . If a content provider implements a sharing mechanism that allows users to exert high information flow control over the sharing process (e.g., Share button), the odds ratio for sharing the content is 58% lower than for the sharing mechanisms with low control given that the other variables in the model remain constant. As discussed in Section 3, users might anticipate the utility and costs associated with the sharing process and thus refrain from sharing when the frictional costs exceed the utility.

To substantiate this claim, we examined the cognitive costs of users who stopped in the middle of the sharing process (<sub>N</sub>  110 users). These users started the sharing process by clicking the button but then stopped when they had to specify their intended audience (friends, acquaintances, custom, etc.). This group of users evaluated the cognitive efort $( M = 2 . 6 1 )$ as significantly higher than users who completed the sharing process $( \breve { M } = 2 . 0 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$

We further assessed the interaction between the mechanisms and demographic variables. We observed an additional interaction efect between age and the option of high information flow control, such that older people tended to share less under this condition (<sub>p</sub> <sub><</sub> 0<sub>.</sub>01, see Table 5 in the online appendix). This could potentially indicate that younger subjects, who are on average more technologically savvy (Breakwell and Fife-Schaw 1988), perceive fewer frictional costs when faced with this design variant while older subjects are particularly impeded by the additional efort needed to complete the sharing process.

This result addresses a point of dissent among the interviewed social media managers with regard to the efects of the high information flow control feature. While some of them value this feature’s high interaction possibility, others see the potential disadvantage of increased frictional costs. Our results validate the latter managers’ concerns. Apparently, a significant number of participants felt that the increased frictional costs of one additional click exceeded the marginal utility of having high information flow control.

<sub>5.4.2.</sub> <sub>Two-Click</sub> <sub>Design.</sub> Next, we tested the efect of the two-click design on participants’ sharing behavior. This sharing mechanism characteristic presented a significantly negative efect on the likelihood of sharing $( p < 0 . 0 \dot { 5 } )$ . The odds ratio for sharing content with twoclick sharing mechanisms was 30% lower than for the alternative design, ceteris paribus. This result reflects a sentiment expressed by one of the interviewees: “It is as with ads, with every additional click you lose your users.” Other interviewees highlighted that the act of explaining the design to users could render privacy concerns more salient and thereby reduce sharing behavior.

To better understand the low performance of the two-click sharing mechanism, we performed several investigations. First, we asked the users who refrained from sharing whether their decision was the result of increased cognitive efort or privacy concerns. There were no diferences with respect to privacy concerns between the one-click and two-click groups $( M = 3 . 7 6$ versus $M = 3 . 3 4 , p > 0 . 1 5 )$ that could explain the nonsharing. We did, however, find significant diferences with regard to efort. Users in the two-click design group who shared at least once perceived the efort as significantly higher (<sub>M</sub>  2<sub>.</sub>85) than the users in the one-click group $( \bar { M } = 2 . 1 8 ,  p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Frictional costs seem to be one of the main obstacles for sharing.

5.4.4. Other Controls and Alternative Explanations. We controlled for personal traits and found that these characteristics had significant efects on the likelihood of sharing online articles. In contrast to the study by Ho and Dempsey (2010), we found a significantly positive efect of users’ “need to belong” on the likelihood of sharing content on Facebook $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , such that the likelihood increased by 31% for every one-unit increase on the “need to belong” scale. The desire to form and maintain social relationships is among the most powerful human motives (Baumeister and Leary 1995), and OSNs obviously constitute a new instrument to support these needs.

Also, people who identified as opinion leaders were more likely to share content on the OSN $( p < 0 . 0 5 ) ,$ which aligns with previous research. Users with a need to be diferent exhibited an opposite efect $( p <$ 0<sub>.</sub>01): those high in this need were less likely to share content on the OSN $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This seems to contrast with previous research showing that individualists tend to voice their opinions and have a higher likelihood of sharing to stand out in the community (e.g., Ho and Dempsey 2010). However, we might be observing the end of a fashion cycle. Individualists tend to refrain from using mainstream products and instead adopt new products, and considering nearly everyone in the Western hemisphere has a Facebook account, the OSN can safely be considered a mainstream product. This is the basis for every fashion cycle as Simmel (1904, p. 544) reported in his essay on fashion more than a century ago and represents an ongoing problem for many marketers of “cool” brands, such as Vans and Diesel, as well as major luxury brands (Hinz et al. 2015). Therefore, it might be that individualists accentuate their uniqueness by not sharing on Facebook.

We also observed a positive relation between altruism and sharing content on the OSN $( p < 0 . 1 )$ , which accords with previous findings from WOM research (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004, Ho and Dempsey 2010). Women were less likely to share content on OSNs $( p <$ $0 . 0 1 )$ , such that the odds of sharing decreased by 55% for women, ceteris paribus. Age and education level presented no significant relation to sharing behavior $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . We also controlled for the content virality drivers as identified by Berger and Milkman (2012), finding that positivity is positively associated with the content’s likelihood of being shared on OSNs.

The experimental round number exerted no significant efect on sharing behavior. In other words, there is no indication that articles presented later were shared less often as a result of users’ mental fatigue or other confounding efects. However, since participants were asked to rate the appropriateness of the sharing mechanism after every article, they might have recognized the intervention and begun focusing on the sharing mechanisms for subsequent articles. To test this potential confounding efect, we estimated an additional model and used only the observation of the very first round for every subject. Although this heavily decreased the number of observations, we still find that a positively connoted label $( - 3 . 4 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and high information flow control $( - 0 . 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ exert significantly negative efects on content sharing. That said, the efect of the two-click button became insignificant in this supplementary analysis.

Finally, it is possible that the results sufered from a demand efect as a result of the experiment’s artificial setting and participants’ focus on the sharing decision. In fairness, the fraction of shared articles (9.9%) in our experiment was comparable to reported fractions: Haile (2014), for example, reported 8.8 Likes on Facebook per 100 visitors. Moreover, this potential demand efect influenced all treatment groups equally and thus would potentially afect the absolute results but not the relative ones.

## 6. Study 2: Studying Sharing Mechanisms Across Media

## 6.1. Data and Coding

For Study 2, we collected data about all articles that (a) appeared in the 10 most popular German online newspapers and magazines (according to Alexa.com) during August 2012; (b) were made available through permanent links, and (c) implemented only one Facebook sharing mechanism (i.e., not a combined Share and Like button). We created a web crawler farm for the purpose of recording each article’s title, link to the full text, publication title, and section in which the article was published. The web crawlers visited the websites every three hours and captured, for each article, the number of shares on Twitter, Facebook, and Google+. Overall, we collected data about 42,250 articles. After excluding blogs, videos, series of images, livestreams, and news tickers, our final data set consisted of 41,904 articles.

We set our dependent variable as the total number of shares on Facebook 24 hours after the article’s online publication. Next, we coded the type of sharing mechanism that the online publication had implemented at the time of analysis (see Table 5), according to whether the site implemented a high information control button, a two-click design, and the word “like” as a label. Three outlets implemented high information control, four implemented the privacy-friendly twoclick button, and two outlets implemented a button with a positively connoted label whereas the rest used a neutral label. The reference category was the low information control button with a neutral label, which neither allowed high control over the sharing process nor used the two-click design. Table 5 also indicates how the mechanisms compare with those employed in Study 1. See Figure 2 in the online appendix for the appearance of the particular buttons across the diferent conditions.

We enriched the data set with the variables listed in Table 6. Additionally, we drew a stratified subsample of 638 articles (1.5%) and manually coded them on several content dimensions and author characteristics. We followed Berger and Milkman’s (2012) methods when controlling for content characteristics: Using an automated sentiment analysis dictionary, LIWC (Pennebaker et al. 2007), we quantified articles’ positivity by calculating the percentage diference between an article’s share of positive and negative words (Berger and Milkman 2012). We also categorized articles into diferent (multiple) topic areas (e.g., science, technology, sports, politics, and business) and used dummy variables to capture their categorical efects.

To complete the manual coding, we asked four coders to classify the articles according to the coding instructions provided by Berger and Milkman (2012) (available at www.marketingpower.com/jmr \_webappendix). They rated the articles on the emotional dimensions of anger, awe, sadness, and anxiety as well as on interest, surprise, and practical utility, all on five-point scales anchored with $\hat { \mathbf { \Omega } } ^ { \prime } 1 = \mathrm { n o t } \mathrm { a t } \mathrm { a l l } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and “5  extremely.” We did not inform the coders about the research question, but we did train them with a test set of articles to ensure good inter-rater reliability (pairwise Holsti Index ≥ 0<sub>.</sub>80; Holsti 1969).

According to Berger and Milkman (2012), men and women tend to have diferent writing styles, so we also controlled for the gender of article authors. In addition, some authors have complex writing styles that can make reading more cumbersome and thereby weaken the desire to share. To measure writing complexity, we applied the Flesch Reading Ease metric (Flesch 1948), which is ubiquitous in this domain (http:// en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch-Kincaid\_readability\_test# Flesch\_Reading\_Ease). Furthermore, we used a dummy variable that captured whether the article was based on reports from news agencies. Because such articles ofer early versions of common knowledge, readers may believe that others are already aware of the information and refrain from sharing them. Finally, we controlled for the article’s publication position (i.e., first page or subpage).

Additionally, content that is prominently positioned is more likely to difuse (Berger and Milkman 2012, Heimbach and Hinz 2016). We therefore included a variable that captured the content’s location. Most users start reading online magazines on the home page, which entails that articles published on the start page are more likely to be read and shared on OSNs. We also included an indicator variable for the 10 online newspapers and magazines that captured the unobserved heterogeneity among the outlets.

Finally, we controlled for the time when the article first appeared online, creating a dummy variable for the time between 6 a.m. and 6 p.m. as well as a dummy variable for a weekend day. On the basis of Szabo and Huberman’s (2010) finding that sharing activities difer across distinct time points, we reasoned that articles that appear at 10:00 p.m. draw less attention than articles that appear at 10:00 a.m. Moreover, people might read online articles more often on workdays than on weekends. Table 6 in the online appendix provides the summary statistics.

The Facebook users in our sample more frequently shared complex articles $( p < 0 . 1 )$ and articles on the landing page $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Finally, and as we initially hypothesized, articles were less frequently shared $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ when news agencies were the sources of the information.

## 6.3. Discussion of Potential Endogeneity Problems and Robustness Checks

The analysis of observational data can sufer from possible endogeneity problems. In our case, content providers’ choice of sharing mechanism could be endogenous when said providers choose the sharing mechanism that works best for them. Although the social media managers that we interviewed stated that they cannot make an informed decision with respect to sharing mechanism design because of missing research in this area—a gap that this paper aims to close—we cannot fully rule out this problem for Study 2. We admit that this matching technique can only control for observable heterogeneity.

Unobservable heterogeneity might still bias the results even after matching. To further address this problem, we estimated a model that includes additional controls related to the readership (gender, age, education, and occupation) of the respective online newspaper and magazine (see Table 7 in the online appendix). The inclusion of these newspaper-specific variables did not change the efects and significance of our main variables. Additionally, our paper utilizes a multimethod approach that combines the strengths of diferent approaches to overcome the limitations of a single method and cross-validate the outcomes.

We acknowledge that missing variables, such as substitution efects between diferent OSNs, might be problematic. We therefore estimated a model that included the number of Tweets for every examined article (see model 5 in Table 7 in the online appendix). The number of Tweets was positively associated $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ with the number of shares, indicating that similar virality factors drive content sharing on both OSNs. The inclusion of the Tweets did not substantially change the main efects of the sharing mechanism features.

As an additional robustness check, we estimated our data set using negative binomial regression with cluster-robust standard errors (see model 4 in Table 7 in the online appendix). This analysis confirmed that the efects and significance of our main variables are robust, independent of the estimation method.

## 7. Study 3: Efects of the Two-Click Design 7.1. Data and Estimation Strategy

In response to Germany’s privacy debates in 2011, a popular tabloid, BILD.de, introduced two-click buttons on its website during the 50th week of 2012 (December 10–December 16), then abolished them nearly a year later, in the 47th week of 2013. This natural experiment constitutes a promising opportunity to analyze the efects of two-click design using a DID approach. The DID approach, one of the most commonly used methods for estimating the efects of interventions or policy changes (Imbens and Wooldridge 2009), relies on the comparison of two subjects: one that changes its policy (a treatment group) and one that does not (control group). The DID method helps to overcome the limitations of comparing heterogeneous subjects (such as in Study 2) (Meyer 1995). The method assumes that any unobservable factors exert the same influence on both subjects, which helps to illuminate the causal efect(s) arising from a policy change.

To identify the efects of two-click design, we compared the daily number of Facebook shares on BILD.de with another site, Focus Online, which did not change its sharing mechanism design in the observation period. The two online newspapers are comparable: both implemented Facebook’s “recommend” buttons at the time of analysis, and both overlap in terms of readership (see Figure 3 in the online appendix for the multidimensional scale analysis) and content as Focus Online adapts parts of BILD.de’s content (see, e.g., Meier 2014).

For Study 3, our data set encompassed 29,763 articles published on either BILD.de or Focus Online between November 9, 2012, and January 9, 2013 (one month before and after the introduction of the twoclick design by BILD.de). We aggregated the number of Facebook shares on a daily basis to handle the serial correlation within the observations in the same time period (Bertrand et al. 2004). The final data set consisted of 122 observations of daily aggregated Facebook shares (Focus Online: $M = 4 , \dot { 7 } 7 9 . \check { 8 } \check { 3 } 6 ,$ , SD  2<sub>,</sub>272<sub>.</sub>427; BILD.de: $M = 5 1 , 6 7 9 . 5 6 , \mathrm { S D } = 2 1 , 5 4 5 . 5 9 )$

The count characteristics of the data prompted us to estimate Poisson regressions with cluster-robust standard errors; in this way, we could account for serial correlation (Bertrand et al. 2004) among the articles published in the same outlet. We arrived at the model

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Prob} (N _ {-} S h a r e s) = \frac {e ^ {- \lambda_ {t}} \lambda_ {t} ^ {N _ {-} S h a r e s _ {t}}}{N _ {-} S h a r e s _ {t} !} \quad \text {with} \\ \lambda_ {t} = e ^ {\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot b i l d + \beta_ {2} \cdot t w o c l i c k + \beta_ {3} \cdot b i l d \cdot t w o c l i c k + x _ {t} + \varepsilon}, \end{array}
$$

where <sub>N</sub>\_<sub>Shares</sub> measures the number of aggregated shares on day $t ,$ and $\mathbf { x } _ { t }$ is the vector of month and weekday controls. The variable <sub>bild</sub> equals one if the observation pertains to BILD.de and zero if it pertains to Focus.de; the variable <sub>twoclick</sub> indicates the date when BILD.de launched the two-click buttons.

## 8. General Discussion

## 8.1. Summary of Main Results

Research on information difusion is vast but has rarely addressed the design of online sharing mechanisms. Applying a framework developed by Markus and Silver (2008), we classified sharing mechanisms according to their functional afordances and symbolic expressions. Across a controlled experiment and two field studies, we then systematically examined the different variants and analyzed their impact on the sharing of German online press articles.

In the controlled experiment, we found that sharing mechanisms that allow a high information flow control over the sharing process lower the likelihood of content sharing by 58%. The first field study further supports this finding (−55%). Furthermore, the two field studies revealed that if content providers implement two-click design mechanisms to preserve users’ institutional privacy, the likelihood of content sharing decreases by up to 58%. Higher frictional costs are likely to be one reason for this reduction. After all, in our controlled experiment, the group of users who shared at least once under the two-click treatment perceived the efort as significantly higher than the users under the oneclick treatment (<sub>p</sub> <sub><</sub> 0<sub>.</sub>05).

With respect to symbolic expression, we found that schema congruity is an important factor as the labeling of sharing mechanisms can significantly afect users’ sharing behavior. Specifically, in the domain of news, sharing mechanisms that have a positively connoted label (“like”) decrease the likelihood of sharing on an OSN by up to 86%.

## 8.2. Research Contribution

Previous research on difusion processes has mainly focused on their outcomes (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006) and the influential people driving said processes (e.g., Hill et al. 2006, Hinz et al. 2011) rather than on the impact of contextual factors such as the sharing mechanism design. We extended this emerging research stream by examining the efect of high information flow control (sometimes called narrowcast), the importance of schema congruity, and the impact of frictional costs. This paper shows that the design of the sharing mechanism can significantly influence the aggregated number of shares and, consequently, the difusion of information. The magnitude of the results also emphasizes the need to consider contextual factors when examining information difusion. The design of the communication channel or the means to share information is at least as important as the characteristics of the shared content or the characteristics and motives of senders and recipients. In any case, this is a factor that firms can actively manage. However, research in the area of information difusion has not examined this factor satisfactorily.

Our work emphasizes the utility aspect for information transmission as the design of sharing mechanisms is closely related to the concept of costs as a type of disutility. Costs can originate from time and efort that an individual needs to invest for the sharing process. Our results indicate that the process of sharing itself has to be as lean as possible. One additional click—as in the two-click design variant—can create enough disutility and frictional costs as to ultimately inhibit content sharing. This is in line with website design recommendations in the area of HCI, which highlight the role of simplicity in successful interface designs. Web users tend to exhibit high impatience and insist on instant gratification (Nielsen 1999), which inclines them to act on impulse. As a consequence, the utility of sharing can easily be overcompensated by its costs, and indeed, our results show that the net utility from sharing is obviously not much higher than the costs of one additional click. This finding parallels observations in the online auction domain, where one more bid assigns a median disutility of e6.08 to a simple transaction that involves keying in a number and clicking the submit button (Hann and Terwiesch 2003). Thus, it is necessary to account for simplicity when designing sharing mechanisms.

Another factor that can increase the disutility of sharing and thus hamper the sharing process is schema incongruity between the mechanism label and the shared content. Our results confirm previous findings from marketing research that user behavior can be a function of schema congruity (e.g., Sirgy et al. 1991). In our research context, most users refrained from using a button with a positively connoted label when sharing an article involving deaths. The resulting high level of discomfort produces a state of cognitive dissonance that disinclines users from sharing. This finding can be used to enhance the model of Markus and Silver (2008). While previous research has already shown that an efective communication of meaning and values can improve users’ impression of a system (see Grgecic et al. 2015), it also seems crucial to align the symbolic expressions with users’ schemata to galvanize use of said system.

## 8.3. Managerial Implications

In our interviews, we directly asked social media managers about the diferences between social plugins and their possible customizations. All of them agreed that some diferences exist but could not specify these in detail. In closing this gap, our paper provides findings that form the basis for clear managerial implications.

According to our results, practitioners should generally implement sharing mechanisms that (a) use a neutral labeling and (b) only support limited information flow control over the sharing process. Plugins with broadcast capabilities that feature a neutral label (e.g., “recommend” or “share”) and can be used with one click seem to be, on average, the most appropriate for news sharing. However, a positively connoted labeling might lead to more shares if a majority of the published content is also positive. To best exploit the positive interaction efect between congruent labeling and content, content providers need to be able to automatically adapt the sharing button’s label based on an automated analysis of the content. Websites such as BILD.de are currently exploring such techniques, and it will be interesting to witness their success.

In general, the developers of social plugins need to pay attention to schema congruity, respecting the fit between labels and content. Facebook, for instance, recently introduced six new reactions (like, love, haha, wow, sad, and angry), which is certainly one way to achieve a higher fit.

Our studies also empirically confirm some practitioners’ expectation that the privacy-preserving feature of the two-click button negatively afects contentsharing behavior in the area of news sharing. However, content providers who publish very sensitive content (e.g., health or finance topics) should still include privacy-preserving social plugins and refrain from using standard plugins that automatically transfer information without the users’ explicit consent.

For most practitioners, the main lesson is to keep the process as lean and simple as possible while still devising novel solutions that address users’ privacy concerns. For example, OSNs and content providers should create new social plugins that minimize frictional costs while still respecting users’ privacy. European websites especially need to address this issue because of recent court decisions. Developers could utilize JavaScript and OSNs’ application programming interfaces to design social plugins that refrain from transferring information on page load while still allowing users to push information to their newsfeed with just one click.

Content providers could also include several social plugins, perhaps by following Facebook’s recommendation to include both the Like and Share buttons. This would allow users to choose the sharing mechanisms that best match their needs. Users with high social privacy concerns could use the sharing mechanism with high information flow control while users with low social privacy concerns could employ the broadcast mechanism. Users could also adapt their choice to the nature of the content. However, including more social plugins can slow down page load and thereby lower a website’s Google ranking. Thus, new and growing OSNs need to provide and promote the very best sharing mechanism that maximizes content virality on their platform as they will likely not receive multiple spots on content providers’ websites. Interestingly, we find that the default plugins for newer OSNs, such as Pinterest, Instagram, and Tumblr, already parallel our recommendations, combining neutral labels or icons with low information flow control.

Finally, our results clearly show that content providers should place the burden of content difusion on social media planners rather than journalists. The implementation of efective sharing mechanisms can facilitate content difusion without authors having to violate journalistic integrity by deliberately crafting content for viral purposes.

## 8.4. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Our research has several limitations that might be addressed by further research. First, we mainly focused on the efects of sharing mechanism designs on contentsharing outcomes rather than on how users specifically respond to diferent designs. Additional studies could concentrate on the specific efects induced by diferent sharing mechanisms and tie those efects to distinct content-sharing outcomes.

Second, some segments of the user population might exhibit radically diferent sharing patterns. Our studies assessed average behavior as indicated by the aggregated outcome on the market level, but it would also be interesting to analyze the impact of various sharing mechanisms on the behavior(s) of diferent user segments (e.g., users with high privacy concerns).

Third, it would be valuable to determine a content provider’s optimal portfolio of sharing mechanisms, especially given the importance of page load times, the value of website space, and the fact that users might be distracted by too many sharing buttons. For instance, researchers could investigate the impact of sacrificing a second button from a leading OSN to integrate the plugin of a newer OSN.

Fourth, we derived our data on sharing behavior from the German media market, particularly using the websites of the leading newspapers and magazines in this domain. As such, our studies might have been afected by both a domain-specific bias and cultural biases. Further studies should strive to generalize our findings to other markets.

Fifth, we analyzed the impetus of the difusion process from the user side, that is, users’ interaction with the sharing mechanism. In doing so, we excluded other important factors that future research might explore, such as the OSN’s algorithm that spreads the shared information.

Sixth, the analysis of observational data can always sufer from omitted variables. We tried to address this point by controlling for numerous potential influence factors and conducted several robustness checks. However, all these measures cannot fully rule out endogeneity problems. Large-scaled experiments might solve this problem in the future.

Finally, there is a need to develop integrated approaches that can account for possible interaction effects among sender and receiver characteristics, content and sharing mechanism characteristics, and other contextual factors, such as attention competition. The studies by Schulze et al. (2014) and Berger and Iyengar (2013) as well as those in the present paper represent pioneers in revealing the interactions between the different components of information difusion. Therefore, there are many new and promising research opportunities to be found at this intersection of psychology, sociology, and information systems research that may ultimately prove to be highly valuable for business practice.
