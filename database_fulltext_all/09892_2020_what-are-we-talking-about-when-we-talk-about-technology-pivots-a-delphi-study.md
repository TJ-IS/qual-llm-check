## Abstract

Technology pivots were designed to help digital startups make adjustments to the technology underpinning their products and services. While academia and the media make liberal use of the term “technology pivot,” they rarely align themselves to Ries’ foundational conceptualization. Recent research suggests that a more granulated conceptualization of technology pivots is required. To scientifically derive a comprehensive conceptualization, we conduct a Delphi study with a panel of 38 experts drawn from academia and practice to explore their understanding of “technology pivots.” Our study thus makes an important contribution to advance the seminal work by Ries on technology pivots.

## Keywords

Digital startup, Lean Startup Approach, Technology Pivot, Conceptualization, Delphi Study

## 1. Introduction

Digital startups have to make hard and fast decisions concerning the definition and selection of the technology design (i.e., architecture, components, interfaces, and their interrelations) of their products and services (IEEE 1990; Yoo et al. 2010). Because of the innovative nature of their offerings, they have to base their decisions on assumptions rather than clear requirements. Once a technology design has been adopted, it inherently outlines – and at the same time restricts – the space of what can be built, as well as creates dependencies that are not easy to resolve (Kraus et al., 2019; Paternoster et al., 2014). In this context of uncertainty, digital startups often have to fundamentally change their technology design and digital innovation during the course of their developmental phase (Bajwa et al., 2017; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016). Hence, for digital startups considerable changes to technology – so-called technology pivots (Ries, 2011) – are the rule rather than the exception, the case of Twitter being a case in point (Aniszczyk, 2013). Twitter performed a technology pivot from a monolith to a scalable microservices architecture to provide better performance, reliability, and efficiency for an existing solution using a different technology.

Technology pivots are one of several types of pivot suggested by the popular Lean Startup Approach (LSA) by Ries (2011). This particular type enables digital startups to perform fundamental course corrections of their technology design. Ries (2011, p. 178) describes technology pivots as a means of “achiev[ing] the same solution by using a completely different technology,” driven by the question of “whether the new technology can provide superior price and/or performance compared to existing technology.” As such, performing a technology pivot represents a landmark decision for digital startups because their value creation heavily relies on the utilization of technologies and digital innovations (Steininger, 2019). However, because of the innovative nature of their value creation, they can only ever obtain limited knowledge of or experience in their fields, with limited resources (Sutton, 2000) and small margins for errors and delays. Unsurprisingly, then, many “wait until far too late to consider a pivot. [Until] the roof is on fire [and] the walls are caving in” (Ries, 2017, p. 110).

In recent years, researchers have started to scientifically validate the knowledge surrounding the LSA, which mainly builds on recalling past cases and anecdotal evidence (Frederiksen and Brem, 2017; Steininger, 2018; Ghezzi, 2018). As part of this effort, first empirical studies analyzed individual types of pivot (e.g., Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017; Khanna et al., 2018), motivated by the fact that “the boundaries between the different types of pivot can be blurry, and sometimes it may even be difficult to determine into which category a pivot belongs” (Terho et al., 2015, p. 560). Research on technology pivots started by analyzing the antecedents and consequences that lead up to and result from them to create a validated knowledge base and support digital startups with performing technology pivots (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017; (blinded for review); Terho et al., 2015). These antecedents and consequences often go beyond Ries’ (2011) initial conceptualization (Bajwa et al., 2016), and their contributions allow first conclusions to be drawn that important adaptations of the seminal contributions made by Ries (2011) are required, while ambiguity remains about what technology pivot actually represent. A similar ambiguity is present when the media mentions technology pivots. Several prominently reported cases (e.g., TechCrunch, 2014; Washington Post, 2015) are not consistent with Ries (2011) descriptions. Especially for digital startups that utilize technologies at the core of their value creation in products and services (Nambisan, 2013), a clear understanding of what a technology pivot entails is of great importance, particularly for effective decision-making and the ability to grow into viable and sustainable businesses. An imprecise conceptualization of technology pivots also hinders the support offered by coaches and investors before, during, and after technology pivots.

The main measure of course correction (i.e., pivoting) in digital startups and the conceptualizations of different types of pivot have received limited attention so far (Bajwa et al., 2017; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016). Moreover, there are no studies that have comprehensively and scientifically conceptualized the term “technology pivot” (Berg et al., 2018; (blinded for review); Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016). This is all the more surprising considering the widespread interest that digital startups and the LSA have recently sparked among the academic community (Edison et al., 2018; Frederiksen and Brem, 2017; Steininger, 2019; Yang et al., 2019; Yordanova, 2018). The insufficient knowledge base would therefore strongly suggest the need for further research on the subject. Thus, the purpose of this study is to explore the underlying meanings both researchers and practitioners assign to technology pivots, derive factors and features1 that clearly conceptualize the term, and allow to serve as a basis for continuing debate on technology pivots. Hence, we pose the following research question:

Which factors advance the conceptualization of the term “technology pivot”?

In the absence of a sound theoretical basis that can be used to answer this question, we conduct a rankingtype Delphi study, which enables group communication through a structured process, using an elaborate expert panel consisting of 19 academics and 19 practitioners. Our findings are presented in the form of an elaborate conceptualization of the term technology pivot. They underline that the conceptualization by Ries (2011) does not equate with the contemporary understanding of academia and practice, and requires several adaptations and extensions. This study is the first to provide a definition for technology pivots in digital startups by involving a panel of experts in digital entrepreneurship and the LSA. The broad range of areas of expertise they represent makes our research meaningful to both academia and practice. We further identify and describe the concordance and discordance among academics and practitioners with regard to technology pivots, which offer meaningful avenues for future research aiming to investigate their origins. Overall, we contribute to the discussion of digital entrepreneurship within information systems (IS) literature (Giudice and Straub, 2011; Ojala, 2016; Steininger, 2019), where the interrelation between technological innovations and entrepreneurship has been much highlighted and discussed (Giudice and Straub, 2011; Keen and Williams, 2013), as well as further research has been encouraged.

## 2. Background and Related Work

Digital entrepreneurship is said to be “not just a research context but an emerging field of research” (Zaheer et al., 2018, p. 4) – a field open to new concepts and theories about how entrepreneurs develop startups utilizing digital innovations, for which proposals for research themes have only recently started to appear (Nambisan, 2017; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016). As evidenced by the calls for research on the methodologies applied in digital entrepreneurship (Berg et al., 2018; Steininger, 2019; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016), this new field of research “demand[s] novel theorizing of how entrepreneurial opportunities are formed and enacted” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 1030). Such digital entrepreneurship methodologies need to consider challenges and outcomes that differ from those of traditional new ventures, because of the unique nature of digital technologies (Zaheer et al., 2018), not least of which the need to evaluate technological choices and integration processes. To fulfil the need for a better understanding of this new field of research – namely, digital entrepreneurship – our study focuses on the particular context of digital startups utilizing digital innovations (Lyytinen, 2018; Steininger, 2019). As noted before “[g]ood science contributing with a clear conceptualization of “technology pivots” to enable further inquiries on an important subject for digital startups.

## 2.1 Digital Startups

Digital innovations are being integrated into an ever-increasing range of business and consumer products and services, expanding the role and relevance of information technology (IT) for any innovation (Steininger, 2019). Back in the mid-1990s, Carmel (1994) had already recognized that small software companies are exceptionally successful in building and delivering innovative products and services. Today, following the definition by Steininger (2019), these companies are called digital startups. Digital startups are immature and inexperienced organizations with a short operating history and limited resources (Klotins et al., 2018; Sutton, 2000). Their aim is to develop innovative software-based products and services utilizing digital innovations, to create novel offerings that address unmet or newly revealed customer needs (Edison et al., 2018). A digital startup’s value creation and business model rely entirely on processes mediated through IT. Utilizing digital innovations lends them a unique technological advantage and helps them realize offerings that are at the high end of a market. Yet, their limited resources also mean that they have to perform under intense time pressure (Paternoster et al., 2014). Initially, digital startups begin with an incomplete knowledge about the problem to be solved and the technologies required to fulfill customer needs (Ojala, 2016; Pantiuchina et al., 2017), which may change over time (Tatikonda and Rosenthal, 2000). Technologically addressing those needs represents a considerable challenge (Giardino et al., 2014) as they have to make crucial decisions regarding the design of their architecture, their components, interfaces, and their interrelations (IEEE, 1990) in a context of high uncertainty, and to select technologies and components that may eventually constitute the product. It is hardly surprising, then, that the main challenge faced by digital startups is how to thrive under such technological uncertainty (Giardino et al., 2015). Simultaneously, digital startups are searching for a “scalable, repeatable, [and] profitable business model” to suit their products and services (Blank and Dorf, 2012, p. xvii). Considering the breathtaking pace of today’s digital innovations, evolving market demands, and intense competition, the ability to quickly change one’s own offerings to keep up with technological changes and to meet emerging customer needs is of paramount importance (Bianchi et al., 2018).

## 2.2 Lean Startup Approach

To build up a new venture from scratch where neither the problem nor the solution is well understood, requires a resource-efficient and discovery-oriented methodology (McGrath, 2010). Such a methodology has to help identify, which problem to solve and which solution to build, both from a technology and a business strategy perspective (Bosch et al., 2013; Klotins et al., 2018). A methodology to suit this specific context was proposed by Ries (2011) in his book “The Lean Startup,” which received a considerable amount of attention since its publication (Frederiksen and Brem, 2017). The Lean Startup involves an iterative and hypothesis-driven approach of venture creation and product development (cf. Figure 1), which includes involving potential customers from the very beginning and proposes minimal up-front planning. This approach enables digital startups to transition from opinion-based to data-driven decisionmaking, while allowing for adaptive product and service design (Bianchi et al., 2018).

The LSA starts by envisioning a future on the basis of which a strategy is employed that consists of falsifiable hypotheses about the business model, a problem to be solved, and a potential solution (the product). The hypotheses focus on aspects that are critical to venture success and that are falsifiable, i.e., they can be validated by means of an experimental method (Klepper and Bruegge, 2018). The experiments are designed to understand perceived customer value and derive requirements and are conducted through an iterative feedback loop – the Build-Measure-Learn cycle. During this cycle, a minimum viable product (MVP) is developed and released, customer interactions are measured, and feedback is collected. This allows a quick turn-around of learnings and responding to new information (Eisenmann et al., 2011; Stayton and Mangematin, 2018). After the Build-Measure-Learn cycle is completed, startups decide whether they are on the right track (“preserve”) or need to conduct adjustments to their strategy (“pivot”).

Pivoting in digital startups is a “universal consequence of the conditions of extreme uncertainty” (Ries, 2017, p. 110) about the problem to be solved, the target customers, and the value proposition of their unique products and services (Milliken, 1987; Ojala, 2016; Packard et al., 2017). Because of these conditions, it became “part of the everyday language of many entrepreneurs, the advisors who guide them and the investors who fund them” (Crilly, 2018, p. 54). The LSA described pivoting as “a change in strategy without a change in vision” (Ries, 2017, p. 108). It allows to perform “structured course corrections designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, business model, and engine of growth” (Ries, 2011, p. 149). Ries (2011, p.49) highlights the role of learning as “the essential unit of progress for startups”. With “each pivot creat[ing] a new series of hypothesis” (Ries, 2017, p. 108), digital startups are generating new knowledge. Through fast iterations and “by reducing the time between pivots, it is possible to increase the odds of success” (Bosch et al., 2013, p. 5). Given the uncertainties described above, it is not surprising that many digital startups that grew into viable and sustainable businesses had to pivot (Ries, 2017). Pivoting is typically required several times during the life of a digital startup (Terho et al., 2015), each of which comes in a particular shape, summarized through so called “types of pivot.” Ries (2011) initially suggested ten types of pivot. Each pivot includes making significant strategic adjustments to a digital startup on different levels, such as on the targeted customer segment (customer segment pivot), the addressed customer need (customer need pivot), or the used technology design (technology pivot).

In contrast to a lengthy linear approach, with predefined phases and fixed deliverables, using the LSA allows digital startups to change their pursuit strategy quickly (Edison et al., 2018; Richter et al., 2018). As such, pivoting is “inevitable for almost all digital startups to survive, grow and eventually obtain sustainable business models” (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017, p. 2374). Yet, Bajwa et al. (2016, p. 170) also state that digital startups “avoid pivot[s] when needed” as a consequence of their limited resources and time pressure, “which is one of the reasons behind many startup failures”. Up to now, our understanding of the individual types of pivot is limited (Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016), especially with regard to what motivates them, what they entail in detail, and what they result in. Through increasing our understanding of the individual types of pivot, the pivoting phenomenon as a whole can eventually be better understood, which in turn might bring to light new insights about the phenomenon and enable more effective pivot performance. Recent studies on the subject of pivoting have analyzed, for example, how psychological ownership of startup ideas by entrepreneurs and external feedback influence the pivoting decision in startups (Grimes, 2018), and how entrepreneurs can manage stakeholder relationships while pivoting (Hampel et al., 2019). Their studies drew on the pivoting conceptualization by Ries (2011). However, the call for future research to “[build] a fundamental framework on [the] reasons for pivoting and their types” by Unterkalmsteiner et al. (2016, p. 100) has not been addressed up to now. Based on the evidence on startups that have pivoted “once or twice, raise 2.5 times more money, [and] have 3.6 times better user growth” (Berg et al., 2018), it is argued that pivoting can considerably help digital startups to grow into viable and sustainable businesses (Marmer et al., 2011), motivating further research on the subject.

The development of the LSA was based on the experiences and knowledge of practitioners. In a recent effort to investigate the scientific efficacy of the LSA, Frederiksen and Brem (2017) deconstruct the LSA into five components, i.e., (1) customer and user involvement (2) iterative new product development, (3) experimentation in new product development, (4) early prototyping, and (5) effectual thinking, and investigate the theories and concepts in the scientific literature that support them. They find considerable support for these five components and conclude that they can be assigned to established scientific theories. Having a sound theoretical basis for the core components of the LSA paves the way for more detailed research on the individual components. The concrete application of the LSA by entrepreneurs still leaves many questions unanswered (Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016), which are worth investigating to increase our understanding of the challenges associated with using the LSA in practice and enabling entrepreneurs in using the LSA effectively. This includes – among others – the performance of individual types of pivot, which is usually challenging for entrepreneurs in practice (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017; Ries, 2011; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016).

## 2.3 Pivoting vs. Conventional Strategic Change

The pivoting literature commonly differentiates between a pivot and a conventional change in strategy, enabling pivoting to be turned into a separate research phenomenon (Grimes, 2018; Hampel et al., 2019; Kunisch et al., 2017).

Conventional strategic change is traditionally associated with large established organizations. Literature on conventional strategic change is concerned with, for example, how established organizations change from one validated strategy to another, with changes affecting their organizational structures, or their corporate policies (Hampel et al., 2019). The reasons for strategy changes in established organizations are manifold and could include – among others – responding to a loss in the value of market shares, or to the identification of new business opportunities (Hampel et al., 2019). However, this literature is typically not concerned with the unique context of digital startups (Aldrich and Yang, 2012; Kunisch et al., 2017).

Pivoting in digital startups, in contrast, occurs while digital startups are in the process of developing their first viable business strategy. They “strategize by doing” and apply learning processes like “trial and error, bricolage, improvisation, and experimentation” (Ott et al., 2017, p. 307). When a previously pursued strategy turns out as not to be viable, digital startups decide to pivot, abandoning their initial strategy to pursue another course that seems more promising. As such, pivoting in the context of digital startups is, as a result of their very nature, fundamentally different to the strategic change approaches in large corporates, in particular shorter times to completion and typically more radical changes to their technology design or business model (Frederiksen and Brem, 2017; Nambisan, 2017; Ries, 2011). While representing a distinct research phenomenon as a result of these aspects, Frederiksen and Brem (2017) associate pivoting with “iterative new product development,” coming closest to the aspect of “strong rearrangements” as described by Salerno et al. (2015). Similarly, the description of “alteration” by Becker et al. (2015) shows some similarities to pivoting.

## 2.4 Technology Pivots

Technology pivots are particularly important for digital startups, as they heavily rely on technology for their value creation and business model. They need to define a technology design (i.e., architecture, components, interfaces, and their interrelations) and select value-creating digital innovations for their products and services (IEEE 1990; Yoo et al. 2010), in a context of great uncertainty (Milliken, 1987; Ojala, 2016; Packard et al., 2017) with regard to specific customer problems to be solved and solutions required. Such uncertainty typically leads to the necessity – or desirability – to undertake technologica readjustments, as validated learnings are generated. Technology pivots were initially conceptualized in a seminal contribution by Ries (2011, p. 172):

“Occasionally, a company discovers a way to achieve the same solution by using a completely different technology. Technology pivots are much more common in established businesses. In other words, they are a sustaining innovation, an incremental improvement designed to appeal to and retain an existing customer base. Established companies excel at this kind of pivot because so much is not changing. The customer segment is the same, the customer’s problem is the same, the value-capture model is the same, and the channel partners are the same. The only question is whether the new technology can provide superior price and/or performance compared with the existing technology.” ,”

This conceptualization has since been frequently used to analyze individual technology pivot instances (e.g., Bajwa et al., 2016; Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017; Terho et al., 2015). The study by Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc, and Abrahamsson (2017) analyzed the cases of Android and Paypal as instances of technology pivots, applying the conceptualization by Ries (2011). They describe Android as having “pivot[ed] from an operating system for cameras to providing a mobile platform (operating system) focusing on handsets” (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017, p. 2392). When comparing this pivot description to the conceptualization by Ries (2011, p. 172), however, it remains ambiguous whether this represents an instance in which achieving the “same solution by using a completely different technology” is the goal. Rather, it seems to be an example of where a different solution using a different technology is provided, which is not in line with the original conceptualization by Ries (2011). Similarly, they identify a technology pivot instance for PayPal, which “pivoted from a money exchange solution for Palm Pilot users to online payment” (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017, p. 2399). Yet, for this description as well, it remains doubtful whether the changes performed by Paypal represent an instance of the “same solution [being provided] by using a completely different technology” or “an incremental improvement designed to appeal to and retain an existing customer base” (Ries, 2011, p.172). For the same reasons, the frequently cited examples of technology pivots mentioned in the popular press, i.e., Nokia, Groupon, and Flickr, do not seem to comply with the original conceptualization either (TechCrunch, 2014; Washington Post, 2015). Thus, there remains a great deal of ambiguity both among researchers and the media about when a pivot can justifiably be called a

technology pivot. This is not surprising as the conceptualizations for identifying and assigning pivots to certain types were based primarily on the specific experiences described by Ries (2011). Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc, and Abrahamsson (2017, p. 2379), even note that “in order to ground our research on some basis, we used the pivot types reported in Ries (2011), with the awareness that these types are subject to systematic and scientific validation.”

For these reasons, we looked at academic sources to understand which contributions in current research on technology pivots use the conceptualization by Ries (2011), which contributions might have emerged that differ from Ries (2011), and the extent of the collected empirical basis on technology pivots. To identify relevant studies, we performed a search on Google Scholar in April 2019 based on the systematic approach by vom Brocke et al. (2009), for the term “technology pivot” anywhere in a study. The results totaled 144 studies. We excluded those that are not peer-reviewed and do not relate to the LSA or digital entrepreneurship in general (131 studies). Performing a forward and backward search on the remaining 13 studies did not lead to any new studies. We excluded one study, which was an earlier version of another study already included in our results. We thus identified a total of twelve studies that elaborate on technology pivots in the context of the LSA or digital entrepreneurship, in addition to Ries (2011).

We read and analyzed all of the twelve identified studies thoroughly. We found that five studies (Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc, Matone Chanin, et al., 2017; Crilly, 2018; Edison et al., 2018; Ochoa-Zambrano and Garbajosa, 2017; Pant et al., 2017) only mention the term technology pivot, yet do not perform any research on technology pivots. For the remaining seven studies (Bajwa et al., 2016; Bajwa, Wang, Nguyen Duc and Abrahamsson, 2017; Comberg et al., 2014; (blinded for review); (blinded for review); Khanna et al., 2018; Terho et al., 2015), we found that all collect empirical data for at least one technology pivot and then analyze the collected instances (121 in total). For their research, they all apply the conceptualization provided by Ries (2011) for pivoting and for “technology pivots.” They did not reference any conceptualization for technology pivots other than Ries (2011). We then analyzed these studies by highlighting any parts that describe and explain the scope and findings of the analysis. For this, we used the frequently used categories on the subject (Bajwa et al., 2016; (blinded for review); Terho et al., 2015), i.e., antecedents, nature, and consequences of technology pivots. We further highlighted whether the conceptualization of Ries (2011) was extended within a study and found that these studies either extended or revised only individual parts of his conceptualization (such as the antecedents or consequences of technology pivots) rather than studying the conceptualization as a whole.

## 2.5 Technology Pivots vs. Incremental Technology Adoption

Technology pivots and incremental technology adoptions in established enterprises can be distinguished from each other along similar lines as the distinction between pivoting and conventional changes in the strategy described above (cf. Section 2.3). Following the conceptualization by Ries (2011), the distinction is based on the following key aspects: first of all, technology adoptions in enterprises are often stage-based and incremental in nature (Cram and Brohman, 2013; Gallivan, 2001), compared with the “major changes” involved in pivoting that usually cannot be split into smaller change increments (Ries 2011, p. 149). Second, technology adoptions in enterprises often take place over several months or even years, whereas pivoting is typically conducted over short periods of time (Ries 2011; blinded for review). Technology change decisions in an established enterprise typically involve a complex bureaucratic process with various levels of approval and extensive reviews (Cram and Brohman, 2013; Ryan and Harrison, 2000), whereas the decision to pivot, described by the LSA, is made in a single so-called “pivot or preserve meeting” after the completion of an iterated Build-Measure-Learn cycle (Ries, 2011, p. 164). However, given the need for a quicker response to radical innovations and swiftly changing markets, some large organizations have started to adopt more flexible approaches to conventional technology strategy change, often by creating independent innovation units. As these share some characteristics with digital startups, they may even make use of the LSA and the measures otherwise typically used by digital startups (Napier et al., 2011; Vinekar et al., 2006).

The empirical and the theoretical knowledge base on technology pivots is, then, still nascent. Especially in light of the fact that because of the differences between them, described above, the knowledge about incremental technology adoption in established corporations cannot be applied to technology pivots in a digital startup context. Since the seminal contribution by Ries (2011), the scientific findings made on the antecedents and consequences of technology pivots (cf. Table 1) suggest the need for scientific validation of the existing conceptualization, and its potential updating. A contemporary understanding of technology pivots, beyond Ries’ (2011) seminal contribution (cf. Section 2.4), which is based on the knowledge of a diverse expert panel, would provide a more robust basis for future research on the subject. Therefore, we aim to contribute to the understanding of “technology pivots” and its conceptualization, to allow for theory generation in the future and enhance the external validity of existing findings. A clear understanding and conceptualization will allow research to better identify and analyze existing cases, and consequently provide better support for digital startups performing technology pivots.

## 3. Research Design

To address our research question, an exploratory research design was chosen to generate further knowledge with regard to the conceptualization of technology pivots in digital startups. For this, the Delphi method is utilized.

## 3.1 Delphi Study

The Delphi method is a well-established approach and particularly appropriate for investigating a complex phenomenon about which a certain level of controversy exists or limited empirical evidence is available (Paré et al., 2013). It is based on the philosophical assumptions of Dewey’s pragmatism (Brady, 2015), which is evident in the following ways in our study. First, the flexibility of the Delphi method suits our exploratory research approach. Second, our study was cost-effective as it incorporated inexpensive questionnaires that were delivered electronically. Third, we sought input from a dedicated panel of experts (academic and from industry) with specific expertise on the subject of technology pivots. Finally, our Delphi study is concerned with not only drawing from but also informing practice, and to create a

The Delphi study enables group communication through a structured process, in which a panel of experts answer questionnaires in multiple iterations (Hsu and Ohio, 2007). Through this group communication, experts not only get to express their own attitudes and understandings, but also get to see the attitudes and understandings of others, which helps them reflect and further develop their own opinion (Marshall and Rossman, 2014). Thus, it allows to draw out their knowledge on the topic or question under investigation, where the research study can “benefit from subjective judgments on a collective basis” (Linstone and Turoff, 2002, p. 4). Delphi allows to combine different opinions to contribute to an incomplete state of knowledge and – if possible – to achieve consensus (Linstone and Turoff, 2002). Developed in the 1950s (Dalkey and Helmer, 1963), Delphi has since been applied in various fields of research (Hsu and Ohio, 2007). For example, in the field of IS, it has been used in the identification of IS grand research challenges (Becker et al., 2015), understanding critical skills for managing IT projects (Keil et al., 2013), and deriving criteria for selecting cloud service providers (Lang et al., 2018). In the area of entrepreneurship, Delphi was used to clarify and conceptualize the term “entrepreneurship” (Gartner, 1990), for example.

We decided that a ranking-type Delphi study is well-suited as a methodology for the purpose of this study. The ranking-type Delphi study allows to gain an understanding about the relative importance of factors conceptualizing technology pivots, as well as facilitating iterative consensus building. The created ranking further allows to understand the level of consensus reached among the participants as well as compare rankings among different panel groups. The study was performed using e-mail and the online survey tool Lime Survey. As a result of the expert panel not meeting face-to-face, we have minimized group bias and group thinking (i.e., individuals influencing one another in a group meeting situation, leading to consensus that does not represent their individual thoughts) (Hasson and Keeney, 2011).

For advice on how to conduct a Delphi study, we followed a number of sources, including Okoli and Pawlowski (2004) on a ranking-type Delphi study, Paré (2013) on rigor in IS-related Delphi studies, as well as Fletcher and Marchildon (2014) and Brady (2015) on the use of qualitative elements. Following Okoli and Pawlowski (2004), a four-phase approach was used to perform the study (cf. Figure 2). Similar to other Delphi studies (Becker, vom Brocke, et al., 2015; Lang et al., 2018), Phase 1 of our study pursues a qualitative approach, using an open-ended questionnaire as input for subsequent Delphi phases, and a quantitative approach for phases 2 and 3. The following sections will explain the phases in more detail, including the precautions taken to address the issue of rigor. The four phases were altogether conducted over a (relatively short) period of four months, between March and July 2018, including the analysis and follow-up of participants for a better response rate.

## Phase 0: Panel Creation

For the results of Delphi studies to be meaningful, the composition of the expert panel is a key success factor. While a Delphi study does not require a large statistical sample of participants, it needs qualified experts with a deep understanding of the topic of interest (Okoli and Pawlowski, 2004), because their experiences, knowledge, and opinions are central to the study. Thus, it is paramount to carefully select an appropriate set of experts. Yet, their availability or willingness to participate can make recruitment of appropriate experts difficult (Paré et al., 2013). For the expert panel composition of our study, we used a multistep approach similar to the one proposed by Okoli and Pawlowski (2004). In a first step, two groups of relevant participants were identified as either a) academics (i.e., postdocs and professors with publications appertaining to lean startup and digital entrepreneurship) or b) practitioners (i.e., startup coaches, startup founders, and venture capitalists) from relevant organizations (i.e., incubators, digital startups, and venture capital firms), which is also suggested by Okoli and Pawlowski (2004). We

considered this split appropriate and necessary as we suspected that the two groups could have somewhat different perspectives on technology pivots as a result of their experiences with the subject. Another advantage of using two expert panels was to allow a comparison to be made between the perspectives of these two groups.

Second, having listed relevant organizations and academics, we then defined the specific requirements that individuals had to fulfill to become part of the expert panel, including having obtained the right type of experience with the LSA as a practitioner “applying,” “coaching,” or “observing” LSA in startups, or as an academic “researching” or “teaching” the LSA in action. Furthermore, participants had to have a minimum of two years’ experience with digital entrepreneurship and the LSA, to ensure a sufficiently deep understanding of the context in which the LSA is applied (i.e., digital entrepreneurship), its core mechanisms, i.e., the Build-Measure-Learn cycle, and in particular pivoting. Only once an expert has gained a sufficient understanding of these aspects, can s/he be deemed to have a grasp of different types of pivot, including technology pivots. We concluded this step by drawing up two ranked lists of individuals relevant to the study, one containing academics and the other practitioners.

Third, the individuals were invited to participate and asked to nominate further experts. We checked their recommendations (n=4) for study fit with the criteria defined in step one and kept sending invitations until the target size of the panel (n>35) was reached. We told the experts that we aim to increase our understanding of technology pivots for the purpose of better supporting startups and that their input would help us to better understand what technology pivots entail. We further told them that we would share the study results with them. A total of 209 invitations were sent to experts through e-mail (cf. Appendix A). The response rate was 18%, resulting in a panel of a total of 38 participants (cf. Table 2). With regards to the geographical distribution of the panel, practitioners were exclusively from countries across Europe, while the majority of academics were from Europe, except for two from North and one from South America.

## Phase 1: Brainstorming

As part of the brainstorming phase, panelists were provided with an explanation about the purpose of our study, the subject under investigation, the individual steps of the Delphi process, as well as the requirements for participation in the study. During the brainstorming phase, a total of two questionnaires were sent to each subpanel. Prior to sending out the first questionnaire, a validation was conducted with a prepanel consisting of seven relevant participants (five academics and two practitioners) to test the length, precision, and overall adequacy of the questionnaire. As a result of the pretest, the first questionnaire was shortened and questions simplified.

## Questionnaire 1:

The first questionnaire (Q1) asked the panelists two open-ended questions:

(1) “What is your understanding of the term ‘technology pivot’? Using examples from your own experience, what would you describe as the main characteristics of technology pivots? Please give as much detail as necessary for readers who are not familiar with technology pivots.”

(2) “What is your expertise with regards to the Lean Startup methodology? Please briefly outline (in 1-2 sentences) how and where you were able to apply, coach, teach or observe the Lean Startup methodology.”

The first question corresponds directly to the research question, the second was used to ensure and validate their suitability for the study. We considered this especially important for practitioners as a validated judgment of their knowledge about the LSA was in some cases difficult for us to assess. To validate our assumption about the suitability of each participant for the purpose of our study, we first analyzed the answers to question (2) in all the questionnaires (n=38) by checking the description of experts’ experiences with the LSA against our criteria (described above). The received answers included the following quotes: “I have been using Lean Startup methodologies since 2011 as a startup owner” (founder), “I accompany a large number of startup teams who often act according to the Lean Startup principles” (startup coach), “Many of our portfolio companies take advantage of the Lean Startup methodology” (venture capitalist), “Lean Startup approach is one of my research interests since 2014” (academic), or “I am regularly teaching courses on digital entrepreneurship, business modeling, and digital strategy where I present the Lean Startup approach to students and where they also then apply it for their own projects” (academic). Fortunately, no participant had to be excluded from the panel as part of this step. We then read through all the answers to question (1) to check that all panelists provided an answer on their understanding of technology pivots. Fortunately, all participants provided answers that indicated a reasonable understanding of technology pivots, and thus, no participant had to be excluded from the panel as part of this step.

After these validity checks, we started with the analysis of the answers for question (1). In a first step, using the qualitative data analysis software Atlas.ti 8, the replies to the first question were analyzed by one author to identify key characteristics and elements conceptualizing technology pivots. For this, we highlighted response passages that contained descriptions about characteristics and elements of technology pivots and assigned inductively developed codes to these, while sticking closely to the words of the interviewees (Brady, 2015). Each reply was coded individually through this approach and then crosschecked with the other responses for refinement. An initial set of 225 codes were created. Similar codes were then color-coded and duplicate codes removed. This helped with grouping them together and identifying the most commonly occurring factors of technology pivots. In addition, we created, a brief, one-sentence explanation for each factor that summarized its meaning. This list was then discussed among the authors and refined. Furthermore, we documented “the consolidated list [given by] the number of panelists that initially suggested each factor” (Okoli and Pawlowski, 2004, p. 25).

Then, as part of our qualitative data analysis, we discussed how these factors could conceptually be grouped based on their content. We discussed inductive groupings based on semantic similarities of the factors as well as groupings based on a time dimension (i.e., “antecedents of technology pivots,” “nature of technology pivots,” “consequences of technology pivots” and “examples of technology pivots”), which was previously used in literature (cf. Section 2.4). We found that for the aim of our study and in consideration of the factors that have emerged from our study, the most suitable assignment would be based on the time dimension. This led us to assign the factors into four categories. As suggested by Crosby et al. (1990) and Van Wijk et al. (2008), it would be easier for panelists completing the next questionnaire to understand the identified factors when they are grouped together. Accordingly, each factor was assigned to one of four categories (i.e., (1) antecedents, (2) nature, (3) consequences, and (4) examples). The first (antecedents) includes factors that describe the type of event or situation that leads up to a technology pivot (e.g., “Scalability – Technology pivots are triggered by the need for better scalability”). The second category (nature) describes the essential parts of what technology pivots represent (e.g., “Fundamental Technology Change – Technology pivots are fundamental changes to the technology in use”). The third category (consequences) includes factors that describe what results from technology pivots (e.g., “Unique Selling Point – Technology pivots result in changes to the unique selling points”). Finally, the fourth category (examples), entails real world examples of technology pivots (e.g., “Application Programming Interface (API) – Technology pivots involve, for example, changes of used APIs”). A good illustration of this analysis is provided in the form of a reply of one participant in Appendix C. Afterwards, in a one-hour workshop (plus individual preparation time), the grouping and final list were validated through three independent entrepreneurship researchers, who each gave feedback on the extracted factors and their grouping. The feedback of these researchers (e.g., on the assignment of factors and explanatory sentences – among others) was then integrated and a final consolidated list of factors emerged (cf. Appendix D).

## Questionnaire 2:

The second questionnaire (Q2) served as a validation of the consolidated list of extracted factors and their individual grouping as created by the authors of this study. Each study participant was asked to validate the factors extracted from their answers and the respective assignment into groups for each factor (Fletcher and Marchildon, 2014). For this, they were provided with their original answers, the consolidated list containing all factors and short descriptions, as well as their assignment to individua groups. Five panelists provided feedback on the extracted factors and their descriptions, or provided new factors, which had not previously been considered. Following the retrieval of the participants’ feedback, one author checked the replies for suggested improvements and integrated them with a cross-check by the second author. This led to changes in the naming and description of individual factors, as well as the addition of two more factors. The result of this validation was a refined consolidated list of extracted factors and grouping. With panelists approving the consolidated list, we can be assured that the inductively derived analysis results adequately represent their thoughts (Paré et al., 2013). It is therefore essential to perform this step, as otherwise “there is no basis [for the] claim that a valid, consolidated lis has been produced” (Schmidt, 1997, p. 769).

## Phase 2: Narrowing down

The goal of phase 2 is to narrow down the number of factors on the consolidated list to extract the most important ones through a questionnaire. We treated the panelists as two distinct subpanels, similarly to Nakatsu and Iacovou (2009), starting with phase 2, i.e., (1) academics (n=19) and (2) practitioners (consisting of venture capitalists, startup founders, and startup coaches; n=19). The aim of this split was to allow each subpanel which, because of their personal expertise and experiences, should think differently from the other subpanel, to independently decide, which factors are the most important for them and consequently rank these. We considered this worthwhile as the academic subpanel engages with the LSA through researching and teaching, while the practitioners’ subpanel regularly coached startups on the LSA, or performed it. Reconciling such different areas of expertise and perspectives in a single panel can be very difficult (Okoli and Pawlowski, 2004).

## Questionnaire 3:

The third questionnaire (Q3) provided panelists with the revised consolidated list, consisting of 83 factors, and asked them to:

(1) “Please select at least 10 factors in total from the categories below that you consider the most important to answer the following question: "What is a technology pivot"? Each factor represents a statement to explain what technology pivots are. You can, but do not have to, select factors from every category.”

The ordering of factors was randomized to avoid any selection bias.

In a first step following the retrieval of all replies (90% participation rate; n=34 split into academics (16) and practitioners (18)), two lists of factors were created consisting of a reduced set of only the most important ones. Four participants did not participate in this questionnaire. In total, 595 votes for factors were provided (median per participant = 15). We aimed to reach a target size of factors for each subpanel of 20-23 items. This reduction of factors is important as it is difficult for the panelists to comprehend and manage the ranking of a long list of factors, resulting in less meaningful results, as described by Okoli and Pawlowski (2004). We kept those factors that were chosen by over 31% (academics) and 32% (practitioners) of the participants in each panel, resulting in two shortened lists, one for the academic panel of 22 factors and one of 19 factors for the practitioner panel. The literature does not prescribe any exact rate for reducing the factors, ranging between 30% (e.g., Piccinini (2015)), 50% (e.g., Okoli and Pawlowski (2004)), and 70% (e.g., Singh et al. (2009)). We found that 31% and 32% result in a more manageable number of factors, while considering the selection frequency of individual factors during the selection process. The difference in the length of the lists results from equally frequent nominations of individual factors, i.e., academics nominated each of the factors 18-22 five times and the practitioners nominated each of the factors 13-19 six times, not allowing us to cut the lists to the same length (cf. Appendix E).

#

## Phase 3: Ranking

The goal of phase 3 is to reach consensus through ranking of the factors within each subpanel based on the narrowed-down lists of factors created as a result of phase 2. For this, two iterations were needed, using the same questionnaire.

## Questionnaire 4:

For the ranking, the fourth questionnaire (Q4) provides the shortened list of factors based on the results of phase 2 and poses the following question:

(1) “Please rank the following factors according to their importance for answering the question: "What is a technology pivot"? Please arrange the factors in the list below, starting with your highestranking factor, moving through to your lowest ranking factor.”

The ordering of the factors in Q4 was again randomized to avoid any selection bias. Following retrieval of all replies (84% participation rate; n=32 split into academics (16) and practitioners (16)), we now obtained two ranked lists. These represent the priorities that each panel placed on the different factors in terms of their relevance of explaining what a technology pivot is. For each ranked list, Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was calculated as suggested by Okoli and Pawlowski (2004) to analyze the extent of the consensus reached among the panelist groups. The value of W ranges from 0 to 1. When W is greater than .7, it indicates strong consensus, a value between .5 and .7 indicates a moderate consensus, and a value below .5 indicates a weak consensus among the panelists (Schmidt, 1997). For the academic panel, the calculated Kendall’s W was .16, for the practitioner panel it was .21, indicating that neither subpanels reached a moderate consensus through the first ranking. Following Okoli and Pawlowski (2004), we decided to continue the ranking process until either: (1) a moderate consensus among the panelists was reached in both panels, or (2) the degree of consensus for the subpanel levelled off in two successive rounds, or (3) panelists indicate that they are unwilling to participate in further iterations.

## Questionnaire 5:

Consequently, we conducted a second iteration of ranking for which we used a fifth questionnaire (Q5), which is a duplicate of Q4, where we once again asked the panelists to review the factors and provide a ranked order. To control feedback, following Singh, Keil, and Kasi (2009), we provided the following information to each panelist: (1) the absolute and average group rank of each factor, (2) the ranking by that panelist for each factor in the first round of ranking, and (3) the panelist’s deviation from the group’s absolute and mean ranking for each factor. This gave each panelist the opportunity to compare and adjust their own ranking in light of the group’s ranking.

After all replies for this second iteration of ranking had been retrieved (76% participation rate; n=29 split into academics (14) and practitioners (15)), we recalculated Kendall’s W. This time it yielded .31 among the academic subpanel and .55 among the practitioner subpanel, which suggested that a weak level of

consensus remained among academics and a moderate level of consensus had been reached for practitioners. The differences in improvement for reaching consensus can be explained by – among others – the fact that some academics (n=4) stated that their opinion remains the same independently of the opinions of other panelists (e.g., “I stand by this ranking based on my experience”).

At this point, we discussed whether conducting another round of ranking to obtain a greater level of consensus among the panelists was desirable. We considered the trade-off between feasibility (participation rates, participants’ willingness, and our own resources) and the potential gain to be achieved (Schmidt, 1997). Considering the comments we had received by academics not willing to reconsider their rankings and comments received by practitioners (e.g., “My time does not allow me to participate in another round”), we had the impression that after sending out five questionnaires in total, a certain tiredness had set in among our panelists (reflected in the increased drop out of 24% of the panelists since the start of the study) and the number of reminders required to get them to respond. As the second round of ranking yielded a moderate consensus for the practitioners and a weak consensus for the academics, we decided against a third round. Thus, following Schmidt (1997, p. 771) who pointed out that “for panels of more than 10 experts, even very small values of W can be significant,” we were satisfied with the results. To confirm this decision, we conducted a robustness check for our obtained results, based on a twofold sensitivity analysis. First, we dropped each of the factors, one at a time, and recalculated Kendall’s W for both lists individually (Singh et al., 2009). The consensus levels of either subpanel did not change considerably (W ranged between 0.25 and 0.33 for academics and 0.51 and 0.58 for practitioners). Second, we then removed each of the panelists from the subpanels, one at a time, and recalculated Kendall’s W for both lists individually. Again, the consensus levels of the subpanels did not change considerably (W ranged between 0.29 and 0.36 for academics and 0.53 and 0.62 for practitioners). This confirmed us in our decision to not conduct another ranking round, as neither individual factors nor individual panel members would have influenced the results of our study significantly.

## 5. Discussion

Digital startups develop novel technology products and services under conditions of absolute uncertainty (Packard et al., 2017). If successful, they can represent a serious challenge to established market players or revolutionize slow-moving markets (Nambisan, 2017; Ojala, 2016). However, many of those who do succeed reach their goal only after having performed one or more technology pivots (e.g., Twitter (Aniszczyk, 2013)). On the other hand, many digital startups pivot either too late or for the wrong reasons, which can hinder their endeavors and even lead to their demise (Terho et al., 2015; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016). With the term “technology pivot” being insufficiently conceptualized in the existing literature, we lack a foundation for further research on the subject, which would enhance the understanding of how digital startups deal with technology pivots. While existing studies (cf. Table 1) focused on the identification of new and the validation of existing antecedents and consequences of technology pivots, they have not offered a comprehensive and precise conceptualization of the term. With the results of this study, we significantly contribute to closing this gap.

## 5.1 Conceptualizing Technology Pivot

Based on the ranked lists of factors by the academic subpanel, the following conceptualization for technology pivots was formulated. It starts by mentioning factors that describe the “nature” of technology pivots, then it moves on to factors, which either precede (antecedents) or follow on from their performance (consequences) and finishes with some “examples.” They appear in order of their ranking:

Technology pivots are fundamental changes to the technology in use that are of critical and strategic importance to the business, consisting of either substitutions of technologies with established technologies or the implementation of technology innovations, as well as of ITarchitecture design changes. They are caused by validated learnings created to test crucial business hypotheses, a change in the business model, or a business goal being performed, customer feedback received, scalability desired, changes in the competitive landscape, or a start-up’s own market position. A technology pivot may result in changes to the business model, the value creating technologies and activities, or the value proposition. Typical examples of technology pivots include usages of cross-platform engines, changes from a monolith to a microservice-based architecture, switches from on-premise hosting to cloudbased solutions, switches from closed source to open source and vice versa, API changes or switches in programming languages.

Similarly, referring to the ranked list of factors by the practitioner subpanel, the following conceptualization for technology pivots was created:

Technology pivots are fundamental changes to the technology in use, which includes the substitution of individual technologies, as well as the introduction of new technology

concepts. As such, technology pivots are critical for the business, as they allow to integrate innovations and realize changes to IT-architecture designs. Through this, changing capability requirements may occur aligned to the new technology. They are caused by creating validated learnings through testing of crucial business hypotheses, changes in the own market position, technological ecosystems, aim of scalability, or customer feedback received. A technology pivot can then result in changes to the business model especially its value proposition. For this, new software features can be realized and the product quality enhanced. Typical examples of technology pivots include changes from a monolith to a microservice-based architecture, switches in programming language or the implementation of third-party solutions.

First, we found that “N3. Fundamental Technology Change,” “N4. IT-Architecture Design,” “N5. New Technology Concept” and “N6. Technology Substitution” are important factors of technology pivots, which Ries (2011, p. 172) anecdotally described as “using a completely different technology.” Similarly, we found that increasing “A7. Scalability” as part of what Ries (2011, p. 172) described as “superior performance” is reflected by technology pivots, following the conceptualizations of our panelists.

Second, we also found considerably contrasting factors to Ries’ (2011) initial conceptualization. While Ries (2011, p.172) describes that a technology pivot is “sustaining innovation” and an “incremental improvement,” both of our subpanels agree that a technology pivot rather represents a “N1. Business Critical” change that includes the integration of “N7. Technology Innovation.” Furthermore, while Ries (2011) describes that after a technology pivot, the “customer segment is the same, the customer’s problem is the same, the value-capture model is the same, and the channel partners are the same,” both subpanels in our study see a strong impact of technology pivots on the business model (cf. “C1. Business Model Changed” and “C4. Value Creation Changed”) and in particular the value proposition (cf. “C5. Value Proposition Changed”) of digital startups. Especially our experts’ finding that technology pivots have a considerable influence on the value proposition stands in stark contrast to Ries (2011). One of the panelists even described that “technological changes that are not directly linked to the value proposition of the business model cannot be considered to be technological pivots.” Thus, the extent of the technological change performed during a technology pivot and its impact is perceived to be considerably larger by our panelists than by Ries (2011, p. 172), who also stated that “so much is not changing.” Moreover, Ries’ conceptualization highlighted that “superior price” would be an important driver for technology pivots (Ries, 2011, p. 172). However, the factor “cost reduction” (cf. Appendix D) did not feature among the final set of selected factors in either subpanel and, thus, is much less important than in the initial conceptualization. Instead, according to our two subpanels, a number of other antecedents can trigger the performance of technology pivots.

Third, for several aspects described by Ries (2011), none of our conceptualizations either support or oppose them. Ries (2011) describes that technology pivots are “much more common in established businesses,” yet neither of our panels’ conceptualizations explicitly referred to established businesses. Instead, many of our panelists explicitly highlighted that they occur in startups (e.g., “In my understanding, a technology pivot is a change in the core technology of a startup”). Concerning the impact of technology pivots on the targeted customer segment, our panelists did not state that technology pivots are “designed to appeal to and retain an existing customer base,” only that the business model may well change as a result of a technology pivot, which in turn may or may not include changes to the targeted customer segment. However, Ries (2011) considered changes to the target customer segment to represent a separate type of pivot, i.e., customer segment pivot.

Fourth, our results further identified several factors that were absent in Ries’ (2011) conceptualization. This includes, for example, that technology pivots can entail “N2. Changing Capability Requirements” with regard to the technical skills and capabilities of employees. Further, we found that the external environment of digital startups can influence the necessity for technology pivots, especially with regard to changes in the “A3. Competitive Landscape” or the technological “A5. Ecosystem.” Similarly, an improvement of the startup’s “A6. Market Position” through technology pivots can be desired.

In sum, we found that the contemporary understanding of technology pivots is broader in scope than previously conceptualized by Ries (2011). We were able to confirm certain aspects of the initial conceptualization, while adding new findings that were previously absent, and highlighting several contrasting findings. The identified conceptualizations of both subpanels show several aspects that oppose Ries (2011). This underlines the relevance of our study in which we scientifically derived a conceptualization of the term “technology pivot” based on its contemporary understanding.

## 5.4 Technology Pivots and Related Technology Pivot Literature

When comparing the conceptualizations of our two subpanels (cf. Table 3) to existing academic studies on technology pivots (cf. Table 1), it can be seen that our findings about technology pivots confirm existing findings and extend the results of the existing literature.

First, existing literature has so far focused mainly on the identification of antecedents and consequences of technology pivots (cf. Table 1). While our findings also contain antecedents and consequences, it can be observed that those described in the existing literature are located on a more detailed level. For example, (blinded for review) identified very specific technical antecedents such as “increased system performance,” “increased architectural future viability,” and “increased system maintainability.” The antecedents described by our experts describe antecedents for technology pivots on a higher level of abstraction, such as the generation of “‘A8. Validated learnings,” changes in technological “A5. Ecosystems,” or the need to improve the startup’s own “A6. Market Position.” This includes digital startups reacting to changes in the “A3. Competitive Landscape.” Furthermore, we found that technology pivots help to resolve “A4. Customer Feedback,” which is in line with (blinded for review). We also Business Goal” being changed or adapted.

Second, with regard to the nature of technology pivots, we were able to identify that technology pivots are “N1. Business Critical” and “N3. Fundamental Technology Change[s]” including “N6. Technology Substitution[s],” the implementation of “N7. Technological Innovation,” as well as the application of new “N4. IT Architecture Design[s]” or “N5. New Technology Concept[s].” These findings refine the contributions by (blinded for review), who described that technology pivots are “structured technological course corrections that allow the introduction of significant technical improvements for an existing offering as well as the introduction of IT-innovations.”

Third, the relation between value propositions, business models, and technology pivots had previously been indicated by (blinded for review) and was extended upon by our results. They described that technology pivots help to “distinctly adapt and enhance the value created of products and services.” We found that extensive “C1. Business Model Change[s]” can result from technology pivots. This can include that subsequent to technology pivots the “C4. Value Creation Changed” and “C5. Value Proposition Changed.” Furthermore, we identified that new “C3. Software Features” can become viable and the “C2. Product Quality” can improve considerably as a result of technology pivots.

Fourth, the examples for technology pivots identified by our study, such as changes in “E6. Programming Language” or switching from “E5. On-premise to Cloud” architecture, provide a reference for future studies with regard to the extent and scope of technological changes that technology pivots represent for digital startups. These examples overlap with those described by (blinded for review) and Bajwa et al. (2017). Nonetheless, new examples such as “E1. API changes” were also discovered.

In sum, comparing our results with the existing literature, we were able to considerably extend the understanding of the nature of technology pivots and confirm existing findings, as well as contribute new characteristics of technological changes that technology pivots represent in digital startups.

While the case of Easy Learning is close to the existing conceptualization of Ries (2011) i.e., “to achieve the same solution by using a completely different technology,” the cases of Wix and Android cannot be explained by Ries (2011), and require a new conceptualization.

## 6. Conclusion

Involving an extensive expert panel consisting of 38 participants equally split into academics and practitioners, we perform research on one specific type of pivot – technology pivots – to clarify its conceptualization. Our study is the first to conduct an explorative study on technology pivots, which entirely focuses on their meaning. Eliciting the judgments of a panel of experts, we systematically identify factors that conceptualize technology pivots. Using the Delphi method, we were able to achieve strong content validity (as the content is generated directly by the panelists) and reliability (through a group of experts, on which we provide demographical and professional details) (Goodman, 1987; Schmidt, 1997). Our study generated two lists of ranked factors, each selected and ranked individually by the academic subpanel (19 factors) and the practitioner subpanel (22 factors), respectively. We also identified the areas of concordance and discordance between the academic and practitioner panel, which provides the basis for further research on the subject. The interpretations drawn from these results are the most comprehensive conceptualization of the term “technology pivot” available so far – to the best of our knowledge – adding new insights on the subject and a more fine-grained understanding. We summarize our contribution by presenting the conceptualization of each subpanel, comparing them to Ries’ (2011) initial conceptualization of technology pivots, and to the existing literature.

As a result of this new conceptualization of technology pivots, we can solidify technology pivots as an important measure for technology course correction in digital startups and as a distinct phenomenon, which stands in contrast to traditional incremental technology adoption in established businesses (cf. Section 2.5; Napier et al., 2011; Vinekar et al., 2006). Our findings describe technology pivots as a measure that is critical for undertaking fundamental changes to the technology in use for the specific context of digital startups, which are characterized by uncertainty, scarcity of resources, and intense time pressure. Therefore, we encourage further research on this new phenomenon.

## 6.1 Implications

Our study provides an important next step toward extending knowledge about technology pivots. It analyzes the perspective of academics and practitioners on technology pivots through two ranked lists of factors that are central to explaining the concept. Our study has implications for both research and practice. First, it fills a research gap in the context of digital startups applying the LSA (Eisenmann et al., 2011; Frederiksen and Brem, 2017; Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2016) and digital entrepreneurship in general (Ojala, 2016; Steininger, 2019). Our study results contribute to the explanatory level with regard to

theorizing about technology pivots (Gregor, 2016). This paves the way to further explanatory and predictive research on the subject, which will enable startups to perform technology pivots more effectively, and at the right time. Second, it enables startup founders and other practitioners (e.g., startup coaches and venture capital investors) to better understand what technology pivots are and what they entail. Neither startup founders nor their advisors and financial supporters can afford to rely on ambiguity and wrong evaluations of the term technology pivot during decision-making and its performance.

## 6.2 Limitations and Future Work

Our study is not without limitations. Our results should be interpreted in the context of the limitations regarding their generalizability. First, our study results in an overall limited degree of consensus. It shows a Kendall’s W of .31 for the academic subpanel and .55 for the practitioner subpanel, respectively. While this level compares to other Delphi studies reported in the IS literature (Kasi et al., 2008; Schmidt, 1997; Singh et al., 2009), it is still considered a weak and moderate consensus, respectively. While an additional iteration of the ranking might have increased the level of consensus among our panelists, we decided to stop after the second iteration because we recognized that some academic panel members stood by their rankings, independently of the rankings of their peers, and others showed notions of participation fatigue with the fifth questionnaire, as reflected by the increased number of e-mail reminders that had to be sent out during the final round. Also, our performed robustness checks confirmed this decision. Second, based on our panel composition, we can currently only refer to our understanding of technology pivots in the context of digital startups but cannot make statements about other types of startups, such as IT-mediated or IT-facilitated startups (Steininger, 2019), nor about established businesses.

Despite the limitations described, we believe that our study not only contributes to both research and practice, but also provides promising avenues for future research. First, further research could focus on analyzing the steps involved in the effective performance of a technology pivot, supporting both startup founders and coaches in this endeavor. Second, future research could analyze the relation between design choices (i.e., architecture, components, and interfaces and their interrelations) and technology pivot occurrence. Third, future research could further explore the differences of the understanding of technology pivots between academics and practitioners.
