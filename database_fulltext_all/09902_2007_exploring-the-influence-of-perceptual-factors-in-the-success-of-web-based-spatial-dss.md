# Exploring the influence of perceptual factors in the success of web-based spatial DSS

Suprasith Jarupathirun <sup>1</sup>, Fatemeh <sup>b</sup>Mariam<sup>Q</sup> Zahedi<sup>\*</sup>

University of Wisconsin Milwaukee, School of Business Administration, P. O. Box 742, Milwaukee, WI 53201, United States

Available online 11 July 2005

An earlier version of this paper was presented at the Pre-ICIS SIG DSS Workshop in December 2003 in Seattle.

## Abstract

Increasing reliance on the web for decision-making combined with higher demand on technologies that can efficiently deal with large volumes of data make visualization an important decision-making tool. Spatial decision support systems (SDSS) using the latest advances in geographic information systems (GIS) could be the appropriate approach in making DSS available to mass web-users for making decisions that have spatial components. Hence, it is important to explore the factors that impact perceived successful use of web-based SDSS. In this paper, we synthesize task–technology, goal setting, and self-efficacy theories in developing a conceptual model and the subsequent empirical study for exploring the perceptual factors impacting the perceived performance of web-based SDSS. © 2005 E1sevier B V. A11 rights

Keywords: Decision support systems; Visualization; Spatial orientation; Task–technology fit; Goal setting; Self-efficacy; Web-based SDSS

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid increase in computational resources and increased reliance on visualization in decision analysis, decision support systems (DSS) with spatial capabilities (spatial decision support systems or SDSS) have gained in popularity. The advances in geographic information systems (GIS) technology and its great potential for use on the web make it a natural candidate for creating web-based SDSS. In the context of this research, we refer to DSS systems using GIS for spatial decisions as SDSS (while recognizing that other technologies could potentially be used in creating such systems). In this paper, we define SDSS as systems designed to aid decision-making for semistructured problems that have spatial or location components [29]. Unlike DSS, SDSS also include the functionalities to visualize and analyze spatial data such as detecting spatial patterns or evaluating network structures.

As the Internet becomes the primary source of information, increasingly a larger number of webusers turn to the web to find support in their decisionmaking processes. At the same time, the explosion of data and information demands more efficient technologies for processing the ever-increasing volume of information [54]. Visualization techniques that can process and summarize high-volume or complex data and information are rising in popularity [54,69]. This trend, combined with the observation that more than 80% of business data has some spatial characteristics [1], makes GIS one of the most appropriate technologies to deal with high volume or complex data in decision making. There are now many web vendors that offer interactive maps for various decision tasks on the web. For example, Expedia.com provides interactive maps that help users find hotels close to their favorite attractions, Realtor.com offers map functions for helping home buyers find houses that match their decision criteria, and Visa.com provides credit card holders with interactive maps for locating the nearest ATM. The next natural step is to provide more comprehensive decision supports by incorporating additional GIS functionalities in DSS for dealing with the complex and multi-dimensional nature of spatial information.

Since the technology for putting GIS-based SDSS on the web is already in place, it is important to explore the critical success factors in the use of web-based SDSS by web-customers with varied backgrounds. Davis and Kottemann [12] report that decision-makers’ perceptions of DSS effectiveness influence the use of systems. Decision-makers continue using inferior systems because they perceive the systems to be most effective even though the systems do not have any effects on the performance. In the online environment, web-customers’ perceptions of a website’s quality and performance are important factors in forming their beliefs, which can influence their subsequent behaviors, such as buying from the online vendor [34,48]. We argue that the use of web-based SDSS has a similar nature in that web-customers’ favorable perceptions regarding the performance of SDSS systems are critical to their use of such systems. Hence, we formulate the main research question in this study as the identification of perceptual factors that influence users’ evaluation of SDSS success. The answer to this question could be of value to companies that offer web-based SDSS services as a part of their e-commerce activities.

Several studies have evaluated the GIS technology and found it to impact decision performance [10,15,43,58]. In addition, Dennis and Carte [15] report that decision-makers using GIS map representation make faster but less accurate decisions when tasks do not require analyzing the relationships among geographic areas.

Although these studies report on various facets of GIS technology, there has been little effort to investigate the impact of perceptual constructs on decisionmakers’ subjective evaluations of using the technology. The use of SDSS hinges greatly on the users’ perceptions about the nature and quality of decision outcomes and processes, which are not necessarily related to objective measurements, such as speed or accuracy of results. In other words, we argue that the success of SDSS in being used by the mass audience on the web depends to a large extent on their perceptual constructs. More specifically, in this research, we attempt to conceptualize a theoretical model for investigating the success of SDSS in terms of user satisfaction, perceived decision quality, and perceived efficiency. In this model, we posit that users’ perceptions of SDSS success depend on their perceived task–technology fit and goal commitment, which in turn are influenced by personal spatial aptitude, selfefficacy, and outcome expectation. In doing so, we synthesize task–technology fit, self-efficacy, and DSS cost–benefit theories in IS with theories in psychology (spatial ability) and organizational behavior (goal-setting theory) to formulate the conceptual model for evaluating SDSS.

## 2. Theoretical perspective

## 2.1. Task–technology fit

We posit that the successful use of SDSS, particularly web-based SDSS, depends on users’ perceived task–technology fit. We base our argument on the fit theory, which has been used in a number of fields, particularly in strategic management for strategy-context fit [65] as well as in IS for task–technology fit (TTF). Goodhue and Thompson [23] have developed the <sup>b</sup>technology-to-performance chain<sup>Q</sup> model, in which technology performance depends on technology utilization and the fit between the technology and the tasks it supports. Moreover, based on Venkatraman’s work, Zigurs and Buckland [71] have proposed a model for TTF in GDSS effectiveness. They report that group performance depends on the fit between task categories and technology dimensions. Todd and Benbasat [62] use the TTF theory in formulating their model for exploring the choice of decision-making strategy; and Zigurs et al. [72] apply it to examine the link between TTF in GSS and group performance.

According to the TTF theory, systems will help improve users’ performances when the technology is <sup>b</sup>a good fit with the tasks it supports<sup>Q</sup> [23, p. 213]. The TTF theory posits that using technology for accomplishing complex tasks requires expending effort, and as the degree of TTF diminishes, more effort is required to perform the task. A high degree of TTF is found to have a positive effect on individual performance, and a low degree of TTF has the converse effect [17,23]. TTF, in this study, is based on the fit as profile, which is similar to the TTF used by Zigurs and Buckland [71] and Zigurs et al. [72], who investigated the effectiveness of each tool in GDSS for a particular task. We suggest that advanced tools of web-based SDSS are appropriate (fit) for a more complex task, while a simple task can be effectively/efficiently performed using basic tools of webbased SDSS, such as a map representation.

Furthermore, since individuals have different abilities and cognitive capacities, the perception of fit between task and technology varies from one person to another. This may be even stronger for web-based SDSS users, who have a wide variety of backgrounds, perceptions, and capabilities. Moreover, the GIS technology has yet to become a commonplace fixture on computer interfaces and requires more than basic computer skills, causing the perception of TTF to vary with SDSS users’ abilities and skills in utilizing the technology. This argument is in line with Vessey and Galletta’s observation [67] that the cognitive fit depends, among other things, on the decision-maker’s mental model of the decision process. Furthermore, Goodhue and Thompson [23] focus on users’ evaluations of the TTF and operationalize the TTF construct as the perception of individuals about TTF. Following their conceptualization, we posit that perceived TTF influences users’ perceptions of SDSS performance, as shown in Fig. 1.

Marcolin et al. [40] take Goodhue and Thompson’s point of view a step further and argue that it is the individual’s characteristics that impact the perception of TTF, and not necessarily the characteristics of technology or the level of task complexity. In the case of SDSS, the novelty and relative unfamiliarity of the GIS technology to the web audience make webusers’ abilities in using SDSS an influential factor in forming their perception of TTF. In determining the relevant personal abilities that may influence perceived TTF, we follow the suggestions by Marcolin et al. [40] and draw a theoretical distinction between users’ natural aptitudes and their self-efficacy in using SDSS. More specifically, we distinguish between selfefficacy in using SDSS to make a decision and users’ spatial abilities.

Fig. 1. The conceptual model.

SDSS-related personal abilities are spatial aptitudes necessary for each individual to effectively and efficiently solve everyday spatial tasks [9,21]. There are several dimensions of spatial ability, but <sup>b</sup>visualization<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>spatial orientation<sup>Q</sup> are viewed as the most dominant dimensions of spatial aptitudes [9,21], and are the ones we employ in this research. We posit that decisionmakers’ spatial abilities in terms of visualization and spatial orientation positively impact their perceived TTF (Fig. 1).

Although, according to Bandura [3], self-efficacy can either overstate or understate abilities that individuals actually possess, it has been shown to be a good indicator for individuals’ perceived abilities [40,53]. In the context of SDSS, self-efficacy in using SDSS reflects users’ evaluation of their own abilities in successfully utilizing the SDSS. Venkatesh and Davis [63] report that self-efficacy has a significant influence on perceived ease of use. Since an increase in perceived ease of use contributes to the positive perception about TTF [23], we posit that self-efficacy influences TTF.

Hence, the hypotheses regarding personal spatial aptitudes and self-efficacy are:

H1a. SDSS users’ perceived TTF is positively influenced by their high visualization abilities.

H1b. SDSS users’ perceived TTF is positively influenced by their high spatial orientation abilities.

H1c. SDSS users’ perceived TTF is positively influenced by their high self-efficacy in using SDSS.

## 2.2. Goal commitment

In goal-setting research, goal is a major factor in motivating people to spend effort in accomplishing tasks [31,35,38]. Individuals’ goals influence their levels of performance. However, for a goal to have any impact on an individual’s performance, the individual needs to commit to it [26,27], making goal commitment an important factor in performance.

In goal-setting theory, Hollenbeck and Klein [26] have identified two antecedents of goal commitment: <sup>b</sup>attractiveness of goal attainment<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>expectancy of goal attainment.<sup>Q</sup> Applied to the use of SDSS, we identify the dimensions of goal attractiveness as <sup>b</sup>expected decision quality<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>expected decision efficiency.<sup>Q</sup> In the cost–benefit theory in DSS [62], goal attractiveness constitutes the perceived benefits of using SDSS.

We also identify the perceived level of goal difficulty to indicate the expectancy of goal attainment. Hollenbeck and Klein [26] argue that people may fail to commit if they perceive the goal to be to difficult, hence unrealistic. This point is further supported by the observation of Locke et al. [38] that <sup>b</sup>difficult goals generally are accepted to a lesser degree than easy goals<sup>Q</sup> (p. 27). Furthermore, in a study by Taylor et al. [61], it is found that individuals with easy goal assignments report a higher degree of commitment than those with more difficult goals. Further support is also provided by the theory of goal-energy-effort requirements for performance motivation proposed by Wofford [68]. In this theory, goal commitment is negatively impacted by required effort, which in our study is captured by the level of goal difficulty.

Moreover, in the parlance of cost–benefit theory in DSS [62], the perceived level of goal difficulty is the expected expenditure of using SDSS by decisionmakers. Since our study focuses on examining the use of technology, we argue that the expected cost and benefits constitute the situational factors motivating the goal commitment in using SDSS for decision making. Furthermore, expectation has been shown to influence the perception of performance and subsequent satisfaction in websites [41]. In our model, expectations of cost and benefits fall into three categories: (i) expectations related to efforts spent in using the SDSS in the form of perceived goal difficulty; (ii) expectations related to the increased quality of decision; and (iii) expectations about the efficiency of the decision process. The expectation of benefits as a result of using SDSS would enforce commitment to the goal, whereas the cost expectation of spending more effort would lower commitment to attaining the decision goal using SDSS.

H2a. SDSS users’ goal commitment is negatively influenced by their perceived high level of goal difficulty.

H2b. SDSS users’ goal commitment is positively influenced by their high expected decision quality.

H2c. SDSS users’ goal commitment is positively influenced by their high expected decision efficiency.

## 2.3. Self-efficacy

In the social cognitive theory, the evaluation of self-efficacy in successfully accomplishing a task is found to influence an individual’s outcome expectation [6–8]. Johnson and Marakas [30] propose that computer self-efficacy impacts post test outcome expectancy mediated by performance. In our model, expected decision quality constitutes outcome expectation, and, according to the self-efficacy theory, should be impacted by self-efficacy. We draw a parallel argument for process expectation and argue that self-efficacy has a similar impact on the expected process in terms of decision efficiency.

Self-efficacy has also been found to impact perception of goal difficulty. In their comprehensive review of the self-efficacy literature, Marakas et al. [39] report on a number of studies that have found strong association between self-efficacy and goal-related factors. Locke et al. [37] argue for the association of self-efficacy and goal difficulty and, in a later study, observe that self-efficacy is related to expectancy of success [38]. Furthermore, Hollenbeck and Klein [26] report that people with high self-efficacy would view goals as easier than those with low self-efficacy. Following these findings, we posit that self-efficacy is an important antecedent for the perception of goal difficulty and the expectations about decision quality and decision efficiency:

H2d. SDSS users’ perceived level of goal difficulty is negatively influenced by their self-efficacy.

H2e. SDSS users’ expected decision quality is positively influenced by their self-efficacy.

H2f. SDSS users’ expected decision efficiency is positively influenced by their self-efficacy.

## 2.4. TTF and SDSS performance

In their empirical studies, Goodhue and Thompson [23] have found that TTF influences performance, a finding supported by Marcolin et al. [40]’s results that perceived TTF impacts users’ performance. In the context of DSS use, performance is conceptualized as <sup>b</sup>decision performance<sup>Q</sup> [62] or users’ performance [72]. In our research, we identify decision performance as the perceived success of using SDSS, as manifested in terms of users’ satisfaction with the decision and their satisfaction with SDSS technology. Furthermore, users’ evaluations of decision quality and efficiency reflect the extent of confirmation of the expected benefits (expected decision quality and expected decision efficiency) in using SDSS for decision making.

Satisfaction has been widely used as a measure for information systems success [13,14,70]. In evaluating the DeLone and McLean IS Success Model, Seddon [56] reports significant support for the relationships among factors in the model, which include the relationship between satisfaction and system utilization. According to DeLone and McLean [13], satisfaction is impacted by information quality and system quality, a result further supported by McKinney et al. [41]. Goodhue [22] identified quality of information, system reliability, and production timeliness as dimensions of TTF. In the context of SDSS, one may view perceived TTF as the appropriateness of information and system quality for supporting spatial decisions. A perceived mismatch between task and technology may lead users to believe the technology to be ineffective and hence inferior for the decision purpose at hand, causing dissatisfaction with the technology.

In this study, we differentiate between satisfaction and perceived benefits of using SDSS, because it is conceivable to be satisfied with the decision outcome or the SDSS technology while still believing that the decision quality is not high (not quite as accurate or error free as expected) or that the process is somewhat inefficient. In other words, the perceived benefits depend on the level of expectation as observed in the theory of expectation–confirmation [41], whereas satisfaction relates to positive feelings regarding the outcome and the SDSS technology. Therefore, the set of hypotheses related to the impact of TTF on SDSS success is as follows:

H3a. SDSS users’ perceived TTF positively impacts their perceived decision satisfaction.

H3b. SDSS users’ perceived TTF positively impacts their satisfaction with SDSS technology.

H3c. SDSS users’ perceived TTF positively impacts their perceived decision quality.

H3d. SDSS perceived TTF positively impacts their perceived decision efficiency.

## 2.5. Goal commitment and SDSS performance

Goal-setting theory postulates that stronger goal commitment increases the likelihood of higher performance. Goal commitment is theorized to be an indicator for attaining a goal [26,27], insufficient commitment to the goal may foster the perception of poor decision-making performance. Therefore, the last set of hypothesizes is as follows:

H4a. SDSS users’ perceived goal commitment positively impacts their decision satisfaction.

H4b. SDSS users’ perceived goal commitment positively impacts their satisfaction with SDSS technology.

H4c. SDSS users’ perceived goal commitment positively impacts their perceived decision quality.

H4d. SDSS users’ perceived goal commitment positively impacts their perceived decision efficiency.

## 3. Methodology and experimental design

The model as depicted in Fig. 1 and its related hypotheses were tested using laboratory experiment, which is an appropriate method to establish the cause and effect of constructs being examined [19]. The experimental design was full factorial, involving 2 sets of tasks, 2 levels of goals, and 2 levels of SDSS functionalities $( 2 \times 2 \times 2 )$ . The factorial design was intended to induce variability in perceptions of TTF and goal difficulty, not to control them. Due to individual differences in self-efficacy and spatial abilities, we believed that such control would not be possible. Hence no manipulation of expectations and perceptions were performed in this experiment.

Two weeks prior to the experiment, participants registered by supplying demographic information and finishing two spatial-abilities tests. About 30 students participated in each session. At the beginning of each session, task and goal descriptions and a brief manual for the SDSS prototype were distributed. The participants went through a 20-min training session prior to the start of experiment. All participants were required to finish a training task before they could proceed. After the training, participants completed questionnaires for self-efficacy, expected required effort, expected decision quality, and expected decision efficiency. Then participants used the SDSS prototype to perform their assigned tasks. After completing their tasks, participants completed post-experiment questionnaires.

## 3.1. Web-based SDSS

Using ArcView and ArcIMS software, we developed two web-based SDSS applications that have different levels of functionality. The applications interface was accessible through the web (Fig. 2).

User inputs and table results were displayed in the clipboard area, and maps were displayed in the middle of the screen. The first application provided a set of GIS functions, and the second application provided the same functionalities plus additional advanced tools.

## 3.2. Tasks

Vessey [66] classifies elementary tasks as spatial and symbolic. The tasks in this study were dominated by the spatial component. The decision tasks involved selecting an apartment or a house based on a set of decision criteria and were adapted from Todd and Benbasat [62]. Participants made their decisions based on symbolic attributes (such as price and size) as well as spatial attributes (distance and location). Each decision task involved 5 decision attributes. Hence, the multiplicity of alternatives, decision attributes, and objectives made the assigned tasks relatively complex [4,51,71]. This complexity was needed to justify spending effort in using the SDSS to perform decision tasks. However, the degree of complexity was changed by having two types of tasks: one type involved a pool of 17 candidate apartments and houses, and the second type involved selection from a pool of 45 candidate apartments and houses. Each participant was given three tasks from one type.

## 3.3. Goal difficulty

Goal difficulty in this research refers to the participants’ perception of the difficulty of their assigned goals. The experiments involved two goal levels. The first level required participants to do their best and try to finish at least one decision task, whereas the second level required them to finish all three decision tasks. In doing so, we followed the protocol suggested by Chesney and Locke [5], who varied goal levels by either asking participants to do their best or instructing them to complete the assigned tasks. Locke et al. [38] have found that individuals may reject (not commit to) goals set by the authority, and they might decide to set their own goals based on their personalities and moti vations for achievement. Hence, having two goal levels in this study was intended only to increase the variability in perceived goal difficulty, and participants were allowed to form their own perception about the difficulty of their assigned goal.

## 3.4. Participants

The participants in the experiments were undergraduate students in a business school at a large metropolitan Midwestern university. The GUV WWW Survey [25] characterizes web-users as highly educated (88% had at least some college experience) with an average age of 38 years (average age decreased with the increase in number of years on the Internet and level of skill). The similarity of web-users’ profiles and undergraduate students have been reported in a number of IS research (see, for example, [41]). The participants in our study were college students with an average age of about 20.3 years; more than 88% had more than three years’ experience with the Internet, and 40% had used Internet-based GIS technology at least once. We argue that due to the recency of web-based GIS technology, profiles for users of this technology are similar to our participants in that both groups have limited exposure to GIS technology. Furthermore, Mennecke et al. [43] compare the effect of the GIS technology utilization on performance between students and experts and observe no significant differences. Therefore, we believe that students are among the appropriate, though younger, segments of the target web-based SDSS users, and do not pose a significant threat to the validity of the study. Participation in the experiments was voluntary, and participants earned a relatively small credit in their coursework.

## 3.5. Construct measurement

We began the instrument-development process with previously tested instruments. Table A1 in Appendix A shows the construct definitions and sources for scale development.

## 3.5.1. Perceived TTF

Goodhue [22] developed items for measuring 8 dimensions of TTF. His instrument was designed in the context of organization setting that involved the use of several technologies. Drawing ideas from the definition and operationalization of TTF from Goodhue [22], Goodhue and Thomson [23], and Vessey [66], we developed scales for perceived TTF in the context of web-based SDSS.

## 3.5.2. Spatial abilities

We measured two types of spatial abilities: visualization and spatial orientation. The visualization ability was measured using the standard VZ-2 test and the spatial orientation ability was measured using the S-1 test [18]. Each test has two sections and each section must be completed in 3 minutes Both tests have been used in previous studies in spatial ability and GIS technology [58,60].

## 3.5.3. Goal commitment

Goal commitment is measured using items adapted from Hollenbeck et al. [27]’s nine-item self-report scale. This measure was modified as the result of the pilot test.

## 3.5.4. Self-efficacy

Compeau and Higgins [7] have developed a computer self-efficacy measure that consists of eight items asking respondents whether or not they can complete a task using a particular software (WordPerfect and Lotus123) under different conditions. Marakas et al. [39] review measures of self-efficacy, including that of Compeau and Higgins [7], and find most selfefficacy measures to be problematic. They suggest a five-step guideline, which is used in measuring computer self-efficacy by Johnson and Marakas [30]. We followed this guideline to develop scales for SDSSspecific self-efficacy that were appropriate for the level of our analysis, as recommended by Marakas et al. [39].

## 3.5.5. Expected decision quality

Expected decision quality is the individual’s expectation of the quality of decision-making choices when using web-based SDSS and the GIS technology. In our review of literature (cited in Table A1 in Appendix A) of IS performance, we identified frequently used keywords for decision quality for subjective and objective measurements, which we utilized in developing the scales measuring expected decision quality.

## 3.5.6. Expected decision efficiency

Expected efficiency is the expectation of time spent and number of decision tasks completed when using web-based SDSS and the GIS technology. As cited in Table A1 in Appendix A, our review of literature on the measure led to the identification of frequently used keywords for efficiency in both perception and objective measurements. We used these keywords in scale development for this construct.

## 3.5.7. Satisfaction

User satisfaction, in this study, is broken down into satisfaction with the decision and with the web-based SDSS technology. Traditionally, expected outcome and intention to use are used as manifest variables to measure satisfaction construct [24,55]. Since, in this study, we differentiate between expected decision quality, expected decision efficiency and satisfaction, we focused on scales that elicited the feeling of satisfaction, and adopted scales from McKinney et al. [41] and Spreng et al. [59].

3.5.8. Perceived decision quality and perceived decision efficiency

The items in these two constructs are similar to those of the expectations. The difference is that the perception items were evaluated after the completion of experiments, whereas the expectation items were evaluated before the web-based SDSS was used.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Construct validation procedures

Card sorting and extensive pilot testing procedures were used to assess the initial convergent and discriminant validity of constructs [45]. In card sorting, items that were excluded by judges were reviewed for clarity and dropped if all judges placed items in the wrong category. The overall item-placement ratios were 82%. As a result of the card-sorting procedure, we dropped one item from the decision-efficiency measure.

A pilot study was conducted with 325 participants who participated in the experiments. The results were used to assess the experimental design and the reliability and validity of measures. The factor analysis and correlation analysis led to removing some items. Specifically, two items in decision quality were removed due to the poor correlation with a general question item. The factor analysis of goal commitment showed that the nine-item self-report scale was not a unidimensional measure of goal commitment. This was in line with findings about this construct by

DeShon and Landis [16] and Klein et al. [32]. Two items were dropped initially due to poor factor loading and another four items were removed due to low correlation to the general question. Thus, three items and a general question item were used. An additional item was added for SDSS technology satisfaction. The main experiments were carried out with the SDSS prototypes and the refined instrument. The instrument is reported in Appendix B. The subsequent data collection for model testing resulted in 434 usable records.

The reliability measures for constructs in the pilot test and in the model test are reported in Table 1. The results support the assertion that the constructs are reliable. All the reliability values–Cronbach alpha, composite factor reliability, and average variance extracted from both studies–are well above the recommend thresholds.

In the exploratory factor analysis, we found high factor loading for each construct and lack of significant cross loading, both results supporting the convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs. ## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The use of geographic information systems (GIS) in DSS for supporting spatial decisions and its webbased technology opens a new avenue for making spatial DSS (SDSS) available to web-users. Such systems could be used in many business areas, such as tracking materials in supply chains, analyzing root causes of networking jams, and discovering new insights through data mining. The success of web-based SDSS depends to a large extent on the perception of web-users regarding the performance of SDSS. This study made one of the first attempts to explore the role that perceptual factors could play in the success of a SDSS in terms of users’ perception of its performance. In conceptualizing the factors impacting the success of SDSS, we proposed two main constructs that may have a major role: perceived task–technology fit (TTF) and perceived goal commitment. Relying on theories in task–technology fit and goal-setting theory as well as addi tional theories in psychology and DSS research, we identified antecedents of TTF and goal commitment. We theorized that spatial abilities and self-efficacy could impact perceived TTF, which in turn influences the success of SDSS in terms of users’ satisfaction (with decision and technology) as well as their perceived benefits of SDSS in terms of perceived decision quality and efficiency. Using goalsetting theory, we introduced perceived goal commit ment as the second major force in boosting the perception of SDSS success. Synthesizing theories in DSS and goal setting, we hypothesized that <sup>b</sup>expectancy of goal attainment<sup>Q</sup> in the form of perceived goal difficulty negatively impacts goal commitment, which is equivalent to the cost of using DSS in the cost–benefit theory in DSS. Furthermore, we identified expected decision quality and efficien cy as dimensions of <sup>b</sup>attractiveness of goal attainment,<sup>Q</sup> which, in the parlance of cost–benefit theory, is the benefit of using SDSS. The attractive ness of goal attainment, we argued, has a positive influence on goal commitment, which in turn increases the success of SDSS. Relying on research in self-efficacy and synthesizing it with motivation theory in psychology, we posited that self-efficacy is a central antecedent for expectancy and attractiveness of goal attainment.

Two prototypes of web-based SDSS using the GIS technology were developed for testing the model through lab experiments. The experiment had full factorial design for two levels of tasks, technology, and decision goals in order to induce variability in perceptions. The instrument for testing the model was developed based on a careful review of literature, applying the card-sorting procedure, and extensive pilot testing. The exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (the latter through the measurement model) indicated excellent support for construct reliability and validity.

The results of the model estimation supported the main thrust of the research in that perceived task– technology fit and perceived goal commitment have a major role to play in the perceived performance of SDSS decision satisfaction, SDSS satisfaction, perceived decision quality, and perceived decision efficiency. The results showed that spatial abilities do not have any impact on perceived TTF. This is good news for those who intend to put SDSS on the web users do not need to have spatial aptitudes to judge the technology to be fit for their intended decision tasks. Of the two <sup>b</sup>expected<sup>Q</sup> benefits of using SDSS, expected decision efficiency emerged as the motivator for goal commitment, and not expected decision quality. While decision quality is as important as decision efficiency, if not more so, our findings indicate that web-users, at least in their early encounter with the SDSS technology, expect the use of SDSS to contribute significantly to their decision efficiency. This could be due to the perception that SDSS by its nature should improve decision quality (otherwise why should anyone bother to develop it) but may take too much time and effort to use it. Therefore, in developing SDSS, designers need to be aware of the importance of time- and effort-saving measures in using the system.

Self-efficacy emerged as a central antecedent for main constructs in our study. It has strong positive impact on TTF as well as on the expected benefits of using SDSS. It also showed strong negative influence on perceived goal difficulty. This is an important finding for businesses that intend to offer SDSS service on their websites. They need to provide opportunities and options for their target users to gain learning experiences and feel capable of using the SDSS. According to Bandura [2], enactive attainment <sup>b</sup>provides the most influential source of efficacy information because it can be based on authentic mastery experiences<sup>Q</sup> (p. 126). In the context of SDSS, the repeated, successful completion of tasks in using the system could enhance decision-makers’ self-efficacy. Therefore, training and learning can reinforce experiences of successful task performance in using SDSS and thus enhance the attainment of self-efficacy.

Furthermore, the critical role of self-efficacy in SDSS indicates the need to incorporate a careful system design that includes effective and easy-to-use on-line help and tutorials. There is insufficient research in the role of online system-user support in general and online help and tutorials in particular in web-based systems. In the context of relatively complex functionalities with which users and decisionmakers lack adequate familiarity and self-efficacy, designers of SDSS need to pay particular attention to providing learning supports. Furthermore, researchers need to explore the extent of the impact of learning support on users’ self-efficacy.

A major source of online support for SDSS users could be an intelligent interface guiding decisionmakers in using the SDSS. For example, integrating intelligent agents with SDSS makes it possible to provide help for novice SDSS users in selecting and using the appropriate SDSS functionalities. The extent of help could subside as decision-makers attain mastery, become more comfortable using SDSS, and reach a higher level of self-efficacy. Such a personalized level of intelligence could seamlessly train users and enhance their self-efficacy with the added burden and time demand of the formal training. Decision-makers will see the SDSS as easy to use regardless of their levels of expertise with the system, and hence may be willing to make a stronger goal commitment to it all leading to a higher level of perceived SDSS success.

The model may enhance SDSS designers’ insight about factors that impact decision-makers’ perception about the usefulness of the SDSS by indicating that perceived decision quality requires fit between task and technology, self-efficacy, and motivational factors–goals. Since this study focuses on exploring the factors that impact perceived successful use of webbased SDSS, one can draw on the technology acceptance model (TAM) to interpret our results. TAM highlights the importance of <sup>b</sup>ease of use<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>perceived usefulness<sup>Q</sup> in the acceptance and use of information systems. Venkatesh et al. [64] formulated a more extensive model: the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) that incorporates <sup>b</sup>performance expectancy<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>effort expectancy<sup>Q</sup>, and <sup>b</sup>social influence<sup>Q</sup> as main factors influencing intention to use the system. In UTAUT, performance expectancy is defined as <sup>b</sup>the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance<sup>Q</sup> [64, p. 447]. Our research model is based on the premise that <sup>b</sup>perceived performance<sup>Q</sup> is a key factor in determining a decision-maker’s intention to use the system in the future. In this regard, our model complements TAM and UTAUT in that it adds perceived performance as an <sup>b</sup>after-use<sup>Q</sup> construct that complements performance expectancy as factors impacting the adoption and use of SDSS. In addition, with the established relationship between satisfaction and intention to use or actual use [14], one might argue that the perceived task–technology fit and perceived goal commitment may have a direct effect on intention to use the technology or a mediated effect through satisfaction. Examining these additional impacts require the extension of the model to include TAM or UTAUT constructs and a careful experimental design for testing the more complex extended model, which constitute a possible next step in this research.

This study has the same limitations as most lab experiments the question of how realistic the experimental settings are, the limited types of decisions, and the types of participants. Furthermore, in this research we focused only on perceptual constructs and did not include objective measures, such as the length of use and the optimality or correctness of decisions, because a number of studies support the relationship between TTF with perceived decision quality (e.g. [62,71,72]). However, further extension of the model should include the objective measures of performance for comparison with perceived performance. The extended model would be interesting and helpful to DSS designers and DSS researchers in that it would allow them to find out how decisionmaker perception of decision performance and actual decision performance match and to explore factors that may cause perceived performance to deviate from objective performance.

Another direction for further research is an investigation of the dynamic evolution of web-based SDSS users’ perception over time. The question of interest is how perceptions about TTF, goal commitment, and SDSS performance change as decision-makers use the system over time and become more familiar with its functionalities and limitations.

Moreover, it is possible to use SDSS with the mobile technology that, at present, has more limited capabilities. Mobile web-based SDSS raises a number of issues that may not be present in the stationary web-based SDSS. For example, how do users react to the interface and communication limitations when viewing and manipulating the visual outputs of the system? What type of task and decisions are most appropriate for web-based mobile SDSS? What type of interface design is needed to help alleviate problems caused by limitations of the technology? The fit framework, including task–technology fit and cognitive fit as formulated in this research, may provide a starting point for investigating novel issues raised by mobile web-based SDSS.

Her present areas of research include IS quality and satisfaction, DSS, e-commerce and web interface design, intelligent interface, IS design (for components, health networks, and maintenance), and policy and decision analysis. She has published many papers in major refereed journals, including: Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Decision Sciences, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Professional Communications, Decision Support Systems, IIE Transactions, European Journal of Operations Research, Operations Research, Computers and Operations Research, Journal of Review of Economics and Statistics, Empirical Economics, Socio-Economic Planning Sciences, Interfaces, and others. She has numerous publications in conference proceedings, and is the author of two books in Quality Information Systems and Intelligent Systems for Business: Expert Systems with Neural Network. Dr. Zahedi serves on the editorial board of a number of journals and has consulting and managerial experience in developing information systems and performing policy analysis in the private and public sectors.
