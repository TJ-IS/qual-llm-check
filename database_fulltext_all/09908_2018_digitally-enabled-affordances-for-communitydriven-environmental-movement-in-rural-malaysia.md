## Abstract

The immense environmental challenges facing society today have necessitated a research effort toward exploring digitally enabled solutions for environmental problems. Only limited research exists today to inform our understanding on how technology could assist groups of individuals in cultivating collective commitment and engaging in actions for environmental sustainability. By presenting an in‐depth case study of a social media‐enabled grassroots environmental movement in rural Malaysia, this paper aims to illuminate and understand an underresearched phenomenon of communitydriven environmental sustainability. This study makes 2 contributions: (1) we draw on the perspective of technology affordances to shed light on both the enabling power and unintended consequences of social media in the pursuit of environmental sustainability; and on that account, (2) we contribute rich, empirically informed insights toward understanding the underresearched phenomenon of digitally enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The rapid depletion of natural resources and the growing salience of environmental problems over the past decades have made environmental sustainability one of the most urgent challenges of the 21st century (Cooper & Molla, 2016; Dao, Langella, & Carbo, 2011; Melville, 2010). Driven by intensified concerns over environmenta degradation and the recognition of potential technological implications (both positive and negative) on the environment (Corbett, 2013), Information Systems (IS) scholars have embarked on research to explore the role and impact of technologies on environmental sustainability. One particular stream of research that has emerged from this effort is green IS research. It focuses on examining how technologies could serve as the enabler of both sustainable solutions (Corbett, 2013; Dedrick, 2010) and behavioral change among individuals, organizations, and society (vom Brocke, Watson, Dwver, Elliot, & Melville 2012) Green IS research thus encompasses diverse topics (Loock, Staake, & Thiesse, 2013), ranging from sustainable transformation of business processes to public participation in common environmental concerns (Elliot, 2011).

Most existing green IS studies have been conducted at the organizational level (Corbett, 2013; Jenkin, Webster, & McShane, 2011; Loock et al., 2013). These studies view businesses, institutions, or policymakers (eg, the government) as the drivers of change in initiating environmental actions. Taken together, this body of work explores the role of green IS from a command‐and‐control point of view. It discusses how decision makers make use of technology to cultivate sustainability practices among individuals within an organization. The present work. however, advocates a different focus. In this paper, we present a study of green IS with the objective of illuminating and understanding the underresearched phenomenon of digitally enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability. The motivation for our research emerges from 2 insights provided by existing research. First, in addition to considering corporate green responsibility, advancement in environmental sustainability also demands fundamental changes of behaviors at the societal level (Bengtsson & Ågerfalk, 2011; Elliot, 2011). Second, limited research exists to date to inform our understanding on how technology could assist groups of individuals in cultivating collective commitment and actions for environmental sustainability (Elliot, 2011).

This paper represents an early attempt to illuminate digitally enabled environmental sustainability at a societa level. Specifically, we explore an underresearched phenomenon of social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability. In the last decade, we have witnessed the unprecedented power of social media in the contex of environmental sustainability, opening up new avenues for green IS research. A number of remarkable success stories—from the annual Earth Hour campaign (https://www.earthhour.org) to Greenpeace's series of social mediaenabled environmental movements (Greenpeace, 2010)—have demonstrated the potential of social media in enabling collective environmental efforts. Nevertheless, research in this emerging phenomenon of “social media for green” is still at its nascent stage. In this paper, we present an in‐depth case study on the use of social media for an intense, community‐driven environmental movement in rural Malaysia that was sparked by one of the world's biggest rare earth processing projects, to illuminate and understand this underresearched phenomenon.

We adopt the perspective of technology affordances (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012; Strong et al., 2014) to examine the use of social media in enabling a community‐driven environmental movement. Technology affordance perspective highlights the action potentials of technology and is, therefore, particularly suited to examining previously unrecog nized roles of emerging technology (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012). This perspective provides a new opportunity for developing theory (Majchrzak, Faraj, Kane, & Azad, 2013; Treem & Leonardi, 2012), as it allows researchers to move beyond the features of technology and focus on theorizing the digitally enabled actions and their outcome (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012; Strong et al., 2014; Treem & Leonardi, 2012).

In a nutshell, by bringing together the technology affordance lens and our in‐depth case study of a social mediaenabled, community‐driven environmental movement, this study addresses the following research question: How does the use of social media influence community‐driven environmental sustainability? The contribution of this stud is twofold. First, drawing on the perspective of technology affordances, this study sheds light on the actualization o social media affordances in advancing grassroots environmental sustainability and the unintended consequences that arise from an increased use of social media. Second, on that account, this study contributes rich, empirically informed insights toward understanding the underresearched phenomenon of digitally enabled, community‐driven environ mental sustainability.

## 2 | CONTEXTUAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Environmental sustainability at the societal level

Technology assumes a duality position in the context of environmental sustainability: It can act either as a contributo to environmental degradation (Zhang, Liu, & Li, 2011) or as a solution for environmental sustainability (Elliot, 2011

Seidel, Recker, & vom Brocke, 2013). Accordingly, research on technology and environmental sustainability has coalesced into 2 different streams. First, we have the “green IT” research that focuses on exploring ways to minimize the negative environmental implications of technologies (vom Brocke et al., 2012). This stream of research contributes insights into developing the design, production, use, and disposal of ICT in a more environmentall friendly and sustainable fashion (vom Brocke et al., 2012). One of the most prominent themes of green IT research focuses on reducing the carbon emissions contributed directly by technology (Dedrick, 2010). Existing work in this area is driven largely by the observation that approximately 2% to 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions are directly attributable to technology (Corbett, 2013; Elliot, 2011; vom Brocke et al., 2012). Research in this area is therefore focused on (1) exploring ways for organizations to reduce this first‐order, direct effect of IT and (2 formulating recommendations for policymakers to make better decisions on carbon productivity (Corbett, 2013; Dedrick, 2010).

The second emerging stream of research investigates how technologies can support sustainability transformation (vom Brocke et al., 2012; Watson, Boudreau, & Chen, 2010). This stream of research—often referred to as the “green IS” research—highlights the transformative power of technology in providing or creating solutions for complex environmental problems (vom Brocke et al., 2012). Green IS research is motivated by the observation that technology could serve as a potential enabler of behavioral change and sustainable solutions (vom Brocke et al., 2012). Consider ing the same example of global carbon emissions, green IS research focuses on the role of technology in enabling solu tions and behavioral change to address the remaining 98% that falls outside the discussion of technology‐led greenhouse emissions (vom Brocke et al., 2012; Watson et al., 2010). Green IS research allows investigations of broader changes resulting from the use of ICT but has become the subject of IS research only in the last few years (Dedrick, 2010; vom Brocke, Seidel, Loos, & Watson, 2012). The research presented in this paper falls into this stream of studies.

To date, most green IS research has adopted a strategic perspective to examine sustainability issues at the organizational level (Corbett, 2013; Du, Pan, & Zuo, 2013; Loock et al., 2013; Malhotra, Melville, & Watson, 2010). These studies investigate and suggest how companies could embark on technological solutions, such as implementing green data centers or green supply chains, to proactively transform their organizational practices and processes toward sustainability (Corbett, 2013; Melville, 2010). Taken together, these studies have primarily adopted a top‐down perspective and focused on how technology can be used by “upper‐level agencies” (such as decision makers in a organization) to instill behavioral change in “lower‐level recipients” (such as the employees).

Nevertheless, “environmental sustainability is a multilayered and complex phenomenon” (Seidel et al., 2013, p. A1) and its scope extends beyond transformations at the organizational level. In this paper, we present a gree IS study that is centered at the societal level. Our decision to focus on the societal level was informed by an emerging stream of research that draws attention to consider environmental sustainability at a broader spectrum (Bengtsson & Ågerfalk, 2011; Elliot, 2011). Among them, Elliot (2011) has presented an influential agenda for environmental sustainability research, which our study builds upon. Through an extensive review of the existing literature, Elliot (2011) proposed 6 categories (ie, environmental, societal, governmental, industrial and alliances, organizational, and individuals and groups within organizations) to classify research on environmental sustainability. The societal category, ie, “collec tive and individual activity addressing environmental issues relevant to societies locally, nationally, and internationally” (Elliot, 2011, p. 216), which covers topics such as public awareness building and societal impact of stakeholder activity, is found to be one of the most underrepresented categories in existing research

The cumulative contribution of individuals' behaviors on environmental improvements is significant and should not be overlooked. Elliot (2011) called for further research to explore the roles technology may assume to transform the behaviors of individuals and groups in society toward environmental sustainability. Meanwhile, other scholar have also suggested that establishing multistakeholder collaborations is a more encompassing approach toward achieving sustainability (Van Osch & Avital, 2010). Existing environmental sustainability research that is mostly centered on economic perspectives may fail to capture the full range of impacts. Therefore, attention on the roles of loca communities and the greater society is necessary (Corbett, 2013). Overall, these studies reflect on the need for research to adopt a wider perspective on sustainability by paying attention to environmental sustainability phenomena at the societal level.

## 2.2 | Social media for environmental sustainability

In this paper, we echo this call and seek to expand the focus of environmental sustainability research from “green consumer” or “green corporate” to “green community” at the societal level. Specifically, we do that by exploring an emerging, underresearched phenomenon in this area, ie, social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmenta sustainability. In recent years, we have witnessed grassroots environmental movements that were enabled by socia media at a scale that requires reconsideration of the power of communities in advancing environmental sustainability For instance, the phenomenal application of social media for Earth Hour (a globally orchestrated initiative in partnership with the World Wildlife Fund where millions of people turn off their lights for 1 hour to express their solidarity on climate change) has demonstrated the power of social media in enabling self‐organized environmental action (Hasan & Dwyer, 2010). Another prominent example where social media is used to mobilize like‐minded individuals to promote a common environmental cause is demonstrated in a series of victories recorded by Greenpeace, an independen global campaigning organization (http://www.greenpeace.org/international/en/). Greenpeace enacted the collectiv power of communities through social media and successfully pressured a dozen leading brands to adopt more sustainable practices. Among all, their Kit Kat campaign, which brought together hundreds of thousands of individuals to raise their voices on social media and eventually put a stop to Nestlé's deforestation (Greenpeace, 2010), has delivered a strong signal to us—the rise of an empowered social norm could contribute to significant environmental change.

Taken together, social media is enabling new forms of dynamics on a scope and scale not previously witnessed in the realm of environmental sustainability. ## 3 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 3.1 | Technology affordances

A technology affordance refers to an opportunity for action (Hutchby, 2001)—that is, “what an individual or organiza tion with a particular purpose can do with a technology” (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012, p. 1). A technology affordance perspective frames the action potentials of technologies (Leonardi, 2013; Markus & Silver, 2008) and offers concrete understanding of the uses of the technological artifact and the related implications for individuals' behaviors (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012; Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016). Technology affordances are distinct from tech nology features, which are the designated functionalities built into the technology (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012). A technology affordance perspective focuses on identifying patterns of the symbiotic relationships between human action and technological capability (Zammuto, Griffith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). Scholars using a technology affordance perspective do not stop with only the study of the features of technology but continue b examining what behaviors it affords.

There are 2 streams of understanding of technology affordances in existing IS literature. The first stream of literature conceives affordances as action potentials embedded in technological artifacts (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012). A pumber of extant studies in this stream focus on identifving and defining functional affordances of a partic: ular technological artifact (eg, Treem and Leonardi, 2012, and Majchrzak et al., 2013). Only recently, the focus of thi stream of research has evolved into understanding and theorizing how such action potentials could be perceived interpreted, and actualized by users (eg, Strong et al., 2014). The present study continues this focus on affordance actualization (as detailed in the following section). The second stream of literature conceives affordances as “emergen from sociomaterial practices that involve technology” (Zheng & Yu, 2016, p. 292). This alternative perspective of affordances emerged recently within the sociomateriality stream of research (Bygstad, Munkvold, & Volkoff, 2016 and aims at theorizing affordances‐for‐practice (Fayard & Weeks, 2014; Zheng & Yu, 2016) that are “contingent upon the purpose of human agency as well as historical and socio‐institutional settings” (Zheng & Yu, 2016, p. 292).

The technology affordance perspective serves as an appropriate lens for the present study, as it allows researchers to sketch the precise nature of a technology (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2012). It is therefore par ticularly suited for researchers who are interested in exploring the roles of an emerging technology, such as social media in our case (Vaast & Kaganer, 2013). Social media, which has emerged and grown quickly to the mainstream over the past decade (Treem & Leonardi, 2012), prompted a reconsideration of the conventional views of technology (Davison, Ou, Martinsons, Zhao, & Du, 2014; Kane, Alavi, Labianca, & Borgatti, 2012; Ou & Davison, 2015; Tan, Pan, & Hackney, 2010). In an attempt to understand this emerging technology, scholars have been focusing on defining th technology, its characteristics, features, and roles. Existing studies have broadly defined social media as “a distinct cat egory of technologies” (Treem & Leonardi, 2012, p. 145), and some scholars have attempted to provide more specific definitions. Kaplan and Haenlein (2010), for example, defined social media as “Internet‐based applications that build on the ideological and technological foundations of Web 2.0, and that allow the creation and exchange of User Generated Content” (p. 62). A majority of the existing studies have also been “defining” social media by pointing toward the technologies that are implicitly recognizable as social media (eg, wikis and social networking sites) (Treem & Leonardi, 2012).

However, the existing discussion of social media based on characteristics and features does not fully capture the extensible nature of social media (Treem & Leonardi, 2012), the possibilities for action, and the unique impacts it affords (Albert & Salam, 2013; Urquhart & Vaast, 2012). The technology affordance perspective offers great utility to address this knowledge void—it serves as an illuminating perspective that aids in developing theory around socia media use (Treem & Leonardi, 2012) because it sheds light on how social media “becomes perceived and used in sim ilar and different ways across various social environments” (Vaast & Kaganer, 2013, p. 80).

## 3.2 | Affordance actualization and the immediate concrete outcomes

Against this backdrop, an emerging body of work has embraced the concept of affordance to conceptualize the use o social media (as summarized in Table 1). Taken together, these studies primarily focused on identifying different type of social media affordances, ie, “what affordances commonly emerged from social media use?” (Treem & Leonardi 2012, p. 148). In the present study, however, we move beyond existing focus on defining functional affordances to embark on an understanding of affordance actualization, as advocated by Strong et al. (2014)

As previously discussed, affordances represent the action potentials of a technology. However, given that an action potential could be (1) nondeterministic, perceptible, or hidden (Gaver, 1991) and (2) its realization could differ from what was initially expected and intended (Markus & Silver, 2008; Strong et al., 2014; Tan, Tan, & Pan, 2016), developing an understanding of technology affordances necessitates an understanding of both affordances (ie, action potentials) and the actualization process (ie, how such potentials are realized) (Strong et al., 2014). Accordingly, Strong et al. (2014) proposed the Affordance Actualization lens (hereafter AA lens) to demonstrate the importance of theo rizing both the affordances and the actualization process. An actualization process captures the actions taken by actors to realize one or more action potentials through their use of the technology to achieve specific immediate concrete outcomes in support of their goals (Strong et al., 2014). As suggested by the AA lens, an immediate concrete outcome is the specific outcome arising from the actualization process, which is viewed as useful for realizing the overarching goals of the actors (Strong et al. 2014). That said, focusing on the actualization process and the immedi ate concrete outcomes allows us to capture the real “moments” where affordances are exercised or realized. Immediate concrete outcomes that emerge during actualization serve as better pieces of evidence for the deduction of affordances, ie, the outcomes act as specific and actual explanations that help researchers theorize “how and wh IT‐associated change unfolds” (Strong et al., 2014, p. 79). Taken together, this technology affordance perspective offers explanatory power through its 3 core constructs: (1) the technological affordances (Gibson, 1979: Hutchby 2001; Leonardi, 2011; Majchrzak et al., 2013), (2) the actualization process (Strong et al., 2014), and (3) the immediate concrete outcomes (Strong et al., 2014). This perspective serves as the sensitizing device for our analysis discussed in following sections. We summarize our literature review in Table 1.

## 4.1 | Data collection

On‐site semistructured interviews serve as our primary data source. In total, 32 individuals, ranging from local residents who lived nearby the rare earth processing plant to several members of the Parliament of Malaysia, were interviewed (see Appendix A for a complete interviewee list). Each interview typically lasted between 40 an 90 minutes. Interviews were all open ended and occasionally guided by some rudimentary questions that were structured around the interviewee's role and involvement in the environmental movement. Interviews were further cus tomized along the way; new interview questions were devised based on findings gleaned from previous interview (Klein & Myers, 1999).

Each on‐site interview was digitally recorded (with permission), translated (when necessary), and transcribed for data analysis. Field notes were also taken during site visits and every interview to ease the transcribing process. In sum, interview data collected amounted to a total of 210 pages of textual transcript. ## 4.2 | Data analysis

Our data analysis follows an interpretive approach. Throughout the data analysis process, we adopt the perspective of technology affordances as our “sensitizing device” (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 75). The perspective of technology affordances provides us with theoretical sensitivity, which allowed us to view and make sense of the large volume of previously unclassified data. Consistent with guidelines on conducting interpretive case studies (Walsham, 1995), we examined and made sense of our data through multiple readings, developed the main corpus of our analysis by summarizing the narratives in a tabular form, and categorized data into emerging themes.

Along the way, as our understanding toward the phenomenon deepened (through the reading and rereading of data, literature, and theoretical lens), we are able to piece together the emerging themes. Specifically, the technolog affordance perspective provides us with the theoretical sensitivity to organize our data into themes, describing th consistent ways in which communities use the features of social media to accomplish particular collective goal. In comparing our emerging themes to existing literature, we adopt existing labels, concepts, or explanations when we found commonalities; and we develop new labels, concepts, or explanations when we found voids and opportunities in which our data could extend the literature. Specifically, as our interpretations continue to evolve, we recognize our 3 emerging themes as 3 affordances of social media. We adopt existing expositions of social media affordances (ie information democratization and network‐informed associating) as the basis for our articulation when we see fit; and we articulate a third, novel affordance (ie, emergent organizing) based on our empirical data (see following sec tions for detailed discussion). Subsequently, we were able to identify and associate empirical subthemes (eg, th immediate concrete outcomes of each affordance and the unintended consequences) to the 3 affordances, yieldin a meaningful comprehension of the phenomenon.

With this conceptual interpretation, we commence analysis at the next level, in our attempt to identify potentia linkages or relationships between the categories as appropriate. At this stage, the AA lens serve as the key‐sensitizing device that assists us in understanding our empirical findings and makes us more aware of the core constructs of technology affordances (eg, the potential immediate concrete outcomes and actualization process). We assessed and reassessed the data with questions such as “What did social media enable the community to do?” “What did it make it more difficult to do?” “What happened once the community started to use the technology?” or “Were there things that the community expected to be able to do but were in fact not possible?” (adapted from Volkoff and Strong, 2013) to integrate and refine interpretations developed in the previous stage. From this exercise, we were able to conceptualize the actualization process of each affordance and integrate the emerging constructs to form a larger theoretical scheme. Once we had the core concepts developed, we placed them in the context of existing literature, and finally, we organized these empirical patterns into a coherent framework. We present more details on our methodological considerations in Table 2 and discuss the interpretations arising from our data analysis in the following sections.

## 5 | CASE DESCRIPTION

For the past few years, many Malaysians have devoted their biggest environmental efforts to oppose Lynas Corporation's controversial attempt to build the world's largest industrial rare earth refinery, the Lynas Advanced Materials Plant (LAMP), in the country (Lynas, 2006). Rare earth elements are a group of essential metals used in manufacturing a wide range of modern technology products, from mobile phones to hybrid car batteries. Rare earth production used to be dominated by China and has a reputation for being highly polluting (NYTimes, 2013). The processing of rare earth refineries produced tons of radioactive contaminants: the air emissions, con taminated processed water, and residual solid wastes all have caused excessive damage to the environment (NTN, 2012). These environmental issues have prompted China to restrict its rare earth production (BBC, 2013 NYTimes, 2013).

The rare earth processing plant in Malaysia—LAMP—was designed to meet nearly a third of the world's demand for rare earths (Guardian, 2012). The industrial estate near Kuantan, Malaysia, was chosen as the location of the processing facility. The LAMP plant is built on a 100‐hectare site close to the Kuantan deepwater port (Lynas, 2006). This part of Malaysia is a prime coastal tourism destination, and the communities rely on fishing for their livelihoods. The close proximity of this rare earth processing facility to the sea thus added to the problem and risks.

According to the analysis of local anti‐Lynas campaigners (our interviewees), there are approximately 700 000 people living within 30 km of the LAMP (StopLynas.org, 2014). Nevertheless, the local community was not aware of the commencement of this project. Ironically, most of them first learnt about this project from a New York Times article (see NYTimes, 2011):

“It started from the New York Times. The reporter Keith Bradsher came to Kuantan, and he sat down with me to share about this rare‐earth refinery. I wasn't aware about rare earth, and I didn't know it has Ultrium, which is dangerous. Only since then did I start to do my own research. I have to search for reports and documents from Australia because we don't have the freedom of information in Malaysia.” —MP 1

The authorities—both Lynas Corporation and the government—were accused of withholding critical and accurate information from the public (NTN, 2012). Their failure to sincerely and constructively engage the communities led t voices that rejected the project. Considering the scale of the project and the lack of transparency in planning and execution, the public had little faith in the authorities' ability to regulate and monitor such hazardous waste. These concerns have triggered a strong local opposition, which has then expanded significantly into a burgeoning national‐scale opposition.

Nevertheless, the awakening of such environmentalism among communities in Malaysia was unprecedented Malaysians have had a relatively limited history of involvement in environmental issues (NTN, 2012), and this lack of exposure has resulted in a relatively low environmental awareness and sensibilities. Moreover, given the long standing reliance on the news media for information, cultivating public engagement for environmental issues not advocated by the authorities has been challenging and difficult:

“The problem we face in Malaysia is the restrictions of mainstream media, such as newspapers, TV, and radio.” —Assistant of MP 2.

“The local press, they are all quiet about Lynas. Obviously the citizens would not know about this.”

—Administrator of Facebook group “I am from Malaysia! I say Stop Lynas!”

In such a polarized context, social media comes into play for resolving the challenges in igniting environmentalism A number of social media‐enabled bottom‐up initiatives have sprung up among the aware citizenry. In our case study communities in Malaysia mainly engaged on 3 of the most prominent social media platforms (ie, Facebook, Twitter and YouTube) to realize their environmental efforts. Specifically, social media was used to cultivate awareness and realize collective environmental actions:

“Creating awareness among the people is especially difficult in Malaysia because the mainstream media is not covering the overall picture. By using social media, we aim to give a second opinion to the people. When people have the balanced inputs, they are well informed and can make decisions wisely.” —MP 3

Various anti‐Lynas communities have been mushrooming not only in the city of Kuantan but also in other cities of Malaysia. Among them, several Facebook community groups—such as Himpunan Hijau (Green Assembly) and Save Malaysia Stop Lynas (SMSL)—have spearheaded the efforts for collective action. The collective efforts of communitie have led to several massive environmental movements with sweeping impacts. For example, the operation of the USD 800 million LAMP facilities was hugely delayed because of the intense opposition initiated by the communities. The suspension order entailed a costly financial damage to the Lynas Corporation. Even the CEO of the company admitted that he had underestimated the power of social media:

“I would have dealt with the emerging community debate by social media a little bit more intensely, a little bit earlier; we probably didn't recognize the power of social media to create an issue.” —CEO of Lynas Corporation (Bloomberg, 2012)

Despite the fact that Lynas Corporation was able to secure a tenured license and eventually began rare earth production, the community‐driven environmental efforts were not misspent:

“We use social media to create the awareness on protecting the environment, not only for Lynas but also fo other environmental issues. At least we would want to make sure that the public are critical o environmental issues that are impacting their lives. Besides being supporters, they have the right to voice out for any issues that can impact their lives. The change we have is that people have started to practice proenvironmental behaviors and have become critical now. They did environmental checks fo all types of activities—industrial, agricultural, and even fisheries. When you see these actions, you know that the mind‐set of people has now changed, and this can even cause pressure to the government—they have to take action.” —MP 3

In all, our case study demonstrated the power of social media in cultivating environmental awareness and empowering communities to ignite bottom‐up environmental initiatives for sustainable development

## 6 | CASE ANALYSIS

In this section, we provide the detailed analysis, ie, our rich description and interpretation (Darke et al., 1998; Walsham, 1995) of how the use of social media advances community‐driven environmental sustainability.

In dealing with the closed‐door arrangement of building one of the world's largest rare earth refineries, igniting and sustaining community‐driven environmental engagement became the focal concern among the Malaysians. Yet several obstacles pose difficulties for realizing bottom‐up environmental initiatives. First, communities in Malaysia are less sensitive to environmental change. The level of environmental awareness among Malaysians is relativel low, as environmental issues have not been the central concern in this country. Second, in this case when authorities' support was absent, communities have limited capacity to initiate bottom‐up environmental initiatives at a sig nificant scale:

“You will find that the awareness of local Malay residents is actually not high, even though they are very close to the plant. There are a lot of factors behind this lack of awareness. First. the area is an industria area, so the locals have become accustomed to all the construction work. Second, to ignite a green movement, local people used to require support from political parties. The political atmosphere makes it difficult to start mobilization.” —Participant of Green Walk event

Moreover, the media blackouts in Malaysia, which was constantly highlighted by our interviewees, had largely reduced the means and the ease to cultivate environmental awareness.

“When we talk about Malaysians, maybe 70% of them don't really get both sides of the information Therefore, no one was really serious about a plant that is 15 km away from his or her house.” —MP 2

“There are factual differences between the mainstream media and the private one. The reporters from the mainstream media are not transparent. If we were given the freedom [on media], I feel that we will be able to spread information to the public easier and faster.” —Local resident living near LAMP

Given the pressing importance of the problem, a group of highly aware citizenry—particularly those with strong voices and those who were granted more access to information (eg, the members of Parliament)—started to develo strategies to keep the rest of the community informed. Yet at the outset, environmental awareness was difficult to arouse.

“In the beginning, we tried to create awareness about Lynas by distributing flyers and brochures. However, the impact was not good. At the same time, we were not given any coverage in local newspapers. So people were really not well informed about the hazardous effects that Lynas could impose.” —MP 3

Given all the limitations and restrictions in establishing awareness and in implementing, gathering, and sustaining support for environmental initiatives, communities resort to social media, which could seemingly afford them severa important action potentials.

## 6.1 | Actualizing the affordance of information democratization

The first move toward environmental sustainability is often by establishing environmental awareness. In the midst of an environmental crisis, presenting a complete picture of the situation is particularly important. Nevertheless, in Malaysia, most of the rural residents who live near the refinery facilities were, ironically, not privy to information regarding the LAMP facilities

“There are some people in rural areas who've really never heard of Lynas. There may be a lot of misunderstanding on this issue especially in rural areas because of insufficient information.” —Assistan of MP 2

Social media affords communities the possibility to disseminate information that were once restricted and controlled to a wide scope of audiences through its unique visibility and openness. In our case study, a handful of concerned communities make use of several features of social media platforms (particularly Facebook) to write, post and share information. Given their familiarity with the platforms, they are able to enact social media for informatio gathering and dissemination to spearhead different environmental initiatives

One of the most impactful examples that evidenced the actualization of information democratization affordance was observed from the use of social media in supporting a 14‐day, 300‐km Green Walk event (from Kuantan to the capital city Kuala Lumpur) on November 25, 2012. Executing an environmental movement that involves a 300‐km journey on foot is not an easy task by itself. Not surprisingly, the event organizers were confronted by a number o challenges at the beginning. First, this environmental movement failed to obtain approval from the governmen and, therefore, was not given any coverage in the mainstream media. Second, given the relatively low level of environ mental awareness and understanding toward the rare earth refinery project, the movement could not attract muc attention at the outset

Given such restrictions, the walk started off with around 70 participants, with most of them mobilized by wellrespected local leaders. Nevertheless, starting from the first day, communities used social media to spread strategized and targeted content (eg, pictures and emotional posts) and to establish and maintain a strong online presence for the event. For example, communities strategically maximize the impact of their Facebook postings by aiming at a peak timing that could maximize readership. They also strategically maximized the visibility of their content by constantl editing or adding comments on their own posts so that the posts could be featured as “top stories” on Facebook. Such actions brought about several concrete impacts. First, the scale of the movement multiplied every day, as nonpartic ipating individuals were constantly exposed to a large amount of information about the event:

“Initially, I did not pay specific attention to the Green Walk, but it kept appearing on my [Facebook] wall.

The information I received was quite frequent and detailed—for example, where they have reached, what

situation they met. After that, I joined the walk, and I draw together people around me to participate.” —

Participant of Green Walk event

Second, the carefully crafted information and targeted content disseminated by the organizers and the partici pants were effective in reflecting the spirit and emotions of fighting for a common environmental cause. This information tremendously helped in cultivating further participation:

“I will contact those expert writers, they are really good in writing, and those emotional contents can easily get 1,000 “likes.” If you want to use social media for mobilization, you need to make the audience feel that the event is as real as possible.” —Organizer of Green Walk even

Given that every individual will have the ability to locate and share information on social media, the accumulation of diverse voices further strengthened the online presence of the event:

“At first we started a fire. After that, it burns on its own. It is because people involved will write thei own stories. When others see them, they are touched and they want to participate. Then they write their own stories. There is such a multiply effect that makes the event a “living” thing.” —Organizer of Green Walk event

From 70 participants on the first day, the snowball effect brought about 20 000 participants to the event on it last day at the capital city of Kuala Lumpur. The affordance of social media in enabling the dissemination and cultivation of environmental information has been actualized:

“I think Green Walk is the major event that really boosted up the audience. The momentum was out of ou expectations actually. I think it was because of the stories and the nice photos that we shared on Facebook throughout the walk. People could actually follow our journey. It was difficult to get media coverage, so we uploaded photos every day. The stories spread, and so did the spirit.” —Assistant of MP 2

## 6.2 | The unintended consequence of information cluttering

However, despite the initial success, our analysis revealed an intriguing paradox. The fact that social media provides an unbound space to a large audience is also driving a paradoxical effect. For example, there are instances when socia media is being used to distribute manipulative information, rumors, or irrelevant contents that carry a personal (and most of the time, political) agenda. These actions subsequently result in a clutter of irrelevant information on the plat form, which leads to an unintended loss of trust and information credibility. The flexibility and autonomy of socia media turn the table around to create doubts that could reduce the practicality or the influence of the information shared. Such information dissonance throws new challenges to the communities, particularly among the administrators, since additional efforts were required to manage and monitor their information sharing activities on social media From our case study, we observed how this paradoxical implication has prompted the communities to take a more proactive role in managing information on the platform:

“Initially we didn't have experience in managing the Facebook group, and when commercial information began coming in, people started to leave the page. If we want the community to trust us, we have to do a better job of filtering the non‐relevant stuff on the Facebook page. I have to keep delivering new content and keep an eye on the posts to delete whatever is not relevant.” —Administrator of Facebook group “I am from Malaysia! I say Stop Lynas!”

In summary, social media affords each and every individual the power of locating and contributing informatio toward promoting the collective environmental initiatives. Nevertheless, at the same time, the enactment of socia media brings forth the unintended consequence of information cluttering. Perhaps, more intriguing is the fact that this unintended consequence has, in turn, adjusted the actions of communities in appropriating the value of social media More specifically, we observed that this unintended consequence of information cluttering has made necessary a more careful control of information. With the careful monitoring of information, communities further enhance the usefulness of social media in serving to cultivate environmental awareness. Social media has then emerged as a reli able channel for information dissemination and a tool for environmental leaders to cultivate awareness.

## 6.3 | Actualizing the affordance of network‐informed associating

The effort to address environmental issues, however, needs to go beyond awareness. In our case study, communities used social media to locate potential connections, to engage with other like‐minded individuals who were a concerned about the environment, and to establish collaboration. More specifically, we observed that communitie actualized the affordance of social media to develop and cultivate network‐informed association

We observed several examples of communities using social media to unite the concerned individuals. First, we observed how social media enabled calls to action around communities with common interests and promoted mobi lization that could unfold organically:

“When we were planning for the event, we were worried about how many people would turn out. But eventually 20,000 people turned out. After that, we realized the numbers actually came through Facebook. When I posted, “There will be a gathering in Kuantan,” my friends would share it and contact other friends. Then they would encourage each other to come. I think it was the connections through Facebook that gathered more people.” —Event organizer of HH

This enablement is made possible by the association afforded by social media. Social media makes existing net works and connections visible, thereby allowing communities to browse, view, and strategically establish new connec tions. From our case study, we observed that the network explicitness of social media allows individuals to selectively establish associations by bridging among appropriate networks. Several existing clusters of connections could be united for an immediate propagation of capabilities and capacities:

“When I post something, I will share it on pages that already have a large number of active members so that I can get more power and more people to join my page. By doing so, in one day I could get more than 500 “likes” and we could unite our forces in a few weeks.” —Administrator of Facebook group “I am from Malaysia! I say Stop Lynas!”

Furthermore, when groups of communities who are inspired by a common purpose are united together, a share identity could be developed. We observed that the concerned communities in Malaysia were knitted together and identified themselves as one “green” collective.

“On 2013 New Year's night, we had a demonstration in front of the LAMP. The organizers were the group that wore green clothes. Every ethnic group participated in the protest that night. When it was exactly twelve midnight, all lights were turned down, and all ethnic groups became one. There were thousands of us, and the roads were packed. We were really proud being in a community that is aware of this issue.” —Local resident living near LAMP

## 6.4 | The unintended consequence of exclusion

Nevertheless, our case study revealed that communities have encountered both enabling and constraining factors Our data has shown that while communities could browse and select visible connections on social media, they could hardly establish associations beyond that boundary. That being said, communities were granted the possibility to enrich the existing relationships but at the same time were detached from potential connections that were beyond the associating capabilities of social media. For example, reaching out to people outside the boundary of existing net work is difficult and ineffectual:

“Facebook still has limitations. We cannot expect the community to grow very big, especially to grow into a circle of strangers. Do not expect someone who's never heard of the story to see your Facebook page. It is quite difficult." —Assistant of MP 2

Furthermore, establishing association with those who were digitally excluded and digitally illiterate was beyond the action potential of social media. One evident example from our case study is the unintended exclusion of rura communities. While it was critical to establish connections with the rural residents, the digital divide that restricts thei access to social media has made social media an impractical platform. Given that most of the associations related t this environmental movement were promoted through and sustained on social media, the off‐line communities have been isolated from the conversation and initiatives on social media:

“There are still people who are living in remote areas, and they don't have access to social media. Even though they do not form a big community, they have a big influence on the movement because they are the people who stay close to the plant. They are people who are being impacted—for example, fishermen and farmers—but they do not have the access.” —MP 3

More important, however, is how communities embark on further actions to address the problematic unin tended consequences. Prompted by the unintended consequence of exclusion, communities move beyond onlin interaction and start to initiate real‐life contact to promote the environmental initiatives. One impactful example was an off‐line signature petition run by a green movement group Himpunan Hijau (translated as Green Assembly hereafter HH), whose leader physically remained for 36 days and nights at the Independence Square of Kuala Lumpur to disseminate environmental awareness through off‐line interactions:

“The purpose of staying 36 days and nights at the Independence Square of Kuala Lumpur was to establish personal contact. That is also why this signature petition was so successful. There were some times when people walked through, they felt curious and came over to ask. After we explained the issue to them, they were surprised why such a hazardous project was not covered in the television. They eventually became volunteers and helped us collect the signatures.” —Event organizer of HH

This off‐line action helped to extend the influence of social media and significantly promoted the environmen tal effort. Taken together, we see that social media affords the possibilities for communities to unite like‐minded individuals in supporting collective environmental efforts. Nevertheless, at the same time, it isolates the unin formed and the digitally excluded. Such paradoxical implications necessitate communities to strategically enric their online associations while at the same time expand their reach through off‐line contacts.

“We cannot deny that face‐to‐face information sharing is the most effective way of getting support from people because it involves not only information but also emotion. But how many people can we meet? We can't meet thousands of people at one time. So that's why we have to do it both ways.” —MP 3

## 6.5 | Actualizing the affordance of emergent organizing

Moving forward, a strategic collaboration among the communities is required to sustain the collective effort. Socia media affords bottom‐up collaboration by allowing organizations to emerge without centralized control. In our case study, we observed how communities actualized the affordance of social media to realize emergent organizing, eg, to crowdsource for expertise, to allow for self‐driven role taking, and spontaneous organizing. We observed severa exemplary large-scale environmental movements that have been successfully organized through Eacebook within a short period and, in most cases, without detailed prior planning.

“Social media has made a great impact on the environmental movement. People responded to us, and we can organize events easily using social media. Until today we are still using it to support our anti‐Lynas movements. Social media connects people all over the world. Even Greenpeace started to get involved.” —MP 3

Among them, one of the most significant events that clearly demonstrated how social media affords emergent organizing is the “Green Assembly 2.0,” which was organized by HH and was attended by more than 20 000 Malaysians on February 26. 2012

“A lot of people asked me how we got so many people to join our Green Assembly 2.0, but it was actually very organic. We do not have a special unit to coordinate. I remember, just a few days before the event, I received a lot of friend requests on Facebook. They asked me some details about the event because they were arranging buses themselves from different states in Malaysia. As the organizers, we were equally amazed by the numbers that turned out later and their disciplined behaviors. You will be surprised that people will help to clean up the places after the event, and we don't even know who they are.” —MP 2

To coordinate this large‐scale event, social media was used by the communities to crowdsource required expertise and resources:

“People will just approach us through Facebook. They will ask us, “What do you need?” and they will spread the information to the right people. For example, during the Green Walk event, a doctor messaged us and asked if we needed medicine. He is not based in Malaysia, but he spread the information to his friends, and they brought us some medicines.” —Assistant of MP 2

These self‐organized actions that were not conceivable in the past have played a significant role in advancing environmental initiatives: community participation and engagement have been enhanced; confidence increased, and the local community is now empowered to pursue their environmental agenda:

“During that time, we decided to make Lynas our national issue. One way to do this is through the new media. We use blog, Twitter and Facebook. Because even we have very close relationship with the mainstream media, both printed news and the local media still could not give a good coverage for us to create impact.” —MP 3

## 6.6 | The unintended consequence of disorganization

However, while establishing spontaneous, decentralized organizations is a feasible solution in the context of commu nity‐driven movement, it is still critical that people rally around common themes and goals. In our case study, the greater use of social media for emergent organizing has resulted in an unintended consequence. While social media affords the freedom of self‐organization, unintended chaos tends to sway the group's focus. Such paradoxical impli cation was observed in several instances where disorganization among communities hindered the possibilities for communities to sustain effective collaboration.

“There are few things that would deter people from continuing their support for the movement. One of them is the lack of proper organization. Some politicians will try to gain benefits for their own politica agenda. I think it is better to have people concentrate on this full‐time.” —Administrator of Facebook group “I am from Malaysia! I say Stop Lynas!”

While the emergent nature and the flat hierarchy of social media maximize bottom‐up organization, it also hinders the possibilities of leadership and control:

“There is a downside, where some people will distort the meaning of the activities to their own agenda, some people will still insist to do publicity their way, but we cannot control them because on Facebook, we are all equals.” —Organizer of Green Walk event

These unprecedented implications have constrained communities from fully enacting the flexibility of social media and make more careful management and control necessary:

“As the leader, how should I write each post? How do I leverage people's different strengths to administrate this page? These are the things that I don't know well enough.” —Administrator of Facebook group “I am from Malaysia! I say Stop Lynas!”

“It's difficult to control people on Facebook. People can write anything they want. We do not know how much of it is true. At the same time, we also expose ourselves to complaints and legal implications.” — Assistant of MP 2

In all, we observed how the emergent nature of social media affords self‐organized environmental initiatives. Nevertheless, at the same time, such affordance can also backfire. Communities have encountered a unexpected stumbling block—the bottom‐up, emergent form of organization is subject to conflicts and disorga nization. More importantly, however, we also observed how this paradoxical dynamic has, in turn, fostered an increase in commitment among the communities and intensify their efforts to preserve collaborative interactions:

“The challenge for me is that the communities on social media actually have to be organized, to make it such that they don't step into each other's toes. So I as a leader have to provide support so that they can focus on their work. It took me more than a year to make them organized, letting each of them take charge of the area that they are good in.” —MP 1

## 6.7 | Summary: advancing environmental sustainability at the community level

Over the last couple of years, the grassroots environmental movement in Malaysia has successfully delayed the implementation of the rare earth plants and led to the suspension of Lynas Corporation's operating license. We observed notable advancements of environmental sustainability that were driven by the actualize affordances of social media. First, environmental awareness among the communities has been significantly promoted. “Yes, we have seen the impacts. I think if we had had this level of awareness back in 2009, Lynas would not have even started. Now people have started to become critical about any changes in the environment.” —MP 3

“I think the whole phenomenon is very encouraging, and it has broken the past social norm. Only by socia media, you can connect people together. This gives us a lot of confidence. For me, this is an advancement, a new force and a whole new era.” —Participant of Green Walk event

Third, our analysis shows that social media affords emergent organizing. Unlike a formal organization with rigid structure, an emergent organization involve little imposition of formal planning; it allows organizing principles, patterned actions, and role taking to emerge. A emergent organization supports the unique organizing needs of grassroots‐based efforts by providing the low entry barrier and opportunities for flexible dialogues and collaborations. In our case study, we have seen how communities actualize the affordance of social media to set up ad hoc environmental activities and even expect swift responses. Moreover, the flexibility of social media in allowing organizing activities to be performed online has furthe enabled the environmental movements to grow substantially beyond spatial and temporal restrictions.

## 7.2 | The unintended consequences of social media

Nevertheless, perhaps more intriguing is the identification of unintended consequences resulting from the actualization of social media affordances. Firstly, our analysis shows that while the broadening of individuals' control of and access to infor: mation leads to preferable outcomes, the greater use of social media has also led to some unintended consequences. In particular, we observed that the greater use of social media for decentralized information sharing has led to an unintended consequence of information cluttering. We define information cluttering as the undirected and unwieldy accumulation of information. From our case study, we have seen how the inflow of individualized and sometimes provocative content could lead to a dissonance of information. More specifically, we found that communities were confronted by the weakening of credibility and trust as rumors and irrelevant content (eg, political contents and commercials) started to accumulat on social media. In a decentralized network that features self‐direction and self‐control, trust is critical to “promote open, substantive and influential information exchange” (Jarvenpaa, Knoll, & Leidner, 1998, p. 30). The unintended consequenc of information cluttering may hence contribute to an attention‐deficit effect and subsequently lead to disengagement of the self‐organized members from information exchange (Gibbs, Rozaidi, & Eisenberg, 2013). As a result, this unintende consequence has necessitated a serious commitment to information management, as illustrated in our case study.

Secondly, while social media enables associations to be established between individuals with a common cause, th actualization of this affordance also brings forth unprecedented consequences. We observed that the greater use of social media may lead to an unintended exclusion. This unintended consequence is particularly salient in the case when the greater use of social media has created an unprecedented boundary between the digitally included and the digitall excluded. Given the lack of digital access and competency, a large part of the population is excluded from the discursive space opened up by social media. In our case study, we observed how marginalized communities who do not have access to social media were generally uninformed and therefore unable to play a role in the environmental initiatives Off‐line, physical interactions have thus become essential to connect every individual beyond the digital divide

Lastly, the emergent nature and volatility of social media could also result in unintended impacts, such as fleeting membership and pursuit of conflicting purposes (Majchrzak, Jarvenpaa, & Hollingshead, 2007). Specifically, in our case study, disorganization has emerged as an unintended consequence. We define the unintended consequence of disorganization as the disarrangement or breaking of a community structure and the inability for the structure to realize common values. Given the flat hierarchy and the absence of a centralized leadership structure, social media communities face difficulties in sustaining consensus and moving toward a common goal in the longer term. Communities, particularly the movement organizers and groups' administrators, faced challenges in dealing with the growing scale and spontane ity. To keep up the momentum of the environmental initiatives, concerted effort and serious commitment among th organizers became necessary to ensure that the development of communities is in line with their environmental goals

In summary, adopting the perspective of technology affordances has allowed us to unveil insights on the use of socia media in achieving collective sustainability goals. We have identified a set of action potentials afforded by social media which could be actualized by actors to achieve environmental goals, Our findings highlighted the affordance actualization ie we demonstrated how goal-oriented actors could actualize the affordance of social media to achieve several immedi ate concrete outcomes. Our focus on the affordance‐actualization process was informed by the AA lens proposed by Strong et al. (2014), which calls for more attention on “how the potential captured by an affordance is actualized” (p. 71).

More importantly, our analysis has generated a second, intriguing finding (ie, the unintended consequences), which extends existing understanding on affordance actualization. The analysis presented in the previous section has shown that while social media affords certain action potentials (just as how a chair affords “sitting”), the actualization of affordance (ie, the realization of action potentials) may well be unintended (iust as a chair could be eventually used for “standing on”), Ou findings have unveiled 3 unintended consequences and unraveled the feedback effects triggered by these unintended consequences. Specifically, our findings show that not only may some unintended consequences result from the actualization actions of individuals but also these unintended consequences may, in turn, provide feedback for adjusting those actions. While the unintended consequences seem detrimental on the surface, these outcomes serve to enforce the goal oriented actors to embrace further actions so as to work around or address the undesirable implications. For example from our analysis, we observed that the unintended consequence of exclusion has prompted the communities to se up some off‐line movements, which ended up being instrumental in advancing their environmental initiatives. Similarly, the unintended consequence of information cluttering has pushed the communities forward to deliver better‐quality information. The improved information management was found to be instrumental in advancing environmental sustainability—it cultivated strong awareness among the communities and thus enabled them to make more appropriate deci sions to ameliorate environmental issues. Overall, these findings bring forth the paradoxical impacts of social media and demonstrated how the unintended consequences are contributing feedback effects that further influence the actions of actors. By doing so, our findings extend the existing understanding on the affordance‐actualization concept by pro viding a more complete explanation on the actualization process and the collective outcomes of actualization.

## 8 | THEORETICAL IMPLICATIONS

In this study, we uncover the process and implications associated with the use of social media in an intense, commu nity‐driven environmental movement in rural Malaysia. Our findings identified 3 affordances of social media and how these affordances were actualized by the local communities to facilitate collective environmental initiatives toward advancing environmental sustainability. We have also identified 3 unintended consequences that arise from th increased use of social media and discussed the implications of these unintended consequences on communities actions. Below, we discuss the 2 theoretical implications of this work.

## 8.1 | Understanding affordance actualization and the unintended consequences of social media

This study extends existing understanding on technology affordances in 2 ways. First, moving beyond identifying the affordances of a technology (ie, the focus of most existing research), we echoed the call from Strong et al. (2014) to theoriz the process of affordance actualization. More importantly, this study represents an early attempt that explores the affordance actualization of an emerging technology, ie, social media. Theorizing the actualization of social media affordances provides insights into the extensible nature of this emerging technology, which is largely underexplored in existing research Our findings could thus serve as an important foundation to stimulate the much‐needed research efforts in understandin the possibilities for action and the unique impacts social media affords (Albert & Salam, 2013; Urquhart & Vaast, 2012)

Second, this study uncovers some intriguing unintended consequences that resulted from the actualization of affordances. Recently, scholars have highlighted that “ICT's consequences are not universally positive” (Majchrzak et al. 2012, p. 1) Technology makes some things easier to accomplish and renders others difficult, if not impossible Nevertheless, existing studies predominantly focus on discussing the antecedents of technology use and “subconsciously veer away from acknowledging ICT‐related harms” (Majchrzak et al., 2016, p. 273). As a result, we know little about the unanticipated uses and conseguences of technology (Maichrzak et al. 2012, 2016). “The recognition that ICT can have both positive and negative effects, both intended and unintended, deepens our field's theorization of ICT” (Majchrzak et al., 2016, p. 273). By identifying the unintended consequences of social media, we now provide a more comprehensive understanding on the use and implications of this emerging technology. More importantly, ou findings also go beyond identifying the unintended consequences to uncover the influence of these consequences on affordance actualization and the actions of actors. Our data capture an intriguing feedback effect: the unintende consequences generated from the use of technology (ie, actions in actualizing technology affordances) will, in turn provide feedback for adjusting the actions of actors and thus the uses of technology

Drawing on these findings, we developed a conceptual framework (Figure 1) to illustrate the affordance‐actuali zation process (adapted from Strong et al., 2014) and the unintended consequences that arise from the actualizations As an overarching framework, this framework illustrates our aforementioned contributions to theory. It provides an extended understanding of the affordance‐actualization process by highlighting the possible unintended conse quences of technology use and proposing an intriguing feedback effect of unintended consequences in adjusting the actions of goal‐oriented actors. By unveiling the affordance actualization of an emerging technology (ie, socia media) in a novel social context, this framework first extends the AA lens, which was developed in the context of Electronic Health Record associated organizational change (Strong et al., 2014). Second, by conceptualizing the unintended consequences and the feedback effects, this framework deepens existing theorization of affordance actualiza tion and more generally enriches our understanding on ICT's consequences (Majchrzak et al., 2016).

## 8.2 | Providing an empirically informed understanding of digitally enabled, communitydriven environmental sustainability

As discussed in the preceding sections, there is a limited understanding on the active role of communities in serving as the change agent for their own sustainable development. Moreover, few empirical studies exist to explore technology as a change actant for environmental sustainability (Elliot, 2011; Ryoo & Koo, 2013), as most of the existing work is based on conceptual research and literature review (Bengtsson & Ågerfalk, 2011). “Investigating empirically the potentials of Green IS has become increasingly important for researchers and practitioners to advancing Green IS research” (Ryoo & Koo, 2013, p. 800). In the present study, we present an early effort to address this knowledge void

Specifically, we built on rich empirical evidences to develop an understanding of the power of social media in advanc ing community‐driven environmental sustainability. We believe that our theorization on the affordance actualization of social media in advancing community‐driven environmental sustainability makes an important contribution in the form of a type II theory (“theory for explaining”) (see Gregor, 2006). Type II theory, as proposed by Gregor (2006), goes beyond mere descriptions to serve as a “theory for understanding” (p. 624) that addresses the question of “how and why som phenomena occur” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624). This type of theory closely corresponds with the interpretivist paradigm we adopt in this study (Gregor, 2006; Klein & Myers, 1999): an explanatory theory does not aim to generate testable pre dictions about the future but seeks to contribute “new and interesting” knowledge “to explain something that was poorly or imperfectly understood beforehand” (Gregor, 2006, p. 635). We believe that the extended understanding and conjectures derived from our study (ie, how affordances of social media were actualized for environmental sustainabil ity at the societal level) contribute to knowledge as a type II theory, as we provide “new and interesting insights” for “how and why” the phenomenon of social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability occurs.

## 10 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Our research opens several new avenues for future inquiry. First, the various components in our framework (Figure 1) and our conceptualization of affordance actualization and unintended consequences require further research and development. For instance, the new concept of unintended consequences and the feedback effect proposed in ou findings provide opportunities for further conceptual development. Our analysis has uncovered 3 unique unintended consequences of social media, but more exploration on the implications of such unintended consequences will b insightful from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Future studies could also work on enriching our theorization by developing further understanding about when and how a particular affordance is actualized and why (Strong et al., 2014; Zheng & Yu, 2016) (see, for example, Zheng and Yu, 2016's work that enriches the theorization of affordance by taking into consideration heterogeneous contextual elements)

Another valuable step forward would be to extend our conceptualization of the emergence of collective outcomes and goals. In the present study, we focus on theorizing the affordance actualization of social media and the unintended consequences. Following Strong et al. (2014), we see the collective outcomes as the aggregations of the immediate concrete outcomes of individuals' actions. Future research could further develop this theorization by focusing explicitly on the emergence of collective outcomes and identifying the possible dependencies between mul tiple interrelated affordances and actualizations (Strong et al., 2014).

In addition to developing further the conceptualization on affordance actualization, more generally, the present study seeks to encourage more research about the affordances of emerging technologies in addressing global challenges, including environmental sustainability and beyond. We situated the development of our theoretical findings in the context of social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability. Given that the discussion of technology affordances is technology specific (Strong et al., 2014), our contribution is thus limited to an explanatory theory rather than a grand theory, This limitation opens up new opportunities for future research to develop new explanatory theories to shed light on the use of emerging technologies in the context of environmental sustainabilit as well as other similar social contexts.

## 11 | CONCLUSION

Information Systems scholarship has an immense responsibility in solving high‐visibility, high‐impact global challenges. This paper addresses the issue of environmental sustainability—one of the most critical global challenges facing societies today (Malhotra, Melville, & Watson, 2013; Melville, 2010; Watson et al., 2010). Our study represents an early attempt to illuminate and understand an underresearched phenomenon, ie, social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmental sustainability. Using an in‐depth case study of a grassroots environmental movement in rural Malaysia, our findings contribute rich insights on the use and implications of social media for community‐driven environmenta sustainability. Specifically, our findings uncover the process where the affordances of social media were actualized to achieve collective sustainability goals. Moreover, we uncover the unintended consequences that arise from the actualization of social media affordance and explicate how these consequences further shape the use of social media (Table 3). This rich empirical understanding has allowed us to develop a conceptual framework that extends the existing theoretical understanding on the concept of affordance actualization (Figure 1). Furthermore, we propose a framework of lessons to guide practitioners in managing social media‐enabled, community‐driven environmental initiatives (Table 4). In closing, we are hopeful that our findings will encourage future research aimed at exploring (1) the affordance actualization of social media in facilitating community‐based environmental actions and more generally (2) digitally enabled environmental sustainability at the societal level.
