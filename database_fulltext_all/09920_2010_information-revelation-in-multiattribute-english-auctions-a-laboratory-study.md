# Information revelation in multiattribute English auctions: A laboratory study

Stefan Strecker

Institute for Computer Science and Business Information Systems, University of Duisburg-Essen, Essen, German

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 14 November 2007 Received in revised form 4 December 2009 Accepted 8 March 2010 Available online 12 March 2010

Keywords: Multiattribute auction Electronic procurement Laboratory experiment

## a b s t r a c t

The effects of information revelation on allocational and Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency are studied in a multiattribute reverse English auction. The setting is a single sourcing problem in which a buyer seeks to acquire an indivisible object from exactly one of <sup>fi</sup>ve potential suppliers. Bids are three-dimensional with two nonmonetary attributes in addition to the object's price. Results from a computer-based laboratory experiment are reported, and observed differences between two information revelation policies are analyzed with respect to the buyer's utility and suppliers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. The results suggest that revealing the buyer's preferences increases allocational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Suppliers successfully use the additional information to make more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, but not at the expense of the buyer whose utility increases slightly albeit not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly.

## 1. Introduction

Multiattribute auctions extend the standard auction framework to allow simultaneous negotiation over price and non-price attributes of an object or contract [21]. First mentioned in the context of franchise bidding [29], they have recently been applied to corporate and government e-procurement when differences in quality exist and suppliers exhibit comparative cost advantages in producing to a certain quality level [12]. Applications of multiattribute auctions to buying processes have been reported with contract programming [26] and other IT services [28]. Bidding on non-price attributes in addition to price, promises to reduce the downward price pressure inherent in reverse price auctions and to alleviate subsequent risks, particularly of eroding input quality and of alienating high quality suppliers [14]. Multiattribute reverse auctions allow buyers to retain <sup>fl</sup>exibility during negotiation by expressing tradeoffs across different outcomes without making an up-front commitment to a particular combination of attribute levels. On the other hand, they allow suppliers to differentiate in the auction process and to bid on their competitive advantages, for example, offer a faster delivery if production capacity is available.

Multiattribute auctions are scoring auctions in that the buyer (bidtaker) speci<sup>fi</sup>es preferential tradeoffs among price and non-price attributes by means of a scoring function [2]. During the auction process, the buyer solicits bids from invited suppliers (bidders) according to the rules of a bidding procedure. The scoring function is used to evaluate submitted bids and to designate the contract to the bidder providing the highest score. In an iterative multiattribute auction such as the English (<sup>fi</sup>rst-score, open-outcry) auction format, bidders receive information feedback during the bidding process and may revise their bids accordingly. A revelation policy in iterative multiattribute auctions determines the information bidders receive about the buyer's scoring rule and about the state of competition before and during the auction process [18].

The present work addresses a major barrier to the adoption of multiattribute auctions in procurement practice. Buyers face a dilemma with respect to revealing information about their scoring function to participating suppliers: On the one hand, buyers have an interest in revealing as little information about their preferences as possible. The bid-takers' reasoning is that suppliers exploit revealed information to shift gains from trade from the buy side to the supply side. On the other hand, suppliers often cannot readily make sensible assumptions about the buyer's scoring rule with respect to the non-price attributes [11]. Even if the direction of preference for each non-price attribute can be inferred from the procurement situation, it remains unknown to the suppliers how the buyer trades off among the attributes. In particular, suppliers cannot identify ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids in the light of their own production costs.

It is, however, possible to utilize the information feedback in iterative multiattribute auctions to design a policy that reveals only the direction of preference, in the form of the monotonicity constraints of the buyer's scoring function, to the suppliers prior to the auction (limited information revelation policy), and theoretically leads to ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes identical to a policy in which the scoring rule is known to the suppliers (full information revelation policy) (cf. Section 3.4). Still, if guidance by the concise scoring rule is missing, the cognitive efforts of suppliers to identify an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bid and, hence, the cost of bidding are multiplied [25]. Since bidder behavior often deviates from theoretical predictions [15] and the effects of changes in the information policy are usually not predictable by established theories [18], a computer-based laboratory experiment designed to compare the economic performance of a multiattribute reverse

English auction in the cases of limited and full information revelation is conducted. The experimental study is based on the hypothesis that—contrary to theoretical prediction—the limited information revelation policy leads, in practical applications, to a lower allocational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency compared to a full information revelation policy and that, hence, the two policies do not lead to identical and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes.

## 2. Related work

In iterative multiattribute auctions such as the English institution, the multidimensionality of bids in combination with the instant information feedback allows for the design of different information revelation policies. Before an auction, the buyer may, for example, opt for complete revelation of her scoring rule, or reveal only limited information. A range of options concerning the information feedback during an auction exists depending on whether the scoring rule is announced or not [18]. Bidders may receive information on the actual value bid on each attribute if the scoring rule is disclosed, or learn the percentage of buyer's utility of submitted bids relative to the highest bid received so far [17].

Bichler is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to experimentally study multiattribute English auctions [4]. Of the few laboratory studies on multiattribute auctions, Koppius resp. Koppius and Van Heck investigate the issue of information revelation [17,19]. Their experimental design involves two different information revelation policies in a multiround variant of the English auction with three-dimensional bids and four bidders. The buyer's scoring rule is not disclosed. Information revealed to the bidders after each round either shows only the highest-score bid received so far and its bidder's identity (“restricted” information policy) or reveals all bids submitted in a round together with their percentage of buyer's utility relative to the highest-score bid submitted so far (“unrestricted” information policy). They <sup>fi</sup>nd that revealing more information about the bid-taker's preferences and the other bidders' submissions signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and decreases the number of Pareto-improving bids remaining at the end of an auction. However, it does not affect the number of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes. Relative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of auction outcomes is surprisingly low under the restricted policy with only 21% allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes and 63% efficiency in terms of actual gains from trade realized. In the restricted case, 31% of the auction outcomes are Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and Pareto inef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes leave ample room for improvements with an average of 42 weakly Pareto-improving bids. These results deviate severely from other <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the standard and multiattribute English auction [4,15]. Apparently, subjects had great dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in locating their Pareto optimal combination of non-price attributes under “restricted information”. One explanation offered by the authors is that the <sup>fi</sup>xed limitation of two and four auction rounds may have prevented bidders from locating their Pareto optimal bid. In contrast to the multiround English auction variant, the bidding procedure considered here is based on real-time bid submission instead of sequential bidding rounds; bidders are neither forced nor prevented from bidding; and may submit any number of bids.

Chen-Ritzo et al. compare a three-dimensional reverse English auction with a corresponding price-only auction in the laboratory and <sup>fi</sup>nd that outcome performance depends on bidder experience and that bidders deviate from the dominant strategy prediction in both auctions [7]. In their multiattribute setting, the buyer's scoring function is not revealed to the bidders. Instead, each bidder receives information on (i) how much he must lower the price given the levels of non-monetary attributes of the submitted bid in order to obtain a score approximately equal to the current standing bid; and (ii) on the buyer's marginal utility for improvements in both non-price attributes at the levels of the submitted bid. With inexperienced bidders, allocational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency ranges from 25 to 75% depending on the set of induced preferences used. With experienced bidders, the multiattribute auction is ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in three of the four parameter sets. The authors <sup>fi</sup>nd that experienced bidders achieve 100% Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in one parameter set and generate high degrees of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the other settings. Inexperienced bidders achieve a slightly lower degree of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, yet still close to the theoretical maximum. The limited information revelation policy chosen in the present experiment differs from Chen-Ritzo et al.'s multiattribute setting in that bidders receive indicative verbal information on the monotonicity constraints of the buyer's scoring rule prior to the auction and learn about the standing bid's attribute values.

Experimental evidence on English forward price auctions regularly shows convergence to the dominant strategy price. Overbidding occurs only rarely [8] and is only observed in the <sup>fi</sup>rst rounds of a sequence of auctions [16]. This observation is explained by the fact, that if a bidder overbids and wins, he receives a negative payoff and immediately recognizes his mistake and in subsequent rounds will not bid above his valuation. Moreover, bidders may learn during the course of bidding by observing the standing bids and by relating that information to their own, private information considering their pro<sup>fi</sup>t or loss if winning without incurring any costs. The latter is denoted as “observational learning” due to the instant information feedback. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in experimental investigations of standard price-only auctions is measured by the number of auction outcomes, in which the highest value (or—in reverse auctions—lowest cost) bidder is awarded the contract, and by the percentage of the gains from trade actually realized. Earlier laboratory studies repeatedly report close to 100% ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the English forward price auction [15]. The present experiment extends previous studies in two respects: It studies a generalization of the standard English (<sup>fi</sup>rst-price, open-outcry) auction to the multiattribute case in a procurement setting, i.e., a multiattribute reverse English auction.

## 3. Experiment

## 3.1. Environment

A buyer (bid-taker, auctioneer) seeks to acquire an indivisible object from exactly one of <sup>fi</sup>ve potential suppliers (bidders), $i \in \mathcal { T } = \{ 1$ …5}. The problem is referred to as single sourcing and occurs frequently in the context of government and corporate (electronic) procurement [3,5]. The buyer announces the intention to acquire the object and asks invited suppliers to submit bids on three negotiable attributes of the object: the price p and two non-price attributes, x and y. Each of the two non-price attributes can take on a discrete, abstract quality level out of a set of six quality levels: x $\mathcal { X } = \{ 1 , . . . , 6 \} \mathrm { a n d } y \in \mathcal { Y } = \{ 1 , . . . 6 \}$ . The quality levels can be thought of as a measure of reliability, warranty or tolerances, but are deliberately kept abstract throughout the experiment to avert preconceptions on part of experimental subjects [13]. Attribute levels have discrete values to render bidding manageable. Both settings are common in laboratory auction experiments [15]. A technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (x, y) of the object is a combination of quality levels of the two non-price attributes in the set of feasible technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations $( x , y ) \in \mathcal { X } \times \mathcal { Y }$ . The price p is a nonnegative integer: $p \in \mathcal { P } = [ 0 , 1 5 0 ]$ . A bid b comprises a technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and a price, $ { \mathbf { b } } = ( x , y , p ) \in \mathcal { X } \times \mathcal { Y } \times \mathcal { P } .$ . The buyer perceives the discrete quality levels in each non-price attribute as measures of increasing quality. Other attributes being equal, she strictly prefers a higher quality over a lower quality in each non-price attribute, but she is also willing to accept a lower quality level in one non-price attribute for a higher quality level in the other attribute and vice versa. The higher the quality levels, the less additional value is created for the buyer. Thus, the buyer may impose a high value on some level of quality, but does not demand perfect quality. The buyer's valuation function $\iota \colon \mathcal { X } \times \mathcal { Y } \longrightarrow \mathbb { R }$ increases in both x and y and contains a preferential trade-off among the two nonprice attributes. Moreover, the buyer trades off price off against quality demanding a lower price for a lower quality and being willing to pay a higher price for a higher quality. A change in price is applied linearly to the utility function to facilitate understanding on part of experimental subjects. In the experiment, the buyer's utility function is given by

$$
s (x, y, p) = v (x, y) - p.\tag{1}
$$

Each supplier has an initial technology, knowledge and commodity endowment adequate to produce any technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation in the set of feasible technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. The suppliers' initial endowments are <sup>fi</sup>xed throughout the bidding process. A supplier i's production cost function $c _ { i } \colon X \times Y {  } \mathbb { R } , i { = } 1 , . . . , 5$ is increasing in both x and y. The higher the level of quality the more additional costs each supplier incurs. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects production environments, in which the cost of providing high quality increases overproportionally with higher quality, for example when manufacturing parts to very high tolerances or providing extremely short lead times. In the experiment, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of supplier i is given by

$$
u _ {i} (x, y, p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} p - c _ {i} (x, y) & , \text {   if   } i \text {   supplies   the   object } \\ 0 & , \text {   otherwise. } \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

In the experiment, a multiattribute reverse English auction is investigated [11]. The institution constitutes an iterative bidding procedure in which each bidder is eligible to submit multiple subsequent bids. Each submitted bid b is evaluated and validated by the buyer against a scoring rule s(b). At any point in time, the bid b\* that achieves the highest buyer's utility is publicly announced as the current high bid. If a bid b′ is submitted which provides a higher score $( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , s ( \mathbf { b } ^ { \prime } ) { > } s$ $( \mathbf { b } ^ { * } ) )$ , b′ becomes the new high bid $( \boldsymbol { \mathbf { b } } ^ { * } ; = \boldsymbol { \mathbf { b } } ^ { \prime } )$ . The auction ends if no new high bid is received for 120 s. The bidder î̂ who submits the bid with the highest score is awarded the contract of the bid-taker. Producing and delivering the object to the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the winning bid leads to the payment of the bid price by the buyer. The payoff of the winning bidder î̂ is $\hat { p } - c _ { l } ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } )$ ; the other bidders do not produce and sell and receive a zero payoff.

The following analysis assumes that the scoring rule re<sup>fl</sup>ects the buyer's true preferences and thus coincides with the buyer's true utility function. Misrepresentation of preferences is not subject to the present experiment (for a discussion, cf. [3,6,11]). It is further assumed that ex post qualities are monitored and correspond to ex ante promised qualities [9]. The buyer is committed to the scoring rule and suppliers' bids are <sup>fi</sup>rm. Furthermore, bidding itself is not costly.

## 3.2. Performance measures

The supplier selected by the auction institution to supply the object is denoted by î̂ and the transaction price and the delivered technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation by p̂ and $\left( \hat { x } , \hat { y } \right)$ , respectively. Hence, an outcome ô is denoted by $\hat { \boldsymbol { o } } = ( \hat { \imath } , \hat { x } , \hat { y } , \hat { p } ) \in \mathcal { T } \times \mathcal { X } \times \mathcal { Y } \times \mathcal { P } .$ . The social welfare (total value) of an outcome ô is given by the sum of the buyer's and the selected winning supplier's surplus [23]. It follows from Eqs. (1) and (2) that the social welfare depends on the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (x̂,ŷ) and the supplier î, but not on the transaction price p̂, since $w ( \hat { \imath } , \hat { x } , \hat { y } , \hat { p } )$ $= s ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } , \hat { p } ) + u _ { \hat { l } } ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } , \hat { p } ) = \nu ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } ) - \hat { p } + \hat { p } - c _ { \hat { l } } ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } ) = w ( \hat { \imath } , \hat { x } , \hat { y } )$ . The price p̂ represents a transfer between the winning bidder and the bidtaker and does not affect the social welfare, since both the buyer's and the suppliers' utilities are linear in price.

To ensure comparability of results, individual auction outcomes are standardized. The maximum social welfare of a standardized outcome is denoted $\overline { { w } } ( \bar { l } , \bar { x } , \bar { y } )$ and set to $\overline { { w } } = 7 7 . 0$ adjusting the value of the buyer's utility score and the transaction price of the auction outcome accordingly. The maximum achievable social welfare for each auction was randomly drawn from a uniform distribution over [64,89] and rounded to the nearest integer before the experiment, i.e. w̄ is set as upper median. Note that this standardization does not affect the value of the suppliers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of the respective outcome. However, due to the standardization, prices are stated with one decimal place in the analysis, although in the experiment, prices were restricted to integer values. Furthermore, the standardized buyer's utility shown in the analysis may be negative, despite the limitations in the auction institution.

A sole focus on maximizing the buyer's utility may chase suppliers away in the long run, which is counterproductive in the procurement case where repeated buying is key [23]. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency has therefore been promoted as primary objective in the design of multiattribute auction institutions for the application to corporate procurement [24]. The relative performance of an auction is measured by the allocational and Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of an auction outcome ${ \hat { o } } .$ An outcome ô is called (allocational) efficient if and only if the auction outcome ô maximizes the social welfare, i.e., $( \hat { l } , \hat { x } , \hat { y } ) { \in } { \arg \operatorname* { m a x } } _ { ( i , x , y ) } \left\{ \nu ( x , y ) - c _ { i } ( x , y ) \right\}$ } among all <sup>fi</sup>ve suppliers. Note that whether an outcome is ef<sup>fi</sup>cient depends on the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation $( { \hat { x } } , { \hat { y } } )$ and the winning supplier l̂, but not on the transaction price ${ \hat { p } } .$ Allocational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is measured in terms of deviations of actual outcomes (<sup>fi</sup>nal allocations) from maximum achievable social welfare with misallocations representing foregone gains from trade. The relative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of an outcome is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the actual realized social welfare as a percentage of the potential maximum social welfare, $\mathrm { R E } = w ( \hat { l } , \hat { x } , \hat { y } ) / \overline { { w } } ( \bar { l } , \bar { x } , \bar { y } )$ . Larger values of RE indicate a higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

An outcome ô is called Pareto efficient if and only if the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation $( \hat { x } , \hat { y } )$ supplied by the selected supplier maximizes the social welfare given the buyer's valuation function and the selected supplier î̂'s production costs schedule, $\mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , ( \hat { x } , \hat { y } )$ )<sub>2</sub>arg $\mathrm { m a x } _ { ( x , y ) } \{ \nu ( x , y ) -$ $\textstyle c _ { \hat { l } } ( x , y ) \}$ . Note that the buyer's valuation function and suppliers' production costs schedules have been prepared, so that there is exactly one Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation $( x ^ { \prime } , y ^ { \prime } ) _ { i }$ for each supplier i I in an auction. Given his cost function c (x, y) and the buyer's valuation function $v ( x , y )$ , the maximum welfare supplier i is able to create is denoted as supplier i's pseudotype k<sub>i</sub> with $k _ { i } = \boldsymbol { \mathrm { m a x } } _ { ( x , y ) } \{ \nu ( x , \boldsymbol { \mathrm { ~ } }$ $y ) - c _ { i } ( x , y ) ]$ }.

If an auction's outcome is inef<sup>fi</sup>cient, either a combination of nonprice attributes exist that would make both the buyer and the supplier better off (the outcome is not Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient) or the buyer could pro<sup>fi</sup>t from choosing another supplier (the outcome is not allocationally ef<sup>fi</sup>cient) or both. In case of a Pareto inef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcome, the number of Pareto-improving bids is employed as a measure following [17]. NPareto denotes the number of weakly Pareto-improving bids, i.e., the number of bids that provide the buyer with a utility at least as high as the utility of the actual winning bid and at the same time provide the winning supplier with a pro<sup>fi</sup>t at least as high as the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the actual winning bid. NParPlus denotes the number of strictly Pareto-improving bids—the number of bids that provide the buyer with a utility higher than the utility of the actual winning bid and at the same time provide the winning supplier with a pro<sup>fi</sup>t higher than the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the actual winning bid.

To investigate whether group or individual idiosyncrasies account for the Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes, the individual and group performances are computed. An individual bidder's relative performance is given by the percentage of the number of auction rounds in which a bidder submits a Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bid in relation to the maximum number of auction rounds. Individual bidders are grouped by identical sequences of production cost schedules. The group performance is measured by the number of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids submitted by a group of subjects during a session in relation to the maximum number of winning bids.

## 3.3. Experimental design

In the experiment, two treatments are investigated. In treatment L (auction with limited information revelation), the buyer does not reveal the concise scoring rule s(b). Instead, the buyer publicly announces that:

“The valuation of an offer by the buyer has the following characteristics:

• Given identical qualitative attributes, the buyer prefers the bid with the lower price.

• The buyer prefers a higher quality level to a lower quality level within a qualitative attribute—all other attributes being equal.

• A lower quality level in one qualitative attribute can be compensated by a higher quality level in the other qualitative attribute.

• Likewise it is possible to compensate a lower quality level by a lower price and a higher price by a higher quality, respectively.

You do, however, not know to what degree the buyer trades off the three attributes.”

Since the buyer's scoring rule is not known by the suppliers and suppliers do not receive information on the score of the current high bid, bidders may submit bids which achieve a score lower than the score provided by the current high bid. If a bidder turns in such a bid, the bid is rejected and an appropriate message is sent to the respective bidder (but not to any other bidder). Note that bidders are not restricted by their or other bidders' previous bids with respect to the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and the price they offer in a (future) bid. Fig. 1(a) shows the bidding screen in treatment L. The right-hand panel contains the bid submission interface. The bidder selects a quality level for each qualitative attribute from the drop-down lists and enters a price. The software automatically calculates the production costs given the three attributes and the potential pro<sup>fi</sup>t in case of winning the auction with that bid.

Contrary to treatment L, the buyer reveals the scoring rule in treatment F (auction with full information revelation), and bidders are prevented from submitting bids, which do not provide the buyer with a score higher than the current high bid. In addition to the verbal information given in treatment L, the bidders receive a printout of the buyer's valuation for each technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and a printout of the buyer's score for each technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation in a price range between p=41 and p=150. The bidding screen in treatment F contains a decision support tool, which a bidder may use to calculate the score of a triple (x, y, p). The left-hand panel in Fig. 1(b) allows the performance of the calculation. If it results in a valid bid, one where the calculated score is higher than the score of current best bid, the transfer button becomes active and the bidder is able to transfer the current triple to the bid submission interface in the right-hand panel. The functionality of the bid submission interface is analogous to treatment L.

In both treatments, the standing bid as well as the remaining time for bid submission is permanently shown to the bidder in the panel labeled “Current high bid” (cf. Fig. 1). Note, however, that the score of the standing bid is not shown in treatment L. During the bidding phase, bidders have access to the history of standing bids in the present trading period. During the entire experimental session, subjects also have access to their experiment account balance (current payoff) and to the history of winning bids in previous trading periods.

Each treatment is investigated in eight experimental sessions. In every session, <sup>fi</sup>ve experimental subjects participate in six consecutive procurement auctions (rounds or trading periods) of the same treatment. Each of the <sup>fi</sup>ve subjects takes on the role of a supplier. Subjects know that the buyer is not physically present during an experiment session but built into the experiment software. In the experiment, the buyer's utility and the suppliers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are induced through a reward mechanism as is customary in experimental economics [27]: At the beginning of a session, each subject is credited a show-up fee of 10.00 experimental currency units (ECU) to an experiment account. The minimum bid price increment is one ECU. In each of the six consecutive auctions, the payoff of the winning bidder is tallied on the experiment account: A loss is deducted from the balance, while a pro<sup>fi</sup>t is credited to the account. The preset equilibrium payoff of the winning bidder ranges from 3.30 to 4.70 ECU. At the end of a session, the account balance is converted to Euro at a rate of one ECU to one Euro. Both the buyer's valuations and suppliers' cost schedules are constructed by the experimenter to guarantee a unique equilibrium outcome and adequate variation between auction rounds. The subjects do not know the underlying functions and are only provided with printouts of tabulated discrete values. In each of the six consecutive auctions within a session, the buyer has a different valuation for technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and each supplier is assigned a different production cost schedule. The suppliers' production cost schedules and the buyer's valuation functions were randomized a priori and varied between auctions, but the sequence of cost schedules was kept identical across sessions in order to ensure comparability of results. In other words, in every session the same production cost schedules and buyer's valuations are deployed in the same sequence.

The number of competing suppliers and the number of consecutive auctions are public information. The bidders also know that in each auction only one supplier is designated the contract by the buyer and that only the winning bidder makes a pro<sup>fi</sup>t or loss. Throughout the experiment, the production cost schedules remain private to the respective supplier and a supplier is provided no information about the competing suppliers' production costs. The subjects remain anonymous during an experimental session and communication among subjects is not permitted. The history of winning bids is available to all bidders, but the identity of the winning or any other bidder is not revealed. It is thus not possible for the subjects to identify which competitor submitted a bid or is awarded the contract of the buyer.

The experiment was conducted at Universität Karlsruhe (TH) and carried out in German to facilitate comprehension by subjects and to reduce language-born misunderstandings of the instructions and computer screens. Subjects were randomly drawn from a pool of 500+ student volunteers. The subjects were undergraduate and graduate students from different disciplines with a majority from the Department of Economics and Business Engineering. None of the subjects had ever participated in a sourcing experiment before and none of the subjects participated repeatedly. Upon arrival at the laboratory, each subject was randomly seated at a visually isolated computer terminal. Sixteen sessions were conducted in which 80 different subjects participated. The subjects received written instructions which were read aloud by a research assistant prior to the bidding. Before the session started, each subject had to answer an extensive questionnaire about the rules of the auctions and the experiment. At the end of an experimental session, the subjects were paid privately in cash according to their <sup>fi</sup>nal account balance. The average earning of a subject was 13.33 Euros including the showup fee and a session lasted on average about 95 min including reading the instructions and answering the questionnaire.

## 3.4. Equilibrium forecasts

The experiment is conducted under the assumptions of a private values model in that each bidder knows his costs with certainty but has no information about other bidders' valuations ([20], p. 3). Contrary to literature on the independent private values model (e.g. [21]), bidders do not know the distribution from which the other bidders' costs (valuations) are drawn. The institution investigated in this experiment is an adaptation of the standard English auction to the multiattribute case [11]. It has been shown that under the independent private values model the English price auction has a dominant strategy regardless of the number of bidders, risk attitude of bidders, or the distribution of private values [15]. Given the environment in Section 3.1, bidders have a dominant strategy in both treatments, even though subjects are not informed about the distribution of other bidders' costs [10,31]. The relevant difference between both treatments pertains to the fact that a bidder is unable to identify his Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation when only the set of monotonicity constraints is known in treatment L. In that case, a rational bidder is expected to completely enumerate feasible bids and order them by decreasing potential pro<sup>fi</sup>t. His dominant strategy is then to (repeatedly) bid that bid from his bid list which provides him with the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t and which is still feasible according to the set of monotonicity constraints and to the iteratively and cumulatively increasing information about the buyer's scoring rule (available through ever new standing bids). He quits the auction if no feasible and pro<sup>fi</sup>table bid remains in his bid list. Unlike in treatment L, a rational bidder is expected to (repeatedly) bid the Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation $( x ^ { \prime } , y ^ { \prime } ) _ { i }$ at the maximum price that is just low enough to outbid the current high bid until the score of the high bid exceeds his pseudotype k<sub>i</sub> in treatment F. It follows immediately that both revelation policies lead to the same outcome. In equilibrium, the bidder with the second-highest pseudotype drops out the auction as soon as the standing bid exceeds his pseudotype. The bidder with the highest pseudotype is awarded the contract at a score equal to the secondhighest pseudotype (subject to deviations of up to the minimum bid increment). Considering the cognitive efforts required to apply the dominant strategy under the condition of limited information revelation, it seems likely that bidders will tend to “explore” the attribute space by bidding different technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations at gradually decreasing prices in a trial-and-error manner. It is unlikely that this pragmatic approach leads to the same outcome as the English auction with full information on the scoring rule in treatment F. The comparison between both treatments is therefore based on the hypotheses that the limited information revelation policy in the multiattribute English auction leads—contrary to theoretical predictions— to a lower social welfare, and to differences in buyer's and suppliers surpluses.

Contrary to the dominant strategy predictions, the experimental data shows that not all accepted bids offer the Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the respective bidder in treatment F: Only about 46% of the accepted bids involve the Pareto optimal attribute bundle (cf. Apparently, the iterative nature of the bidding process accounts for the selection of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids. The observation of <sup>fi</sup>ve Pareto inef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes indicates, however, that some bidders fail to offer their Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Although bidders do not know about their Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, it is nevertheless interesting from the perspective of the buyer to analyze the Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of bids in treatment L to learn about the magnitude of the effect of limited information revelation. The iterative nature of the English auction in treatment L accounts for the elicitation of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids disregarding the bidders' knowledge about the Pareto optimality. Those bidders whose current high bid does not offer their Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation are outbid and forced to submit another bid from the bid list. This process leads to less and less non-Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids throughout the course of bidding even though the bidders do not obtain information on the Pareto optimality of their bids. There is not one session in which all of the last four auction rounds are Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. In fact, only one session is observed with three Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes. Despite the institution's ability to elicit Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient accepted bids, it cannot force a bidder to stay in the auction until the bid list is exhausted. The remaining two overbid bids were submitted in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round. It seems that overbidding depends on prior bidding experience and vanishes with increasing experience. The number of early withdrawals is also surprisingly low in treatment L. Recall that the subjects' efforts to locate a candidate for a new high bid from the bid list are far larger than in treatment F. Nevertheless, the number of early withdrawals, which can be interpreted as a subject's capitulation confronting the efforts, is close to triple the number of the other treatments.

Although it is not possible to specify the exact number of subjects withdrawing from an auction before exhausting the bid list, the following calculation relates the magnitude of underbidding to the theoretical maximum: Given that in 32 rounds with <sup>fi</sup>ve subjects participating in each round, out of a total of 160 possible withdrawals, only 21 occured in the last four rounds, two conclusions can be drawn: (1) the incentives to perform the calculations and to stay in the auction until the bid list is empty must have been suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and (2) the information provided to the subjects was suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to perform the calculations.

Conclusion 4. The limited information revelation policy does not lead to a complete collapse of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the studied multiattribute reverse English auction. The iterative nature of the English auction accounts for the elicitation of Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>cient bids even disregarding the bidders' knowledge about Pareto optimality. However, observed Pareto improvements are stochastically greater with limited information. Overbidding is rarely observed and disappears with increasing experience. Early withdrawals from the auction are clearly higher than in the benchmark treatment, which is not surprising considering the efforts to identify a candidate for a new high bid from the bid list. In fact, the low absolute number of early withdrawals is remarkable and indicates a high motivation of subjects. Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency depends on the performance of individual bidders, but not on group idiosyncrasies or assignment to cost schedule sequences.

One can only speculate about the subjects' actual strategies, but the above results indicate that a considerable number of bidders actually adopted the dominant strategy and some of those, who initially followed a trial-and-error scheme, were drawn to the dominant strategy by the auction institution.

## 5. Summary and discussion

The laboratory study compares a full and a limited information revelation policy in a multiattribute reverse English auction under the assumption of a private values model. Information is revealed by means of indicative verbal information on the direction of the buyer's preferences under the limited information revelation policy, and by additional tabulated printouts of the buyer's utility for combinations of the two non-price attributes and the price in the full information revelation scenario. In the latter case, a decision support tool is embedded in the bidding screen. The revelation of information about the buyer's scoring rule and the information feedback provided to bidders during the bidding process has proven critical to the outcome performance in the multiattribute reverse English auction.

The study <sup>fi</sup>nds that revealing the buyer's scoring rule leads to a higher allocative and Pareto ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the studied multiattribute reverse English auction, which documents a behavioral breakdown of the predicted outcome equivalence of the two auction institutions. This result is in line with earlier experimental evidence on a multiround variant of the English auction in the sense that revealing more information about the buyer's preferences increases allocational ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency [17,19].

The behavioral breakdown is attributed to the limited information bidders receive about the buyer's scoring rule, which implies, in particular, that bidders are unable to identify their Pareto optimal technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and do not receive information about the Pareto optimality of bids during the auction process. Yet, limited information revelation does not lead to a collapse of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the English auction. The multiattribute English auction format investigated has proved to be particularly robust with respect to limited information revelation. The iterative auction process accounts for a comparatively high level of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency even if only the set of monotonicity constraints is known by the bidders. This result is promising, especially for applications where the disclosure of the scoring rule is not desirable or feasible.

However, revealing the scoring rule, including the preferential tradeoffs among attributes, has been shown to lead to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher social welfare and to a higher number of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient outcomes. The analysis of the distribution of the gains from trade between the bid-taker and the bidders reveals that suppliers successfully use the additional information about the buyer's scoring rule and make weakly signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. The buyer does not seem to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from revealing full information, but the results indicate that the suppliers do not gain at the expense of the buyer.

In practical applications of multiattribute English auctions, the effect of full information revelation on buyer's utility is probably indirect: The present study ignores the (transaction) costs of participating in a procurement auction. Given the complexity of multiattribute bidding procedures, participation is presumably costly in terms of time and labor. By revealing complete information about the scoring rule, a buyer can make the auction more attractive for suppliers, since the information revelation adds value to the market and thus makes the market more attractive for suppliers' entrance. Attracting more suppliers to compete in an auction increases the level of competition, which has been shown to increase the auctioneer's revenue in standard price auctions [22] and is therefore likely to increase the auctioneer's utility in a multiattribute reverse English auction as well.

Future paths for research on information revelation in multiattribute auctions might include the investigation of further revelation policies in order to establish the degree of information revelation recommended to render the auction attractive to both the buyer and the suppliers. Since the effects of subtle changes in the information policy are not always predictable by established theories, further laboratory experimentation could help to isolate the impact of different information policies on the auction outcome [1].
