# SUPPLY-SIDE NETWORK EFFECTS AND THE DEVELOPMENT OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY STANDARDS<sup>1</sup>

Juha Uotila Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL UNITED KINGDOM {juha.uotila@wbs.ac.uk}

Thomas Keil Department of Business Administration, University of Zurich, Seilergraben 53, CH-8001 Zurich SWITZERLAND {thomas.keil@business.uzh.ch}

Markku Maula Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Aalto University, P.O. Box 15500, FI-00076 Aalto FINLAND {markku.maula@aalto.fi}

Standards are central to many information technology (IT) applications, and the development processes for these standards play a key role in the evolution of information systems (IS). We model the development of IT standards by technology suppliers as a coevolutionary technological search process under supply-side network effects, and we examine how the characteristics of the standards development process influence its outcomes. In line with common intuition and prior research, we find that perfect coordination among suppliers generally facilitates convergence on the best available standard. However, for complex IT standards, we make a novel contribution and find that this “best available” standard may be inferior to alternative, undiscovered solutions because coordination may lead to an overly narrow search. Consistent with prior research, we also find that either highly influential organizations or highly influential alliances and consortia can coordinate standard selection in order to prevent network effects from generating lock-in to an inferior option and to help set the best of the known alternatives as the standard. However, in contrast to the previous literature, we find that when coordination is imperfect and controlled by a moderately influential organization or consortium, it may lead to a technological lock-in dynamic in which suppliers adhere to an inferior solution and are subsequently unable to reverse this commitment, even when a technologically superior alternative emerges later in the search process. Further, we reveal the following paradox involving intellectual property rights (IPR) and related imitation costs: Although imitation costs can lead to the emergence of multiple standards, thereby reducing social welfare in the short term, this effect may have long-term benefits by broadening the search for future generations of a standard.

Keywords: Network effects, technological search, lock-in, simulation, standards

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) standards are sets of specifications for communicating or performing actions that ensure that various technologies or products that implement certain specifications are compatible (e.g., David and Greenstein 1990; Lyytinen et al. 2008; Nickerson and zur Muehlen 2006; Weitzel et al. 2006). Such standards are ubiquitous and central to the effective operation and performance of information systems (IS) (Lyytinen and King 2006; Venkatesh and Bala 2012) and range from the specifications of the physical and electrical properties of IT infrastructure, such as the X.25 standard, all the way to those standards of interorganizational systems that facilitate organizational integration in an industry, such as the OGC standards (Zhao and Xia 2014).

IT standards offer a broad range of benefits for both IT suppliers and users by ensuring compatibility and interoperability among heterogeneous IS within and across firms (Venkatesh and Bala 2012). As a result, IT standards help promote and direct innovation in IT industries (David and Steinmueller 1994) and can even reshape the structure of whole industries (Wigand et al. 2005). Concurrently, setting IT standards also results in considerable challenges that must be considered by both the creators and adopters of such standards (Chen and Forman 2006; Markus et al. 2006; Shapiro and Varian 1999; Zhao and Xia 2014). Of particular interest is the risk that an industry may establish and be locked into a technologically inefficient standard (Arthur 1989; Backhouse et al. 2006), which can hinder the introduction of new and potentially superior alternatives. Moreover, such a development can limit the evolution of an entire IT field, such as in the case of the many firms in the early personal computer industry that were frustrated by the need to conform to standards created by the leading industry players—mainly IBM, Intel, and Microsoft—which were generally considered to be suboptimal (Ferguson and Morris 1993).

Given their importance for IT suppliers and users alike, IT standards have emerged as the focus of a substantial body of literature. Most IS research on standards has focused on the diffusion and adoption of standards (Adomavicius et al. 2012; Lyytinen and King 2006) and has examined firm strategies that promote a standard (e.g., Gallaugher and Wang 2002; Lin and Kulatilaka 2006; Liu et al. 2011); processes that govern standards diffusion such as network effects, herd behavior, and adaptive learning (Sun 2013; Weitzel et al. 2006; Zhu et al. 2006); and the effects of standards adoption on the adopting firms’ processes and operations (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2012; Bala and Venkatesh 2007; Wang 2010; Zhao and Xia 2014). Relatively less research effort has been expended on understanding how candidates for standards emerge in the first place and the factors that influence these standardsdevelopment processes (Lyytinen and King 2006). The limited previous research in this field has often been descriptive and has focused primarily on a limited number of processual issues, such as participation in standards committees (Zhao et al. 2011), which is somewhat surprising, given the profound influence that the development processes of IT standards can have on standard-setting outcomes.

Take, for example, the emergence of the Bluetooth standard for wireless data exchange over short distances. The history of Bluetooth dates to the early 1990s, when several electronics, IT, and telecommunications firms experimented with alternative technologies that enabled the wireless connection of devices such as mobile phones, laptops, and peripherals. However, rather than developing full-fledged technological solutions that would compete in the product market to become the de facto standard, a consortium of firms attracted other players to abandon their search for alternative technological solutions and to join in the development of a common specification long before any product could be introduced to the market, creating network effects among the suppliers that resulted in a bandwagon lined up behind the emerging standards candidate. Thus, the Bluetooth standard had gained a dominant position during the technology development stage, long before it was introduced to the market (Keil 2002). A study that focuses on the diffusion and adoption of the Bluetooth standard and the network effects created by technology adopters after market introduction would miss many of the decisive events that, for instance, led to the absence of competing standards entering the market.

In this paper, we theorize about standards development prior to market introduction, often referred to as anticipatory standardization (Lyytinen and King 2006), by focusing on the development of candidates for standards in the presence of supply-side network effects: positive externalities from additional suppliers that join a technological development path in support of a standards candidate under development. Although network effects have traditionally been conceptualized as a demand-side phenomenon (Besen and Farrell 1994; Economides 1996a; Farrell and Saloner 1986; Katz and Shapiro 1985, 1994), recent studies have increasingly begun to consider network effects in a more general sense and have thus incorporated supplier networks into research and theorizing (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 2013; Hagiu 2006; Suarez 2005). In line with this literature, we define network effects as the “positive externalities in which participants’ utility increases with the number of other network participants” (Park et al. 2004, p. 10) that are applicable to networks of both end users and suppliers.

We conduct a simulation study that models IT standard setting as a coevolutionary search process in which the various supplier organizations search for alternative technological solutions in the technology landscape (Fleming and Sorenson 2001; Kauffman 1993; Levinthal 1997), while being influenced by supply-side network effects. In our analysis, we revisit the longstanding question of how to achieve maximum social efficiency in terms of the quality of the technological standards that emerge from standard-setting processes (David and Greenstein 1990; Farrell and Klemperer 2007; Farrell and Saloner 1985; Katz and Shapiro 1986). In addition, we examine how the outcome of the standard-setting process is impacted by the characteristics of the underlying technology, the composition of the population of supplier organizations, the level of coordination among the suppliers, and the imitation costs related to intellectual property rights (IPR).

In line with common intuition and prior research, we find that perfect coordination among suppliers, such as by means of the leadership of influential decision makers or through industrywide consortia, typically facilitates the selection of the best available candidate as the standard. However, for complex IT standards, in particular, we make a novel contribution and find that this “best available” standard that is selected may in fact be inferior to alternative, undiscovered solutions because coordination may lead to an overly narrow search for alternatives. Furthermore, when coordination is imperfect and controlled by a moderately influential firm or consortium, it may lead to a technological lock-in dynamic that it subsequently cannot reverse—even as a technologically superior alternative emerges. We further find that the imitation costs that are associated with formal and informal intellectual property protection (Levin et al. 1987; Sheremata 2004) may lead to a trade-off between the short term and the long term. In the short term, imitation costs may lead to multiple incompatible standards and consequently reduce social welfare. However, over the long run, imitation costs may yield social benefits over multiple generations of a standard because they maintain the diversity that is essential for a sustained broad search and ultimately for the socially optimal evolution of standards over multiple generations of technology.

Our research makes three important contributions to the literature on IT standards and standards at large. First, whereas prior research on IT standards has focused mostly on the diffusion and adoption of IT standards, we develop a complementary theoretical perspective that focuses on supply-side network effects and their interaction with the technological search in shaping the development of IT standards prior to market introduction. Our results suggest that suboptimal lock-in may emerge from supply-side network effects, from myopic technological search, and from their interaction. Second, our results enhance the understanding of the coordination effects of influential organizations and standards consortia during the standards-development process. In particular, with regard to complex IT standards, influential organizations and consortia not only can negatively impact the quality of an emerging standard by overly narrowing the search but can also contribute to early lock-in, while lacking the strength to break such lock-in if superior technological alternatives emerge at a later stage. Finally, we identify the following paradox involving IPR-related imitation costs: although seemingly beneficial in facilitating convergence to a single standard in the short run, low or no imitation costs may lead to substantial long-run costs by restricting the diversity of technologies explored and thereby reducing the quality of a standard over multiple generations. Thus, setting a low IPR cost may allow for a Trojan horse strategy in which firms or groups of firms may tie markets to an ultimately inferior IT standard for multiple generations.

## The Literature on IT Standards

Given the importance of IT standards to both creators and adopters of standards, a substantial literature on IT standards has emerged. The most relevant contributions of this literature are summarized in Appendix A. This literature can be roughly divided into three themes: the impact, diffusion, and creation of IT standards.

## The Impact of IT Standards

The literature on the impact of IT standards has broadly addressed the significance of IT standards for both the firms that create these standards and those that adopt them. The creators of proprietary standards may gain a monopoly position and competitive advantage that translates into substantial economic benefits, as the case of Microsoft for PC operating systems highlights (Farrell and Saloner 1985; Katz and Shapiro 1985; Lyytinen and King 2006; West and Dedrick 2000), and even open standards can confer significant competitive benefits to their creators (Chen and Forman 2006). In addition, it has been widely argued that IT standards provide substantial benefits for their adopting firms. IT standards are central to the integration of heterogeneous IS within organizations and thereby reduce the cost of managing IT infrastructure (Chen and Forman 2006). Interorganizational system and process standards provide compatibility and interoperability across organizations and enable adopters to manage value chain activities across firms and achieve substantial performance gains (Bala and Venkatesh 2007; Venkatesh and Bala 2012; Zhao and Xia 2014). Without compatibility and interoperability standards for interorganizational IS, e-business would be unthinkable.

The literature on the impact of IT standards has also considered the challenges involved in IT standardization. For instance, even consortium-based standards do not automatically translate into widespread adoption of or full compliance with a standard, and in the absence of widespread adoption and full compliance, the potential for interorganizational systems to facilitate collaboration and improve productivity may be substantially curtailed (Zhu et al. 2006). In some instances, the poor quality of a standard may hamper the potential benefits of standardization, as Backhouse et al. (2006) show for the international information systems security standard ISO/IEC 17799. IT standards also carry the risk of locking adopters into an old technology when subsequently developed superior technologies emerge, thereby threatening the early adopters’ competitiveness (Shapiro and Varian 1999). Furthermore, settling on a single IT standard may lead to reduced competition and higher prices for customers (Fuentelsaz et al. 2012; Lee and Mendelson 2007).

## IT Standards Diffusion

The majority of the IT standards literature has focused on IT standards diffusion processes (e.g., Katz and Shapiro 1986; Kauffman et al. 2000; Lee and Mendelson 2007; Park 2004; Zhu et al. 2006). Within this stream of research, several studies have focused on strategies that those IT vendors that control standards can follow during the diffusion process. Notably, these studies have investigated issues such as preannouncements (Swann and Shurmer 1994), bundling (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999), pricing (Fuentelsaz et al. 2012; Gallaugher and Wang 2002; Lee and Mendelson 2007), licensing (Lin and Kulatilaka 2006), second sourcing (Farrell and Gallini 1988), and compatibility strategies (Besen and Farrell 1994; Li and Chen 2012; Liu et al. 2012; Sheremata 2004).

The seemingly largest share of research on standards diffusion has investigated a variety of theoretical mechanisms in seeking to explain the diffusion process and the adoption of different standards. Network effects have been the most widely studied diffusion mechanism (e.g., Au and Kauffman 2001; Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996; Kauffman et al. 2000; Liu et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2012; Weitzel et al. 2006; Zhao and Xia 2014; Zhu et al. 2006). Network effects describe the phenomenon in which the value of a technology for a firm considering adoption tends to increase with the number of adopters either based on the value gained directly from other adopters’ use of the same technology—as in communications technologies—or indirectly by means of the increased availability of compatible products and services—as in the case of computer software and hardware (Farrell and Klemperer 2007). Studies that employ network effects as a key explanatory mechanism are highlighted in Appendix A.

A second important mechanism that has received substantial attention in the literature on standards diffusion consists of switching and adoption costs (e.g., Chen and Hitt 2002; Zhu et al. 2006). Firms incur direct costs when they adopt a technological standard, such as in the form of licensing fees (Lin and Kulatilaka 2006). Firms may also incur indirect costs when they switch to another technology, such as in the form of technology-specific investments and learning related to the first technology (Farrell and Klemperer 2007; Polites and Karahanna 2012). Such switching costs may lead to intergenerational path dependence in technology diffusion and eventually may trap firms in a standard (Zhu et al. 2006).

Herd behavior is a third mechanism that may explain the adoption of a standard and has accordingly received substantial attention in the IT diffusion literature (Bikhchandani et al. 1992; Duan et al. 2009; Sun 2013; Walden and Browne 2009). In herd behavior, uncertain adopters observe the adoption behavior of others and assume that they are likely to have made the correct decision (Abrahamson 1991; Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1997; Bikhchandani et al. 1992; Sun 2013; Walden and Browne 2009). Central to theories of herd behavior is that the adopting firm disregards its own information about the technology to be adopted and relies on others observation, eventually imitating their choices (Sun 2013).

In addition to these three mechanisms that are frequently employed to explain the diffusion of candidates for standards, myriad additional factors, including firm expectations, adaptive learning (Au and Kauffman 2003), and network positions (Weitzel et al. 2006), to name only a few, have been proposed to explain the diffusion of candidates for standards. This stream of literature overlaps with a broader steam of literature that has investigated the diffusion and adoption of information technologies without explicitly focusing on IT standards, although a full review of this broader IT diffusion and adoption literature is beyond the scope of this paper. Nonetheless, its main theoretical mechanisms are summarized in Appendix B.

A key topic of interest in the literature on IT standards diffusion, and the literature on technology choice in general, has been the possibility that firms may choose, and may possibly be locked into, technologically suboptimal alternatives. As Appendix B shows, the threat of such suboptimal lock-in is particularly pronounced in IT standards that feature significant network effects. Because compatibility with a large network of other adopters is often a major consideration in the choice of a technological standard, a firm in such a network that is established around a possibly inferior standard may have little incentive to switch to the superior standard and forgo these compatibility benefits. This scenario may lead to a persistent lock-in to a suboptimal standard that can limit the evolution of entire IT fields (Arthur 1989; Katz and Shapiro 1994).

## IT Standards Development

Although there is a large body of work on the factors that influence the diffusion of candidates for IT standards, studies on the development of these standards candidates are rare (Lyytinen and King 2006; Zhao et al. 2011; Zhu et al. 2006), with the exception of a 2006 special issue of MIS Quarterly (Lyytinen and King 2006) and a few other more recent contributions (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Hanseth and Bygstad 2015; Zhao et al. 2007, 2011).

Some of the limited research on the development of standards has focused on presenting descriptive accounts of the development processes of candidates for standards in the context of de jure standards (Backhouse et al. 2006; Hanseth et al. 2006) and, in particular, in the context of consortium-based standardization (Markus et al. 2006; Nickerson and zur Muehlen 2006). These studies have generated important insights into the institutions that facilitate the emergence of standards, the processes that occur in these institutions, and the problems encountered in these processes (e.g., Davidson 2002; Hanseth and Bygstad 2015; Hanseth et al. 2006; Lyytinen et al. 2008; Lyytinen and King 2006; Nickerson and zur Muehlen 2006).

A second stream of studies has developed more in-depth insights into the factors that explain IT standards development and its consequences in the context of consortium-based standardization (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Zhao et al. 2007, 2011). These studies not only highlight the important role of consortia in the development of IT standards (Zhao et al. 2007) but also suggest that the success of these consortia depends on the contributions of a variety of participants (Zhao et al. 2011) and that the impact of consortium participation on financial performance depends on the size of the consortium (Aggarwal et al. 2011).

## Supply-Side Network Effects, Technology Development, and IT Standards

Despite the substantial progress that has been made in explaining the development of IT standards, important gaps remain, particularly in terms of assessing the nature of IT standards development as the interplay of technology development and anticipatory standardization. As Appendix A shows, the use of network effects theory has been almost exclusively confined to research on the diffusion and impact of IT standards. Although some of the standards development literature has identified the expected size of the developer and adopter networks as a key determinant of a firm’s choice to join a standards consortium (Axelrod et al. 1995; Zhao et al.

2007), research on standards development has not substantively incorporated the mechanisms of network effects into its theorizing. Nonetheless, as we illustrated with the Bluetooth case in the introduction, supply-side network effects may frequently exist during the standards development phase and may shape a firm’s search strategy with regard to the development of standards.

From a theoretical perspective, supply-side network effects can consist of direct compatibility benefits between supplier organizations and indirect effects based on market expectations. Direct supply-side network effects are positive externalities generated by the number of suppliers that support a particular standards candidate that stem from, for example, learning effects, decreased technological uncertainty, the provision of complementary and enabling technologies, and increased legitimacy associated with a technological solution (Arthur 1989; Jacobsson and Bergek 2004; Van de Ven and Garud 1989; Wade 1995; Zhao et al. 2011). Indirect supplyside network effects are generated by a dynamic in which the number of IT suppliers that develop a standards candidate increases the value of the standards candidate for end users and thereby increases the potential size of the market, which, in turn, makes the standards candidate more attractive to other suppliers (Wade 1995; Wang and Xie 2011). Because a larger supplier network increases the size of the expected market and, consequently, customers’ network benefits and their corresponding willingness to pay, this positive feedback loop between suppliers and end users may arise even when all suppliers are direct competitors (Economides 1996b; Sun et al. 2004). Such self-reinforcing dynamics driven by direct or indirect positive network effects among technology suppliers have been revealed in a variety of IT contexts (Christ and Slowak 2009; Cusumano et al. 1992; Wade 1995).

Similar to the dynamics of IT standards adoption caused by demand-side network effects (Besen and Farrell 1994; Economides 1996a; Farrell and Saloner 1986; Katz and Shapiro 1985, 1994), the dynamics of the development of IT standards that are caused by supply-side network effects are driven largely by expectations of future success. As the Bluetooth example illustrates, battles between competing standards candidates may be resolved based on suppliers’ decisions long before any significant competition in the end user market has occurred (Christ and Slowak 2009; Hagiu 2006; Hill 1997). In the information and communication technology industries in particular, firms frequently share information regarding technological choices in formal and informal industry forums and engage in anticipatory standardization (Lyytinen et al. 2008) when de facto standards and compatibility play a role (Axelrod et al. 1995; David and Greenstein 1990). Thus, although the rationale that underlies supply-side network effects may frequently be indirect and dependent upon expectations regarding the adoption of future standards, technology suppliers’ choices still directly impact the perceived valuation of different technologies by other suppliers.

Despite these similarities, supply-side network effects that occur in the development stage differ from the more commonly studied demand-side network effects that occur during the diffusion stage along three important dimensions. First, the process by which technology suppliers develop IT standards is different from the process of standards diffusion among end users in that suppliers’ development resembles a path-dependent search process (Fleming and Sorenson 2001; Hanseth et al. 2006), which contrasts with end users’ discretechoice process (Farrell and Saloner 1985; Weitzel et al. 2006). In their technology development efforts, suppliers make path-dependent investments in uncertain alternatives in potentially complex technological landscapes (Fleming and Sorenson 2001), while they are simultaneously influenced by the supply-side network effects associated with the presence of other IT suppliers and their technologies. As a result, suppliers may choose not to develop a new standard candidate due to the strong support behind an existing standard or they may choose to abandon a technological search path because of the perception of a higher likelihood of success with an alternative path. Thus, supply-side network effects can influence the set of alternative standard candidates that becomes available in the first place, in contrast to demand-side network effects, which are generally considered to operate based on an exogenously given set of standard alternatives. Second, supply-side network effects typically emerge among a relatively small number of suppliers, which makes coordination among suppliers more practical and more feasible, for example, through standards consortia (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Zhao et al. 2011). Finally, a supplier’s choice to use standards candidates that are also used by other suppliers often comes at a cost due to market or technology considerations, such as intellectual property protection (Lemley 2002; Nickerson and zur Muehlen 2006). Therefore, the network dynamics of suppliers’ development efforts with regard to standards can be expected to differ from those of end users’ standards adoption decisions. However, such supply-side dynamics of standards development have received only scant attention in the previous literature on the emergence of IT standards.

## Modeling IT Standards Development under Network Effects

Prior standards research has found simulation modeling to be particularly useful due to the path-dependent nature of standard-setting processes that makes causal relationships difficult to discern empirically (Shurmer and Swann 1995).

Qualitative or quantitative empirical methods necessarily focus on a particular context—including a limited time period and a limited number of firms—and often fail to account for abandoned technological options due to limited data access. By contrast, simulation modeling allows us to study the longitudinal process of technology and standards development— including abandoned options—over time and in many simulated contexts.

We develop and employ an extension of the NK framework for hill-climbing search to model the IT standard-setting process as a coevolutionary technological search by a population of interacting organizations in a technology landscape. Originally developed in the natural sciences (Kauffman 1993), NK models have found a wide variety of applications in the management literature (e.g., Levinthal 1997; Siggelkow and Levinthal 2003; Sommer and Loch 2004), including the modeling of technological search processes (e.g., Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell 2010; Fleming and Sorenson 2001; Frenken 2006). Appendix C presents an overview of the NK model and its origins.

In our model, a technological solution consists of N components that can be thought of as the underlying design choices constituting a candidate for an IT standard, such as decisions regarding transmission frequency, transmission protocol, bandwidth, signal strength, and power consumption in the case of Bluetooth or decisions about the integration versus separation of the user interface in mobile operating system platforms such as Symbian, Apple iOS, and Android. Following the NK framework, each of these components assumes a value of 0 or 1. Different configurations of these N components form the points on the fitness landscape.

To map the choice of components onto the quality of the technological solution, the NK model features a second parameter, K, which denotes the interdependence of the various components (Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell 2010; Fleming and Sorenson 2001). Intuitively, this parameter reflects how the power consumption of a standard candidate for short-distance data exchange (such as Bluetooth), for instance, might depend on the signal strength and the transmission frequency used. The performance contribution of each of the N components in the technology landscape is affected not only by the value of the component itself but also by the value of K other components. With a high K, this approach creates a rugged technological quality landscape with multiple local peaks, as shown in Figure 1a. On a local peak, a change in any one component diminishes the quality of the overall technological solution; thus, no additional improvement is possible through local adaptation. Conversely, when K = 0, there is only one peak in the landscape in which all components are individually optimized, as shown in Figure 1b.

The complexity of a technological system is determined by the number of components (N) and their interdependence (K) (Fleming and Sorenson 2001; Simon 1962). Following common practice (e.g., Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell 2010; Ethiraj and Levinthal 2004; Ganco and Hoetker 2009; Levinthal 1997; Siggelkow and Rivkin 2005), in our main analyses, we will maintain N constant and vary the parameter K to create landscapes of different complexities.

Formally, the performance contribution $p _ { i }$ of each component $i \in \{ 1 , . . . , \mathrm { N } \}$ is randomly drawn from the uniform distribution U[0, 1] for each combination of the values (either 0 or 1) of the focal component and its K interacting components, i.e., $p _ { i } { = } f _ { i } ( e _ { i } ; e _ { i 1 } , \ldots , e _ { i K } )$ , where $e _ { i } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ is the binary value of component $i ; e _ { i 1 } , . . . , e _ { i K } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ are the binary values of the K other components on which the performance contribution of component i depends; and f is a randomly initialized function that provides a unique, fixed performance value from U[0, 1] for each unique set of binary inputs. Therefore, increasing K increases the complexity of the technological landscape, as more components must be taken into account to determine the performance contribution of any individual component. The quality of the overall technological solution $P _ { 0 }$ is the average of the component performance contributions, that is, $P _ { 0 } = \sum p _ { i } / \Nu$ This approach generates a technology landscape with $2 ^ { \mathrm { N } }$ unique component combinations, each yielding a unique level of technological performance. The development of standards is modeled by simulating the concurrent adaptive search of M autonomous technology supplier organizations aiming to maximize their performance in the landscape.

We extend the standard NK model of technological search by considering the positive influence of the number of supplier organizations that support a particular technological solution on the attractiveness of that solution. We use the parameter c to denote the strength of the supply-side network effects, that is, the increase in the perceived value of a specific technological solution for other organizations as a result of one additional supplier organization supporting that solution. Organizations thus make their technological choices by considering not only the stand-alone technological quality of a technological solution but also its potential compatibility benefits in the form of supply-side network effects. Formally, the perceived attractiveness of a landscape position l is $A ( l ) =$ $\begin{array} { r } { P _ { 0 } ( l ) + c n ( l ) } \end{array}$ , where $P _ { 0 } ( l )$ is the stand-alone quality of the technological solution represented by landscape position l, n(l) is the number of other organizations that currently occupy landscape position l, and c is the adjustable parameter that represents the volume of increasing returns per organization that supports a specific solution.

At the beginning of the simulation, each organization is assigned a random landscape position l, that is, a vector of N components, $l = ( e _ { 1 } , . . . , e _ { N } ) .$ , from which to begin its technological search. Organizations attempt to improve their position in the technology landscape by means of two concurrent processes: they can attempt to improve their performance through their own technology development efforts, in effect improving their current candidate for a standard, or they can choose to switch to a superior candidate developed by another organization.

Due to bounded rationality and technological path dependence, firms’ technology positions have been found to evolve mainly through local adaptation (Stuart and Podolny 1996). Therefore, consistent with most prior simulation studies that utilize the NK framework (Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell 2010; Siggelkow and Levinthal 2003; Sommer and Loch 2004), we model organizations’ technology development efforts as processes of local search. During each time period, each organization examines one randomly chosen onecomponent change to its current technological solution, and if it finds a solution with higher perceived attractiveness $A ( l ) ,$ it switches to that solution and continues its search from there in the next time period.

In addition to their own technology development efforts, organizations also continuously evaluate whether it is worthwhile to choose an existing standard candidate instead and to imitate the position of any other firm in the landscape. We follow the prior literature (Ethiraj and Levinthal 2004; Levinthal 1997) and define the probability that a particular firm switches to imitation to be proportional to the performance difference between the focal firm and the bestperforming firm among the set of M firms and the probability that a particular firm is imitated to be proportional to its performance in comparison with the other firms. Formally, during each time period, each firm attempts imitation with probability $1 - A _ { o } / A _ { m a x } ,$ where $A _ { o }$ is the attractiveness of the focal firm’s landscape position and $A _ { m a x }$ is the attractiveness of the position of the highest-performing firm. If the firm engages in imitation, the performance values $A _ { i }$ of all $\mathbf M - 1$ other organizations are summed as $\mathrm { S } _ { \mathrm { p } } = \sum A _ { i }$ , a random variable $x _ { i m i t }$ is drawn from $\mathrm { U } [ 0 , { \cal S } _ { \mathrm { p } } ]$ , and organization m is chosen for imitation such that $A _ { 1 } + \bar { A } _ { 2 } + \ldots + A _ { m - 1 } < x _ { i m i t } < A _ { 1 }$ $+ A _ { 2 } + \ldots + A _ { m - 1 } + A _ { m } .$ This approach offers each firm the probability of being a target of imitation that is proportional to the attractiveness $A ( l )$ of its landscape position. The focal firm then adopts the position of the target firm if the perceived attractiveness of the new position is greater than its existing position.

Thus, there are two processes in each time period. First, all M organizations scan the existing standard candidates and may or may not adopt (imitate) one of these candidates, depending on the relative performance of the available candidates, as described above. Second, all the firms then conduct their own technology development efforts to try to improve their landscape positions by means of local search. The simulation continues until no organization has the potential to improve its performance either through technological development or by imitating an existing solution. The simulations are therefore run until equilibrium is reached, which typically takes between 5 to 30 time periods in our model setup. The detailed pseudocode of our baseline simulation model is presented in Appendix D, and an example simulation is shown in Appendix E.

Consistent with much of the economic literature on standard setting under network effects (e.g., Farrell and Saloner 1986; Katz and Shapiro 1985), we use social efficiency as our dependent variable, which we define in our baseline model as the stand-alone quality of the technological solution that emerges as the standard from this coevolutionary search process. Consequently, we define a socially optimal search process as one that produces higher social efficiency, on average, than any other type of search process in that particular technology landscape. Because imitation in our baseline model is costless, the process always leads to an equilibrium in which all firms choose the solution supported by at least one organization that has the highest attractiveness $A ( l )$ at the end of the simulation, after considering both its stand-alone technological value and network effects. Thus, the baseline model leads to a winner-take-all outcome and the emergence of a single standard, denoted by the landscape position $l _ { w }$ (this assumption is later relaxed in our analysis of imitation costs).

The quality of the standard is defined as $P _ { \mathrm { 0 } } ( l _ { w } )$ , the standalone quality of the underlying technological solution in which the adjustment factor is not considered. However, $l _ { w }$ is not necessarily the highest-quality position, and there may be another position $l _ { o p t i m a l }$ for which the stand-alone value $P _ { 0 } ( l )$ is the highest. This solution may or may not be found in the search process, and if it is found, it may or may not become the standard based on supply-side network effects. Thus, the technological solution that emerges as the standard from the search process may not be the standard that is a priori optimal, and our dependent variable captures this variability in the quality of the standard emerging from the standard-setting process. We next present a number of analyses that examine the manner in which the quality of the emerging standard depends on various characteristics of the standards develop ment process.

## Simulation Results

## Concentration of Search and Technological Lock-in

Very different populations of organizations can engage in IT standards-development processes, and this variety can affect the quality of the emerging standards. We therefore first focus on how the quality of the IT standards set by organizational populations is affected by the number of organizations, M, there are that are independently conducting concurrent technological search in the presence of network effects. We conducted simulation analyses with values for the complexity parameter K of $0 , 1 , 2 , . . . , \Nu - 1$ and with a range of values for the network effects parameter c between 0 and $0 . 6 . ^ { 2 }$ The results below are representative of the general patterns found in these analyses.

Figure 2 shows the quality of the final standard as a function of the number of organizations, M, engaged in the search effort in technological landscapes with $\Nu = 7$ components in both maximally simple $( \mathrm { K } = 0 )$ and maximally complex $( \mathbf { K } =$ 6) landscapes with different values of the strength of the network effects c. In the figure, the quality measure is standardized such that, in each landscape, the quality value of the worst possible solution is set to 0 and the best possible solution is set to 1. In the figures, quality thus represents the percentage of optimum performance achieved. All data points are averages over 10,000 simulation runs.

A comparison of Figures 2a and 2b shows that increased complexity generally lowers the standard’s quality by making it more difficult for organizations to find the optimal solution. Figure 2 further suggests that stronger network effects generally lower the standard’s quality. This result occurs because, due to network effects, organizations may stop searching prematurely if they encounter standard candidates that are already chosen by other organizations, and they might therefore be locked into a technologically suboptimal standard.

As shown in Figure 2a, in a smooth technological landscape with only one peak $( \mathrm { K } = 0 )$ , the optimal number of organizations that are independently engaged in technological search $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { o p t i m a l } }$ is one, and this result is independent of the degree of network effects. When the technological problem is simple, a single organization can identify the optimal solution through local search. Increasing the number of organizations only increases the risk of premature lock-in due to network effects and lowers the quality of the emerging standard.

The results for a rugged landscape (K = 6) are substantially different, as depicted in Figure 2b. In a rugged technological landscape, any single organization is unlikely to find the globally optimal solution. Thus, it is advantageous to have several organizations independently engaged in technological search because it increases the chances of finding multiple local peaks and of achieving better global solutions, as a result. However, as the number of organizations grows beyond a certain point, the marginal effect of additional organizations on search breadth decreases because most regions in the landscape are already being searched, while the risk of suboptimal lock-in due to network effects increases, thereby lowering the social efficiency of the search process. As the strength of network effects increases, the optimal number of searching organizations decreases. For example, Figure 2b shows that when $\mathrm { K } = 6$ and $c = 0 . 0 2$ , the socially optimal number of organizations $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { o p t i m a l } }$ is ten, whereas if $\mathrm { K } = 6$ and $c = 0 . 2$ , the optimal number of independently searching organizations is only two.

Our results indicate the existence of two factors that can lead to a suboptimal IT standard. The first is lock-in due to network effects, in which organizations adopt solutions that are already chosen by others and cut off their search efforts, content with reaping the benefits of increasing returns with the choice of the established solution. The second is technological myopia due to narrow search, in which too few independently searching organizations conduct technological search in a complex technological landscape. In the latter case, it is highly likely that the optimal regions of the landscape are not searched at all. These two factors have fundamentally different implications for the optimal number of searching organizations. Lock-in due to network effects is generally a smaller threat when there are fewer organizations that independently conduct technological search, whereas a large number of independently searching organizations reduces the threat of technological myopia. Thus, the two factors present conflicting challenges to the optimal organization of technological search, and the optimal composition of the organizational population engaged in the development of an IT standard depends on the strength of the network effects and the complexity of the technological problem.

## Coordination through Influential Organizations and Consortia

In the previous section, we focused on the effect of the number of independently searching organizations, assuming that all organizations create network effects of the same strength and therefore have the same impact on a standard’s perceived attractiveness. However, in reality, not all organizations have an equal impact, and key organizational actors can have a disproportionate influence on other firms’ technology choices (Abrahamson 1991; Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1997). If a large corporation such as AT&T, IBM, Google, or Microsoft, or an influential standards organization or consortium develops or supports a certain technological solution, the perceived attractiveness of the solution may be much greater than if a small, unknown start-up were to propose a technologically similar solution by itself (Wang and Xie 2011).

Asymmetries in individual organizations’ influence on supplyside network effects can arise from market factors and technological factors. Their large customer bases allow prominent firms to better appropriate the benefits from a technological switch (Majumdar and Venkataraman 1998), making them less constrained by the network effects created by other organizations that use existing technologies. At the same time, weaker firms prefer to use technological solutions that are compatible with these powerful firms in order to reap the benefits of a large customer network (Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). An organization’s impact on a standard’s attractiveness can also arise from its sophisticated technological capabilities. For example, a technologically prominent organization may be able to provide the necessary inputs and complementary goods itself, as it is much less dependent on the support and learning spillovers from other suppliers, while it serves as a significant provider of such spillovers to other organizations. Such factors related to either market power or technological prominence may make some organizations more influential than others in terms of the supply-side network effects of the candidates for standard that they support.

In addition to influential corporations, alliances of firms or formal and informal standard-setting organizations may wield considerable influence on the technological search process (Rysman and Simcoe 2008). Although such standardization alliances may present dynamics that are somewhat different from the behavior of an individual corporation with a high level of influence (Bekkers et al. 2011; Chiao et al. 2007; Zhao et al. 2007), we abstract from these differences and treat them as a single influential actor. As the Bluetooth example suggests, standards alliances and consortia are actors that can have a strong impact on standard-setting processes.

To examine the effects of particularly influential supplier organizations, we ran several variations of our base model. We introduced the notion of a leader organization that generates larger network effects while being less affected by them itself and examined how the presence of one or more such leaders affects the search process, repeating these experiments with varying degrees of leader influence. We further allowed firms to form consortia of varying sizes that we then modeled as single, more influential actors in the standard-setting process. These simulations supported our finding that network effects call for increased search concentration, whereas technological complexity calls for broader search. In a smooth landscape in which network effects are a greater threat than technological complexity, introducing particularly influential leader organizations or forming consortia is generally advantageous. Such coordination methods increase the concentration of technological choices and reduce the risk of suboptimal lock-in due to network effects, as a result. Conversely, in a complex landscape with low to moderate network effects, broad search by weak leaders and large numbers of independently searching organizations typically leads to better solutions than a more concentrated search by helping the population avoid technological myopia.

However, in addition to observing these general and wellestablished effects, we found an important and novel nonlinearity: contrary to the established wisdom in the network effects literature, increased coordination sometimes exacerbates the potential for premature lock-in due to network effects. Such premature lock-in may occur when, due to concentration, the search occurs in the presence of moderately influential organizations or consortia.

We find that influential actors have a somewhat paradoxical effect on technological search processes: they can both facilitate and disrupt technological lock-in. Figure 3 depicts an example of this effect. Here, we examine the search efforts of a population of $\mathbf M = 1 0$ organizations on different technological landscapes with ${ \bf N } = 7$ components. We assign one of these organizations as a particularly influential leader organization such that, for example, due to the leader’s greater market power, other organizations prefer the standard supported by this leader to the standard of similar technological quality supported by a non-leader. We set the average network effect in the population as $c _ { a \nu g ^ { 9 } }$ but we divide the influence such that the leader organization has an influence of proportion $\mathrm { p _ { d } }$ in the population. Thus, the leader influences the network benefits of its chosen solution by $c _ { l } = c _ { a \nu g } \mathbf { M } \mathfrak { p } _ { \mathrm { d } } ,$ whereas the other organizations have a network effect of $c _ { o } =$ $c _ { a \nu g } \mathbf { M } ( 1 - \mathfrak { p } _ { \mathrm { d } } ) / ( \mathbf { M } - 1 )$ . At the extremes, if $\mathrm { p _ { d } } = 0 . 1$ , all ten organizations are equally influential, whereas if $\mathrm { p _ { d } } = 1$ , only the choice of the leader organization matters.

Figure 3a shows the quality of the emerging standard as a function of the strength of the leader organization with moderate network effects $( c _ { a \nu g } = 0 . 0 5 )$ and varying levels of technological complexity, K. Figure 3b shows the same analyses on moderately complex technological landscapes (K $= 3 )$ with different values for the average strength of the network effects $c _ { a \nu g ^ { \cdot } }$ . In the figures, we observe that as the strength of the leader organization grows, the quality of the emerging standard initially decreases. The lock-in to inferior solutions becomes more frequent and occurs earlier in the technological search process because the solution supported by the leader is more attractive to others and is thus frequently chosen by other organizations, even when it is of poor standalone quality. At such moderate strength, by attracting other organizations, the leader tends to initiate a lock-in process that it is then unable to control, and it is also stuck with the inferior standard as a result of the cumulative increasing returns that stem from the many weaker organizations that support the standard.

However, when the leader organization becomes even more influential, the quality of the standard again increases. In this situation, the leader can disrupt an emerging poor-quality lock-in before it fully sets in and continue the search for better solutions, even when weaker organizations have chosen the leader organization’s former solution. A sufficiently strong organization can ignore the other organizations and decide to pursue a new standard, forcing the others to follow it once again. Whether this solution set by the concentrated search efforts of a single leader is better than the solution found by a population of homogenous organizations is again dependent on the technological landscape’s complexity and the strength of the network effects, as discussed in the context of our baseline model above. As Figure 3a shows, increasing technological complexity decreases the quality of a standard set centrally by a strong leader (as shown at the far right of the graph) more quickly than it decreases the quality of a standard set through a distributed search effort, which is represented at the far left of the graph; moreover, when $c _ { a \nu g }$ is held constant at 0.05, a distributed search becomes superior when $\mathrm { K } > 3$ Conversely, Figure 3b shows that network effects decrease the performance of distributed search more quickly than the performance of a centrally led search, and when K is held constant at 3, a centralized search is superior to a distributed search when $c _ { a v g } > 0 . 0 5$ . However, in most cases, a moderately concentrated search yields the worst outcomes.

This apparent paradox of the influence of leaders in both initiating and breaking the process of technological lock-in can be explained by understanding that the two types of leader effects kick in at different degrees of relative influence. While it takes only above-average influence to attract other organizations and initiate lock-in, it takes dominant influence to break such a lock-in process. In this context, dominant influence refers to the ability of the technological leader not only to influence but also to ignore other players in the technological field when it becomes necessary to ignore the prior choices of others (and its own prior choice) to improve the present standard. Taken together, these dynamics produce a U-shaped relationship between the relative influence of the leader organization and the quality of the emerging standard, as shown in Figure 3.

Similar dynamics can be observed when supplier firms are allowed to form consortia in their standard-setting efforts. Figure 4 depicts an example in which there are originally 12 equally influential firms engaged in standards development in different landscapes. We then arrange these firms into consortia of varying sizes with corresponding increases in the network effects generated by these consortia. For example, if the strength of network effects is c = 0.05 for individual firms, a consortium of four firms generates a network effect of $4 \times 0 . 0 5 = 0 . 2$ . As Figure 4 demonstrates, consortia of intermediate strength operate similarly to other moderately influential organizations and accelerate rather than solve the problem of premature lock-in. Optimal standards are typically generated by either a fully independent search or by a single, industry-wide consortium, depending on whether the threat of overly narrow search (represented by parameter K) or the threat of suboptimal lock-in due to network effects (represented by parameter c) is greater on a relative basis. For example, as shown in Figure 4b, when the complexity of the technology landscape is set at $\mathrm { K } = 3 ,$ the optimal arrangement is an industry-wide consortium when $c > 0 . 0 5$ and a completely distributed search when $c < 0 . 0 5$

Our baseline model suggests that when each organization’s influence on network effects is held constant, there are an optimal number of searching organizations such that the need for both concentration of choices and broad search are balanced. However, policy makers and industry actors typically do not have the option of simply removing organizations to increase concentration to optimum levels; instead, increased concentration is usually achieved by coordination through influential organizations or consortia, and reducing the number of independently searching actors is generally associated with the increased network influence of the remaining actors, which can accelerate lock-in. As Figures 3 and 4 suggest, imperfect coordination by means of moderately influential organizations or multiple consortia may exhibit the worst of both worlds, narrowing the search process while increasing the threat of premature lock-in due to network effects.

## Discussion

## Implications for the Literature on IT Standards

## Supply-Side Network Effects and Technological Search in IT Standards Development

Our first contribution is to extend the concept of network effects and to combine it with the concept of technological search to explain the development of IT standards. As our review of the literature suggests, the majority of the work on IT standards has focused on issues related to the diffusion and adoption of IT standards, while research on the joint effects of supply-side network effects and technological progress on the development of IT standards is completely nonexistent.

While studies on the diffusion and adoption of IT standards have provided many important insights, they generally assume the candidates for standards as given. However, standards candidates are the outcome of technological development, and particularly in the information and communication technology domain, standard setting occurs alongside technological development as anticipatory standardization (Lyytinen et al. 2008). The key overall contribution of our study is therefore to develop a novel theory of how such standards development occurs as a coevolutionary search process under supply-side network effects. Our results suggest that both supply-side network effects and myopic technological search can lead to lock-in scenarios with suboptimal standards, and the implications of these effects for the socially optimal organization of standards development can diametrically oppose one another. We thus extend the literature on standard setting under network effects to consider the technology development processes that occur—at times— years before the market diffusion that has been the focus of prior research. Our study thereby answers recent calls for research that examines the intersection of supply-side technological dynamics and technological standards in the information and communications technology industries (Adomavicius et al. 2012; Lyytinen and King 2006).

## Influential Organizations, IT Standards Consortia, and Lock-in

Our second contribution relates to the limits of coordination in standards development and a paradox in the effects of influential organizations and consortia. Prior research on standards diffusion (Farrell and Klemperer 2007; Farrell and Saloner 1988; Katz and Shapiro 1994) suggests that powerful actors such as influential organizations, standardization alliances, and/or industry consortia may resolve the coordination problem that exists regarding the diffusion of alternative standards by facilitating the convergence to the best available alternative. In our investigation of standards development, we find that while coordination through powerful actors can be advantageous in many situations, there are important limits to such coordination that have been mostly overlooked in prior research.

First, with regard to complex IT standards, in particular, our results suggest that coordination through powerful actors may lead to an overly narrow search for alternatives and the selection of a suboptimal technological standard, as a consequence. Second, influential organizations or consortia that are moderately powerful may create a critical mass for lock-in to occur, but they may lack the power to lead the field toward an alternative solution if a superior technological path emerges later in the technological development process. For instance, despite its strong but no longer dominant position in the personal computer industry, IBM was unsuccessful in its attempts to abandon the PC bus architecture it initially created and to move the industry in the direction of a superior alternative (Gawer 2000). Similar challenges were later faced by the Symbian consortium for mobile operating systems. We would expect that many consortia and alliances, as well as most individual firms, lack the power to abandon the very technological standards they helped to establish. These results may have implications for the social efficiency of coordinating organizations such as standard-setting consortia (Markus et al. 2006), and they suggest that the paradoxical role of coordination in positive feedback processes warrants further research attention.

## The IPR-Related Cost of Imitation in IT Standards Development

Our third important contribution relates to the paradoxical effect of IPR-related imitation costs in the development of IT standards. Our results extend the prior literature on the important role of IPR in IT standards adoption (e.g., Bekkers et al. 2011; Lin and Kulatilaka 2006; Rysman and Simcoe 2008; Simcoe et al. 2009) by suggesting that, whereas low imitation costs may be beneficial in the short term by facilitating the emergence of a single standard, they may also increase the risk of suboptimal lock-in across technological generations.

Specifically, our results suggest that a relatively low cost of adopting standard candidates developed by others is central to the emergence of a single standard, and high costs of imitation in the form of high licensing fees for essential IPR can lead to multiple standards and ultimately to market fragmentation. In line with this argument, firms often initially set their licensing fees low to encourage other suppliers to join the development of their standards candidate. For instance, organizations developing digital media standards such as MP3, PNG, or MPEG4 have adopted low-cost or even cost-free licensing strategies to garner support for their technologies (Liu et al. 2011). Similarly, alliances and consortia pursuing standards development typically adopt clear rules on sharing or licensing essential IPR (Rysman and Simcoe 2008). Notably, our results suggest that while such behavior may help avoid market fragmentation, it may also lead to technological myopia over multiple technological generations and therefore inhibit technological development in the long run. One implication of these results is that these low-cost licenses may represent a Trojan horse (Yoffie 1996) that encourages other suppliers to abandon the development of alternative standards candidates, which may ultimately lock these suppliers onto a suboptimal technological path. In fact, large IT firms have increasingly opened up their software at no cost to third parties—as Sun Microsystems has done with Java—as part of a strategy to force the industry to a specific standard. The observed tension between the short-term efficiency benefits of a single standard and the long-term benefits of broader search facilitated by a diverse set of incompatible standards warrants further research attention.

## Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Any simulation model makes certain simplifying assumptions that might potentially be relaxed in future research. In our modeling, we assume that there is one optimal technological solution that is the same for all suppliers, and the differences between the suppliers’ technological preferences are reflected only in their different starting positions in the technology landscape. However, in many IT fields, different supplier firms may have different preferences regarding the performance characteristics of the standard, which may influence their behavior with regard to the standard-setting process. Furthermore, we focus on the optimality of outcomes after the standard-setting process reaches equilibrium, whereas the speed at which standards are developed can also be important (Simcoe 2012). We focus solely on supply-side dynamics and implicitly assume, as a result, that the process of standards development occurs in the absence of market feedback. However, new standards are frequently developed when competing standards are already established in the marketplace; thus, standards development may be simultaneously influenced by both supply- and demand-side network effects. Examining how changes in simplifying assumptions such as those discussed above regarding the characteristics of the technological and market environment affect the emergence of IT standards provides natural avenues for future research on the dynamics of standards development and technological lock-in.

We have examined the socially optimal organization of IT standards development processes, and our dependent variable thus represents a policy perspective on setting IT standards. Future research might investigate the strategic considerations of individual firms engaged in IT standards development in the presence of network effects, such as how the need to consider both coordination and broad search affects firms’ technology strategies, including technology alliances (Hagedoorn 1993; Leiponen 2008), the strategies that firms can use in IT standards battles (Besen and Farrell 1994; Schilling 1998, 2002), and the use of free licenses to foster the acceptance and development of standards (Yoffie 1996).

Using simulation modeling, we have shown that the interaction between IT standards development processes and increasing returns dynamics due to network effects can affect the emergence of IT standards in nontrivial ways. Our results therefore suggest that future empirical research on real-world cases of network effects and technological lock-in should consider standards development not as an exogenous input for standards adoption processes but as an integral part of the standards emergence process under network effects.

## Modeling Technological Search with the NK Model<sup>2</sup>

The NK model originated in biology, where Kauffman (1993), building on the work of Wright (1932), introduced a model of hill-climbing search in a fitness landscape to simulate adaptive evolution. Kauffman modeled an organism’s fitness as determined by N genes, each of which is epistatically influenced by K other genes. The parameters N and K gave the NK model its name, and such a model can be used to simulate the evolution of populations of organisms that are described as strings of genes. Despite its origin in biology, the NK model was later adapted and extended to simulate the evolution of a broad range of complex systems that are characterized by the interplay of interdependent components, including technological systems (e.g., Fleming and Sorenson 2001; Frenken 2006; Tesfatsion and Judd 2006).

Central to the NK model is the notion of a fitness landscape. Such a landscape consists of one dimension for each component plus an additional dimension for the fitness of the system. In this landscape, each location represents a particular configuration of components, and each configuration is associated with a fitness value that indicates the performance or usefulness of that specific configuration. In an information system, the components might be elements of the hardware, the operating software, and the application software, or as in the Bluetooth example, they might represent the transmission frequency, transmission protocol, bandwidth, signal strength, and power consumption. The fitness value might then reflect the performance of the system.

Following Kauffman’s approach, a technological system can formally be described as a binary string of N components.<sup>3</sup> To illustrate, a system of three components (N = 3) can assume eight different configurations (000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, and 111) that correspond to different points in the fitness landscape. The calculation of the fitness value of each configuration depends on K, the second parameter in the model, which reflects the interdependence between the components and describes the system’s inner structure. When components are interdependent, the overall fitness contribution of a component is dependent not only on its own state but also on the state of K other components.

Despite its striking simplicity as a model with only two parameters in its simplest formulation, the NK model can and has been used to mode a broad range of complex systems. Its power arises because it captures the very essence of a complex system. Simon (1962, p. 468) defines a complex system as a system that is “made up of a large number of parts that interact in a nonsimple way.” Therefore, in Simon’s view, system complexity arises from the number of components and their interdependence. It is these two dimensions of complexity that the two parameters N (number of components) and K (degree of interdependence between components) of the NK model directly model.<sup>4</sup>

To characterize the effect of different levels of complexity, it is useful to compare the fitness landscapes arising from two extreme cases of interdependence: (1) K = 0 describes a case of no interdependence and (2) K = N – 1 describes the case of maximum interdependence. NK models typically assume local search in the fitness landscape. In other words, the searching agent (e.g., organism, organization, or other actor) randomly changes one component at a time and assesses whether that change improves fitness. If fitness is improved, the agent retains the change; otherwise, it returns to the pre-change configuration. While this simple search process may seem simplistic and of low intelligence, it has been found to describe individual and organizational behavior in a broad variety of contexts (Fleming 2001; Fleming and Sorenson 2001; March and Simon 1958; Nelson and Winter 1982; Sørensen and Stuart 2000; Stuart and Podolny 1996).

One central insight arising from the NK model is that landscapes differ dramatically depending upon the interdependence of components and that these differences have important implications for the efficacy of local search. Landscapes with K = 0 exhibit one minimum, one maximum, and a high correlation of performance between adjacent points. In other words, the surface of the landscape is relatively smooth. Local search can identify the global maximum (i.e., the global peak) over time on such smooth landscapes by simply experimenting with one component at a time and selecting the option that leads to a better fitness contribution in each component. By contrast, landscapes with higher values of K have multiple local minima and maxima, and adjacent points exhibit low correlation in their fitness values. In other words, landscapes with high values of K are rugged (Levinthal 1997). Furthermore, the locations of local maxima become increasingly dispersed and unpredictable. On such rugged landscapes, local search may lead to the searching agent becoming mired in a suboptimal local peak (such as location “100” in Figure C2), in which any change of one component would lead to a configuration with a lower fitness value and, as a result, the agent may never reach the global maximum.

Research drawing on the NK model typically modifies the basic structure of the core model somewhat (Siggelkow and Levinthal 2003), adds extensions to the model (Lazer and Friedman 2007; Lenox et al. 2006, 2007; Rivkin and Siggelkow 2003), or modifies the search mechanism (Baumann and Siggelkow 2013; Gavetti and Levinthal 2000) to adapt the base NK model to the specific context to be modeled. In our paper, we follow this tradition by introducing a parameter c that reflects the strength of the supply-side network effects generated by other organizations occupying a particular position in the technological landscape, where each position represents a particular (tentative) technologica solution to the technological problem underlying the emerging standard. Specifically, we make the attractiveness (i.e., the perceived fitness value) of a landscape position l (i.e., a particular combination of binary choices on the N components) dependent on the number of organizations occupying that landscape position. Formally, we define the attractiveness of a position l as $A ( l ) = P ( l ) + c n ( l )$ , where P(l) is the stand-alone fitness value of position l in the NK landscape, n(l) is the number of other organizations currently occupying landscape position l, and c is the adjustable parameter representing the number of compatibility benefits generated by one organization supporting a specific solution. In addition, we also extend the baseline model by allowing different organizations to potentially imitate others’ solutions, following the approach in the related NK literature (Ethiraj and Levinthal 2004; Levinthal 1997). The detailed pseudocode of our model, including the imitation algorithm, is described in Appendix D.

2.3.1. Calculate the relative difference d<sub>j</sub> in attractiveness of the organization’s landscape position A<sub>j</sub> and $A _ { m a x }$ as $d _ { j } = \frac { A _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } - A _ { j } } { A _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } }$

2.3.2. With probability $d _ { j } ,$ have the organization consider imitating another organization’s landscape position as follows:

2.3.2.1. Create a variable $\mathrm { S } _ { \mathrm { p } }$ as the sum of the attractiveness of all other organizations’ landscape positions, i.e.,

$$
S _ {p} = \sum_ {k = 1, k \neq j} ^ {M} A _ {j}.
$$

2.3.2.2. Create a variable $x _ { i m i t }$ as a random draw from $\mathrm { U } [ 0 , { \cal S } _ { \mathrm { p } } ]$

2.3.2.3. Set the target organization for imitation as $m \in \{ 1 , \quad 2 , \quad . . . , \quad { \bf M } \} , \quad m \quad \ne \quad j ,$ such that

$$
\sum_ {k = 1, k \neq j} ^ {m - 1} A _ {j} <   x _ {i m i t} <   \sum_ {k = 1, k \neq j} ^ {m} A _ {j}.
$$

2.3.2.4. Compare the attractiveness of organization j’s current position $A _ { j }$ with the attractiveness that can be achieved by imitating the target organization’s landscape position, considering compatibility benefits, and if $P _ { m } + c L ( O _ { m } ) >$ $P _ { j } + c ( L ( O _ { j } )$ – 1), decrease $L ( O _ { j } )$ by 1, set $O _ { j } = O _ { m }$ , and increase $L ( O _ { m } )$ by 1.

2.4. Have all organizations conduct local search: for each organization, $j \in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \}$

2.4.1. Create a random integer variable H such that H takes one of the values $1 , 2 , . . . , \mathrm { N }$ with equal probability.

2.4.2. Create a new vector of binary choices by changing organization j’s choice $c _ { j H }$ from 0 to 1 or vice versa, and let $O _ { j n e w }$ denote this new vector of choices and $P _ { j n e u }$ its stand-alone performance value, i.e., $P _ { j n e w } = f ( O _ { j n e w } )$

2.4.3. Compare the attractiveness of the organization’s current landscape position $O _ { i }$ and that of $O _ { j n e w } ,$ considering the compatibility benefits in both positions, and i $\mathrm { ~ f ~ } P _ { j n e w } + c L ( O _ { j n e w } ) > P _ { j } + c ( L ( O _ { j } ) - 1 )$ , decrease $L ( O _ { j } )$ by 1, set $O _ { j } = O _ { j n e w } ,$ and increase $L ( O _ { j n e w } )$ by 1.

2.5. Test whether equilibrium is reached: for each organization $j \in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \}$

2.5.1. For each other organization m $\in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \} , m \neq j ,$ , test whether imitating this organization’s landscape position would improve attractiveness, as in 2.3.2.4.

2.5.2. For each component $i \in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathrm { N } \}$ , test whether changing component $c _ { j i }$ from 0 to 1 or vice versa would improve the attractiveness of the organization’s landscape position, as in 2.4.3.

2.6. If a potential improvement is found for any organization in Step 2.5.1 or 2.5.2, proceed to the next time period and go back to Step 2.1; otherwise, end simulation run and go to Step 3.

## 3. Recording the results

3.1. Find the vector of choices $O _ { h }$ with the maximum final attractiveness in the population, such that $h \in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \}$ and $A _ { h } \geq A _ { j } \forall j$ $\in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \}$ . (Note that in the baseline model, all M organizations will end up with this set of choices and therefore $A _ { i } = A _ { j } \forall i ,$ $j \in \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathbf { M } \} . )$ )

3.2. Record the technological performance of the newly set standard $P _ { s t d }$ as the stand-alone performance value of landscape position $O _ { h } ,$ i.e., $P _ { s t d } = f ( O _ { h } )$

3.3. Record the best possible stand-alone performance value achievable in the landscape as $P _ { m a x } = \operatorname* { m a x } { f ( l ) } : l \in \{ ( 0 , 0 , . . . , 0 ) , ( 0 , 0 ,$ … 1), $\ldots , ( 1 , 1 , . . . , 1 ) \}$

3.4. Record the worst possible stand-alone performance value achievable in the landscape as $P _ { m i n } = \operatorname* { m i n } { f ( l ) } : l \in \{ ( 0 , 0 , . . . , 0 ) , ( 0 , 0 ,$ $1 ) , . . . , ( 1 , 1 , . . . , 1 ) \}$

3.5. Calculate, and return as the output from the algorithm, the relative technological quality $Q$ of the final standard as the percentage of optimum achieved, i.e., $Q = \frac { P _ { s t d } - P _ { m i n } } { P _ { m a x } - P _ { m i n } }$
