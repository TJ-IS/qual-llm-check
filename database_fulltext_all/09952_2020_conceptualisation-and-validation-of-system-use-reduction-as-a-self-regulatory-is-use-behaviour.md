# Conceptualisation and validation of system use reduction as a self-regulatory IS use behaviour

Babajide Osatuyi <sup>a</sup> and O<sup>fi</sup>r Turel <sup>b,c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Sam and Irene Black School of Business, The Pennsylvania State University, Erie, PA USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences at the College of Business and Economics, California State University, Fullerton, CA, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Psychology, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Focusing on social networking site (SNS) users, this research conceptualizes, theorizes on and tests information systems (IS) use reduction herein de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users’ attempt to reduce their use of an IS to levels that seem reasonable to them to avoid negative implications. IS use reduction is more realistic for many users than full discontinuance, more prevalent than many other corrective behaviors, and can have many desirable outcomes. Yet, knowledge regarding this behavior has been limited. It therefore warrants more attention in IS research and a growing need to develop interventions aimed at helping users reducing excessive IS use such as SNSs. We suggest that IS use reduction can be motivated by negative consequences of excessive SNS use and that the way users translate such states into SNS use reduction intentions can be explained by a contextual and nuanced adaptation of Bandura’s self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive framework. A survey study (study 1, n=257) was used to theorize and test the determinants of SNS use reduction intentions, and a longitudinal experimental study (study 2, n=127) extended the validity of the survey study <sup>fi</sup>ndings, established causality and examined whether the manipulation the survey study predictors can produce actual SNS use reduction.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) have become highly weaved into the daily lives of many users, to the point that they start becoming a distraction, a source of stress, produce addiction-like symptoms and infringe users’ normal functioning in some cases (Benlian, 2019; Tarafdar, D’arcy, Turel, & Gupta, 2015). This situation can produce discomfort, which through self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection leads rational users to attempt to change the status quo (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2015a). The literature on self-regulatory corrective IS use behaviours (i.e., changes in IS use patterns targeted towards alleviating one’s unpleasant states) is split into two streams. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream primarily focuses on IS discontinuance as a corrective behaviour under the assumption that dissociating from the source of problems (i.e., the IS) is the best solution (Maier et al., 2015a; Turel, 2015b, 2016). The other stream of research argues that discontinuance may not always be practical and therefore prescribes other types of self-regulatory corrective IS use behaviours, including switching from the IS to another IS and taking a temporary break from the IS, under the assumption that these behaviours are e<sup>f</sup>ective and more realistic for mitigating harms associated with problematic use of the technology (Stein, Newell, Wagner, & Galliers, 2015).

Given that excess IS use can produce adverse e<sup>f</sup>ects (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016) that are psychologically similar to those in the smoking and drinking contexts (Paswan, Gai, & Jeon, 2015), and that IS use reduction may seem more realistic for many users who may not want to fully quit the use of a system, we examine IS use reduction as a largely overlooked standalone corrective IS use behaviour. We take this approach rather than assuming a deterministic perspective according to which users will instantly discontinue the use of an IS due to the adverse e<sup>f</sup>ects associated with its use. Anecdotal evidence indicates that people are more amenable to explore ways to reduce their addictive use of SNS rather than discrediting its utility since it ful<sup>fi</sup>ls one or more primary objectives responsible for its initial adoption (Burkeman, 2018; Jones 2018). Consequently, application designers are experimenting with ways to implement use reduction solutions with the development of tools such as Hold (Noor, 2018) that reward users for spending less time on devices they use to connect to social media, especially when they are or should be engaged in other activities such as driving, in studying, or work. This may not fully eliminate the negative consequences of the use of the IS, but will strike a balance between such consequences and the value users generate from using the target IS.

Focusing on IS use reduction is also practically important. Recent research suggests that IS use reduction can serve as a gateway for IS discontinuance for reasons ranging from exhaustion, technostress, fatigue (Dhir, Yossatorn, Kaur, & Chen, 2018) to the perception of the excessive social media use by one’s online peers (Han & Myers, 2018). Thus, companies may want to prevent use reduction, but at the same time a<sup>f</sup>ord it for people in need. An understanding of the antecedents of IS use reduction is therefore useful for system designers to develop features that help users manage their interactions with the IS and possibly sway users from discontinuance; or allow them to discontinue use when a true need arises. The importance of this issue is evident in recent developments of use-control features by technology giants. For instance, Apple included a feature called “Screen Time” in their iOS 12 that provides interaction statistics to users in order to help them understand what applications they spend time on. However, not much is known about its e<sup>f</sup>ect on use behaviours, and in general, about what factors can alter peoples’ intention to reduce the use of a system. Thus, the concept of IS use reduction is understudied, and this hampers important theoretical and practical insights.

In order to understand key aspects of IS use reduction, we theorise a model of this behaviour and employ a sequence of studies in the context of social networking site (SNS) use. SNS was selected as an overarching context, because many users contemplate amending their behaviour in response to problems arising from their use of SNS (Maier et al., 2015a; Maier, Laumer, Weinert, & Weitzel, 2015b; Turel, 2015b, 2016). To begin this study, an exploratory study was conducted to examine the prevalence of IS use reduction in relation to other possible corrective IS use behaviours. Following this exploratory study, in study 1 we theorise on and test key determinants of intention to reduce IS use. In a follow-up study (study 2) we build on <sup>fi</sup>ndings from study 1, and theorise on and examine potential interventions that can help users reduce their SNS use.

To provide a theoretical framework for both studies (study 1 and study 2), we turn to the literature on corrective behaviours in the health domain, such as adopting a non-sedentary lifestyle, embracing a healthy diet or quitting smoking in response to health concerns. Such behaviours can be similar to IS use reduction in response to IS use issues that a<sup>f</sup>ect one’s health and wellbeing, because they represent post-re<sup>fl</sup>ection actions that are aimed at <sup>fi</sup>xing the problem or preventing expected problems, and/or alleviating their symptoms. We hence suggest that it is reasonable to rely on a framework that explains corrective behaviours in the health domain as a lens of analysis here, namely the social-cognitive theory model of Self-Regulation of Motivation and Self-Reactive action (Bandura, 1998).

Contextualising this perspective to the case of SNS, study 1 posits that observing one’s own IS use addiction symptoms can serve as a self-observation trigger that motivates self-reaction in the form of use reduction intentions. This process is assumed, as per Bandura’s model, to be mediated via a judgemental process that produces realisation (the state of coming to a rational re<sup>fl</sup>ection about the extent of one’s problematic status quo). In addition, given the key feature of SNS that allows people to observe others’ behaviours, and the idea that changes in peer behaviours are important motivators of corrective behaviours in social media use (Han & Myers, 2018) and other problematic behaviours such as smoking (Hitchman, Fong, Zanna, Thrasher, & Laux, 2014), the model suggests that peer reduction in SNS use further motivates corrective behaviour. The hypothesised relationships were tested with structural equation modelling (SEM) techniques applied to a two-wave survey data collected from SNS users.

A follow-up study (study 2) adds to this perspective by examining whether triggering realisation via providing real information on one’s own use and manipulating peer reduction of SNS use via the provisioning of information on peer behaviour can lead to actual reduction in SNS use. The model was tested with SEM techniques applied to a two-wave survey data, some of which were experimentally driven, as well as objective use data extracted from SNS use logs. Overall, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the exploratory study and the two main studies lend support to the proposed perspective and (1) show that IS use reduction is a prevalent corrective IS use behaviour that users contemplate, (2) demonstrate that realisation and peer behaviour are important motivators of IS use reduction, (3) point to the need to separate self-observation and judgemental constructs in IS research and point to non-linear intricacies among them, and (4) direct researchers and practitioners to possible interventions that can help users to adopt healthier IS use lifestyles. Together, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings pave the way for further studying this largely ignored behaviour and for advancing IS research into a deeper and more nuanced understanding of user responses to suboptimal IS-related states.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Corrective IS use behaviours

Research on correcting problematic IS use behaviours, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as IS use behaviours that directly or indirectly cause harm and are less bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the people who enact them (Turel, 2016), has focused on people’s intention to change from their current behaviour to a target behaviour that is considered to be better by personal and societal standards (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016). This stream of research points to a range of possible corrective behaviours that people can engage in, including quitting (Maier et al., 2015a; Turel, 2015b, 2016), switching to another IS (Maier et al., 2015b), vacillating (Stein et al., 2015) or taking a temporary break (known as “vacationing”) (Perri, Richards, & Schultheis, 1977; York & Turcotte, 2015) from the target behaviour. The IS literature had focused primarily on quitting/discontinuance and switching (Maier et al., 2015a, 2015b; Turel, 2015b, 2016) with relatively few references to vacillation (Stein et al., 2015). While use reduction can often be a <sup>fi</sup>rst step or a “gateway” for discontinuance, it is a di<sup>f</sup>erent corrective behaviour (Hitchman et al., 2014). For example, discontinuance does not always involve use reduction (e.g., the “cold turkey” strategy), and use reduction may simply lead to an acceptable level of use rather than to discontinuance. Nevertheless, the literature has been relatively silent regarding users’ attempts to simply reduce their use to levels that seem reasonable to them.

## 2.2. IS use reduction as a corrective behaviour

Intention to reduce the use of an IS captures one’s aim to change IS use patterns such that future use still exists but is lower than the current use levels. As opposed to quitting/discontinuance intentions that focus on fully avoiding the use of an IS (Cho, 2015; Recker, 2016), use reduction is a less extreme and possibly a more manageable act that does not require total disengagement from using the target IS. It can hence be a more acceptable step for many users who experience issues associated with the use of an IS, but feel a strong need to still use this IS (Turel, 2015b, 2016). Use reduction is also di<sup>f</sup>erent from vacillation, or switching between using and not using an IS, because the initial intent is not to avoid using the IS temporarily or to take a calculated temporary break from IS use (e.g., during exam periods). Instead, the intent in use reduction behaviours is to keep using the IS but at relatively constant reduced levels.

Regardless of the corrective behavioural choice of IS users, these self-reactive choices re<sup>fl</sup>ect users attempts to gain control over use, alter the current situation and alleviate current and expected aversive outcomes of their existing system use and psychological state status quo. The impetus to engage in corrective behaviours can be external to the individual, for instance, organisational regulations (Evans, Farrelly, & Montgomery, 1999). However, internally motivated changes are more e<sup>f</sup>ective than those that are externally induced (Bandura, 1998), and we hence focus on examining both sources of in<sup>fl</sup>uence in this study.

While such behaviours can be enacted in response to any discomfort, research demonstrates that an overarching discomfort associated with voluntary IS use can be encapsulated in the addiction-like symptoms users experience in relation to their use of an IS; and this is especially true in the case of SNS use (Tarafdar, Maier, Laumer, & Weitzel, in press; Turel, 2015b, 2016). We therefore focus on SNS use in response to such overarching discomfort and extend prior research on corrective behaviour in several ways. First, we position IS use reduction in relation to other corrective behaviours and show that it is di<sup>f</sup>erent and more prevalent. Second, we theorise on and test a process leading to IS use reduction decisions; this process di<sup>f</sup>ers from prior described processes not only by focusing on a di<sup>f</sup>erent outcome (reduction vs. discontinuance or switching), but also by focusing on nuanced mediators which have not been the key focus in prior studies. Such mediators (e.g., realisation) can serve as important and potent targets for interventions, as we show in our Study 2. Third, our model explains why not all users with high degrees of addition decided to reduce their use; some perceive their state as benign and fail to realise it is problematic. Fourth, through the proposed and validated theoretical lens, we show that it is possible to manipulate drivers of IS use reduction and help people amend their actual SNS use behaviour.

## 2.3. Realisation

One of the central tenets of behaviour change models, especially in the domain of problematic behaviours, is realisation. Realisation is a stable state of coming to a rational conclusion about one’s problems through re<sup>fl</sup>ection (Paswan et al., 2015). Importantly, it is a necessary condition for intentionally changing one’s current state (Birnbaum & Parker, 2014; Houston, 2010; Tiebout, 1953). The famous quote that reads “a problem known, is half solved” suggests that the starting and necessary point of correcting an observed behaviour is to <sup>fi</sup>rst realise the magnitude of the problem this behaviour causes and accepting its harmfulness. This perspective suggests that observing a behaviour (e.g., using the cell phone many times per day) may not be enough to initiate change; rather, one needs to realise that this behaviour or state is problematic or sub-optimal. This view is alluded to in Bandura’s self-reactive behaviour model, according to which self-observation (e.g., observing phone use pattern) and judgemental constructs (in this example, realising that the use pattern is problematic) are di<sup>f</sup>erent but necessary constructs for formulating corrective actions; and that self-observation predicts judgementalphase constructs such as realisation.

Consequently, realisation of addiction and its associated unpleasant symptoms is di<sup>f</sup>erent from simply recognising (i.e., being aware of) the symptoms.

Observing addiction symptoms does not always motivate action, because people may be oblivious to such symptoms and have low motivation to control their behaviour, even if they have the ability to control their behaviour (Turel, He, Xue, Xiao, & Bechara, 2014). What is often necessary for mobilising action is realisation; people need to acknowledge that their status quo is problematic and hence require amendment. As such, realisation di<sup>f</sup>ers from mere awareness by adding an assessment of the extent of the problematic nature of observed symptoms. This means that even people who may be highly addicted to SNS use can observe the symptoms, but if they do not perceive them as problematic (low realisations) they may have low desire to change their behaviour. For example, it was shown that people who are addicted to Facebook are sensitive (respond fast) to Facebook cues, but have intact re<sup>fl</sup>ection and observation abilities (Turel et al., 2014); many simply lack the motivation to change their behaviour (though they can) because they do not perceive it as highly problematic or as a key disturbance to their normal functioning (He, Turel, & Bechara, 2017a; He, Turel, Brevers, & Bechara, 2017b).

This aligns with Bandura’s (1998) model according to which realisation mediates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of awareness on behaviour change (Khantzian, 2014), and is a necessary condition for awareness translation into action potential. It also aligns with coping theories (Lazarus, 1996) according to which changes (coping with a stressor) are motivated, in part, by primary appraisals that involve perceiving the source of stress as threatening. Such theories uniformly suggest that perceiving stressors, problems and suboptimal states are not enough to motivate action; one needs to cognitively accept the problematic nature of the status quo before he or she is motivated to mobilise resources to amend the situation.

In the context of this study, realisation is used with reference to SNS addiction symptoms and what may be, for some, perceived as excessive level of use. As such, realisation is expected to emerge in varying degrees in response to one’s observation of addiction symptoms (study 1) and one’s own SNS use levels (study 2). This perspective is consistent with the way Bandura’s framework portrays the e<sup>f</sup>ect of selfobservation (addiction symptoms or level of use) on judgemental (realisation) states (Bandura, 1998). Ultimately, realisation of the possible problematic nature of one’s own use presents an opportunity to help users develop healthy SNS use behaviours and consequently warrants attention from IS scholars.

## 2.4. SNS self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action framework

The self-reactive action framework (Bandura, 1998) is a broad framework that points to components (but not to speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs) of the process of decision-making related to corrective actions. It suggests that people selfre<sup>fl</sup>ect on their cognitions, personal factors and cues they receive from their environment; and based on these re<sup>fl</sup>ections self-regulate their behaviours. Applied to the case of SNS use reduction, the framework implies that users will engage in self-regulatory corrective behaviours after re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on their past use of the SNS, as well as on cues from their environment, such as signals from the behaviour of friends on the SNS. These observations will serve as inputs to a judgemental state that will produce a certain level of realisation (an encapsulated recognition that one’s current behaviour is problematic), which in turn, will motivate behavioural changes (Bandura, 1998).

In the self-observation stage, people pay critical attention to their actions and states; this information is used for specifying their status quo. In the judgemental stage, people go through a re<sup>fl</sup>ection process to assess the information about their current state vis-à- vis personal and social standards, and judge the problematic nature of the current state, which in turn, can motivate corrective action. One may use a combination of personal standards, referential behaviours, valuation of activity and performance determinants for the judgement process (Bandura, 2001). Thus, people with the same level of addiction symptoms may develop di<sup>f</sup>erent realisations given their di<sup>f</sup>erences in personal and social standards. Lastly, changes in behaviour stem from the extent to which the assessments from the judgement process (realisation in our case) favour action or inaction. Favouring action can be motivated by realisation, but also by peer behaviours; a person often engages in mental calculation of relevant pros and cons vis-à-vis his or her personal and social standards. People with the same level of addiction symptoms may vary in corrective action motivation given variations in realisation and in cues regarding the desirability and feasibility of the behaviour that are signalled by peer behaviours (Schunk, 1987). Figure 1 applies this model and <sup>fl</sup>ow to corrective behaviours in the SNS use context.

This framework suggests that users <sup>fi</sup>rst observe the SNS addiction symptoms they experience or merely the excessiveness of their use. They then employ this information in the judgement process and produce a stable realisation. In essence, users go a step beyond merely observing their status and examine these factors in relation to referential norms and personal standards. The emergent realisation motivates corrective action. This motivation is further augmented by social cues, such as observing reference others, as their behaviour can be used for (1) adjusting the standard of what is “acceptable” or “normal” use, and (2) showing that behaviour changes are likely desirable and/or feasible, as others engage in them (Schunk, 1987). This framework, albeit with di<sup>f</sup>erent factors, has been shown to explain discontinuance decisions.

SNS self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action framework.

Here, we not only extend it to use reduction decisions and portray more nuanced (non-linear) associations between various framework factors, but also focus on overarching cognitive judgemental process factors that are feasible to manipulate. For example, Turel (2015b) focused on emotions (guilt) and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy perceptions as judgemental factors. Without discounting the importance of such factors, in the current study we focus on an overarching cognition that serves as a precondition for other judgemental factors, and theorise on its non-linear associations. For instance, without realisation, a person is unlikely to feel guilty. This judgemental factor is also potent in terms of interventions. As we show in Study 2, it can be altered and through this alteration, we can help people amend their behaviour. We further extend prior applications of this framework by accounting for the social context in which people make such decisions. We show that this is also a potent intervention target that allows manipulation of use reduction decisions. Overall, the current application of Bandura’s framework does not void prior applications; it instead theorises on overlooked judgemental phase factors, and on more complex than previously assumed associations; and points to important judgemental phase factors that can serve as targets for interventions.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Determinants of intention to reduce SNS use

Addiction to the use of an SNS is a maladaptive psychological dependency on one’s use of the SNS that manifests in typical addiction symptoms, including salience, withdrawal, relapse, con<sup>fl</sup>ict, tolerance and mood modi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Turel, Serenko, & Giles, 2011). It is typically self-observed via the frequency of addiction-like symptoms and can be driven by stressors (Tarafdar et al., in press); hence, it represents an overarching sense of discomfort with current use levels and patterns. It can produce discomfort because it may be associated with time waste and deteriorated work, school, and social functioning (Turel, 2015a; Turel & Serenko, 2012). The ability to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on these problems and recognise them as harmful is generally not impaired among SNS addicts (Turel et al., 2014). Hence, consistent with the self-observation→ judgement path (Bandura, 1998), it is reasonable to assume that peoples’ level of addiction to using the SNS, as manifested through generally observable symptoms (e.g., withdrawal, inability to control the behaviour), will be associated with increased realisation that they have a problem that may require a correction.

However, research shows that high levels of addiction can distort peoples’ perceptions (Turel, Brevers, & Bechara, 2018), including regarding the IS (Turel et al., 2011). High levels of addiction in<sup>fl</sup>ate positive views regarding the system and consequently drive behaviour maintenance, rather than change, including in the context of SNS (Serenko & Turel, 2015). Perception distortion is minimal/non-existent when addiction levels are low (Turel et al., 2018). Hence, under low addiction levels, addiction symptoms will likely be observed, perception will be marginally distorted (Turel et al., 2011) and the addiction symptoms will lead to realisation that the status quo is suboptimal. In contrast, as per the perception distortion hypotheses (Turel et al., 2011) and empirical evidence regarding distortion among people who present strong addiction symptoms (Turel et al., 2018), people will likely cater to their cognitive dissonance at higher levels of addiction and develop a rosy view of their use behaviour in order to shelter themselves from realisation pains.

Hence, as addiction grows, reasoning leading to problem realisation will diminish; people will recognise their symptoms (they are still rational), but will likely trivialise them and consequently fail to cognitively accept the problematic nature of their SNS use behaviour. This process represents the summation of countervailing forces, reasoning and bias (the perception distortion hypotheses), and a turning point where the bias overtakes reasoning. This is the basis for an inverted-U relationship (Haans, Pieters, & He, 2016). Hence, the relationship between addiction and realisation is expected to be positive due to natural ability to recognise problems until a point where the perception distortion hypothesis overshadows this natural ability; at this point the association becomes negative as perception distortion grows. Thus, we hypothesise:

H1: There is a curvilinear (inverted u-shape) relationship between addiction to using SNS and realisation, such that the positive relationship between addiction and realisation increases to a point where realisation begins to diminish as addiction continues to increase.

As per the self-reactive action framework, behavioural intentions to engage in corrective behaviours in response to addiction often stem from a realisation (judgement) of the problems associated with this addiction (Khantzian, 2014; Tamutienė, 2014). This happens because recognising the problem is essential for developing the motivation to mobilise resources in order to change one’s behaviour (Bandura, 1998); such resource mobilisation towards behaviour change is not easy nor is it spontaneous (Turel, 2015b). For example, a smoker may know that smoking is bad but continue smoking; only after realising that he or she has cancer or seeing friends die due to smoking they may mobilise su<sup>fi</sup>cient mental resources to try to quit smoking (Westmaas et al., 2015). Similarly, in the SNS context, users will contemplate ways to reduce their excessive use of SNS when they realise that this use pattern causes fatigue and technostress (Dhir et al., 2018). We hence expect that:

H2: Realisation of the problematic consequences of one’s SNS use is positively associated with one’s intentions to reduce the use of the SNS.

Addiction makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to change one’s behaviour (Banyai et al., 2017), especially at high levels of addiction (Crews & Boettiger, 2009). It can therefore serve as a double-edged sword in self-regulation processes (Turel, 2015b). On the one hand, as expressed in H1 and H2, it can motivate use reduction. It can, however, also in<sup>fl</sup>ate or bias users favourable perceptions, which can ultimately motivate use increase rather than reduction (Turel et al., 2011). This is especially expected at low levels of SNS addiction, a condition under which people lack the motivation to change their behaviour and weigh the SNS use enjoyment gains more heavily than the harm caused by addiction symptoms (Turel et al., 2014). When addiction levels and the related symptomology are low, the symptoms are often perceived as benign and may consequently be dismissed by most people (Friedrich, Polet, Schuch, & Wagner, 2014). The reward at this level is higher than the perceived harms, and this demotivates most people to change their behaviour (Turel, 2015b).

However, as addiction symptoms reach higher levels people may feel stronger incentive to act and reduce use given that the perceived harms may grow larger than the diminished reward (Webb, Sniehotta, & Michie, 2010). This is similar to lack of motivation to reduce smoking at low levels, and growing motivation at higher levels (Siru, Hulse, & Tait, 2009). Hence, it is reasonable to expect that SNS addiction will reduce users’ intentions to engage in corrective behaviours, in parallel to its role in promoting self-reactive corrective actions through observation and judgement processes, and that this reduction e<sup>f</sup>ect will reverse after addiction symptoms reach a level of severances that stimulate behaviour change. This represents, again, the integration of two countervailing forces, perceived harms and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the balance of which changes as addiction levels increase. Such integration of two countervailing forces drives a u-shaped association (Haans et al., 2016). Hence, we hypothesise:

H3: There is a curvilinear (u-shape) relationship between SNS addiction and intention to reduce SNS use such that intention to reduce SNS use will decrease as addiction increases to a point where intention to reduce SNS use will start to increase as addiction increases.

Given that health and corrective behaviours are driven by a mental calculus of various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of the target behaviour (Green & Murphy, 2014), and often include consideration of peer behaviours, expectations, and expected reactions (Schunk, 1987), we account for such peer behaviours in our model (for parsimony reasons, we do not include costs in our model). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, motivation for corrective behaviour are not only driven by internal states; they can be informed by cues pertaining to changes in the environment (Hitchman et al., 2014; Shi<sup>f</sup>man, 2005; West & Sohal, 2006). Such changes may be particularly pronounced in the SNS use context because the core functionality of this system is the sharing of others’ activities. Focusing speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally on changes in friends’ behaviours or states is important in the context of amending addictive or what is perceived to be excessive behaviours because such changes convey useful information that can be used as a reference for behaviour assessment. For instance, in the smoking context, changes in the number of smoking friends (Hitchman et al., 2014) and information regarding a relative who was diagnosed with cancer due to smoking (Butler, Rayens, Zhang, & Hahn, 2011) motivated people to reduce smoking. Given the social visibility attribute of SNS, it is reasonable to expect that <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the smoking domain may be applied and even augmented in the case of SNS.

We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally expect that when SNS friends start reducing use (a key environmental factor in the SNS use context), three mechanisms may operate to motivate use reduction. First, it may be used as a reference point for normal or expected behaviour and send a signal of concern to users that their use has been abnormal. Second, in line with social identity theory (Hogg & Terry, 2000), people are expected to adjust their behaviour to group norms, and when these norms change, people will adjust their behaviour accordingly. Third, it can increase the perceived feasibility of use reduction by showing to users that others have managed to do it. These processes are consistent with the judgemental stage e<sup>f</sup>ects on self-reactions; judgements can often be vis-à-vis peer behaviours (Bandura, 1998). For instance, alcohol abusers do not reduce their drinking behaviour if others around them keep on drinking heavily; they do so when others change their behaviour (Hitchman et al., 2014). This means that changes in peer behaviour can serve as a key motivator of behaviour change in Bandura’s framework, beyond realisation, especially when such changes are visible to others, like in the case of SNS. We therefore expect:

H4: Decrease in peer use of SNS will increase intentions to reduce SNS use.

## 3.2. Intervening to reduce SNS use

Realisation regarding problems associated with SNS use can motivate not only intentions to change behaviour as described in study 1, but also result in actual behaviour change (Paswan et al., 2015). This can stem from the partial translation of intention to actual behaviour (Ajzen, 1991), and/or from the notion that people with high levels of realisation that their status quo is problematic, will have stronger motivation to mobilise psychological and physical resources towards use reduction e<sup>f</sup>orts compared to those with low realisation (Bandura, 1991). We hence hypothesise:

H5: (Manipulated) realisation of problematic SNS use status quo will drive actual reduction in SNS use.

Following the logic of H4, in line with social identity theory, with the role of social contexts in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the judgemental phase (Bandura, 1998), with notions of learning from peers (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, & Welch, 1998), and with demonstrated e<sup>f</sup>ects of changes in peer behaviour or states on one’s behaviour change (Butler et al., 2011; Westmaas et al., 2015), it is reasonable to expect that when peers reduce their SNS use, one’s actual use will be adjusted (reduced). Such peer use reductions are likely to result in increased motivation to reduce SNS activity and in the mobilisation of more resources to do so (Butler et al., 2011; Westmaas et al., 2015); and these will, imperfectly though, translate into actual changes in behaviour (Bandura, 1998). Thus, we hypothesise:

H6: (Manipulated) perceived decrease in peer use of SNS will produce actual reduction in SNS use.

## 4. Research studies and results

## 4.1. Rationale for multi-method research design

Two studies were conducted to investigate the hypotheses proposed in this research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst study (study 1) had two parts. In a preliminary pilot study, we aimed at establishing the idea that use reduction is a standalone phenomenon, di<sup>f</sup>erent from full discontinuance, or taking a break from using the IS. In the main part, we sought to examine how the intention to reduce SNS use is informed directly by peoples’ perceptions of their addiction to using SNS and indirectly through their realisation of the extent of the problematic nature of their addiction. The study also examined how perceived peer reduction of SNS use sends a signal to SNS users to reduce their SNS use. Next, a follow-up experimental study (study 2) was conducted to examine the potency of manipulation the determinants from study 1 to produce actual change in use behaviours via time-lagged observations.

This research design a<sup>f</sup>orded the opportunity to ful<sup>fi</sup>l the three purposes of multi-method research, i.e.,, corroboration, expansion, and compensation (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Mingers, 2001; Venkatesh, Brown, & Bala, 2013; Venkatesh, Brown, & Sullivan, 2016). First, both studies con<sup>fi</sup>rm the link between use reduction and realisation as well as peer use reduction. Using two di<sup>f</sup>erent instrumentations to produce consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings increases the reliability of our conclusions. Second, the experimental study expanded the survey study by including use reduction self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and extending the conceptualisation of realisation as a higher order construct with timewasted and problem realisation. Finally, the experimental study compensated for the limitations of the survey study by testing actual behaviours rather than users’ perceptions and intentions. Third, the overall research design leveraged the strengths of each approach and compensated for their limitations. Study 1 provided external validity of use reduction as a common corrective behaviour and study 2 demonstrated high internal validity of the determinants of use reduction as a corrective behaviour.

## 4.2. Study 1: determinants of intention to reduce SNS use

A pilot study of 251 SNS users was performed to explore corrective behaviours types contemplated by SNS users. Details of the pilot study design and procedure are fully described in Appendix A. Results showed that among SNS users, SNS use reduction intentions are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more common than taking a break or discontinuance intentions and that it is a standalone phenomenon, statistically distinct from taking a break or discontinuance intentions. This result provides support for the need to investigate use reduction intentions among SNS users, leading to the design of study 1 to accomplish that goal. Study 1 therefore theorises on the drivers of use reduction intentions from the perspective of response to SNS addiction symptoms, as they can represent the end state of users stemming from other stressful states (Tarafdar et al., in press), can motivate behaviour change (Ryan, Chester, Reece, & Xenos, 2014), yet, make quitting di<sup>fi</sup>- cult (Turel, 2015b).

## 4.3. Study 1: sample and methods

Data for this study were collected from 257 Facebook users who were university students [42.4% women; average of 436 contacts on Facebook and 7.5 years of Facebook experience; modal age bracket = 31–40 years old], with online surveys posted on a university bulletin-board. Sample, survey instruments, and preliminary tests are presented in Appendix B. All constructs demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability.

This not only allowed extending the understating of the roles of realisation and peer use decrease in IS use reduction processes in the case of SNS, but also allowed increasing con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of study 1, increased con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the assumed causality direction, and paved the way for developing applied IS use reduction interventions.

## 5. Discussion

This paper sought to shed light on a relatively unexplored yet prevalent IS use behaviour, namely IS use reduction. It is important to study this behaviour not only because it is a distinct, understudied yet fairly common IS use behaviour, but also because it can inform interventions aimed at helping users live more responsibly with their IS and adjust their levels of use such that they more closely meet their own and social personal standards. Moreover, even though this is beyond the scope of this study, we note that IS use reduction may serve as a gateway behaviour for other system use behaviours, such as full discontinuance, because it can alter users’ system and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy perceptions. It hence merits stronger emphasis in IS research; and this study makes <sup>fi</sup>rst strides towards this goal.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, drawing on prior corrective behaviour studies in multiple research domains, this study examines the viability of using the self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action framework to understand the determinants of IS use reduction behaviour in the SNS context. Both studies demonstrated that SNS use reduction can be similar to SNS discontinuance in that it can be explained via the lens of analysis of the selfregulation of motivation and self-reactive action framework. Using this framework, study 1 showed that SNS addiction symptoms are an important observation phase factor that acts as a double-edged sword: It informs the judgement phase and generates realisation that something is wrong; but it also, at least at low levels, de-motivates behaviour change. Realisation is shown to be an important impetus for behaviour change in the form of use reduction. ## 5.1. Theoretical implications

Several implications are noteworthy. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the exploratory study part of study 1 illuminate the need to theorise on and better understand use reduction phenomena, as these can be prevalent and are di<sup>f</sup>erent from other corrective behaviours that have been conceptualised in IS research (Maier et al., 2015a, 2015b; Stein et al., 2015; Turel, 2015b, 2016). While other corrective behaviours may be explained with the overarching self-regulation of motivation and selfreactive action framework (see example in Turel, 2015b), the self-observation and judgemental factors that may motivate them may di<sup>f</sup>er and the nature of association (liner vs. non-linear) may also vary. A key di<sup>f</sup>erence between use reduction compared to the other corrective behaviours studied in IS research (i.e., vacillate, switch, temporary break or quit) is the underlying utility assumption associated with the IS. Use reduction seem to place a high value on the utility of the IS and thus requires the user to adapt his or her use behaviour to ful<sup>fi</sup>l set objectives for the IS, whereas a common underlying assumption of the other corrective behaviours is that the utility of the IS is marginal/ smaller than before and can be replaced temporarily or permanently. Another key di<sup>f</sup>erence is operational in nature. When facing realisation that something is wrong with the status quo, users can choose among the di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches available to them: quitting, taking a break or reducing use. Here, we show that realisation can lead to at least one operationalisation of this need, namely IS use reduction. One possible reason is that use reduction provides a permanent, relatively easy to implement long-term solution, withou the pains of another permanent, long-term solution, namely quitting. In contrast to such approaches, vacil lation provides a temporary solution and may <sup>fi</sup>t situations in which the need is temporary (e.g., during exam week). Future research should examine possible similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences between the self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action factors tha drive various corrective behaviours. It should also examine di<sup>f</sup>erences in the nature of associations and under what circumstances people prefer IS use reduction over other corrective behaviours, such as vacilla tion. In this sense, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend prior works with new factors pertinent to an overlooked corrective behaviour, and rea<sup>fi</sup>rm the validity of the contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c application of Bandura’s framework for explaining such behaviours.

Second, realisation, and generally speaking, the separation of the self-observation and judgemental constructs in models of self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action is a relatively under-examined concept in IS research. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that it is a central element in the mechanism that mediates the translation of addiction (and possibly other problems) into self-reactive action. While prior research focused on emotions related to the judgemental phase (Turel, 2015b), we focus here on an important, easy to manipulate, overarching cognitive assessment. Hence, future research should further consider this concept in models of corrective behaviours. For instance, realisation regarding the “problematic” nature of an o<sup>fi</sup>ce application may be a key mediator between observed problems (e.g., low usefulness or user fatigue and stress)

and user responses to these problems (Recker, 2016). Studies examining such mediated-through realisation models can be informed by our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is also supported by the delineation between the primary and secondary appraisal stages in the coping model of user adaption (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). However, rather than the opportunity vs. threat assessment of an IS event, realisation of the extent of the problematic use of an IS is employed here, and is independent from a single event. Hence, even if a user’s evaluation of an IS is positive (i.e., provides opportunities) the addictive use of the IS can be problematic and lead to negative outcomes. For instance, a positive evaluation of Facebook can lead to the conclusion that it is useful because it avails the opportunity to connect with my friends across the globe. However, that connection can become addictive leading to the realisation of the problematic nature of Facebook use. Hence, the tool itself is not bad, but the time I spend on using it is problematic. Therefore, while it is relevant for connectivity (positive utility), my use of it is problematic and requires amends.

Third, further extending prior works that used Bandura’s framework, albeit with di<sup>f</sup>erent factors (Turel, 2015b, 2016), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest more complex than previously assumed and non-linear addiction e<sup>f</sup>ects. Relying on the perception distortion hypothesis (Turel et al., 2011) and supporting it, they speci<sup>fi</sup>cally show that low level of SNS addiction produce realisation which is consistent with normal cognitive functioning, but high levels of addiction reduce realisation production, presumably because people in such circumstances overly in<sup>fl</sup>ate their positive perceptions. They also show that addiction follows a U-shaped relationship with use reduction intentions. At low levels, it reduces reduction intentions, but as the pain of symptoms grows larger, it becomes a nuisance and motivates use reduction. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only extend application of the self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action to IS use behaviours (Turel, 2015b, 2016), but also suggest possible non-linear extensions of the framework itself. Future research applying this framework to any addictive behaviour (e.g., substance use) may consider similar non-linear e<sup>f</sup>ects.

Fourth, further extending such works that have primarily focused on intended behaviours and/or subjectively reported behaviour, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of study 2 demonstrate that the examined factors can stimulate actual (objectively measured) behaviour change. This adds con<sup>fi</sup>dence to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of study 1 and especially to the importance of realisation and observations regarding peers as drivers of corrective IS use behaviours. Moreover, it points to relatively easy to manipulate factors that can help people amend their behaviours. While much research has focused on identifying factors that can predict technology addictions, there is a shortage of studies on interventions and whether such interventions are e<sup>fi</sup>cacious (Vondrackova & Gabrhelik, 2016). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings partially bridge this gap and suggest that future research can examine how realisation and peer use reduction can be manipulated and whether such interventions can produce long-term results, especially in cases of SNS addiction that require treatment, but perhaps also for simply reducing the problematic use of SNS (Banyai et al., 2017). Lastly, it is useful to consider ties to other IS studies that have focused on “dark sides” of personal use IS. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, there can be many negative aspects of IS use that can trigger realisation. These can include, for example, privacy invasion (Benlian, 2019), techno-stressors and strain (Tarafdar, Cooper, & Stich, 2019). We hence call for extending the mediational role of realisation for studying the way users react to other “dark side” facets. The same studies also point to possible interventions. For example, providing system anthropomorphic features (Benlian, 2019), or capabilities that maintain stress at desirable levels (Tarafdar et al., 2019). Future research should therefore test the e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of combined interventions, the ones we propose here, and ones that were proposed in past research, to alter users’ IS use reduction as needed.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Study 1 pointed to two variables, realisation and decrease in friends’ activity that can drive SNS use reduction. Use reduction may be needed not only because people can commonly engage in problematic use patterns or present addiction-like symptoms in relation to SNS use (Banyai et al., 2017), but also because the use of electronic devices is associated with unhealthy lifestyles, such as high sugar and caffeine intake (Bradbury, Turel, & Morrison, 2019). Study 2 proposed one way to modify these variables: to expose users to their activity levels and to manipulated information about peer activity. This suggests that if users want to help themselves to self-regulate their SNS use, they should monitor their own behaviour using SNS use logs. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also imply that if people try to reduce SNS use in groups and report to each other on their progress, they may be more e<sup>fi</sup>cacious than otherwise, given the importance of peer use reduction for self-regulating one’s behaviour.

From service providers’ standpoint, they may want to help users with problematic use levels overcome their addiction in order to reduce legal risk and possibly show empathy to users in need (Turel et al., 2011). This can be done, based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, by including optional features that make activity levels more visible and by providing information on others’ successful attempts to reduce use. Interestingly, this is exactly what Apple has done in iOS 12; they now provide weekly activity summaries. Therapists can also use our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to help people with di<sup>fi</sup>culty controlling their SNS use. Based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings asking patients to manage SNS activity logs can serve as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step for motivating use reduction. Families and friends can also help users in need by reducing use in support of someone who <sup>fi</sup>nds it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to reduce SNS use. The e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of such approaches should be examined in future research.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Several limitations that provide opportunities for future research should be acknowledged. First, data for all the studies were gathered using US-based Facebook users who were students; and we employed a quantitative deductive research paradigm. Our models should be tested in other contexts that vary in culture, socio economic status and IT artefacts to increase generalisa bility; and enriched through studies employing qualita tive inductive approaches. Second, this study has focused on a limited set of self-observations, judgemental and reaction variables. Since Bandura’s framework is broad and general, this is an important <sup>fi</sup>rst step that allowed for theorising on the hypothesised e<sup>f</sup>ects. Nevertheless, the framework can be extended by developing and adding speci<sup>fi</sup>c measures for the sub-functions in the self regulatory variables that are pertinent to SNS. For instance, perceived enjoyment and emotional attachment could be explored as measures of valuation of activity sub-function under the judgemental function of the self regulatory model. We also omitted variables that focus on the cost of discontinuance for parsimony reasons, and these can be included in future extensions of our work. Third, this study focused on one corrective behaviour, future research may examine and compare the proposed model across various corrective behaviours. Future research can also expand the measure of realisation to include a dimension that captures stigma associated with excessive use of SNS. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, stigma consciousness, which refers to the magnitude of an individual’s self consciousness of his or her stigmatised status and expects to be stereotyped and discriminated against in social interactions (Pinel, 2004) can be used as a dimension of realisation to examine its psychometric reliability in explaining disruptive SNS use behaviours including SNS use reduction. Fourth, given that our exploratory study indicates signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between IS use reduction, IS quitting (discontinuance), switching (vacil lation) and vacationing (or taking a break), future research can examine the extent to which IS use reduction can serve as a gateway behaviour for IS discontinuance since upon realisation of the problematic consequence of IS. Lastly, in study 2, participants knew that they were observed, and this might have a<sup>f</sup>ected their behaviour (Noah, Schul, & Mayo, 2018). Future research should <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to monitor social media behaviours without letting participants know.

## 6. Conclusion

This research employed a series of studies to extend theoretical and practical knowledge regarding a common but overlooked corrective IS use behaviour, namely SNS use reduction. It <sup>fi</sup>rst revealed that SNS use reduction intentions and attempts are prevalent. It next showed that sub-optimal states related to IS use, manifested here as addiction symptoms, can drive realisation regarding the problematic nature of one’s status quo and ultimately motivate use reduction. This motivation was shown to be augmented through social conformity and signalling processes driven by peer use reduction behaviours. Lastly, it demonstrated that realisation and peer use reduction can be manipulated to produce actual SNS use reduction. Given that many SNS users (and possibly users of other IS) present excessive use patterns, further examining the mechanisms and interventions that can help users self-regulate their IS use is important and worthwhile. By no means does this study o<sup>f</sup>er Bandura’s self-regulation of motivation and self-reactive action framework as the only model for understanding IS use reduction. Rather, this study shows this model’s viability in explaining SNS use reduction as a selfregulatory corrective behaviour and calls for other models that may further uncover drivers of IS use reduction behaviours.

## Exploratory Examination of Common Corrective IS Use Behaviours

This is the descriptive and exploratory part of study 1, aimed at exploring the prevalence of common corrective behaviours. It sought to examine (1) the prevalence of use reduction attempts relative to other corrective behaviours, and (2) the distinctiveness of use reduction intentions from intention to quit and intention to take a temporary break/vacillate.

## Participants and Procedures

SNS (Facebook) users (n = 251) were recruited from a population of 275 university students (91.3% response rate) via a class bulletin board. Those who agreed to participate completed an online survey in exchange for 1% bonus in the class. Their average age was 23.60 (18–50, SD = 0.403) and 48.2% were women. They used Facebook on average 6.2 days per week (1–7, SD = 1.33) and for 1.9 hours per day (0.1–7, SD = 1.60).

## Measures

First, participants were asked to report on the frequency in which they tried and the number of people they know who tried the three behaviours of interest in the last three months. Second, participants were asked to report on their intentions to quit (loadings = 0.95–0.98, α = 0.97), take a temporary break from (loadings = 0.97–0.98, α = 0.98) and reduce use (loadings = 0.95–- 0.96, α = 0.96) of the SNS. These measures were rotated in the survey and were adapted from the quit intentions measure in Turel (2015b), after pilot testing (n = 30, αs = 0.95–0.97). They were deemed to be valid and reliable. The instrument is presented in Table A1.

## Discussion

The descriptive results of the exploratory study demonstrate that trying to intentionally reduce SNS use is more common than other behaviours aimed at curbing problematic SNS use. ## Appendix B. Additional Details – Study 1

## B.1. Elaborated Methods – Study 1

Data for this study were collected with an online survey administered to 329 students in a southern state in the United States. This was done after pilot testing (n = 75). For the main study university students were chosen for the following reasons. First, they are prone to exhibit varying levels of addiction with SNS use. Second, they represent a reasonably large segment of the target population of people who present SNS addiction symptoms (Turel, 2015b). We focused speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on Facebook because it is very popular among students and provides the intrinsic rewards needed for addiction formation (Serenko & Turel, 2015; Turel et al., 2014). Participants were awarded extra credit points in exchange for their participation. Six versions of the same survey were created in which the order of the pages and the items were rotated to reduce common method variance and to control for self-generated validity (Chandon, Morwitz, & Reinartz, 2005; Mackenzie & Podsako<sup>f</sup>, 2012). Each participant was randomly assigned to one out of six versions. Only those who indicated they were current Facebook users were allowed to complete the survey. Out of the 329 who were invited to participate, 257 were Facebook users who provided valid responses (78% response rate). Table B1 presents key characteristics of the sample.

## B.2. Elaboration on Survey Instrument – Study 1

Items were adapted from reliable and established scales as summarised in Table B2. Addiction items were adapted from Turel et al. (2011). This scale has been shown to work well in the SNS context (Serenko & Turel, 2015). Realisation items were adapted from Paswan et al. (2015). Items capturing change in the number of friends engaged in SNS use were adapted from Turel (2015b). Use reduction followed Turel’s (2015a) adaptation of “intentions to quit” scale by slightly modifying the wording of items in order to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the new target behaviour.

The scale adaptations to the SNS context were pilot tested with a total of 75 participants (out of 80 invitees). Validity and reliability tests revealed that all the constructs are reasonably valid and reliable with Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and average variance extracted values greater than 0.7, 0.7, and 0.5, respectively. Face validity was also established with this group. Hence, the measurement instruments were deemed to be valid, reliable and appropriate for use in the main study. Only one item had loading slightly below 0.7 in the pilot study, but we decided to retain it for content validity purposes. This item had a loading >0.7 and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p = 0.001) in the main study.

## C.1. Elaboration on Methods – Study 2

Potential participants received training on how to extract use logs from their Facebook account (at t ). A week later (t ) use logs pertaining to their activity were collected for twelve activities including number of likes, number of comments, number of times Facebook was used to play games, number of status updates posted, number of links shared, number of private messages sent, number of times Facebook chat was used, number of friend requests accepted, number of photos posted, number of photos tagged, number of videos posted and number of videos watched. Participants were asked to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on these activity logs in order to trigger realisation. Participants were asked to manually report their use of Facebook in the past week for the twelve di<sup>f</sup>erent activities mentioned earlier and then asked if they were surprised about their use patterns in the past week before asking them to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the numbers they just reported as they responded to the realisation items. The question asking about participants’ feeling of surprise reads “how surprised are you about your Facebook use activity?” and it is measured on a 7-point Likert type scale ranging from 0 = not at all surprised to 7 = highly surprised. In the treatment condition, participants were more surprised $( p < 0 . 0 5$ , mean = 0.33, SD = 2.55) about their use activity in wave 2 (mean =

3.58, SD = 1.88) compared to wave 1 (mean = 3.25, SD = 1.77). Similarly, in the control condition, participants were more surprised $( p < 0 . 0 5$ , mean = 0.56, SD = 1.85) about their use activity in wave 2 (mean = 3.78, SD = 1.69) compared to wave $1 \mathrm { \ ( m e a n } = 3 . 2 2 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 7 8 \mathrm { ) }$

Two hours later they were asked to complete an online survey pertaining to their Facebook use behaviours and future intentions. Using a scenario-based factorial design, participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions as they logged onto the survey page: (1) no peer use reduction, and (2) peer use reduction. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst condition, it was stated on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the online survey (after the consent form was completed) that based on the provided logs their peers did not change their SNS use levels over the last week. In the second condition, they were provided a statement saying that based on the provided logs their peers reduced their SNS use over the last week by 15% (pilot tested with 15 users to show that it is reasonable). As a manipulation check, participants were asked to recall the percentage of use reduction of their peers presented at the beginning of the survey. A week later (t ) they were asked to again provide a copy of their use logs. Multivariate analysis of variance (Pillai’s Trace = 0.027, F(6,120) = 0.558, p < 0.76) indicated that there are no omnibus di<sup>f</sup>erences between participants in the two conditions in terms of all the predictors and control variables. A simple recollection question at the end of the survey (whether based on the provided information, their peers reduced or did not reduce their SNS use) was used for testing the manipulation. Responses were perfectly aligned with the manipulation (n = 62, did not change; n = 65 reduced activity).
