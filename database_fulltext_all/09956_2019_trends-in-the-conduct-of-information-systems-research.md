# Trends in the conduct of information systems research

Varun Grover<sup>1</sup>, Michelle Carter<sup>2</sup> and Dan Jiang<sup>3</sup>

Journal of Information Technolog 2019, Vol. 34(2) 160–177 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621983412DOI: 10.1177/0268396219834122 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

With the rapidly changing institutional and technological environment for information systems research, it is useful to examine how the conduct of research itself has been changing. In this Debates and Perspectives article, based on analysis of over 1800 articles, we first describe trends from 2000 to 2015 in co-authorship, research areas, unit of analysis, and research direction-setting initiatives. Our results indicate a rising trend among information systems researchers toward co-authorship. We also find that information systems research converges on a few broad research areas, and there is increasing diversity of topics within these broad themes. Concerning level of analysis, our results indicate a decreasing trend toward macro (organizational) studies. Finally, we note a lack of positive significant trends in the publication of special issue articles, review articles, and meta-analytic studies. We then offer our perspective regarding these trends along with a discussion of the importance of managing vicious and virtuous cycles that may result from community adaptations to environmental changes.

## Introduction

The information systems (IS) field frequently engages in robust introspective debates. A large portion of the debate has been directed to the issue of distinctiveness and identity of the IS field. For instance, reflective discussions on the IS discipline have raised issues such as the identity of the discipline (Benbasat and Zmud, 2003), the core of IS (Agarwal and Lucas, 2005; Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001), research focus (Agarwal and Lucas, 2005), research content (Dickson and DeSanctis, 1990), the form of publications (Grover and Lyytinen, 2015; Lee, 2000), as well as how research balances relevance and rigor (Kock et al., 2002). These studies largely focus on the content of IS research, and not so much on its conduct or its evolution over time.

While the debates have been ongoing, the context for IS research has been evolving, specifically with respect to the information technology (IT), institutional, and global environments. The most obvious change has been in the IT environment itself. From an environment characterized by mainframes to PCs to mobile computing, we are now seeing an explosion of social media, cloud, big data, analytics, and three-dimensional (3D) technology, all increasing the ability to cultivate a wide diversity of IT-based solutions and customer experiences. Technological developments have increased connectivity between people, applications, and devices, leading to an explosion in variety and volume of information (Merali, 2004). Changes in the institutional environment are well documented in Hirschheim and Klein (2012) and reflect not only the increasing robustness of the field’s professional associations (like Association for Information Systems (AIS)) and journals but also the increasing institutional pressure to publish in top refereed journals. The third set of changes reflects the increasing globalization of the IS research environment. From a US-centric emphasis, the field now truly includes researchers from all regions of the globe, vying for space in an increasing diverse and international set of conferences and journals.

While the evolution of the IS research context is significant, an interesting question is are corresponding changes taking place in the way the field conducts its research? Some changes are apparent. For instance, digitization in organizations and society is increasing the diversity of the problems that IS research is addressing (Grover, 2012) and compels IS scholars to turn to theories (e.g. flow theory, absorptive capacity theory, dynamic capabilities theory (Larsen and Eargle, 2015)) from reference disciplines such as psychology, marketing, or economics, rather than traditional references such as computer science (Grover et al., 2006). The field is also using more diversified methods to meet the needs of studying new phenomenon and new data, such as text mining, web mining (Chen et al., 2012), and neuroscience (Dimoka, 2012). Furthermore, with stronger institutional structures (e.g. prominence of AIS, strength of field’s top journals; Hirschheim and Klein, 2012), there is greater tightening of research standards, while globalization of the field adds to the pressure to publish.

Along with these changes, there are questions that may not be apparent regarding research conduct. For instance, are IS researchers collaborating more? How are research topics evolving? As the knowledge base grows, are journals putting more direction-setting initiatives, like meta-analytical studies or special issues, in place? To explore these questions, this study first examines trends in the field over 16 years, based on articles published in eight leading journals. The objective in doing so is purely descriptive—to observe how IS research conduct is (or is not) changing over time. After presenting our observations, we use systems theory to interpret these trends. We then try to assess whether it is possible that these trends are pushing the field toward virtuous cycles or steering it toward vicious cycles. Finally, we conclude with recommendations for the field in order to avoid possible detrimental outcomes for the field.

## Descriptive study of trends in IS research conduct

To observe patterns of IS research conduct over time, we identified four constructs that represent a set of choices made by researchers as they engage in research. These are research collaboration, research areas and constituent topics, level of analysis, and direction section initiatives. Admittedly, these are not the only constructs that could be examined. However, these constructs are particularly germane to our analysis since they reflect a large part of what IS researchers do—that is, choose collaborators, select areas and topics for study, and consolidate knowledge to advance the field.

Consistent with previous studies examining themes and topics in IS research (Alavi and Carlson, 1992; Swanson and Ramiller, 1993; Vessey et al., 2002), this study uses a classification process approach to address our research questions. Our sample is drawn from eight leading IS journals during the 2000–2015 period: European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of Information Technology (JIT), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), and MIS Quarterly (MISQ). Based on a recommendation from the IS Senior Scholars Consortium, the AIS has adopted these journals as the “basket” of top eight journals in the IS field (Liu and Myers, 2011).

While other top-ranked multidisciplinary academic journals, such as Management Science and Decision Sciences, publish a substantial number of IS articles per year, we restrict our analysis to IS journals only. Also excluded are practitioner journals, such as Communications of the ACM. Our decision to draw our sample from the “basket of eight” does have limitations, in that we deliberately exclude a number of publication outlets from analysis. However, we believe that the selected journals are representative of the field’s priorities and provide a comprehensive set of papers with which to evaluate IS research. Table 1 summarizes the dimensions and measures of research conduct. Appendix provides details of the classification procedures and measures.

To describe IS research community conduct, we regressed the dependent variable (year) on the measures of our independent variables to generate trend estimates (regression coefficients) on our sample, pooled across journals. Trend results are derived from the trend of annual changes in percentage of each dimension being measured rather than trends in changes of absolute numbers counted.

Table 2 and Figure 1 show the pattern of article authorship across IS journals over the period 2000–2015. Overall, sole authorship represents only 10% of the articles published by IS journals. The dominant pattern of research collaboration is multiple authors per article (90%). The estimated trends indicate a decline in sole authorship (–0.011, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and rise in co-authorship (0.008, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ from 2000 to 2015 (23% in 2000 to 8% in 2015). These trends can be largely explained by an increase in articles with three authors (0.007, p < 005) and four or more authors (0.010, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). In 2000, articles with three authors accounted for 25% of published articles versus 42% in 2015. Similarly, articles with four or more authors rose substantially from 8% in 2000 to 24% in 2015. Moreover, collaboration with colleagues from other disciplines (referred to here as co-author diversity) is also increasing (0.007, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . In 2000, co-author diversity represented 5% of the total articles published; by 2015, this percentage had increased to 19%. Thus, research collaboration is clearly increasing over time. It is possible that these changes reflect the increasing challenges placed on research due to the technological and institutional environment. The trend estimates presented in Table 3 show that IS research converges on a few broad research areas, even though there is increasing diversity of topics (i.e. trends within subtopics under the broad areas). Of N = 1827 articles in our sample, a large subset, $\mathrm { N _ { s } } { = } 1 6 4 1$ (89.8%) focused attention on 14 research topics. As a percentage of the total articles in our sample, our analysis supports earlier findings (Vessey et al., 2002) that organization concepts (80.4%) are the most frequently studied research area. In the organization concepts research area, technology transfer (17.90%), IT impact (16.60%), and IT usage/operations (15.80%) are the most frequently studied research topics, which echoes findings from earlier studies (Córdoba et al., 2012). While the positive trend in IT usage studies (0.122, $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ is unsurprising, the significant positive trends for IT impact (0.115, p < 0.01) and management of the computing function (0.095, p < 0.001) are encouraging, given the discipline’s focus on effective deployment and use of IS in organizations. Strategy (2.0%) and computing/information as a business (2.0%) represent a smaller percentage of articles published; however, their positive trends indicate continued interest in understanding IT’s role in defining the digital economy.

To investigate emergent patterns further, we considered the overall diversity of research topics across journals over time. Figure 2 shows published articles in the top research areas, across IS journals from 2000 to 2015. The black area represents problem domain-specific topics; light gray (with black lines separating each topic) represents topics related to systems/software management concepts, and the dark gray area (with white lines separating topics) represents topics focused on organization concepts. As Figure 2 illustrates research topics aggregate into broader research areas at a higher level of analysis. Also, evident in Figure 2 is a growth in topic diversity within the area of organization concepts over time. This is characteristic of a “fragmented adhocracy” (Whitley, 1984), suggesting that IS researchers integrate emerging research topics into existing research areas—potentially, by drawing on extant theoretical frames, literature-bases, and methods (Córdoba et al., 2012).

Table 4 presents findings for the level of analysis of the research papers in our sample. Of 1827 articles in our sample, eight could not be classified as micro- or macro-level studies. These articles were excluded and a subset (N ) of 1819 articles was retained for analysis. The significant negative trend in organizational-level studies (–0.013, $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , together with a significant positive trend in interorganizational-level studies (0.005, p < 0.05), indicates that some IS researchers who may previously have undertaken organizational-level studies are responding to calls for research that deals with broader issues of digital transformation across organizations (Agarwal and Lucas, 2005). Still, a significant negative trend in macro-studies, overall, suggests that organizational-level studies are challenging to conduct or publish.

Figure 3 summarizes the proportion of published IS research papers by level of analysis between 2000 and 2015. Trend lines in the chart indicate the significant negative and positive trends, respectively, for organizational and inter-organizational research. In 2000, organizational research accounted for 58% of published articles versus only 29% in 2015. Inter-organizational research represented 10% of all empirical publications in 2000. In 2015, 15% of published research focused on inter-organizational issues.

Table 5 and Figure 4 depict the publication pattern of direction-setting initiatives across the eight leading IS journals over the period of our study. Overall, special issue, review, and meta-analytic articles accounted for 512 (28%) of articles in our sample of N = 1827. These direction-setting initiatives contribute to building a cumulative research tradition by providing researchers with access to common interpretive schemes and rules for behavior. As Table 5 illustrates, across journals, promoting new areas of research (i.e. special issues; 24.1%) appears to significantly outweigh attempts to structure existing research areas/topics (i.e. review and meta-analysis articles; 3.94%). However, the overall trend in the pattern of direction-setting initiatives over time across IS journals was not significant (0.007, p = 0.128). Even when analyzed separately for SI and Review/Meta-analysis, the trends were insignificant.

Differences were observed among the journals, perhaps reflecting different editorial focuses. For example, EJIS showed a significant positive trend in the proportion of Review and Meta-analytic articles, while despite MISQ’s dedicated Theory and Review section, MISQ showed a significant negative trend in Review articles $( - 0 . 0 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and Review and Meta-analytic articles combined $( - 0 . 0 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Only JIT exhibited a positive trend in both promoting new research areas (0.014, p<0.001), per its editorial aims,<sup>1</sup> and structuring existing research (0.015, p<0.001). Overall, these findings alert us to the possibility that the IS research community may not be sensitive enough to the need to conduct research that consolidates its growing knowledge base.

While these trends by themselves are interesting, the more useful question is can they be explained? Even better, instrumentally, is there a diagnostic that can provide guidance on whether these trends are beneficial or detrimental to the field? To answer these questions, the following section proposes an evaluative framework based on systems theory to offer a guided opinion on both explanation and diagnosis of these trends.

## Framing the IS community as an adaptive system: explanation of trends

Organizational studies have established that context influences conduct (Johns, 2006). Mowday and Sutton (1993)

characterize context as “stimuli and phenomena that surround and thus exist in the environment external to the individual, most often at a different level of analysis (p. 198).” Such a context offers opportunities and constraints for individual behavior (conduct).

A useful and simple framework for evaluating IS research could be based on systems theory. Conceptualizing the IS research community as an adaptive system with many components, building blocks, or agents, capable of interacting with each other and with their environment (Levin, 2003), allows us to offer an explanation of observed trends. By adaptive, we mean that the system makes small incremental changes to self-organized patterns of behavior in response to temporal changes in the external environment. We presume that the IS research community is engaged in research with a common commitment to create and advance the state of knowledge in the field as well as to influence practice through actionable research (Stahl, 2012).

The components of the IS research community system are the external (IT, institutional and global) environment, its agents (IS researchers), and their interactions that result in behaviors reflected in the conduct of IS research. This conduct emerges from conditions created by the environment, which require adaption dynamics of self-organization and autopoiesis, driven by interactions between IS researchers. The outcomes of adaptations could be progressively favorable (i.e. virtuous cycles) or progressively unfavorable (i.e. vicious cycles) for the IS field. Virtuous and vicious cycles arise because feedback does not immediately take effect—that is, a set of consequences may manifest at one level and lead to a different set of outcomes in another level of the system. For instance, based on the trend of a growing global field (environment), there is increased research that adds to a growing research base (contextual condition). One adaptation could be for researchers to engage in consolidative research like meta-studies (research conduct) to manage growing research in an area. This could be a virtuous adaptation (i.e. have positive reinforcing implications for the field) as it could lower knowledge barriers and facilitate new researchers to readily assimilate knowledge in an area. Since feedback is an inherent part of systems, the systems perspective provides insights into how virtuous and vicious cycles arise—and frames how the IS research community can design interventions that optimize virtuous cycles and steer around and out of vicious ones.

To explain the descriptive observations above, we can examine how contextual conditions, created by the environment, could foster trends in research conduct. Figure 5 illustrates the environment, conditions created for IS researchers (middle box), and the conduct of research. The first link is between the environment and contextual conditions. Environmental IT trends create conditions of rapidly emerging IT-based phenomena that can be studied. These trends also create conditions of ubiquitous digitization, which has many implications, including increased availability of large data sets. The institutional environment around IS research and global trends spawn a growing cadre of researchers vying for the same journal space, which has increased the challenges of publishing. Emergent IT phenomena, pressure, and globalization—along with the natural accumulation of knowledge—create the growing research base and many incommensurate mid-level models (Grover and Lyytinen, 2015).

To explain our observed trends, we then examine the link between contextual conditions and research conduct. The trend in both extent and diversity of collaboration can be explained by the need to transcend individual limitations and facilitate achievement of a codified knowledge base in response to the demands and opportunities for research created by the external environment (i.e. an increasingly vast repertoire of IS issues, technologies, data, theoretical perspectives, and research approaches). Hence, as the external environment brings more diverse IT phenomena and the codified knowledge base grows, collaboration is one useful way to cope with the knowledge and workload demanded by these changes. We also assert that the institutional environment is adding to demands. Pressure to publish in top journals imposed by more stringent promotion and tenure requirements requires collaboration as a possible means to diversify risk over a broader portfolio of projects. Correspondingly, the journals themselves demand more, in terms of quality of theorizing, data, novelty of method, or triangulation of results. These demands can be facilitated by collating diverse expertise through collaboration.

The trend in research topics indicates that broad research areas are stable, but there is an increase in diversity of topics being researched within each area. Stable but broad research areas could reflect a way to cope with rapidly emerging IT phenomena and ubiquitous digitization. For instance, in dealing with an emerging IS phenomena, a strong and known theoretical model can provide an organizing frame allowing researchers to add familiar structure to phenomena that seem novel. In addition, stable research areas generate a shared understanding of the field and its thematic elements— thereby creating an identity that community members can embrace. A shared understanding among community members facilitates an aggregate response to environmental changes, which, in turn, reinforces the field’s confidence in its identity as a research discipline. Researchers, dedicated to synthesizing technological and institutional changes within the field’s identity, incorporate fragmented research topics into a smaller number of research areas; thus, over time, research topics that align with the identity of the field will flourish, while those that do not will decline (Sidorova et al., 2008).

The trends for level of analysis can be explained through the ubiquity of digitization. The clear trend in inter-organizational-level research indicates that with ubiquitous digitization, more interesting and pervasive research phenomena occur across organizational boundaries. Fostering digital innovations through collaborations, crowdsourcing, and platforms is increasing, and competition is occurring between IT ecosystems that involve numerous organizations.

Finally, the trends for direction-setting initiatives are curious in that there is no overall trend, but there are idiosyncrasies across journals. Direction-setting initiatives such as special issues, review articles, and meta-analytic studies provide researchers with global feedback, ensuring access to common interpretive schemas and rules for behavior (Dooley and Van de Ven, 1999). Special issues focus on emerging topics and methodologies and direct researchers’ attention to what to study and how. Both also serve to reduce knowledge barriers for a research area. Review articles and meta-analytic studies emphasize research consolidation by structuring existing research areas/topics, as well as provide research direction by identifying “gaps” or opportunities for future research. Only JIT and EJIS exhibited positive trends in direction-setting initiatives. With the growth in the research base as well as emergent IT phenomena, lack of a positive trend across leading journals, as a whole, raises the question of whether these kinds of studies are too challenging for IS researchers, too difficult to publish, or simply a manifestation of the editorial policies of individual journals.

## Vicious and virtuous feedback cycles: diagnosing the trends

Are the observed trends positive or negative for the field? Establishing a definite diagnostic is difficult—since a trend like increasing collaboration—could result in positive or negative outcomes for the field depending on what the collaboration intends to accomplish. In the following, we argue, somewhat speculatively, the conditions where observed trends could spawn virtuous or vicious cycles and the key questions worthy of debate by the research community. Our goal is to sensitize the community to these questions to alleviate the probability of detrimental outcomes.

## Research collaboration adaptations and recommendations

The results clearly indicate that collaborative research with high authorship (>3) is substantially increasing. We believe that, in general, collaboration is a positive adaptation. This would be the case when collaborative teams are put in place specifically to adapt to environmental changes as a selfcorrecting feedback loop. The virtuous cycle of collaboration indicates that more co-authors contribute positively to higher output quality, and evaluative systems can reasonably assess their contribution. If research collaboration reflects people bringing their diverse expertise to complex problems, it can contribute to increasing the quality of IS research overall. Big data, the interconnected social media platforms, the accelerated advances of artificial intelligence, and 3D technologies all pose great challenges to IS researchers and necessitate the utilization of new research methods (such as data/text mining). Collaboration with researchers from other fields such as Computer Science and Neuroscience, which is increasing in our data set, can bring special skill sets to newer IS problems as well as facilitate a greater capacity to tackle complex research tasks. Even collaboration resulting from institutional pressures to publish in highly rated outlets (Dean et al., 2011) can be positive if it means that these outlets demand that the problems being addressed are more substantial and salient.

However, in the IS field, we see the possibility of the same contextual conditions pushing collaborative behaviors in unfavorable directions. With digitization spawning massive data sets, problems taking on more complex inter-disciplinary frames, and the high pressure to publish, vicious cycles are possible. We see two conditions, opportunistic co-authorship and resource-driven co-authorship, creating issues for the field. Intensive pressure to publish can lead to a paucity of colleagues willing to provide pre-publication vetting of papers—requiring “payment” in terms of quidpro-quo arrangements. Similar arrangements could be made by adding authors through a tacit reciprocal agreement, solely for multiplying individual productivity. While such opportunistic models of collaboration are often not sustainable, they surreptitiously occur in environments where topics change and publishing pressure is high. Also, with the “big data” phenomenon spawned by digitization, as well as the increasing use of inter-disciplinary frames that might involve labs (e.g. neuro<sup>2</sup> or behavior labs), equipment (e.g. eye tracking), or software (e.g. machine learning), there could be a tendency to follow more natural science models of collaboration. In these models, due to the resource-intensive nature of the investments, colleagues who participate in the collection or configuration of resources are routinely added as co-authors to any paper emerging from the investment.<sup>3</sup> While co-authorship is often well justified, in both opportunism and resource-driven cases, the virtuous cycle of collaboration, where co-authors contribute positively to output quality and evaluative systems can reasonably assess this, could break down—spawning a free rider problem. This is not good for the field.<sup>4</sup>

Therefore, while we clearly observe a substantial increase in the number of collaborators over time, this could be a cause for concern, and the field needs to be cognizant of the motivation and threshold for collaboration. As a social system, practices spawn norms for behaviors and sensitizing the field to debate this issue could facilitate the establishment of beneficial norms. Therefore, we raise the debate question(s) as follows:

With high pressure to publish, and an increase in resource intensive platforms (like big data sets, labs, software), are opportunistic and resource-driven collaborations a problem that compromises co-authorship and challenges our evaluative systems?

## Research areas adaptations and recommendations

We found that while contextual conditions indicate rapidly emerging research topics, and increasing digitization, IS researchers tend to work within a few broad research areas. This is virtuous if doing so can faithfully enhance our understanding of IS phenomena. The presence of relatively stable research areas contributes to virtuous outcomes in two ways: (1) it generates a common understanding of the field and its thematic elements—thereby creating an identity that the broad community of scholars can embrace and (2) it facilitates development of strong theoretical models that can provide a basis for prescriptive advice. Therefore, researchers can efficiently cope with environmental changes, particularly emerging phenomena, by capturing and framing them within existing theoretical perspectives.

However, we also see these observations as spawning vicious cycles. If broad research areas are tied to theoretical perspectives, often borrowed from other disciplines, then it might indicate homogenization of phenomena (i.e. seeing different phenomena through common theoretical frameworks). This could result in overemphasizing external theory and insensitivity to refinements and nuances taking place in IS practice. For example, consider this thought experiment. Imagine every emergent IT phenomenon, whether it be social media, cloud computing, the sharing economy, or the Internet of things, is examined through a common innovation framework—that looks at stages of innovation (e.g. adoption) and factors leading up to innovation. Since the theoretical framing is similar, the resulting studies look similar. In this way, theory constrains the way we look at phenomena and subsequently creates path dependencies for future studies. Therefore, while common theoretical framing might give comfort to researchers in studying emergent phenomena, it misses nuances in the phenomena themselves (i.e. the difference between, say, social media and cloud innovation) and fails the virtuous test of faithfully enhancing our understanding. If, as a research community, we simply draw on theoretical perspectives from reference disciplines as conceptual lenses to examine new IS contexts, we may fall into a trap of researching increasingly diverse topics in fixed ways—limiting creation of new perspectives (Grover, 2013; Grover and Lyytinen, 2015).

To advance the state of IS knowledge and to stay connected to the end consumers of our research, we recommend IS researchers seek out new IS topics and contexts that also provide opportunities for theoretical advancement, that is, not only studying phenomena through borrowed theoretical lenses but also to explore where theory can be adjusted based on the IS phenomenon itself. By asking how a phenomenon can modify the reference theory, we do not treat theory as sacrosanct but as a malleable unit of knowledge. However, this could lead to many idiosyncratic theories that might have greater fidelity with our phenomena, but are difficult to consolidate for advancement of knowledge. This raises important debate question(s) for the field:

Are we homogenizing our IS phenomena? If we create theories that are idiosyncratic to new phenomena, do we make it difficult to consolidate knowledge? Should we sacrifice fidelity for broader, integrative frameworks and theories?

## Level of analysis adaptations and recommendations

Our analysis reveals that while micro-studies account for a significant proportion of IS research, macro-studies dominate. Like Vessey et al. (2002), we observed that most studies are performed at the organizational level. At the same time, we find that organizational-level studies are declining, while inter-organizational studies are increasing. This finding is a virtuous cycle, if the reduction in organizational studies is reflective of the increased focus/importance on organizations as networks and business relationships in this Internet age.

The key consideration regarding adaptations that might lead to virtuous or vicious cycles is whether the level of analysis is chosen based on the difficulty in conducting research rather than the merit of the research problem at that level. We are aware that conducting macro-level research, particularly at the organizational level, presents unique methodological challenges in terms of obtaining an appropriate sample or establishing causal effects. However, we could argue that because the knowledge it creates is “in context” (as opposed to micro-studies conducted in experimental settings, often with student or convenience samples), its potential to “impact” practice is arguably higher than micro-level research. This is because macro-level studies seek to understand how IT is changing organizations, environments, and strategy. Therefore, if organizational studies decline due to methodological challenges, then such an adaptation can be detrimental to the field.

To illustrate, consider another thought experiment. A researcher wants to study the adoption and institutionalization of social media in multiple organizational settings. This might require a field survey or multiple site visits over time at individual organizations—both of which involve challenges in obtaining access and collecting data. Alternatively, social media data on inter-organizational interactions (strong and weak ties) may be readily available in secondary sources, along with sophisticated toolkits for studying network structures. Would the researcher forgo the arguably more substantive problem at the organizational level and pursue perhaps less substantive questions at a level where data are more accessible? While it is rational to consider investments needed to conduct good research, we would contend that the same contextual conditions, digitization, emerging phenomena, and high pressure to publish that can foster vicious cycles might eschew important questions due to the high costs of studying them. The field is poorer if research is increasingly driven by data accessibility or methodological convenience over important problems or knowledge gaps. While this is speculative reasoning for the observed trend, we do believe that any casual observation of recent papers involving big data might give this perspective some credibility.<sup>5</sup>

Ideally, to understand IS phenomena holistically, both balance and integration of levels are important. Prior IS research has illustrated that under-utilization by individuals often prevents organizations from realizing expected benefits from their technology investments (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Venkatesh and Goyal, 2010). In an increasingly digital world, individual- and group-level studies that focus on the embedded and interconnected nature of IT are needed to extend understanding of IT assimilation into organizational processes. Thus, maintaining a balance between macroand micro-studies is important to narrowing the gap between research and practice and enhancing the IS field’s credibility as a business discipline. In addition, integration of levels through more multi-level studies would enrich our understanding of the plasticity of digital technologies.

We frame the debate question(s) as follows:

Are data, tools, and pressure to publish, driving what we study? Are researchers ignoring important research questions, particularly at the organizational level, because they are too difficult to study at a quality demanded by our top journals?

## Recommendations for direction-setting initiatives

We identified special issues, review articles, and meta-analytic studies as direction-setting initiatives. Special issues directly facilitate direction setting, while review and metastudies offer the additional advantage of knowledge consolidation. Such studies are an important adaptation to counter a rapidly growing research base. While our results did show significantly higher incidence of special issues, the trends for all three categories were not significant across journals, as a whole. Individual journals did show trends, presumably based on their idiosyncratic editorial policies. For instance, JIT stood out as the only journal that exhibited a positive trend in both special issue and review studies.

So, how should we assess the lack of significant trends in our overall sample? Considering review studies, a virtuous circle occurs when we have periodic consolidation of research in areas where the literature has grown to the point of being unwieldy. Organizing our knowledge base in some meaningful way—for example, through review articles that integrate multiple theoretical frames used to study the same phenomena—ameliorates the challenges imposed by the diversity of potential IS research issues on one hand and the sheer volume of literature within wellestablished research areas on the other hand. Such studies enable the system to behave more predictably (by reducing autonomy of individual researchers) and reduces the cognitive burden imposed on researchers by the existing body of knowledge. This makes a research area accessible to new researchers who want to study in that area. However, if as a community, we fail to consolidate our knowledge and, correspondingly, fail to signpost research opportunities, we may find ourselves chasing fads to eschew the burden of going through a formidable literature base in well-established research areas. Some years ago, Eugene Wigner (1950) cautioned against this tendency in Physics (a relatively mature discipline):

Today, we are neglecting the theory of solids in which a student has to study perhaps six hundred papers before he reaches the frontiers and can do research on his own, we concentrate instead on quantum electro-dynamics in which he has to study six papers. (p. 423)

Such a strategy of adaptation by avoidance may enable researchers to shift attention to other areas with a less daunting body of knowledge. This is not good for the field.

Similar positive and negative effects can be found regarding special issues. Such issues can catalyze research in particular areas, methods, or theories, to build a critical mass of research of a certain type that might have taken much longer through natural means. However, who decides the content of special issues? Some contend that editors of A-list journals have primary responsibility for reviewing research and defining the direction of the IS field (Guthrie, 2003). The danger in this approach is that some editors might (a) institutionalize special issues, or (b) use them as tools to attract papers on the newest technology phenomenon, to be a first mover on the topic. Both these approaches could be problematic. In the case of (a), journals that have “standing” special issues on a topic (e.g. as an annual event)<sup>6</sup> are creating a system that devotes energy to favored topics at the opportunity cost of missing more substantive trends. In the case of (b), it is difficult to judge early if a new technology offers a substantially new way of thinking or simply a variation in context. While IT evolves, some of the thinking regarding its management and interaction with the social enterprise demonstrate consistent patterns over time. Is integration of social media in companies today very different from the problems in integrating end-user computing in the 1980s? Conflict between IS and business units over “control” of social media is very reminiscent of the conflict for control of end-user computing. Pushing a special issue in this area might erroneously presume that it will attract new kinds of thinking, simply because the technology is new. This could create path dependencies and overemphasis in one area at the cost of another.

In sum, our results point to a dominance of special issues over knowledge consolidation and lack of clear directionsetting trends (individual journals notwithstanding). Unfortunately, review and meta-studies are tough to conduct and difficult to review. Negative outcomes could occur if these results are indicative of insufficient knowledge consolidation. This could result in researchers avoiding certain areas because of knowledge barriers to entry. In addition, the large number of special issues could be a concern if these are institutionalized or used to promote “fads,” for first mover advantage, by editors who approve these issues. To avoid a vicious cycle, controls should not stifle active attempts at direction setting among researchers.

The important debate question(s) for the field are as follows:

Should editors determine the incidence and topics of direction—setting papers—or should the community has a greater say in such initiatives? How can we discriminate between fads and substantive IT phenomena?

## Practical guidelines

We observed trends in the conduct of IS research based on a sample of over 1800 articles published in eight leading IS journals over a 16-year period. We then offered arguments, somewhat speculatively, on whether these trends reflect adaptations to the changing environment and whether they have positive or negative consequences for the field. The issues raised in our discussion are typically not the subject of much conversation. Therefore, one of our goals is to highlight the questions that should be the subject of debate. Such debate can enable the field to steer around vicious circles and foster positive outcomes. Table 6 summarizes our findings, implications, and recommendations for the field and its stakeholders.<sup>7</sup>

So, what can readers of this article do with this study? We believe that, at the minimum, the field as a collective should engage with the debate issues raised. This can be done in various conferences, colloquia, and other forums. As individual researchers, we can also make changes in the conduct of our research. For instance, if collaborations increase, and papers in IS routinely have 4, 5, 6 or more authors, then do we need to foster social controls of this— by carefully evaluating motivation and threshold for coauthorship? Those of us who have institutional responsibilities for evaluating research contributions should recognize that contributions may not always be cognitive— but could also entail access to equipment, resources or data. Therefore, perhaps the nature of contribution needs to be recognized and schemas created for appropriate evaluation. Regarding the use of common theoretical frames, individual researchers can make more deliberative attempts to not only borrow theories and apply them to IS phenomena but also assume that the theories themselves are malleable and can be modified by the unique nature of IS phenomena. This way, theories become more idiosyncratic to the field, and IS phenomena become less homogenized. But does that create a more fragmented field as digital phenomena continue to emerge? In contrast, should we eschew theory and data or tools drive the problems being studied? Or would this undermine a fundamental building block of knowledge or even reduce study of substantive problems at the organizational level because of the challenges in doing such studies. Finally, should research consolidation be encouraged, despite its challenges, since failure to do so might bias researchers against research areas with high knowledge barriers? Cumulative tradition and exploitation of the research base after all are hallmarks of disciplinary progress. Also, can special issues, while offering easy access for editors to be first movers on emerging topics, be used as political and competitive instruments, if not carefully evaluated by using community-based (review) mechanisms.

## Conclusion

In this article, we proposed that since the turn of the millennium, there have been substantial changes in the IS research environment—IT, institutional, and global. We set out to observe whether these environmental changes have led to changes in research conduct and to offer our assessment of observed trends and thoughts on managing IS research community adaptations. Our observations suggest that within the IS research community, large and diverse collaborations are increasing, frequently researched topics have coalesced to a few dominant areas, inter-organizational studies are growing, and there is no distinct pattern in direction-setting initiatives that consolidate and extend research. Using the simple frame of systems thinking, we go beyond the data to explain the observed trends as “rational” adaptations to environmental manifestations (opportunities and constraints). From a systems perspective, IS community adaptations appear to reflect an evolving research discipline with sensitivity to the environment. Nevertheless, since feedback is an inherent part of any system, we need to be aware that vicious and virtuous cycles, which are often not apparent in the short term, can have detrimental effects in the long term. Thus, developing sensitivity to the co-evolution of the IS research community with its environment is critical to sustainability of the discipline.

In closing, we believe that this article offers three ways in which our community can develop the sensitivity necessary to adapt and thrive. First, we offer a set of debate question that we hope the discipline can embrace and resolve. Second, considering the nature of adaptations presented here, researchers should nurture a sense of individual responsibility for the well-being of the field. Because their seemingly isolated decisions may have far-reaching implications, we offer suggestions for how individuals in the field can rethink the conduct of their work. Finally, we suggest that with the pervasive nature of digitization taking hold and profoundly affecting how business is conducted, we should continue to engage in introspective studies that assess our trends and adaptations and provide meaningful direction. Lacking such feedback, the field may drift in unproductive directions (e.g. the fourth column of Table 6). It is the responsibility of gatekeepers of the field such as senior editors, conference program organizers, as well as individual researchers to foster interventions or engage in behaviors that steer the field around and out of vicious cycles, while amplifying virtuous outcomes.

## Method details and limitations

Choice of articles. To derive measures for variables used in the study, we selected only empirical and review papers from each of the eight IS journals over the period of 2000–2015. We restricted ourselves to empirical and review papers because these provide evidence of commu nity adaptations outlined in this study. Nevertheless, we broadly defined “empirical papers” to mean studies employing at least one of the following types of research methods: (1) Survey, where data are collected through methods such as mail questionnaires, telephone interviews, or from published statistics and analyzed using statistical methods. (2) Case Study, which is an empirical inquiry that investigates contemporary phenomena within real-life contexts, especially when the boundaries between the phenomenon and context are not clearly evident. Data are collected from a small number of respondents through methods such as participant observations, in-depth interviews, and longitudinal studies. (3) Action Research, which contributes both to the practical concerns of people in an immediate situation and to goals of social science by joint collaboration within a mutually acceptable framework. We identified “review papers” as studies that provide a synthesis of previously published research. Methods employed in review studies include qualitative, grounded theory, or text mining. Review papers published in MISQ Theory and Review were included as if they were published in MISQ. Altogether, 1827 papers were used in the analysis: 311 from EJIS, 189 from ISJ, 252 from ISR, 155 from JAIS, 151 from JIT, 299 from JMIS, 177 from JSIS, and 293 from MISQ.

Classification process. Selected articles were classified according to the level and topic of analysis. For level of analysis, we followed the general approach used by Swanson and Ramiller (1993) in their analysis of submissions to Information Systems Research (ISR): that is, we read each paper’s abstract, and whenever necessary the introduction and research methods section, to determine the level of analysis and accordingly classified this as individual, group, organizational, or inter-organizational. We identified topic of analysis using the same approach. In general, the classification process was straightforward. Two researchers followed the protocol after testing it with a subsample of articles. During the testing of the protocol, agreement between the two researchers was above threshold levels. Two other researchers were also invited to test the protocol based on the same sub-sample of articles. A high level of agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.92) was achieved between the researchers.

## Measures of IS research community adaptation to research process complexity

Research collaboration. Table 7 summarizes the dimensions and measures of IS research community adaptations. Research collaboration was measured using number of authors for a given article. In most disciplines, co-authorship is used as an adaptive strategy to cope with increasing diversity of research issues. For example, in disciplines such as chemistry, economics, medicine, and psychology, research collaboration is a common practice (Bayer and Smart, 1991; Sacco and Milana, 1984; Weeks et al., 2004). In the IS research community, we assume that researchers collaborate with others to transcend the limits of individual complexity (in terms of time, resources, and knowledge). By distributing complexity of a given research project among a set of researchers, research collaboration provides a means of dealing with increasing complexity of the research task created by the external environment and by the existing IS knowledge base.

Direction-setting initiatives. In this study, direction-setting initiatives refer to the structuring of published IS research in specific ways to map the critical areas of the field and to facilitate the research process (since this often requires a review of the stock of knowledge in a given area). Measuring direction-setting initiatives required us to survey all the articles in our sample. To arrive at standardized measures, in a given year, we divided the count of each article type by the total number of empirical articles published by a given journal in that period.

## Limitations

Due to some limitations, this study’s empirical findings need to be interpreted with caution. First, our analysis is based on data from eight IS journals that do not represent the full range of publications in the IS field. Consequently, we make no claims that our findings are generalizable to the whole IS field. They are, however, applicable to an area of high interest in the field. In the future, researchers may seek to extend this work by examining the issue of IS conduct across a more comprehensive set of journals.

Second, the interpretation of the trend observations using a systems framing was presented as an opinion—reflecting one possible interpretation. We make no claims that this is the only explanation of the findings. The framing assumes that the environment is changing and the IS conduct is responding to those changes. However, the framing does allow for both explanation and diagnosis of the findings.

Third, when studying collaboration, we considered only the number of authors per article. While this is an objective measure, it ignores many complex processes involved in knowledge generation, as well as idiosyncratic division of labor among different sets of collaborators. A more in-depth exploration of interpersonal and task-related processes among research collaborators would be useful for understanding and enhancing the knowledge sharing and integration process among members of the IS research community. Future research could also consider alternative measures of research collaboration, such as the number of different research institutions at which the co-authors are employed or the number of different disciplines represented by the authors. In addition, there is opportunity to study in more detail the drivers of specific adaptations. Future research could investigate the relative influence of alternative sources of change (e.g. pressure to publish and number of researchers in the field) on individual researchers’ decisions to engage in collaborative research efforts. In addition, a comparative study of research adaption strategies across fields may provide additional insights into the maturation of the IS discipline.

We also acknowledge that by using a pre-specified categorization scheme for research topics, we limit our ability to truly capture diversity of topics within a category. However, the used schema by Vessey et al. offers a fair degree of diversity of research topics, and any attempt to develop an emergent schema would be subject to criticism of justifying the basis for classification. So, we view our use of the current scheme as a reasonable compromise.

On a final note, we admit that in interpreting the results and providing guidance for the field, we cannot separate ourselves from our biases. We advocate for macro research, as well as the importance of lowering knowledge barriers through greater research organization. Regardless of these biases, however, we believe that the framing of the field from a systems perspective adds value to the introspective discourse.
