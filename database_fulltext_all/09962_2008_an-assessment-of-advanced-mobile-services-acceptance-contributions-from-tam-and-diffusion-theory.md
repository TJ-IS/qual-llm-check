# An assessment of advanced mobile services acceptance: Contributions from TAM and diffusion theory models

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 August 2007 Received in revised form 16 April 2008 Accepted 1 May 2008 Available online 13 June 2008

Keywords: Advanced mobile services TAM model Diffusion of innovation theory Social influences Perceived benefits The Netherlands

## A B S T R A C T

Today, in addition to traditional mobile services, there are new ones already being used, thanks to the advances in 3G-related technologies. Our work contributed to the emerging body of research by integrating TAM and Diffusion Theory. Based on a sample of 542 Dutch consumers, we found that traditional antecedents of behavioral intention, ease of use and perceived usefulness, can be linked to diffusion-related variables, such as social influence and perceived benefits (flexibility and status).

## 1. Introduction

In addition to traditional services, mobile devices can now be used to provide advanced mobile services, including banking, commerce, chat room, gaming, parking services, etc. Many advanced services are not new but were based on premium SMS, WAP, GPRS, UMTS and HSPDA protocols, offered individually or in portals such as iMode, Vodafone Live!, T-zones, and Orange World. Here we define advanced mobile services as data services that have the look and feel of Internet pages and are accessible via mobile or handheld devices, and operating at 2.5 and 3G+ mobile telecommunication networks.

Advanced mobile services are either offered by telecom providers or by specific services providers who either use the telecom providers’ portals, or offer their services direct to the customer. Providers therefore find it hard to distinguish themselves on a basis of price and service quality. Switching providers is common, with many services, like banking or tourist information, offered by the same service providers via all available mobile operators portals. Switching costs are low, and often subsidized by telecom providers, who may offer advanced handsets in combination with new subscriptions.

The Netherlands are characterized by high levels of awareness and penetration of mobile phones and services. Advanced mobile services have become widely available. GSM was introduced in 1989, GPRS in 2000, and UMTS networks in 2004. In 2006, experiments began with High Speed Downlink Packet Access (HSDPA). Coverage of UMTS and HSDPA networks rose to 90% [10]. Penetration rate of mobile handsets reached 109% in 2007, while 8% of the population subscribed to 3G services (WCDMA) [22]. Penetration and use of devices like PDA’s and Blackberry’s, as well as laptops is high. Twenty-four percent of all Dutch households access the Internet via mobile phones and/or palmtops, with an additional 14% via laptops when not at home or work. Compared to other countries, tariffs for mobile services are relatively low. Subscriptions and pre-paid arrangements are normally paid by the users, with only 6% paid by employers. According to a recent survey commissioned by the government, users are convinced that they are able to use all the functionalities of advanced mobile phones and mobile Internet services [21].

Although the conditions for the use of advanced mobile services appear to be favorable, market analysis indicates that, although consumers are aware of advanced services, they are reluctant to use their mobile phones to access them. Accordingly, service providers are looking for ways to show consumers the value advanced mobile services are offering. Practitioners and academics try to predict the conditions for usage of mobile services.

TAM [11] has been used to predict the attitudes and behavior of users of mobile services, based on perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) of mobile systems. However, other avenues of research may increase our understanding, such as findings on diffusion of innovations (DOI).

The role of interpersonal (e.g., word of mouth) and external influences (e.g., mass media) can explain mobile service adoption. As has recently been shown [17], we know little about the specific costs and benefits associated with innovative mobile services and the way these determine consumer value perception. In addition, some researchers [8] have explored the role of hedonic benefits of advanced mobile technologies. In our study, unlike others, the theoretical framework was based on concepts from TAM and DOI [28]. There is both a theoretical and a managerial need for an indepth study of the way value is created in advanced mobile services.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. TAM and advanced mobile services

Several models have been used to examine the adoption of advanced mobile services. IS acceptance research has been influenced by intention-based models rooted in cognitive psychology, such as the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [12] and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [2]. The adoption of technological products and services has been predominantly explained using them and their extensions: TAM, TAM2 [33], and UTAUT [34].

The original TAM contained three basic relationships affecting behavioral intention (BI): (1) PEOU leading to PU, (2) PEOU leading to BI, and (3) PU leading to BI. These three links have revealed a high consistency and validity in explaining the acceptance of several technological products and services. However, there has been some inconsistency found in the impact of PU on BI [14,32]. Therefore, further replications of this relationship are needed to determine the usefulness of TAM in explaining customer acceptance of advanced mobile services.

## 2.2. Limitations in explaining acceptance of advanced mobile services

There has been increasing concern about the appropriateness and comprehensiveness of TAM and similar theories and their being too parsimonious and incomplete, more appropriate in an organizational context, deterministic, and tautological [7]. In addition, they are considered lacking in ability to account for social influence and to explain user intentions. Recently, a meta-analysis of 51 studies [29] resulted in two main conclusions: (1) the original relationships were confirmed, mostly based on SEM, and (2) the technology under consideration had a significant moderating effect on user behavior.

## 2.3. Diffusion of innovation and advanced mobile services

The aim of our study was to evaluate the impact of different determinants on BI in the adoption of advanced mobile services, by combining acceptance models with DOI theory. In particular, we assumed that social influence may be a key element in people’s intention to use the services and applications.

## 2.3.1. Social influence

We defined social influence as the degree to which individuals believed that others thought they should use advanced mobile services. In the UTAUT model, social influences were recognized as being one of the four direct determinants of BI to use. However, social influence, also known as normative pressure or subjective norm, may be divided into two parts [5]: external and interpersonal. The first includes mass media reports, expert opinions, and other non-personal information, while the other includes word-ofmouth from friends, colleagues, and superiors. We refer to social influence as interpersonal influence, while media influence involves non-personal influences.

For mobile services, research results [19] suggested that there should be greater emphasis on the importance of internal individual differences and social influences. We assume that, for advanced mobile services, the role of social influence had been underestimated.

The relationships between media influence and social influence have been investigated by using the concept of spiral of silence [24]. We therefore defined media influence as the degree to which people had the impression that mass media reports encouraged them to use a new system. DOI argues that external, mass media influences are generally dominant in the initial stages of innovation adoption, because there are too few existing users for word-of-mouth to have major influence. We were concerned with the influence of media pressure on subjective norm in the adoption of advanced mobile services. Thus, we propose:

H1. Media influence has a positive effect on social influence.

## 2.3.2. The impact of social influence

Mobile devices and services allow people to move around while maintaining access to services and staying (socially) connected. Mobility, availability, and personalization, may also be important perceived benefits of (multimedia) mobile services [26]. Individual potential adopters of mobile technologies are exposed to informal social networks in which the benefits of mobile services are discussed. Opinions, decisions, and behavior are affected by these interactions.

Mobile services are adopted for both functional and nonfunctional reasons [27]. Mobile phones are predominantly used as symbols of social progress in developing economies. When the penetration of an advanced mobile technology is not substantial, users may regard it as fashion and decide to adopt it to enhance their perceived social status. Subjective norm influences image; when members of a person’s social group believe a behavior is correct, it elevates a user’s standing within the group. Thus, we proposed:

H2a. Social influence has a positive effect on perceived flexibility benefits.

H2b. Social influence has a positive effect on perceived status benefits.

Social influences are related to a person’s attitude towards mobile innovations and is based on the concept of personal innovativeness. Personal innovativeness in using IT is a trait reflecting a willingness to try out any new technology [1]. Innovations create uncertainty about their expected consequences, and individuals who are uncomfortable with uncertainty will tend to interact with their social network before making a decision. Overall, using an innovation is seen as a form of public consumption; it can be significantly influenced by friends and colleagues [16]. Based on this, we propose:

H3. Social influence has a positive effect on a person’s attitude towards mobile innovations.

Although PEOU is dependent on technology and user skills, explicit opinions expressed by others will affect it. Recently, Haaker et al. [15] studied the impact of social influences on the

People’s attitudes, behavior, and perceptions are affected by the information he or she receives from the social environment. Social influences may shape his or her confidence in or ability to use a technological system. Potential users of advanced mobile services may feel that adopting the services and technologies does not require much effort if others in their social environment say that the system is easy to use. Social influence encourages people to use a technology, though it has an indirect impact on their intention to adopt, via PU. Based on this, we posited:

H4a. Social influence has a positive effect on perceived ease of use.

H4b. Social influence has a positive effect on perceived usefulness.

## 2.3.3. The impact of perceived benefits

PU is the degree to which a person believes a system will help in performing a task more easily, quickly, and with quality, productivity and effectiveness. Accordingly, a perceived higher flexibility will increase the PU of advanced mobile services. In addition, the influence of image on PU was not expected to weaken over time, since status gains from system use should continue as long as group norms are favorable.

H5a. Perceived flexibility benefits have a positive effect on perceived usefulness.

H5b. Perceived status benefits have a positive effect on perceived usefulness.

In the past, mobile devices were regarded as a luxury item, but now the perception of luxury is related more to the use of new mobile services than to the devices [23]. This could be true for advanced mobile services, where services like TV not only enhanced the customer’s status, but also his or her PU of the services. This also supports the hedonic benefits perceived by customers. Overall, this suggested that customers would have a more positive attitude towards mobile innovations. Thus:

H6. Perceived status benefits have a positive effect on a person’s attitude towards mobile innovations.

## 2.3.4. The impact of attitude towards mobile innovations

Personal innovativeness can moderate the effects of a person’s perception of IT adoption decisions by playing a role in determining the outcomes [1]. Individuals with higher levels of personal innovativeness are expected to develop more positive beliefs from new technologies [18]. With regard to the adoption of mobile services, an innovative attitude towards technological innovations may enhance the perception of usefulness [20]. People’s attitudes towards innovations have been used to predict the rate of adoption of services [31] and of mobile commerce [35]. We therefore posited:

H7a. People’s attitudes towards mobile innovations have a positive effect on perceived usefulness.

H7b. People’s attitudes towards mobile innovations have a positive effect on behavioral intention.

## 2.4. Sample and procedure

The respondents were selected from a panel of 25,000 households that have been used for prior survey research; they are representative of the Dutch population. Potential respondents (N = 900) were first approached via telephone to ask if they would be willing to participate, whether they used a mobile phone, and if they had any experience with advanced mobile services other than mobile phones.

A self-administered questionnaire was developed and pretested on 10 managers and 10 academics. Their feedback resulted in our making several changes to the questionnaire to improve its clarity and ensure effective communication with respondents. Details were provided to the respondents about the purpose of the study and our definition of advanced mobile services. In April 2007, respondents who had agreed to take part and who matched the selection criterion (N = 714) were sent an e-mail with a link to the online questionnaire. Non-respondents were called after 2 weeks to ask if they had received the questionnaire and to remind them of the value of their input. Finally, we received 555 questionnaires, 13 of which were removed from data analyses because they were incomplete; this left 542 valid questionnaires for an effective response rate of 75.9%. The sample was checked against relevant criteria, including gender, age, number of members in family, education, and occupation, to establish that the sample was representative of the Dutch population. Analyses revealed no significant differences between our sample and the population. Moreover, analysis of variance and post hoc Tukey multiplecomparison tests revealed no significant between groups at the 95% confidence level. Overall, the results demonstrated adequate levels of sample representativeness.

## 2.5. Measure development

Our multi-item scales had been used and validated in prior studies. The appendix provides a summary with all items. To develop the concept of social influence we used items from Campbell [9]. A review of studies yielded a set of items that measured social influence in its dual perspective of ‘personal’ and ‘media’. To measure the dimensions of perceived benefits we relied on the work of Bouwman et al. [6], dividing them into ‘flexibility’ and ‘status’. We also included their items measuring attitude towards mobile technologies. PEOU and PU were assessed on the basis of Venkatesh et al. [34]. To measure BI, we decided to use the items suggested by Suh and Han [30].

## 2.6. Scale properties

To refine the measures, we conducted a confirmatory factor analyses using Lisrel 8.7, to determine the validity and reliability of our measures. As can be seen in Table 1, the results provided an acceptable fit of the data $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 4 3 6 ) = 8 9 4 ; ~ \mathsf { C F I } = 0 . 9 9 ; ~ \mathsf { I F I } = 0 . 9 9 ;$ $\mathrm { G F I } = 0 . 9 1 ; \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 4 )$ . All factor loadings for each individual indicator on its respective constructs were statistically significant $\left( p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ , in support of the dimensionality of the constructs. The composite reliabilities [4] and average variance extracted [13] from each construct exceed the usual 0.60 benchmark and 0.50, respectively. We further assessed the discriminant validity of the latent constructs in two ways. First, as suggested by Bagozzi and Yi [4], we calculated the 99% confidence intervals around the correlation parameter estimates between all possible pairs of scales, and established that none of these intervals included 1. Secondly, the square of the correlation of two constructs was less than the average variance extracted estimates of the two constructs. Overall, these results showed an adequate level of reliability and validity.

Once we checked the reliability of the measures, we developed a structural model to test the hypotheses. As can be observed from Fig. 2, the Lisrel analysis showed that all but one hypothesis was supported. ## 4. Discussion

The results of our study show that social factors exert an important influence on people’s decision to adopt advanced mobile services. The opinions of friends and relatives have a significant impact. The positive impact of social influences on PEOU is of special interest. Our results indicated that social influences should be viewed as an antecedent of constructs explaining the adoption of mobile advanced services. From a managerial viewpoint, this means that PEOU, and BI depends significantly on social influences. In addition, it appeared that social influences were determined by mass media perception. Mobile Telecommunication Operators and Service Providers should take these findings into account if they want to distinguish themselves in a highly competitive market. Also, because different people look for different types of benefits when using advanced mobile services, this will affect PU. Organizations should develop services that offer different utilities for different users.

Innovative people have a more positive perception of usefulness, and are more likely to start using advanced mobile services. This would suggest that firms that operate in the industry should segment their customers on the basis of personal innovativeness, and adapt the services to their specific needs.

Although most hypotheses were confirmed, there were some surprising results. The link between perceived status benefits and PU was not supported by empirical analysis. The impact of perceived status benefits on PU was not direct. PU was found to be indirectly affected by the perception of status benefits, via an improvement in user attitude towards mobile innovations. People who used advanced mobile services as a way to enhance their social status considered themselves innovative, and in turn this affected their attitude towards new mobile services, which reinforced their PU. This contrasted with evidence in earlier studies. Nevertheless, our study analyzed Dutch consumers who were familiar with mobile services, and focused on both m-Internet and other advanced services accessed via mobile devices, like mobile TV, location applications, healthcare services, etc.

## 5. Limitations

There were some limitations to our study. First, we proposed a model that was intended to increase understanding of advanced mobile services by focusing on user characteristics. It only was tested in one culture and a single market. There may be differences between US and Europe as well as within the EU.

We approached advanced mobile services in a generic way, based on the idea that they require specific network functionality from the mobile company and have the look and feel of Internet pages: this could present a significant limitation. Using locationbased services is different from carrying out mobile payments, as is the context in which these services are used. In addition, our research focused on the voluntary adoption of advanced mobile services, but we acknowledge that social influences may not be as significant in voluntary settings as they in mandatory ones.

Finally, there are also limitations in some constructs we used, such as perceived flexibility benefits. The two items used to measure them were generic in nature, especially from a practitioner’s point of view. In addition, the weak structural relationship between perceived benefits and PU could also be due to a weak measure of benefits.
