Abstract: Sharing of truthful information involving business intelligence between supply chain partners is a challenge on account of the asymmetric nature of the information, where one party possesses information such as market intelligence that is neither available in the public domain nor verifiable through third parties. While busesinss-to-business (B2B) technology solutions, such as CPFR (collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment), facilitate the sharing of historical information (e.g., transaction records), business intelligence (e.g., potential customer demand) is considered private. Central to CPFR is collaborative demand forecasting (CDF) that allows supply chain partners to share private demand information and incorporate the jointly derived demand forecast into production planning and product replenishment decisions. Implementing CDF, however, is a challenge because of the high costs of the laborious collaboration effort (e.g., to resolve forecast differences). Hence, companies are unable to realize the benefits of CDF and, in turn, the full potential of CPFR. Typically, the issues of information truthfulness and collaboration cost are addressed through an exception management mechanism that defines a range of forecast updates within which collaboration is automated without any human intervention in B2B trading partners. In this paper, we develop incentive-based contracts that explicitly consider the truth-telling behavior and exception resolution in decisions related to the threshold values of demand information. Our first contribution to B2B information management is in establishing the strategic value of exception management and resolution mechanisms in B2B relationships, leading to truthful revelation of demand information. Our second contribution is in developing exception-based incentive contracts, especially in light of the advances in today’s business practices and technology, to address issues associated with unobservable and asymmetric demand information. Specifically, we propose a resolution contract to coordinate the supply chain that directly incorporates both exceptions and resolution in an incentive mechanism. We show that these alternative contracts are all viable solutions in assuring truthful exchange of demand information but excel individually in specific situations and, thus, provide practitioners with alternative demand collaboration tools when price negotiation is not an option.

Key word s and ph rases: B2B commerce, collaborative commerce, collaborative demand forecasting, collaborative planning, exception-based incentive mechanism, forecasting and replenishment, information sharing, supply chain management.

Business-to-business (B2B) mark etplaces offer an Internet or digital technology– enabled platform for the exchange of products, services, information, and payments between businesses. A variety of B2B technology solutions have been introduced to support business relationships that range from transactional exchanges to collaborative partnerships [26, 35, 68], creating value through development of the digital technology–enabled integration capability without relying on the interorganizational ownership or linkage of physical processes [17, 56]. Collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment (CPFR) is a digitally enabled innovative B2B framework that facilitates the exchange of market intelligence and coordination of decision making between supply chain partners [67]. B2B solutions such as CPFR offer a key capability to process supply and demand signals across the supply chain [49]. As such, CPFR is foundational to supply chain multisourcing where business intelligence, such as market demand and ordering information, is shared to improve planning efficiency, services, and performance across organizations [6, 63].

CPFR, as enabled by enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, critically depends on the quality and integrity of demand information shared between B2B partners [26]. Central to CPFR is the framework of collaborative B2B activities of demand and supply management that includes forecasting customer demand at the point of sale; and analysis that includes exception management, whereby planning and operations are monitored for out-of-bounds conditions [41, 63]. It is this collaborative demand forecasting (CDF) and exception management that is the focus of this paper. Specifically, CDF refers to a process that allows B2B partners to share advanced demand information involving important business intelligence, and incorporate the jointly derived demand forecast into production planning and product replenishment decisions. Numerous studies have pointed to potential benefits associated with CDF, which include higher forecasting accuracy, lower inventory costs, improved customer service, and greater sales and market shares [2, 3, 62]. It is not surprising that many well-known companies such as Motorola and Best Buy have enthusiastically embraced CDF as an e-business solution [11], and invest in Internet-based collaborative technologies, including B2B trading exchanges such as World Wide Retail Exchange (W RE), GlobalNetExchange (GNX), and CPG-Market during initial CPFR implementation [1].

However, sharing information involving business intelligence can be challenging, because of the asymmetric nature of the information, that is, one party might possess information such as market intelligence that is neither available in the public domain nor verifiable through third parties [35, 50, 56]. Although the standard B2B agreement and the availability of interorganizational systems (IOS) such as CPFR facilitates the sharing of historical information such as transaction records, business intelligence such as potential customer demands and customer willingness to pay is considered private [5]. The challenge of sharing such information truthfully is far from resolved, as CPFR participants may lack common interests or goals [20, 40]. One such example is a supplier who solicits private forecast information from a manufacturer who has more accurate knowledge of the market condition. To ensure abundant supply, the manufacturer has an incentive to inflate his forecast in a costless, nonbinding, and nonverifiable way of communication known as “cheap talk” [47, 48]. Such behavior fosters mistrust between supply chain partners and dramatically increases the operational inefficiency, transaction risk, and business transaction costs [19, 50]. In order to assure trust in the B2B relationships, incentive mechanisms need to be implemented. Incentive alignment of B2B trading partners is a critical issue in information system (IS) research [4, 21, 68], and is shown to create fit between information and its supply chain context, and to ensure that the order fulfillment specifications are codifiable [16, 34, 39, 67]. It is also fundamental to the success of CPFR in practice [20].

Another challenge of implementation of CDF is the high costs of the collaboration process that may hinder companies from realizing the benefits typically associated with CDF [15, 58]. In particular, because of the “laborious” collaboration process (e.g., in order to resolve the forecast differences) and ineffective retail-level forecasts, CPFR has been limited from realizing its full potential [10].<sup>1</sup> To alleviate high costs and facilitate demand information exchange under CDF, exception-based forecasting mechanisms have been recommended and developed by many technology-enabled CPFR solutions in B2B settings [41, 52, 55, 62]. As illustrated in Figure 1, an exception-based forecasting mechanism defines a range of threshold values for demand forecasts. When the difference between the demand forecasts independently generated by the supply chain partners exceeds the threshold, an exception is generated and then resolved, typically, in a costly manner through iterative human interventions; when the difference falls within the range, collaboration is automated. An example of the managing-by-exception approach in CDF is depicted in Figure 2 [41]. This exceptionbased mechanism automates part of the demand collaboration process, and efficiently reduces the collaboration cost involved in the CDF process [55]. Sherman [54] reported that some companies in their CPFR pilot projects implemented the aforementioned approach. The collaborative technology, BetweenWare, utilized by these companies would generate system alerts when unexpected events appeared, and the companies then would leverage the real-time communication capability of the Internet to engage interventions.

Yet two important issues remain unaddressed. First, defining the threshold values (or exceptions) has so far been an arbitrary process. Many of these judgment calls rely on intuition or practical rules-of-thumbs rather than a rigorous supply chain–wide, systematic analysis [32, 41]. For instance, the exception tolerance level set in the CPFR implementation between the two companies Sears and Michelin, was achieved through a trial-and-error process [55]. For such circumstances where incentives are not incorporated in the presence of asymmetric information, the CPFR solution may be “doomed” [52]. Second, while the notion of managing-by-exception may be cost efficient, how can it be effectively implemented to ensure truthful exchange of information? Given the critical role of truthful information in determining the success of CDF [2], how exceptions and their resolution should be designed and managed to assure information truthfulness has not been studied [52] and, therefore, is the key purpose of this research.

To address the challenges, this paper analyzes four exception-based incentive contracts—that is, revenue sharing, (resolution) cost sharing, transfer payment, (exception) resolution—in order to incorporate the trade-off between the cost efficiency resulting from automating the demand collaboration process, and the collaboration effectiveness resulting from sharing truthful demand information. These four incentive contracts can be adopted respectively to cope with a wide variety of resolution costs arising from resolving forecast differences in CDF. One example of firms’ leveraging the Internet to implement the exception-based incentive arrangement is Best Buy, which utilizes a secure and scalable CPFR platform with an Internet-based solution tool Agentrics (from Manugistics). Attempting to foster “greater trust” and “one version of the truth,” this online CPFR platform allows Best Buy to collaborate on various exceptions with suppliers such as Panasonic and engage further electronic information transfer (EIT) to monitor business activities and coordinate interactions with its suppliers [47, 57]. The existing literature on EIT notes that companies could and should evaluate the fit between the level of EIT and the information processing needs based on contextual factors and strategically determine, in the CPFR case, the amount of efforts and costs associated with EIT in the exception resolution process [32].

Specifically, the objectives of this study are to (1) compare the effectiveness of different exception-based incentive contracts in regulating retailers’ truth-telling behavior, and shed light on the selection and implementation of them in practice; and (2) derive and discuss the optimal choice of exceptions in relation to demand pattern and supply chain cost structures. Our analysis shows that the four contracts proposed in this paper could all be effective in extracting truthful demand information, although they lead to second-best mechanisms driven by demand patterns and cost structures in the supply chain. However, these contracts are not equally effective—the nature (endogenous versus exogenous) and magnitude of exception resolution cost in CDF are found to be critical in the selection and implementation of these contracts. Furthermore, comparisons between the resolution contract and others indicate that the resolution contract functions very differently from the others and it has unique properties in terms of contract design. In addition, the resolution cost along with the manufacturer’s unit production cost, retailer’s unit lost sale cost, and retail unit price are shown to have different effects on the optimal exception level identified in different contracts, due to the divergent effects of these factors on extra production cost and the resolution cost, respectively. The key intuition is as follows: To induce the truthful demand information, the manufacturer shares with the retailer a collaboration cost (“resolution cost” throughout the paper) used to resolve the difference between initial and final forecasts, also known as “forecast update,” that is beyond the exception, and balances such cost with the promise of additional production quantity when the forecast difference is within the exception. These costs (in the forms of resolution and additional production quantity) serve as economic rents to secure information truthfulness, and the balance between the costs plays a unique role of integrating the costs in the presence of the exception so that incentive gaps in communicating the update across the exception are eliminated. Furthermore, by endogenizing the resolution cost (i.e., allowing the manufacturer to strategically determine the efforts in the resolution), the manufacturer can implement a resolution contract that may be more efficient than other commonly used contracts because by applying a lower marginal cost of resolution, the manufacturer could find an optimal exception level that also reduces the extra production quantity and cost required to induce the truthful demand information.

The main contributions of our research are as follows. First, we establish the strategic value of exception management and resolution mechanisms in B2B relationships lead ing to truthful revelation of demand information. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in the B2B literature that investigates the exception-based mechanism involved in information sharing in supply chains. Second, the study contributes to the B2B literature on information sharing [6, 8, 33, 47, 53] by developing a relevant and specific set of supply contracts—exception-based incentive contracts in light of the advances in today’s business practices and technology—to address issues associated with unobservable and asymmetric demand information. More importantly, we propose a resolution contract to coordinate the supply chain that directly incorporates both exceptions and resolution in an incentive mechanism, which differs in many ways from the other commonly adopted contracts. The results show that these alternative contracts are all viable solutions in assuring truthful exchange of demand information but excel individually in specific situations and thus provide practitioners with alternative demand collaboration tools when price negotiation is not an option.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The ensuing section is a review of the relevant literature. In the section thereafter, four exception-based incentive contracts are proposed and investigated to address the deficiency in the CDF mechanism adopted in the current CPFR practice. The conclusion section contains a discussion on the managerial implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

## Literature Review

Th e importance of sh aring d emand information in B2B relationsh ips lies in the challenge of dealing with demand variation and user preferences in making resource allocation decisions that directly affect service levels [53]. Recent studies demonstrate that information technology–enabled resources as facilitated through the Internet have strategic effects on the supply chain’s structure and process improvement, as production and coordination costs in supply chain collaboration could be substantially reduced [15, 17]. Sen et al. [53] indicate that a pricing heuristic can be developed and implemented to provide stable service levels and enhance B2B collaboration. As a form of EIT, demand information sharing may be significantly associated with demand uncertainty via the monitoring aspect of EIT, and with the effort to understand the product and procedure via the coordination aspect of EIT, both of which are significantly relevant in B2B relationships in supply chains [34]. However, simply making information transparent may be a “double-edged sword,” as is evident in Zhu [68], a study that investigates information transparency of B2B markets in a gametheoretic model with asymmetric information. Based on game-theoretical models on a sender-receiver framework, Lin et al. [40] study how information incompleteness and asymmetry may negatively influence knowledge transfer, and identify solutions to effectively address these negative effects. Similar issues involving information revelation in B2B settings are also examined in procurement auctions, supply contracts, and with security information, using game-theoretic or incentive contracting methods [24, 27, 65, 68]. This research follows such research frameworks to study a supply chain information-sharing problem that involves exception-based demand collaboration.

Assuming that market-specific information could be shared credibly between partners, many authors have investigated the benefits of CDF (e.g., [12, 18, 23, 28, 42]). Yet sharing proprietary information that is unique and private is not simple. Because of the asymmetric nature of private information, credible exchange of demand forecasts is not always guaranteed [7, 8, 47, 57]. In fact, it has been identified as one of the biggest concerns in the implementation of CDF in a semiconductor equipment supply chain by Terwiesch et al. [58]. The empirical findings of Terwiesch et al. indicate that buyers may inflate their demand forecasts to the suppliers (which is also referred to as “phantom ordering” in the literature [2]) in order to ensure supply availability. Companies such as Cisco Systems and IBM have encountered such shortage gaming behavior and, in turn, have a tendency to overinvest in production capacity [38]. Over time, such behavior of self-interest generates a negative impact on the buyer–seller relationship. Hence, Aviv [2] has called for careful design and analysis of information-sharing mechanisms and also the need to equip practitioners with tools that will accommodate instances of information sharing where information is unique and private.

Surprisingly, despite the importance of asymmetric information in a supply chain setting and the need for incentive mechanism design, few studies have investigated these issues [7]. While the differences in the opportunities to share information as relationships transition from transactional to collaborative have been discussed, there has been scant scholarly attention on the outcomes of sharing private, strategic information and on the appropriation of gains by buyers and suppliers [35]. The majority of published studies that address this issue belong to the supply contract literature, where various contractual arrangements between two parties are analyzed to align incentives (e.g., [7, 8, 14, 31, 33, 46, 66]), though the pricing mechanism to facilitate knowledge transfer is also relevant here [30]. The most commonly studied contractual arrangements include the rules for sharing the cost of inventory and stock-out, the rules for sharing revenue, and policies governing pricing between the supply chain partners [25, 59].

Although the existing supply contracting literature offers many insights into supply chain behavior, few of the above studies have directly addressed the incentive mechanism design issue in the context of CDF. Notably, Özer and Wei [47] study how a supplier (upstream) uses either a capacity reservation agreement or an advance purchase agreement to achieve credible forecast information sharing from a manufacturer (downstream) (a perspective similar to ours but not in a CDF setting). Among the handful of studies investigating the incentive issues in CDF (e.g., [1, 43, 44]), none of them adequately address the core CDF initiatives [60], namely, exception management and cost of exception resolution. The incentive mechanisms proposed in these studies are also rarely tied to the design of collaborative forecasting technologies, which largely follow the notion of managing-by-exception. As Ba et al. [4] point out, future research should consider incentive alignment as the third dimension in the design of information technology including e-business supply chain coordination technology.

Our paper represents a concerted effort to advance the existing literature on supply contract in B2B relationships and CDF toward the CPFR context, where exception management and cost of exception resolution are the norm for CDF. Specifically, our problem setting bears some similarity to those in the existing incentive-collaborative forecasting papers: a two-stage supply chain consisting of a manufacturer and a retailer in a CDF process. We assume that the manufacturer and the retailer are in a stage of collaborative relationship where a contractual arrangement is conducive to their success in CDF [37]. However, the main contribution of our paper pertains to the issues of incentive alignments, which are critical to IS research [21]. Our paper is different from the prior studies in that it addresses the sharing of asymmetric information issues in demand collaboration by integrating the design of several common supply contracts along with an explicit consideration of forecast exceptions and resolution. Furthermore, unlike related research in demand information sharing and supply contracting (e.g., [53, 65]), we focus on incentive contracts beyond pricing contracts. Importantly, as discussed later, we develop analytical models to enable credible demand information sharing in situations where current CDF practices or conventional contracts fail.

## The Exception-Based Collaboration Model

We consid er an ex ception-based CDF process in a B2B setting between a manufacturer (principal) and a retailer (agent). The retailer owns private demand information and uses it to generate a demand forecast update. During the collaboration process, the retailer is expected to reveal the forecast update to the manufacturer. The manufacturer follows the exception-based mechanism to derive the final forecast based on the retailer’s claimed forecast update, and plans his production accordingly. After the demand is realized, the retailer places an order that is not necessarily consistent with her claimed demand forecast. In the meantime, the actual size of the order deliverable to the retailer is constrained by the manufacturer’s production quantity. One important characteristic of the setting is the existence of incentive on the retailer side to distort private demand information in order to secure supply from the manufacturer [47, 48]. Such behavior is not unusual, especially when the two supply chain partners have not yet established a long-term, ongoing trusting relationship [49]. When such incentive exists, current implementation practice of the exception-based mechanism recommended by CPFR is questionable in terms of assuring the exchange of only truthful information, because the voluntary information sharing within the threshold range as required by CPFR may not guarantee its integrity [52]. Therefore, the manufacturer faces the challenges of inducing truthful information sharing both within and beyond the threshold range. Below, we examine the demand forecast updating process, and the first-best scenario assuming the demand information is shared credibly. We then propose the exception-based contracts as a second-best mechanism and study their effectiveness.

## Exception-Based Incentive Mechanisms

We start by examining the exception resolution process recommended by CPFR [61]. This process describes the steps to first identify exceptions for sales forecast and then resolves/collaborates on exception items for sales forecast [13, 62]. Exceptions can be identified through criteria determined from sales history and conditions, and the resolution of the exceptions “involves resolving sales forecast exceptions by querying shared data, email, telephone conversations, meetings, and so on and submitting any resulting changes to the sales forecast” [13, p. 312]. Under the current CPFR process, the retailer observes a private demand update u that translates into the updated demand distribution $X | u \sim N ( \Psi , \pmb { \sigma } _ { r } )$ , and then claims a forecast update $\hat { u } .$ Before the retailer makes her claim, the manufacturer decides and announces a threshold value aµ as the exception-based CDF contract offered to the retailer. If u[ exceeds the threshold value aµ, an exception is identified under CPFR, and the resolution process required in case of exceptions takes place. This process involves technology resources and human interactions that are costly and time consuming, and the substantial costs are why the exceptions are often kept at a very low level (such as $^ { 5 }$ percent for the short term in Figure 2). One could argue that resolution/collaboration should always be involved regardless of exceptions if the cost is a nonfactor. In fact, effectively managing exceptions is vital to controlling cost in demand collaboration, and demand collaboration is only economically feasible if the cost is accounted for and fully controlled [1]. As our results indicate, this cost, denoted as $C ( \hat { u } ) \geq 0 .$ , in the resolution process plays a critical role in the design of exception-based collaboration contracts. This cost captures the intense communication required in the collaboration, as indicated in the Best Buy case [51], and subsequent increased allocation of personnel-related resources, as indicated in the Panasonic case [45]. After the resolution/collaboration, the manufacturer accepts $\hat { u }$ and updates the demand distribution. If u[ falls under the (exception) threshold value aµ, the manufacturer automatically accepts the claim value and adjusts the demand distribution accordingly without incurring any resolution costs $\begin{array} { r } { [ C ( \hat { u } ) = 0 ] } \end{array}$ . Based on the updated demand distribution, the manufacturer plans the optimal production quantity by considering the trade-off between his production cost, holding cost, and lost sale cost. Here the production quantity is not part of the CDF contract terms, where the focus is on demand forecast sharing as observed in CPFR guidelines or industry practices [61], as discussed earlier.

However, the current CPFR practice cannot be implemented to induce truthful sharing of demand information in the presence of information asymmetry.<sup>6</sup> The reasons are twofold. First is the existence of a gap in the level of commitment between the two parties. The manufacturer commits to the retailer’s forecast update responsively when the update falls within the preset range. The retailer, in contrast, is allowed to deviate from her claimed updates when submitting her orders. This commitment gap creates a motivation for the retailer to inflate her demand updates. Second and more importantly, the manufacturer fails to consider this potential gap in his decision making, resulting in a loss of control to regulate the retailer’s truth-telling behavior.

To address these issues, we propose a mechanism design framework with an increased level of commitment from the manufacturer and to balance the control of truth-telling behavior. The solution is that the manufacturer rewards the retailer by promising a fixed amount of supply available as incentive in exchange for the truthful information. Under this modified CDF scenario, if the retailer announces a forecast update u[ subject to the contract provided ex ante by the manufacturer, the manufacturer will take the information and update the demand distribution as $X | \hat { u } \sim N ( \mu + \hat { u } , \sigma _ { { \scriptscriptstyle f } } )$ . Here, if u[ is less than aµ, the manufacturer will promise a production quantity $e + \mu + k \sigma _ { { \scriptscriptstyle f } }$ regardless of the actual u[; otherwise $( \hat { u } > a \mu )$ the manufacturer will promise a quantity $ { \mu } + \hat { u } + k \sigma _ { { f } }$ , though a resolution cost will be incurred. However, analytically the only value to assure information truthfulness is $e = a \mu$ . That is,

$$
Q = \mu + \max (\hat {u}, a \mu) + k \sigma_ {f}, \forall \hat {u}.\tag{8) \( ^{7} \}
$$

This overcommitment of production quantity $\operatorname* { m a x } ( a \mu - \hat { u } , 0 )$ by the manufacturer is a natural outcome of installing incentive compatibility within the exception range. Any arbitrary quantity that the manufacturer promises in response to a claimed update within the range, as long as it is less than the exception value, will be ineffective in extracting truthful information because the retailer can always gain by claiming a value above the promised value but less than or equal to the exception level. It is only when the promised value is fixed at the exception level that the manufacturer will restore the full control of the retailer behavior within the exception range. It is important to note that by overcommitting in production quantity, the manufacturer does not apply the full and truthful demand information in its production. This loss of efficiency serves as a rent that is necessary to induce the truthful information. Furthermore, this extra production cost also plays the role of balancing against the cost of resolution in the optimization of the exception range. As a result, the higher the cost of the extra production, the smaller is the range of the optimal exception.

In incorporating resolution and its costs, the manufacturer can choose from four different exception-based incentive contracts: three (revenue sharing, cost sharing, or transfer payment) are commonly used to solve incentive issues, the other (with endogenized resolution cost) is unique to CDF practice. In particular, under each contract where the retailer’s profit is given as $\pi _ { \scriptscriptstyle R } ( \hat { u } | u )$ , the retailer is motivated to share the demand update truthfully when subject to the following restrictions:

Incentive-compatibility (IC) constraint:

$$
\pi_ {R} (u | u \geq a \mu) = \pi_ {R} ^ {*} (u | u \geq a \mu , u \geq a \mu) \quad \forall u \geq a \mu\tag{9a}
$$

$$
\pi_ {R} (u | u \geq a \mu) \geq \pi_ {R} ^ {*} (u | u \geq a \mu , u \leq a \mu) \quad \forall u \geq a \mu\tag{9b}
$$

$$
\pi_ {R} (u | u \leq a \mu) = \pi_ {R} ^ {*} (u | u \leq a \mu , u \leq a \mu) \quad \forall u \leq a \mu\tag{9c}
$$

$$
\pi_ {R} (u | u \leq a \mu) \geq \pi_ {R} ^ {*} (u | u \leq a \mu , u \geq a \mu) \quad \forall u \leq a \mu\tag{9d}
$$

Individual-rationality (IR) constraint:

$$
\pi_ {R} (u | u) \geq L \quad \forall \mu .\tag{10}
$$

We will consider the change of the retailer’s profit function when her forecast update claim falls within or outside the exception range. Constraints (9a)–(9d) are a set of IC constraints that require the retailer to always truthfully claim the demand update, regardless of what the actual update is. In particular, relative to the exception threshold aµ, there are two possible ranges for both the demand update u and the retailer claim level u[, yielding four possible combinations. In constraint (9a), $\pi _ { R } ^ { * } ( \hat { u } | u \geq a \mu , \hat { u } \geq a \mu )$ denotes the retailer’s maximum profit when the actual demand update is greater than the exception, $u \geq a \mu$ , and so is the claim, ${ \hat { u } } \geq a \mu$ . In constraint (9b), $\pi _ { R } ^ { * } ( \hat { u } | u \geq a \mu , \hat { u } \leq a \mu )$ represents the retailer’s maximum profit when the retailer claims a level lower than or equal to the exception instead, $\hat { u } \leq a \mu$ , even though the actual demand update is above the exception, $u \geq a \mu$ . Constraint (9b) requires that when an update greater than the exception is observed, claiming it truthfully $( \hat { u } = u \geq a \mu )$ as it is beyond the exception yields no less than claiming a level within the exception, $( u \geq a \mu , \hat { u } \leq a \mu )$ for the retailer. Together, constraints (9a) and (9b) ensure that the optimal choice for the retailer is to claim $\hat { u } = u$ when $u \geq a \mu$

Similarly, the constraints (9c) and (9d) require that the retailer always share truthfully the demand update when $u \leq a \mu$ . Satisfying all the ICs indicates that it is optimal for the retailer to be truthful in sharing $\hat { u } = u$ , which maximizes retailer profit regardless of the actual magnitude of observed demand update u and results in an “implementable” mechanism [22]. Constraint (10) is the IR constraint for the retailer to be willing to participate in the collaboration, regardless of the level of u. Without loss of generality, we normalize the retailer’s expected profit of not participating in the demand collaboration as L.

Below, we examine these IC and IR constraints under the four contracts.

Resolution contract $\{ a , C ( \hat { u } ) \}$ : The resolution cost in the collaboration is endogenized and incurred both by the retailer and the manufacturer. This is a valid option only when the resolution cost is contractible in the supply chain. The manufacturer controls the determination of the exception range and level of resolution cost to coordinate demand forecasting with the retailer. In offering the contract, the manufacturer can promise to escalate the resolution/collaboration intensity to a substantially high level to involve senior-level executives in a lengthy and multifunctional process, for example, when the claimed update is far above the exception range, such that the cost of completing the collaboration is significant for both parties.

The resolution contract is designed to directly capture the resolution effort as an essential and unique component of the exception management under CPFR. This contract allows the manufacturer to determine how much resource for the supply chain partners to invest in the resolution and collaboration process. The manufacturer’s problem is to design an implementable mechanism by selecting $\{ a , C ( \hat { u } ) \}$ to maximize its expected profit, which is formulated as

$$

In this contract, the manufacturer determines the exception range and the rules for sharing revenues and, thus, his problem is formulated as

M1b:

The manufacturer will share a portion $s ( \hat { u } )$ of the retailer’s resolution cost to motivate the retailer in truthful collaboration. Thus, the manufacturer’s problem is formulated as

M1c:

$$
\underset {\{a, s \}} {\text { Max }} E _ {u} \pi_ {M} = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {a \mu} E T _ {x | u} (a \mu) f (u) d u + \int_ {a \mu} ^ {\infty} \left(E T _ {x | u} (u) - s (u) C (u)\right) f (u) d u\tag{14}

The resolution cost is exogenous and is incurred only by the retailer (or the manufacturer, not presented here). The manufacturer will use a transfer payment to coordinate the CDF. The transfer-payment contract $\{ a , t ( \hat { u } ) \}$ is given by

M1d:

\underset {\{a, t \}} {\text { Max }} E _ {u} \pi_ {M} = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {a \mu} E T _ {x | u} (a \mu) f (u) d u + \int_ {a \mu} ^ {\infty} \left(E T _ {x | u} (u) - t (u)\right) f (u) d u\tag{15}

The four new contracts (M1a–M1d) proposed above improve the retailer’s truth-telling incentives by assuring the retailer a supply quantity larger than the updated forecast, when the forecast update falls within the threshold range. Moreover, under M1a–M1d, the manufacturer explicitly captures and controls the degree of potential commitment gap between him and the retailer (i.e., aµ versus u) in his decision making. Therefore,[ the two areas that cause the failure of information sharing in current CPFR practice, as we discussed earlier, should have all been addressed. As demand forecast updates fall outside the threshold range, M1a–M1d offer four different contracts to facilitate demand collaboration. Among them, the resolution contract is proposed when the manufacturer can control and determine the resolution cost in the collaboration process, while the remaining three—the revenue-sharing contract, cost-sharing contract, and transferpayment contract—are better equipped to facilitate demand collaboration when the resolution cost function is known a priori, that is, outside the control of both parties.

Sharing revenue or cost, and arranging the transfer payment are contractual arrangements that are often recommended in the supply contract literature for effective supply chain coordination [64]. It can also be inferred from previous studies that these contracts could be equivalent to some other contracts, including price-related ones such as price discounts and buybacks [9]. We extend the investigation of these commonly recommended supply contracts to examine their roles of facilitating truthful demand collaboration in the presence of collaboration cost. We also evaluate whether different contracts (i.e., resolution contracts, revenue-sharing contracts, cost-sharing contracts, and transfer-payment contracts) induce truthful demand information equally effectively and efficiently.

Solving the IC and IR constraints for the above four contract forms, we find:

1. Resolution contract: $C ( u ) = ( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { \mathit { z } } ( k ) ] ( u - a \mu ) { \mathrm { i f ~ } } u \geq a \mu$

4. Transfer-payment contract: $C ( u ) - t ( u ) = ( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { \gamma } ( k ) ] ( u - a \mu )$ , even if $C ( u ) = 0$

Let us denote the retailer’s net cost of resolution as the amount of resolution cost after revenue sharing, cost-sharing, or transfer payment, if any, in the above contracts. Note, that in a revenue-sharing contract, the revenue shared from the retailer is equivalent to a net (resolution) cost to the retailer. In a cost-sharing contract, the retailer’s net cost of resolution is less than the original exogenous cost due to the compensation offered by the manufacturer. Then the above results can be summarized as follows:

Proposition 1: The collaboration mechanisms (M1a–M1d) are all implementable, given the net cost of resolution by the retailer of $( r + \pi - w ) [ l - F _ { z } ( k ) J ( u - a \mu )$

The key intuition is as follows: In order to induce the truthful demand update, each collaboration mechanism must eliminate the retailer’s potential gain from overclaiming, which is ∂/∂u $\begin{array} { r } { i \pi _ { R } ^ { o } \left( \hat { u } = u \big | u \right) = ( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { z } ( k ) ] > 0 } \end{array}$ as discussed above. The net resolution cost provides the counterbalance, as the marginal net resolution cost at the truth-telling level $\hat { u } = u \mathrm { ~ i s ~ } ( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { \scriptscriptstyle 7 } ( k ) ]$ , when resolution is involved. Incurring or imposing this net cost along with the promise of additional products within the exception range assures that overclaiming does not generate more profit for the retailer than truthfully sharing the demand information. Three important observations can be made from Proposition 1. First, all four exception-based incentive contracts specifically tailored due to the divergent nature and magnitude of the resolution cost are proven to be effective in regulating the retailer’s truth-telling behavior as long as the net resolution cost is $( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { z } ( k ) ] ( u - a \mu )$ . With everything else being equal, the higher the retailer’s unit lost sale cost or the lower the manufacturer’s unit lost sale cost, the higher the net cost of resolution will be. Note that the manufacturer’s unit lost sale cost (1) is actually embedded in the safety factor k through the relationship $F _ { \scriptscriptstyle 7 } ( k ) = 1 - ( C _ { \scriptscriptstyle p } + h ) / ( w + l + h )$ , thus affecting the net resolution cost negatively.

Second, the three incentive contracts (revenue sharing, cost sharing, and transferpayment contracts) are equivalent to the resolution contract in that the net cost of resolution for each is the same as the resolution cost under the resolution contract. The finding of equivalence of these contracts provides flexibility for manufacturers to manage demand collaboration in the presence of resolution cost. When the manufacturer has a direct control of the cost, the resolution cost can serve as an important tool to regulate the retailer’s truth-telling behavior; when the manufacturer does not have a direct control of the cost, he can utilize various supply contracts to reach the target of the net cost of resolution in order to induce truth-telling from the retailer.

Finally, even though all four exception-based incentive contracts are equivalent as shown above, for these contracts to be implementable, different constraints exist. The revenue-sharing contract is appropriate when the exogenous resolution cost is incurred on the manufacturer’s side, while for the cost-sharing contract to be implementable, the exogenous resolution cost incurred by the retailer must be higher than $( r + \pi - w ) [ 1 - F _ { z } ( k ) ] ( u - a \mu )$ , that is, the exogenous cost must be sufficiently high to be worth sharing. In contrast, the implementation of the transfer-payment contract does not rely on the value or the party responsible for the exogenous resolution cost. In particular, after solving for the optimal contracts, we have:

Proposition $2 ( a ) .$ : The revenue-sharing contract leads to the same optimal manufacturer profit and exception level as the transfer-payment contract, when the resolution cost is incurred by the manufacturer.

Proposition $2 ( b ) .$ : The cost-sharing contract leads to the same optimal manufacturer profit and exception level as the transfer-payment contract if and only if the resolution cost satisfies:

$$
C (u) \geq (r + \pi - w) (1 - F _ {z} (k)) (u - a \mu).
$$

Proposition 2 indicates that the revenue-sharing or cost-sharing contract could be equally effective depending on the responsible party and the magnitude of the resolution cost, while the transfer-payment contract is more flexible because it can be implemented regardless of the resolution cost. Our findings suggest that an exceptionbased revenue-sharing contract should be considered first when the resolution cost is incurred only by the manufacturer but not under his direct control, while a cost-sharing contract should be adopted when the resolution cost is incurred only by the retailer and is sufficiently large. The transfer-payment contract, in contrast, works under the settings of both low and high resolution cost. However, the direction of the transfer payment may change. When the resolution cost (by the retailer) is high, the manufacturer should make a payment to the retailer, which is similar to the cost-sharing contract. In contrast, when the cost is low, the retailer should make a transfer to the manufacturer, which is similar to the revenue-sharing contract in penalizing the retailer’s propensity to inflate the demand forecast.

Given the similarities between transfer-payment contracts and revenue-sharing and cost-sharing contracts, the focus of the remaining exception-based incentive contract design will be based on comparisons between the resolution contract, which endogenizes the exception resolution cost in the collaboration, and the transfer-payment contract. On the one hand, the above results indicate that the manufacturer needs to forgo a certain level of planning efficiency to implement the exception-based incentive mechanisms. The manufacturer essentially produces and makes available to the retailer a fixed quantity defined by a predetermined threshold level and thus incurs a cost associated with the additional production quantity $a \mu - u$ when the actual update falls under the threshold. To the retailer, this additional quantity available can be viewed as a “reward” for her to claim her update truthfully when the observed update is under the threshold. This reward effectively removes the retailer’s incentive to overclaim the forecast update when the true update falls under the threshold level, and therefore assures the implementation of the mechanism. To the manufacturer, the cost associated with this reward is equivalent to an information rent the manufacturer has to give up in exchange for the retailer’s truthful communication of the forecast update when $u \leq a \mu$ . On the other hand, when $u > a \mu$ , the information rent paid by the manufacturer to coordinate sharing of true demand information is equivalent to his resolution cost to resolve the exceptions. Here, the manufacturer’s actual resolution cost in the collaboration may take different forms, such as the exogenous cost defrayed by the retailer’s shared revenue (revenue-sharing contract), the portion of shared exogenous cost from the retailer (cost-sharing contract), or the transfer payment to the retailer. Our earlier findings indicate that the optimal $a ^ { * }$ represents the indifference point where the manufacturer’s marginal (production) overage cost (given the retailer’s claim within the exception range) is equivalent to the manufacturer’s marginal resolution cost (given the retailer’s claim outside the exception range). The manufacturer’s marginal overage cost is positively related to the exception range, while the manufacturer’s resolution cost takes two different forms: (1) a direct resolution cost incurred under the resolution contract and (2) a transfer payment incurred under the transfer-payment contract. When the marginal resolution cost increases, the manufacturer is compelled to increase the marginal overage cost accordingly, by applying a higher $a ^ { * } .$ . Naturally, a higher $a ^ { * }$ will lead to a lower profit for the manufacturer since both resolution cost and overage cost increase as the exception range increases.

For example, in markets where demand is more unpredictable, the exception level should be set at a higher level so that the supply chain would not incur a much higher level of costs to manage exception resolution. This intuitive and important finding provides insight to better answer the question: How should exceptions be defined under CPFR [52]?

Results 4(b) and 4(c) appear to be counterintuitive, as the same factors have divergent effects on the optimal exception levels of these contracts, depending on the nature of the exception resolution cost. However, these different patterns can be fully understood given that the role of the exception level in all these contracts is to provide the manufacturer the fundamental trade-off between his overproduction cost when the demand claim is below the exception level, and his resolution cost when the demand claim is above the exception level. It is important to note that under the transfer-payment (or the revenue- or cost-sharing) contract, the exogenous resolution cost is unrelated to unit production cost, retail unit price, or retailer’s unit lost sale value, but the net resolution cost paid by the retailer (the portion of revenue sharing, the collaboration cost after cost sharing, or transfer payment) is. Consequently, the manufacturer’s actual resolution cost (which is equal to the exogenous resolution cost minus the retailer’s net resolution cost) is inversely affected by unit production cost, retail unit price, or retailer’s unit lost sale value.

Specifically, as the unit production cost increases, the cost of overproduction increases, when the demand update claim is below the exception level. Furthermore, according to Proposition 1, the retailer’s net resolution cost increases (as $F _ { z } ( k )$ decreases due to an increase in unit production cost), leading to a decrease in the manufacturer’s resolution cost in the form of transfer payment or shared resolution cost or exogenous resolution cost minus the revenue shared from the retailer. These changes create an imbalance between the manufacturer’s overproduction cost and his resolution cost, resulting in a decrease in the optimal exception level. Similarly, as the retailer’s unit price or unit lost sale value increases, the retailer’s net resolution cost increases. For a given exogenous resolution cost, the manufacturer’s actual resolution cost (reflected as the transfer payment, the shared resolution cost, or exogenous resolution cost minus the revenue shared by the retailer) decreases, when the demand claim is above the exception level. The low resolution cost then helps to reduce the optimal exception level to retrieve the balance between the overproduction cost and the manufacturer’s resolution cost.

Under the resolution contract, the resolution cost designed to induce retailer’s truth telling when the demand claim is above the exception level is increasing in the unit production cost, retail unit price, or retailer’s unit lost sale value. More importantly, the increase of the resolution cost in these factors is more than the increase of the cost of overproduction (except for an extremely high unit production cost). Thus, the balance of costs points to a higher exception level under the resolution contract when these parameters are higher. As illustrated in Figure 5, the optimal exception range is increasing in production cost under the transfer-payment contract, but is decreasing under the resolution contract. These trends are consistent with Proposition 4(b).

## Conclusion

Sh aring business intelligence such as private d emand information is not a simple task, and thus becomes a critical issue in B2B collaborations. Thus, it is imperative that we make an effort to fully understand how to use existing and/or proposed collaboration mechanisms such as CDF to share such information truthfully. To that end, this research investigated exception-based incentive mechanisms in B2B settings that explicitly considered the cost associated with exception resolution and the retailer’s incentives in truthfully collaborating with the manufacturer in the presence of asymmetric demand information.

Specifically, this research proposed incentive mechanisms that involve optimizing forecasting threshold values (exception levels) in CDF to manage collaboration efficiency and effectiveness in extracting and sharing truthful demand information. This design has the potential to serve as a building block for rationalizing CPFR processes in B2B relationships, replacing the current ad hoc determination of exception values [52]. The simplicity of the incentive mechanisms proposed in the study will allow such B2B mechanisms to be readily incorporated into a supply chain collaborative technology, a notable advantage in contrast to most incentive mechanisms proposed in the literature, which while theoretically appealing, are very difficult to implement in the real world.

Four exception-based incentive contracts were proposed based on the nature and structure of the resolution costs—namely, the resolution, revenue-sharing, costsharing, and transfer-payment contracts. Our analysis showed that all four contracts are implementable in their respective scenario with regard to resolution and other costs. A further comparison of the proposed contracts indicates that, on the one hand, when the resolution cost is exogenous, the revenue-sharing or cost-sharing contract can lead to the same optimal profit and exception level as the transfer-payment contract. Specifically, if the resolution cost is incurred by only the manufacturer, then the revenue-sharing contract is more appropriate for the CDF process. If the resolution cost is incurred by only the retailer and is large enough, the cost-sharing contract is more appropriate; otherwise, a transfer-payment contract should be adopted. On the other hand, when the resolution cost is endogenized in a resolution contract to regulate the retailer’s behavior, it could reduce the optimal exception level while improving the profitability of the manufacturer if the marginal cost of endogenized resolution can be consistently lower than the marginal cost of exogenous resolution. Finally, we assessed the relationships between the optimal exception level, demand pattern, and cost structures of the supply chain that critically impact the balance between costs from overproduction and from exception resolution. In particular, we found that the optimal exception level increases with the extent of demand variation after forecast update, as expected, under all contract forms. But counterintuitively, the variation in the optimal exception level is not uniform under different contract forms with respect to the changes in retail unit price, unit production cost, and retailer unit lost sales. Specifically, the optimal exception level increases in unit production cost, retail unit price, and retailer unit lost sales under the resolution contract, but it decreases in the same factors under the other contracts.

These findings can have significant implications for managers involved in implementing and managing CDF solutions. First, the general equivalency of the four mechanisms in extracting truthful private information offers the manufacturer significant flexibility in coordinating CDF. For example, when the resolution cost is not under the manufacturer’s direct control and is incurred by only one supply chain party, the manufacturer can always adopt an appropriate supply contract to control CDF. It is also helpful to realize that a cost-sharing contract can be as effective and efficient as a transfer-payment contract. Therefore, instead of being concerned with how much the retailer should contribute to the total collaboration cost, the manufacturer can structure a relatively simple contract with a lump-sum payment.

Second, the dissimilarity among the four mechanisms regarding their relationships with the nature and magnitude of the resolution cost can help manufacturers choose the best-suitable mechanism under different scenarios. For instance, our results suggest that revenue-sharing contracts should be used when the exogenous resolution cost is incurred by the manufacturer, while cost-sharing contracts should be adopted when such cost is high and incurred by the retailer. When the resolution cost is contractible, the resolution contract can deliver a more efficient CDF solution with properties different from those of other contracts.

Finally, this research provides insight and justification for how the optimal exception level might change within a B2B context. When the planning horizon is moved out to a longer range, leading to higher forecast errors and demand variations, the exception level should be increased accordingly—something the CPFR framework suggests as a rule-of-thumb [32]. However, the firms should also make adjustments to the exceptions based on their retail prices, production costs, customer values, and most importantly, their choices of contract forms. In other words, setting an exception under demand collaboration is indeed a comprehensive process that reflects all aspects of supply chain operations and relationships. By incorporating these factors into a strategic decision on exception levels, firms can achieve a higher quality of demand collaboration with enhanced information integrity and value, which eventually leads to higher profitability.

Several aspects of the models presented in this paper merit further examination. First, our focus in the paper is on the mechanisms that use rewards to induce retailer’s truthtelling. But there are times that a manufacturer may choose to impose certain penalties on the retailer to address incentive issues rather than reward the retailer, especially when the manufacturer is the dominant supply chain partner. As Terwiesch et al. [58] show, it is not uncommon that the supplier will choose to punish the buyer by reducing their supply. Hence, one direction of future research could be to investigate whether a penalty-type incentive mechanism could achieve the same goal as accomplished by a reward-type mechanism.

Another natural extension of this study would be to investigate the same problem addressed in this paper in a competitive environment [17, 24, 65]. In the current setting (where only one manufacturer and one retailer exist), although the manufacturer can obtain the true demand information by implementing exception-based incentive mechanisms, the updated demand information is not fully utilized. By adding one more retailer into the setting, the manufacturer has more potential usage of the true updated demand information that increases the value of implementing incentive mechanisms. In addition, the manufacturer can also be allowed to have private information in the future study, in contrast to the current setting where only the retailer has private information. Allowing the manufacturer to have supply side asymmetric information balances the value of information possessed by both parties, and makes the outcome less predictable.

A third extension of this study would be to consider a scenario in which the retailer orders before the demand is realized.<sup>10</sup> Such a scenario can be expected to yield qualitative results similar to our current model based on our current evaluations, though it would substantially increase the modeling complexities and interpretation difficulties. Finally, future research should also extend the one-period problem to a multiperiod problem using a dynamic game-theoretic approach. It would be interesting to see how the exception level of CDF changes as time evolves and how both parties react in each time period. Such a model could provide useful guidance for short-, medium-, and long-term forecasting. It would also serve as a means to control for retailer’s interest in sharing truthful demand information over time since, in the long term, the retailer might have less interest in forecast manipulation.<sup>11</sup>
