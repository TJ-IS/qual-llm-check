# Is more always better? Investigating the task-technology fit theory in an online user context

Muhammad Aljukhadar <sup>a,1</sup>, Sylvain Senecal <sup>b,</sup>\*, Jacques Nantel <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah 21589, Saudi Arabia

<sup>b</sup> HEC Montreal, 3000 Ch. Cote Ste-Catherine, Montreal, Quebec, Canada H3T 2A7

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 9 December 2009 Received in revised form 9 October 2011 Accepted 25 October 2013 Available online 15 March 2014

Keywords: Task-Technology Fit Website characteristics Task completion Performance Intention User characteristics

## A B S T R A C T

We used Task-Technology Fit (TTF) theory to examine the drivers and consequences of successful task completion by a user in an online context. The theory suggests that the fit between characteristics of the task and those of the website predicts user performance and behavioral intentions. Our hypotheses were tested using the input of two large scale studies performed in twelve industries and involving 13,135 participants. Results, which were replicated in a proximate culture, lend support to the predictions of Task-Technology Fit theory. The site information quality and ease of use were the only technology factors that significantly drove the users to a successful completion of their information tasks, rather than the site’s graphical attractiveness, interactivity, security and privacy factors. The findings further suggested that focusing on the enhancement of site characteristics that have low fit with the task is not effective as it resulted in slowing the successful completion of the online task.

## 1. Introduction

Recent reports suggest that there are low conversion rates of commercial websites (below 4% in 2012) and large cart abandonment rates (60% in 2012).<sup>2</sup> Academic research has also noted the low rates of successful completion of online tasks (e.g., below 60% for shopping tasks [8]). It is therefore necessary to investigate the factors that affect users’ achievement of their online tasks. By identifying the IT characteristics that drive the success of online tasks, this research could contribute to efficient website design and its management.

Task-Technology Fit theory (TTF) [6] has been applied mainly at the organizational rather than the end user level [7]. Researchers however indicate the potential implications of TTF at the individual level. Recent work on the behavior of online users suggests that the site characteristics or the site quality dimensions positively influence the user task completion and behavioral intentions [10]. An unanswered question nonetheless is whether enhancing the IT characteristics has comparable and positive effect on performance; in other words, is more always better?

According to TTF, some technology characteristics exert positive effects on performance, subject to the task at hand and environmental factors. Thus, only a subset of the site characteristics may significantly improve outcomes.

We therefore decided to study the application of TTF to an individual user context in order to pinpoint the technology factors that facilitate users’ successful completion of online tasks and to show the relative importance of these factors in predicting task completion and then compare these results with actual user characteristics as captured in a survey. Our second objective was to investigate the effects of task completion and website characteristics on the user behavioral intentions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. The user online task

Tasks are user actions causing system outputs. Subordinate (concrete and specific) tasks closely affect superordinate (abstract and value-laden) tasks and jointly affect user decision making and outcome [1]. The significance of studying subordinate tasks results from the fact that users focus more on the task at hand (involving proximal and concrete goals) in order to solve more abstract tasks. For instance, users have been seen to abandon their shopping carts because of poor site navigation, hard-to-find shopping carts, and time consuming checkout [4]. Online check-out (a superordinate task) therefore appears to be affected by a set of subordinate tasks which affect the system’s ability to accomplish the overall task, resulting in aborting the purchase [3]. Whereas tasks vary in scope and difficulty, the focus of our study is thus on information, rather than transactional tasks.

## 2.2. Extending TTF to an online user context

An important implication of TTF is that it does not only predict current performance and utilization intensity but it also helps in predicting future utilization. Yet researchers rarely investigate TTF implications in an online user context.

Whereas the characteristics of IT were postulated as having positive consequences, TTF predicts that only a subset of them exert a major positive effect on performance and that a favorable outcome is anticipated when a fit between the task and the technology is achieved. Research has investigated TTF in various contexts, mostly using perceived performance as the dependent variable [2], to the best of our knowledge no research has yet been conducted in an online user context that employed an objective performance measure.

## 2.3. The website characteristics

The TTF dimensions were originally grouped in three categories: the first focusing on the technology characteristics (for using data in decision making); the second on meeting day-to-day operational needs; and the last on responding to changed business needs. In our paper, we studied technology characteristics as they pertained to the site quality (see Appendix A for construct scales) (Table 1).

## 3. Research hypotheses

Both task and technology characteristics affect performance of users of an on-line systems task. User beliefs about the technology, such as ease of use, are strongly related to TTF [9]. Performance is affected by the site capacity to deliver the desired information with the least amount of errors and frustration. Among various characteristics, perceived risk and ease of use have been found to be salientindeterminingtheintenttocompleteatransactionaltask[12]. It is therefore expected that for utilitarian tasks, site characteristics that fit the task (such as information quality and ease of use) will be important in determining the task success. Therefore:

H1. The website perceived ease of use and information quality will positively influence the successful completion of IT tasks.

Differences among the users such as their expertise also affect online behavior. But whereas individual differences affect performance, the site characteristics should affect it more strongly. In effect, research has shown that user differences help by predicting a modest change in performance [13]. Therefore, the individual differences captured by site experience, e-commerce experience, and demographics should significantly affect performance. It is thus expected that the explanatory power of the latter factors will fall short of those due to the site characteristics.

H2. The website characteristics will explain more variance in the successful completion of IT tasks than the user characteristics captured by experience and demographics.

An important prediction is that TTF does not only help predicting performance but also predicts current and future utilization. The role of task completion in determining use intentions entails an important question: Does successful task completion shape future utilization? That is, while site characteristics may have an impact on the intentions to revisit and recommend the site to others, favorable outcomes (i.e., the successful completion of the online task) should also lead to positive intentions. Therefore:

H3. The website characteristics that fit the task will positively influence behavioral intentions. Furthermore, the successful completion of the online task will predict behavioral intentions.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research studies

Data was provided from two large scale studies performed in collaboration with a leading Canadian market research company, whose online panel (a self-selected sample of consumers who optedintoparticipateincommercialstudies)iscomposedofabout350,000 consumers, constituted the sampling frame. Various industries and multiple websites in each industry were used to help controlling for the effects of industry (product category), firm, and site-related factors (e.g., site accessibility, loyalty, and awareness) (see Table 2). Random sample selection and data collection were performed separately (approximately two months per industry).

The two studies differed because the first solicited participants using an English-speaking consumer panel, while the second solicited participants using u the French-speaking consumers. Table 2 shows the sample size per industry and the websites covered in each industry.

## 4.2. Tasks and samples

The panel email list was used to send the randomly selected consumers an invitation to participate. Panel members (who had a monthly chance to win monetary and nonmonetary prizes, such as mp3 players) increased their chance by participating in the study. Response rates were close to those of previous projects performed by the market research company (20%). Upon reception of an invitation, panel members could choose to participate by clicking on the provided link, which opened a webpage that contained an explanation of the task to be performed and a link to the randomly assigned website.

Tasks were identical within each industry and consisted of finding a piece of information on the randomly assigned website (see Table 2 for the tasks). Hence, in all studies consumers were asked to perform an informational rather than a transactional task. After performing the task, participants returned to the same webpage to enter their response to the task and start the questionnaire. The final sample consisted of 7253 responses for the English study and 5882 responses for the French study. The time elapsed between opening the initial webpage and moving to next step (task completion time) was recorded (time was not available for the auto manufacturer industry due to a technical difficulty). ## 4.3. Measures

The rates of task successful completion (i.e., entering the correct answer to the question concerning the assigned task) were 64.2% and 59.2% for the English and French studies, respectively. Time mean difference for participants providing successful versus unsuccessful answers was insignificant (3.40 vs. 3.25 min; F = 1.57, NS) for the English study and negligible (3.75 vs. 3.37 min; F = 7.57, p = .006) for the French study. Although this result implies that participants had expended comparable effort to achieve the task and that time was not a major factor in determining task success, we controlled in a stringent test for time in subsequent analysis to isolate any effect for time on task success.

The first set of items in the questionnaire reflected the latent variables of site characteristics and behavioral intentions. Scales were identical or similar to those in published papers [5,14,15] (see Appendix A for internal consistency, items, their means and standard deviation). Factor analysis tests were performed to show validity of the constructs (see Section 5 and Appendices B and C for the items loading matrix).

The rest of the items pertained mainly to site experience (‘‘Was it the first time visiting this website?’’) and e-commerce experience (‘‘Have you ever purchased goods on the Internet or conducted an electronic commerce activity?’’). The two facets of experience had low correlation $( r = 0 . 1 0 , p = . 0 0 0 ; r = 0 . 0 9 , p = . 0 0 0 ,$ respectively for the English and French studies), which indicated that the two measures were reflecting different dimensions of user experience. The last set of items pertained to demographics. Matching the obtained demographics with those of the census allowed us to conclude that the samples were roughly representative of an adult consumer population for each study.

Because tasks varied for multiple attributes (difficulty, ambiguity, cognitive load, and valence), there was a need to control for the different tasks assigned. This was performed by including the task (i.e., industry) in the equation as a dummy variable.

Discriminant and convergent validity of the latent variables were verified. Items should load high on the intended factor and low (<0.40) on the other factors; this criterion was met by all items except one (Q14, which loaded high on unintended factors in both studies). This item was dropped (the resulting items loading matrix being reported in Appendix B for the English study and in Appendix C for the French study).<sup>3</sup> The mean score for each of the resulting constructs was used in the subsequent analysis.

To test Hypotheses 1 and 2, logistical regression was performed in five steps: The first accounted for the effects of the different tasks, the second for the user facets of experience (site experience and e-commerce experience), the third for demographics, the fourth for task accomplishment time, and the fifth for the site characteristics (see Appendix D). This was done to show the significant effects of the site characteristics controlling for other variables and to underscore the explanatory power of technology related factors compared to others (H2). The results of this test (summarized in Table 4 and Appendix D) lent support to H1 and H2. As illustrated in Appendix D, the results from both the English and the French study indicated that the site ease of use and information quality were the only two factors with significant positive effect on task success (H1). The site characteristics explained substantially more variance in task success than user experience and demographics (H2) (see Table 4 for changes in $R ^ { 2 } )$

Linear regression was performed to test Hypothesis 3 with website characteristics and task success as the independent variables and behavioral intentions as the dependent variable. Similar to the previous test, we controlled for the different tasks and user experience by including these factors as covariates in the model (see Appendix E for ANCOVA table for each the English and French study). As the latter tables show, H3 was partially supported because, while it predicted that only the site ease of use and information quality should have significant effects on behavioral intentions, the results indicated that other site characteristics were significant in predicting intentions (see Appendix E for regression coefficients). In addition, the mean difference of intentions (for the group that failed vs. succeeded in the task) was small and in the unexpected direction for the English study (4.43 vs. 4.28, respectively) and was insignificant (p = 0.10) for the French study.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. General discussion

The Task-Technology Fit theory appears to encompass important applications for the field of IT design and user performance. The confirmation of Hypotheses 1 and 2 shows that only a subset of the technology characteristics exerts major impact on user performance as reflected by the successful completion of the online task. The site ease of use and the site information quality were the only factors to positively and significantly predict the successful completion of the tasks. Contrary to prior research [14,15], our results suggest that improving all the dimensions of site quality would not positively influence performance. Thus, improving site characteristics indiscriminately might not only be a waste of resources but might also hinder user task accomplishment. Our findings showed that the site perceived security and privacy exerted no impact on the successful completion of informational tasks. However, privacy and security factors are important in transactional tasks [12].

The results of Hypothesis 3 suggest that TTF might not be adequate in explaining variance in behavioral intentions when an objective performance measure is employed. It should be noted that study participants were not informed whether they were successful in performing the task and thus their intentions were not affected by the results.

## 6.2. Implications

Our work provides novel implications to research in the IS and marketing domains. First, the TTF theory provides important implications for online user research. Controlling for product category and experience, the fit between characteristics of the task and the technology appears to be key to user performance. Nonetheless, the link between task-technology fit and utilization intentions demands further research, primarily because the results show positive and significant relations between the examined site characteristics and behavioral intentions.

While the role of culture appears to be modest as the majority of findings were invariant across the English- and French-speaking users, some cultural effects were noted. For instance, the effect of site interactivity on task success was negatively significant only in the French culture.

The results bear important implications. As companies strive to deliver usable websites and IT applications, practitioners are asked to discriminate between the technology characteristics and invest more in those that offer adequate fit with the intended tasks. As technology characteristics are controllable, practitioners can benefit by controlling cost while improving outcome. Our results suggest that the investment in improving the site ease of use and information quality should favorably impact outcome when the intended tasks are utilitarian and informational. However, high levels of site interactivity and esthetics do not appear to improve the task success rate. Different facets of user experience were found to have significant effects on online task completion. Finally, applications should be tailored to provide similar levels of performance for users with divergent levels of experience.

## 6.3. Limitations

Both studies in this research considered various industries and multiple websites in each industry to reduce the influence of firm loyalty, brand strength, and site-specific variables. Participants were adult users randomly selected from a large consumer panel, and the final samples were deemed representative. While these factors allowed for generalizing results, they increased the sample heterogeneity which might underestimate the magnitude of some relations (e.g., the low estimations of user experience and demographics shown in Appendix D).

Our research controlled for and showed a significant effect for the different tasks assigned to participants. Because the assigned task was the same within each industry, its effect cannot be isolated (whether the effect resulted from the task demand, industry sector, or both). In addition, the studies did not account for computer anxiety, innovativeness, and self-efficacy and for the user psychological state while performing the task (e.g., apathy, boredom, or cognitive absorption).

## 7. Conclusion

The results from two large scale studies involving over 13,000 consumers and encompassing twelve different industries lend support to our main hypotheses. While both the website and user characteristics influence the successful completion of informational tasks, the role of the website characteristics was greater. However, not all website characteristics positively or significantly influence task completion. The optimization of user task completion requires a focused approach in which the fit between characteristics of the task, the technology, and the user is taken into account.
