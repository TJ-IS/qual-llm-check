# Customized Bundling and Consumption Variety of Digital Information Goods

Jes C. Boc ks ted t and Kim Hua t Goh

He received his Ph.D. in business administration with a specialization in information systems from the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. Ab strac t: Customized bundling retail strategies have become increasingly popular online. In customized bundling, consumers decide the bundle’s components, and the effects of this change on consumption variety have important implications for information goods retailers. Although reduction in transaction and search costs increases supply-side product variety, customized bundling can introduce new types of friction in the consumption process. We show that customization of information good bundles reduces consumption variety through two effects: design cost effects and compromise effects. We present the results of three behavioral experiments and an empirical study using sales data from a national music retailer. This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of the effects of customized bundling on search costs and demand-side dynamics. The results provide insights for information goods retailers on the effects of design and search costs on consumer purchasing behavior. Implications for the design of retail platforms for customizable information goods are discussed.

Key words and ph rases: behavioral economics, bundling, content bundling, customization, e-commerce, information goods, mass customization.

Dig ital distrib ution of information g oods has brought about various supply-side changes in market practices whereby producers can leverage lower production, transaction, and distribution costs. The consumer now has access to large product catalogs of information goods, made possible through digitization and online distribution. At the same time, new product sales strategies have emerged that are focused around customization of information goods and customized bundling. For example, the publisher John Wiley & Sons allows teachers to create customized books that combine content from multiple sources (see http://customselect.wiley.com). A growing trend in information goods retailing is to allow consumers to reconfigure and customize products to better serve their own tastes [1].

The variety of available digital information goods for consumers has increased, largely because of lower inventory costs and the absence of logistical constraints typical in brick-and-mortar stores. Accompanying the larger product offerings, there is evidence of increased sales of niche products, and thus an increase in the variety of goods consumed—a phenomenon popularly known as the “long-tail” effect [2, 9, 21]. However, other research has shown that information technologies (IT) such as recommendation systems and social media can significantly impact sales diversity and, in many cases, drive increasing sales of hit products (e.g., [15, 16]), a phenomenon often referred to as the “superstar” effect [10]. The debate on the effects of IT on consumption variety is considered an important area of continuing research in the economics of electronic commerce [10].

The demand-side impact of customization on consumer behavior and consumption variety is an important and open question in this research stream. In particular, sales strategies that offer customized bundling of information goods provide consumers with more purchasing options than traditional bundling, and it is not obvious how consumption variety will be affected. No longer constrained by traditional bundles or product offerings, consumers may focus their attention on creating bundles of only popular information goods (e.g., a playlist of only hit songs, a news feed of only popular articles, or a textbook of only highly rated chapters), which would lead to a decrease in consumption variety. By contrast, consumers may create bundles of niche products that best fit their idiosyncratic tastes, which would lead to an increase in consumption variety of information goods. Hence, a combination of these factors may be at play. Research on supply-side changes to markets due to IT investment has provided strong evidence for the long-tail effect. What this paper seeks to explore is the additional demand-side effects of IT through product customization. Specifically, we study how customization of information good bundles affects consumer behavior and consumption variety, which are important for retailers since they can shape pricing, inventory, and distribution strategies [10].

The objective of this research is to address the following research question:

RQ: How does customization of information good bundles impact consumption variety?

We propose that information goods customization in retail settings introduces new types of friction in the consumption process. Although reduction in transaction, search, and distribution costs can increase supply-side product variety and thus consumption variety of information goods [2, 10, 15], we argue that a reduction in consumption variety can result from consumer behavior effects associated with customized bundling. We offer two theoretical explanations for this phenomenon based on behavioral economics and decision theory: design costs and compromise effects. Design costs refer to the increased cognitive effort and time required by customers to create customized bundles. As we will show, the well-known availability heuristic can explain how increasing design costs leads to decreased consumption variety. Compromise effects theory [25] explains the impact of the diversity of the choice set on consumer decision outcomes. The inclusion of extreme or risky options in the choice set can drive consumers to reverse preferences and thus compromise for moderate options.

We present the results of three controlled behavioral experiments that examine design costs and compromise effects in customized bundling of information goods. We also present the results of an empirical study that explores these effects using a year of music sales data. We find strong evidence that customized bundling of information goods resulted in a decrease in consumption variety. The findings extend the existing literature on IT and sales diversity, demonstrate the effects of customized bundling on consumer behavior, and provide new insights into behavioral economic issues associated with information goods in e-commerce retail settings.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we review the relevant literature and develop our hypotheses on the relationship between design costs, compromise effects, and consumption variety. Next, we present the methodology and results of our behavioral experiments. Then, we present our empirical study and the associated econometric analysis. We discuss the practical and theoretical implications of our findings and finish with concluding remarks.

## Theoretical Foundations and Hypotheses

In 2005, New York Times c olumnist Daniel Akst identified an evolving trend of retailers using new strategies to sell information goods in nontraditional bundles [1]. Now a common practice for many retailers, customized bundling allows consumers to disaggregate traditional information goods to create their own customized set of information goods. Music services such as Spotify (www.spotify.com) and eMusic (www.emusic.com), allow consumers to create customizable bundles of music by compiling individual songs from different albums. Publishers such as John Wiley & Sons, Harvard Business School Press, and McGraw-Hill give customers the ability to create their own custom books by combining chapters, articles, and cases from multiple sources. Smartphone applications such as Pulse (www.pulse.me) and Flipboard (https://flipboard.com) allow users to create customized bundles of newsfeeds.

It is not obvious how sales diversity and consumption variety are affected when consumers are allowed to purchase customized bundles. Niche-type information goods may become more difficult to sell, since there is no longer a forced subsidy from the hits as is common in standard information good product bundles (e.g., two or three hit singles supporting sales of a full music album). Alternatively, customization allows consumers to bundle together more niche products if they so choose, which could lead to an increase in consumption variety. Therefore, research on the customization in information good bundles will provide additional insights toward understanding the effects of IT on consumption variety—an important research topic in information systems (IS) [10].

## Design Costs

Observations of increased consumption variety in online retail environments is largely associated with the reduction in search and inventory costs due to advances in IT  [9, 18]. In the case of customized bundling, a new type of search cost can emerge. Customized bundling requires the disaggregation of traditional information good bundles, which leads to a significant increase in the number of searchable product options (e.g., a single music album becomes 10–15 individual songs). Moreover, consumers potentially incur new costs associated with “designing” a custom bundle. The economics literature defines two types of search costs: external and internal [33], where external search costs are associated with acquiring information and internal search costs are associated with processing acquired information. Internal, or cognitive search costs, are similar to cognitive effort [20, 34] and closely related to the concept of bounded rationality [31] in decision making. Consumers’ external search costs for discovering product information have been reduced through the use of IT; however, additional cognitive resources are required to process the search results and select items for purchase in the creation of a custom bundle. Therefore, additional internal cognitive search costs associated with creating a customized bundle can be a potential new source of effort for the consumer when making a purchase. We name these costs the design costs of customization and acknowledge that when consumers create customized bundles it is possible that the total search costs increase.

Processing of product information depends on both the quantity of information as well as the complexity of the format in which it is presented [28]. Information load is conceived as the amount of data processed per unit of time. When faced with an increase in load, a decision maker may approach the task through one of the following three strategies [27, 42]: (1) increase information processing effort (e.g., more effort through more sampling or deliberation), (2) restructure the task to restrict attention to certain portions of the incoming information, or (3) use decision aids. In the context of designing a customized bundle, it is likely that consumers are faced with an “overload” of information due to the increase in product variety and an additional cognitive burden associated with designing the bundle. Without decision aids, consumers should be expected to simplify the decision task to compensate for additional effort required in customizing the bundles (e.g., by restricting their attention to selected portions of the incoming information).

In behavioral economics, it is well established that selective information processing affects judgments, and decision makers tend to rely on the availability heuristic [36]: Familiar information is easier to process, therefore it tends to receive greater acceptance. Conversely, novel information is more difficult to process as it requires effortful inconsistency resolution [26]. In the creation of a custom bundle, consumers presented with an overload of information and the cognitive burden of designing the bundle may restrict their attention and focus predominantly on information with which they are familiar (e.g., well-known options). Normative economic theory assumes that rational agents consider all relevant options within a choice set and are able to establish unbiased preference [3]. The theory of the availability heuristic, however, argues that individuals tend to overestimate the value (or significance) of options that are more cognitively salient; therefore, preference can be biased due to cognitive limitations experienced by rational agents. In the context of customized bundling, we expect this to lead to greater focus on more well-known items. We hypothesize that customized bundling leads to increased internal search costs associated with designing the bundle—design costs.

Hypothesis 1a (Design Cost Hypothesis): The introduction of customizable bundling of information goods leads to an increase in design costs as compared to fixed bundling.

Furthermore, we hypothesize that increased design costs activate the availability heuristic, resulting in an increased concentration of sales of popular (or “hit”) products.

Hypothesis 1b (Design Cost Effect Hypothesis): Consumers faced with an increase in design costs will tend to purchase more hit products on average.

## Compromise Effects

The theory of rational choice prescribes that the ordered preference between choices should not change in the presence of other options. For example, if option A is strictly preferred over option B, the introduction of a third option C should not cause B to be preferred over A; this is known as the principle of independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) [3]. Although the principle of IIA applies to choices of a single person, its theoretical underpinnings are intuitive and can be extended to an aggregated pool of heterogeneous individuals. One key extension of this principle is the regularity principle, which states the “market share” of any option cannot increase by adding more options to the choice set [37]. Using the same example in which option A is preferred over option B, but in this case by the majority of a population, the probability of (or proportion of individuals) selecting A from a set solely containing only these two options (A and B) has to be greater or equal to the probability of (or proportion of individuals) choosing A from a larger set containing options A, B, and C. Although these principles of rational choice appeal to logic and establish the foundation for predicting human choice outcomes analytically, numerous behavioral economic studies (for a review, see [25]) have suggested that the decision choice process is more complex and less deterministic than prescribed by these principles. We argue that choices of bundle components can be influenced by irrelevant alternatives, and choice outcomes can deviate from rational choice predictions in customized bundling.

Products are typically evaluated based on multiple criteria. For example, songs may be evaluated based on several criteria, including the artist, genre, melody, or lyrics, among others. The evaluation and selection of products for purchase is essentially a multi-attribute choice problem [40], whereby rational consumers have to select an option that has the maximum aggregated value on all pertinent attributes. To illustrate, consider the prior example whereby a person is required to select between options A and B from a set with only these two options. Further, assume the options are evaluated based on two equally important attributes 1 and 2 (see Figure 1). If both attributes are weighted equally, option A will be preferred over B (aggregated value is represented by the Euclidean distance from the origin [38]). Under rational choice theory, when option C is added to the choice set it should not affect the relative attractiveness of A compared to B. However, Tversky and Simonson [38] suggested otherwise and empirically demonstrated that the third option creates an extreme aversion bias, whereby individuals will prefer option B over both A and C. The presence of a third option creates the impression that both options A and C are “extreme” choices, which fare very well in one attribute but score poorly in the other attribute. The contrast generated between A and C makes option B appear to be the safer, moderate, and lower risk choice. This phenomenon was named “compromise effects” by Simonson [32] and was subsequently shown by Tversky and Simonson [38] to be a form of extremeness aversion. Simply put, the compromise effect is a manifestation of risk aversion behavior where individuals avoid extreme choices that have high variance in scores among their attributes (i.e., score high in a single attribute but low in others). As a result, individuals prefer moderate choices that score generally well in most, if not all, attributes. This effect is amplified when individuals must choose among a large number of options with high variation in attributes.

In traditional information good bundles, consumers have been required to purchase the entire bundle even though their valuations of the constituent items are likely varied. Sellers employ bundling strategies to deal with consumers’ heterogeneous valuations of multiple items [29], and in many cases niche and lesser-valued products are subsidized by more popular or higher-value products in the bundle.

The number of product options available for consideration by the consumer increases dramatically when information good sellers adopt a customized bundling strategy. For example in digital music retailing, it is possible for sellers to allow customized bundling and provide consumers with choices from the full range of songs that were previously prebundled into albums. In this context, consumers are required to compare among the mainstream and niche songs when making their purchase decisions. By definition, niche goods are less known to the general consumer population and appeal to a smaller group of consumers. Some consumers value niche information goods more since the content matches their particular unique interests and has attractive idiosyncratic attributes that may not appeal to the general masses [15] (e.g., alternative rock music). Similarly, niche content often excels in a few unique attributes instead of across a broad range of attributes, and requires longer time to be recognized by the general consumer population compared to mainstream, mass market content [5]. In customized bundling, a larger number of product options can create increased uncertainty and possible behavioral changes in preferences due to the increased complexity of purchase (e.g., [23]).

In a market with heterogeneous buyers, including more niche goods in the choice set increases the instances of extreme options for the consumer to consider. In such purchase environments, behavioral decision-making theorists (e.g., [14, 32]) predict that the average<sup>1</sup> consumer will choose options that are associated with a lower likelihood of error and postpurchase regret (i.e., compromise effect). Although this does not suggest that all consumers will shun niche goods, we predict that there will be compromise effects resulting in measurable average increases in the selection of nonniche products. The arguments of compromise effects are also in line with prospect theory [24], which predicts that a safe, moderate option, such as a well-known hit song, will typically become the decision maker’s initial reference point and switching to an extreme alternative, such as a lesser-known niche song, may be unlikely.

We argue that in customized bundling, the consumer is put into an explicit comparison between niche and hit items. Therefore, customized bundling will increase compromise effects, which may result in systematic bias toward hits for the average consumer. With larger product catalogs, the typical consumer is presented with a more complex decision and increased uncertainty, which is an environment where compromise effects tend to operate most predominantly [32]. Hit products represent a familiar and lower-risk choice for the consumer in these scenarios; therefore, we expect to observe a higher focus of sales on hit products when consumers create customized bundles.

Hypothesis 2 (Compromise Effect Hypothesis): Customized bundling increases compromise effects, which will increase consumers’ tendency to purchase more hit products on average.

Design costs and compromise effects, if operative, both lead to similar outcomes in terms of consumption variety. Specifically, design costs should drive consumers to hit products because of increased internal search costs and cognitive effort in creating a customized bundle, and compromise effects should drive consumers to hit products because of reference comparisons and uncertainty about niche products in a more complex purchase environment. These two phenomena potentially introduce new forms of friction in the purchase process for customized bundles of information goods. If this is the case, we should be able to empirically observe a decrease in sales diversity (i.e., a higher concentration of sales on hit products) with customized bundling.

## Overview of Methodology

We c onduc ted th ree c ontrolled b eh avioral experiments and an empirical study of secondary data to test our hypotheses. The controlled experiments create an effective setup to objectively measure the impact of design costs and compromise effects on consumption variety, which otherwise would be difficult to measure under regular real-world purchase settings. Given that both design costs and compromise effects hypotheses predict similar outcomes, there is a need to have separate experiments to isolate each effect. Results from the controlled experiments provide support under the ideal theoretical purchase setting, albeit with reduced realism. To substantiate the experimental evidence, we conducted an additional empirical study using sales data from a music retailer. The empirical study is designed to test for the impact of customized bundling on consumption variety under real-world purchase conditions, and provide initial evidence of the existence of design costs and compromise effects in a practical setting.

The controlled experiments were developed to test the design cost existence (H1a), design cost effect (H1b), and compromise effect (H2) hypotheses, respectively. We note that design costs have not been explicitly studied in prior research and thus a separate experiment was needed to demonstrate their existence. The experiments were conducted using an online tool with participants residing in the United States who were recruited using an online invitation. Participants were paid for their time based on a \$15 hourly rate and were given a chance to win music downloads at the end of the experiment. There was no overlap in participants across the three experimental studies. We chose to use digital music as the focal information good in our experiments because it is familiar to most consumers [7] and has wide variety in terms of product characteristics and consumer tastes, which makes testing of consumption variety patterns meaningful. Furthermore, there are well-established industry measures for determining popularity of songs.

## Experimental Study 1—Existence of Design Costs in Customized Bundling

In th e first experiment (testing H1a), we designed two treatments (fixed bundles versus customized bundles) to test if the presence of customized bundling results in higher design costs. One hundred participants were randomly assigned to each treatment group (50 in each treatment); however, six were dropped because of incomplete information, resulting in 45 participants in the “fixed” treatment and 49 in the “customized” treatment. In the “fixed” treatment, participants were presented with the task of selecting an album of their liking (which they stood a chance of winning) from a list of 20 albums. Each album contained 10 songs that were randomly selected from a pool of 200 songs. The 200 songs in the pool were randomly selected from the top 400 songs ranked by iTunes in the first week of December 2011. In the “customized” treatment, participants were presented with the task of creating a customized bundle of 10 songs to their liking (which they stood a chance of winning) from a master list of the same 200 songs used in the “fixed” treatment. For each of the 200 songs listed in both treatments, participants were able to listen to a 30-second sample during the task.

Both treatments required cognitive effort from the participant to evaluate songs for potential purchase, but only the second treatment presented a design task. The albums in the “fixed” treatment were randomly generated so that participants could not rely on inferred quality signals based on a single artist (e.g., an album by U2) or theme (e.g., 1990s hits), which are often found in standard albums.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, participants needed to evaluate individual songs to determine the value of each randomly generated album. In the “customized” treatment, song evaluations were necessary as in the first treatment, but the structure of preexisting bundles was removed, thus requiring participants to engage in a design process to create the custom album.

We measured both perceived cognitive effort as well as actual effort to quantify the design costs in each treatment. We measured perceived cognitive effort using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree.” Two items were used: “It took a lot of effort to [choose the album/create the playlist]” and “Mentally, it was difficult to [choose the album/create the playlist]” (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.75). For actual effort, we measured the total amount of time (in seconds) required by the participants to either choose a fixed album or create a custom bundle. Prior literature has suggested that time is a good proxy for cognitive effort in a decision task [6, 22].

To test for differences in design costs across treatments, we performed two analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests with perceived and actual design costs as dependent variables in separate analyses. We included the demographic variables gender, income level, educational level, and age as covariates in the analyses.<sup>3</sup> Our results suggest that the perceived design costs are significantly higher for the customized bundle treatment compared to the fixed bundle treatment (F = 7.454, p < 0.01). Similarly, the actual design costs (measured by time) are also higher for the customized treatment compared to the fixed treatment (F = 5.24, p < 0.05). The marginal means of each treatment for both analyzes are reported in Table 1.

## Experimental Study 2—Effects of Design Costs on Consumption Variety

In th e sec ond experiment (testing H1b), we conducted a 2 × 2 factorial experiment with level of imposed design costs and external search costs as the two main factors. Eighty-four participants were randomly assigned to treatment groups; however, seven were dropped because of incomplete information, resulting in 77 usable participant responses (see Table 2). Participants were presented with the task of creating a playlist of songs to their liking (which they stood a chance of winning) from the same master list of 200 songs used in Experiment 1. As in Experiment 1, participants were able to listen to a 30-second sample during the task. To operationalize the high (low) design cost treatment, participants were asked to create a playlist of 10 (5) songs. Following from Experiment 1, customized bundling requires additional cognitive effort to design the bundle and designing larger bundles should require more cognitive effort than designing smaller bundles. We hypothesize that participants are likely to offset the additional cognitive burden of creating a larger bundle by selecting songs that are more easily recognized, therefore creating bundles with songs that are more well known on average.

For completeness, we added an external search costs condition in our experimental design to distinguish the effects between external search costs and design costs. This separation of effects is useful in demonstrating the impact of design costs above and beyond standard search costs. In the high external search cost condition, we randomized the order in which the 200 songs are presented to the participants; in the low external search cost condition, the songs are sorted alphabetically by artist and then title—a common format in most music libraries and stores.

We performed an ANOVA test with design costs and external search costs as main effects, design costs × external search costs as an interaction effect, and the time taken to create the playlist bundle as a covariate. The dependent variable was the mean rank (i.e., popularity as measured by ranking in the iTunes top 400 list) of all the songs that were included in the playlist created by the participant. A lower (higher) mean rank indicates that a participant created a bundle with more (less) popular songs on average. The results show that only the design cost main effect had a significant effect on the mean rank of the songs in the created playlists $( F ( 7 6 , 1 ) = 4 . 4 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The expected marginal means of the song rank of participants under high design costs is significantly lower than those under lower design costs (see Figure 2). Although higher search costs resulted in a higher expected marginal mean for the song rank, the effect was not significant. The results suggest that the increase in design costs, regardless of the level of external search cost, will result in consumers creating bundles with more popular songs. It is important to note that in the high design costs treatment, participants created larger playlists, which allowed for more potential variation in the popularity of the songs included in the bundle. However, our results show that, even with more room for variation in these larger bundles, participants focused more on popular songs.

We test compromise effects whereby consumers compare their choice set for a customized bundle against an additional external set of options [43]. Since presenting participants with choice sets of varying numbers of extreme options (i.e., niche products) could bias the results, we operationalized the treatments by using two primes to create a comparison while holding the choice set fixed across the two treatments. For the primes, we used a “niche” comparison set to induce compromise effects and a “mainstream” comparison set to act as a control. Priming studies have been well established to be effective in creating a basis of comparisons in consumers [43].

In the niche priming condition (high compromise effects), participants were required to listen to three lesser-known songs (songs chosen from Amazon.com ranked between 19,800 and 20,000 based on sales popularity in the week the experiment was conducted). After listening to these songs, participants were required to create a custom bundle of five songs from a list of 20 available songs (the participant had a chance of winning this playlist at the end of the study). The master list of 20 songs was a random sample from the same set of songs used in Experiments 1 and 2. Participants were able to listen to a 30-second sample of any song in the set. The niche songs in the prime provided an extreme comparison benchmark for participants as they created the custom playlist, which is more likely to induce compromise effects [43].

In the mainstream priming condition (control), participants were required to listen to three popular songs (songs chosen from the top 50 ranked songs by sales on Amazon. com during the week of the experiment) prior to creating a five-song custom bundle from the same 20 songs used in the niche condition. The popular song set provided a nonextreme basis of comparison for the consumer (since participants were selecting other popular songs in the customized bundle task), resulting in lower potential for compromise effects.

Choosing songs from a smaller list (20 instead of 200) allowed participants to better focus on the traits and attributes of the songs and presented an easier opportunity to sample the music available. Furthermore, a set of 20 songs ensures that external search costs and design costs are kept to a minimum in the experiment setting, while still providing a large enough choice set for reasonable variability in the final bundles. After the participants finished the customized bundling task, we conducted a manipulation check for perceived popularity of the three songs used in the primes (questions: “I am familiar with the three songs”; “I have heard these songs before in other situations, e.g., radio play, club, TV, etc.”; rated from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree”). The manipulation check was significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that the primes were significantly different in terms of perceived popularity.

We compared the popularity of the songs selected to the participants’ playlists across treatment groups. Specifically, we used the mean rank of all the songs in the playlist as our measure (lower rank representing higher popularity), as was done in Experiment 2. Our results show that in the niche priming treatment group (high compromise effects) the mean ranking of the songs selected in the custom bundle was significantly lower than the mean rank in the mainstream priming treatment (control) group (9.06 versus 10.30, t-statistic = 2.27, p < 0.05). To explore this difference further, we also compared the popularity of each individual song between both treatments according to the order in which it was added to the playlist (Table 3). Interestingly, we observed that for the first two songs added, the individual popularity of each song was not statistically different across treatments. However, from the third song onward, participants in the high compromise effect treatment group selected songs that were, on average, more mainstream than the songs selected by participants in the control group.

We also tested for significant differences in the variance of the song rank between the control and compromise effect treatment groups. This suggests that compromise effects have influenced participants to select more “hits” (with lower rank) in a more consistent fashion.

The results from Experiment 3 support our compromise effect hypothesis. From prior theory it is known that consumers compare and contrast the attributes of products when making purchase decisions. In our study, the compromise effects conditions (i.e., the stark contrasts of the niche products) provide extreme anchors for participants to base their comparisons, which appears to bias consumer choices toward mainstream products.

To summarize the findings so far, in Experiment 1 we demonstrated that customized bundling leads to design costs as measured by increased cognitive effort. In Experiment 2 we isolated the effect of design costs from external search costs and demonstrated that higher design costs result in a significant decrease in consumption variety. In Experiment 3 we demonstrated that higher compromise effects result in a decrease in consumption variety. These experiments differentiate and provide strong evidence to support two plausible theoretical explanations for increased selection of mainstream products due to the use of a customized bundling sales mechanism. Although these experiments provide prescriptive evidence for the phenomena, the descriptive evidence in real-life practical situations remains to be shown. In the next section, we exhibit evidence of changes in consumption variety due to the presence of customization through an empirical study using data of actual music sales. However, we qualify that the purpose of the following study is not to objectively quantify the increase in design costs or compromise effects (which is not possible under the preset conditions of the real-world purchase environment), but instead to show that in choices under customizable bundling there are observable and significant differences in consumption variety.

## Empirical Study—Design Costs and Compromise Effects in the Real World

We ob tained 20 month s of sales data from a North American music retailer that sells traditional music (digital and physical) as well as customizable bundles of songs at physical retail points-of-sale. Data were collected from January 1, 2005, through September 9, 2006, on the sales of music from digital kiosks located at eight retail locations, which were dispersed geographically across the United States. Each retail location contained two or more digital kiosks. Each kiosk accessed a centralized digital music library that contained both full albums (over 70,000) and individual music tracks (over 1 million). The library was updated weekly with new releases and contained content from the four major record labels (EMI Group, Sony BMG, Universal Music Group, Warner Music Group) as well as many smaller independent labels.

Consumers had two options for purchasing music through the digital kiosks: purchasing a copy of an entire album or creating and purchasing a custom playlist containing music tracks from multiple artists and/or albums. Playlists and full albums were subsequently burned to a CD at the retail location and delivered to the consumer upon payment. Two pricing schemes were employed across the retail locations: (1) \$3.99 for the first song plus \$0.99 for each additional song and (2) \$4.99 for up to the first two songs plus \$0.99 for each additional song. Regardless of purchasing a fixed entire album or customized album, the consumers were subjected to the same pricing structure based on the number of songs purchased within the bundle.<sup>4</sup> The digital music kiosks also allowed consumers to listen to a 30-second sample of any song prior to purchase. Table 4 provides general summary statistics of the data.

The data present a unique opportunity to study consumption patterns under both customized and fixed bundling. Data on the sales of traditional albums exemplify the case of low design costs and compromise effects, where the variety available is less than that in the case of customized bundling. In this situation, consumers use a search engine and music library to find available prebundled full albums before making a purchase. Alternatively, the data on the sales of customizable bundles represent the case where consumers have the freedom to create a customized CD, from a large pool of songs with the aid of the same search engine and music library. In the case of customized bundles, there is an increase in available product variety and low external search costs accompanied by the hypothesized higher design costs and compromise effects associated with the creation of custom bundles. We therefore expect to see a higher concentration of mainstream songs purchased under the customized bundling scenario. To ensure a consistent comparison, empirical analyses were conducted with individual songs (components of the bundle) as the unit of analysis.<sup>5</sup> For customized bundles, we use the individual songs from each customized bundle. For fixed bundles, we disaggregate the fixed bundle into individual songs.

## Difference in Design Costs Between Fixed and Customized Bundling

Our first analysis focuses on determining if the design costs of customized bundles are higher than fixed bundles, as hypothesized in H1a. As suggested in H1a and shown in Experiment 1, consumers spend more time creating a customized bundle as compared to selecting a fixed bundle. To empirically test this relationship, we specify that the design cost, δ, is a function of the bundling traits (bundle: fixed versus customized) and other controls, that is, δ = f (bundle, controls). Design cost is measured by the time (in seconds) taken by a consumer from initiating a session with the kiosk until a purchase is made. This measure includes time for sampling and selecting songs (for customized bundles) or an album (for fixed bundles). Controls include the size of the bundle, pricing scheme, and number of songs sampled—factors that potentially affect the duration a consumer spends on bundle selection. We computed three sets of estimates (ordinary least squares [OLS], Poisson, and negative binomial) for comparative purposes. Poisson and negative binomial estimates take into account that the dependent variable, time (in seconds), is a strictly positive count variable. The results of our three estimations suggested that the customized bundles, on average, take longer to design and create prior to sale (coefficients positive and significant—OLS: 322.538; Poisson: 0.441; negative binomial: 0.432; all $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ (see Table 5). These results corroborated the results obtained from Experiment 1.

## Discussion

Th e finding s of our research h ave various implic ations for both theory and practice. Here, we identify and discuss three key implications for IS research. First, our research highlights the importance of the relationship between IT and product variety and its effects on consumption behavior. Prior research has shown that IT promotes product variety [17]; greater IT investments by retailers can lead to increased product variety within a marketplace. IT lowers direct costs incidental to planning, design, manufacturing, and quality control—costs that are essential to the production and sale of goods and services. Investment in IT potentially lowers the costs for producers to experiment with new product lines for product diversification and further customer segmentation. IT, specifically e-commerce technologies, also reduces indirect costs incidental to logistics, inventory management, distribution, and consumer search—costs that are required to transfer the finished goods to the hands of consumers [4].

As a result of increased product variety, there is a need for more research on changes in consumption behavior. One oft-cited answer is that increased product variety leads to more diffused consumer demand, that is, the “long-tail” hypothesis [8, 35]. It is important to note that our results are not at odds with the “long-tail” hypothesis, which explains increases in consumption variety due to improvements in supply-side dynamics and increased product variety. In contrast, we provide an additional perspective that considers the demand-side behavioral economic biases to which consumers are known to be susceptible. Following the suggestions of Brynjolfsson et al. [10] and Goh and Bockstedt [19], the studies presented contribute to the ongoing research stream on the impact of IT on consumer purchasing behavior, product variety, and sales diversity.

Second, a generally accepted view in the field of IS research is that IT (especially Web technologies) reduces search costs [4]. Technology facilitates information gathering by allowing consumers to search for product and price information with ease. This generalized argument about IT and search cost, however, tends to lump together internal and external search costs, two distinct costs associated with how consumers deal with information [33]. Specifically, external search costs are more closely related to the generally used definition of search costs in economics of IS research. Although the concepts of internal search costs and cognitive bounds are not new to economics research, these concepts remain underrepresented compared to other search cost theories applied in e-commerce research. We raise the issue that, although external search costs are lowered in the presence of e-commerce technologies, the effects may be tempered by a corresponding increase in internal search costs and cognitive effort (i.e., design costs). The underlying IT advancements that promote e-commerce also afford changes to market practices (e.g., customization, creation of user-generated content, formation of social networks), which potentially generate significantly more information to be processed by the consumer. As a result, additional cognitive load is placed on the individual decision maker and new forms of effort are potentially incurred in the purchase process. Advances in IT can create additional “friction” in e-commerce through changes in information quantity and product variety. The resulting demand-side consequences are often neglected in the majority of economics of e-commerce research.

At a higher level of theoretical abstraction, our research draws attention to the importance of behavioral economic theories in IS research. Much of the economics of e-commerce research is focused on the formulation of normative theories and models to explain and predict e-commerce phenomena. The development of this established domain of knowledge is encouraging and unquestionably beneficial to the field of IS research. In a world of rising information complexity, there is also a need to holistically grow the body of economics of IS research to encompass other economic theories that supplement current explanations of real-life phenomena. We believe that the insights of behavioral economics have much to offer in the understanding of the economics of e-commerce. As Tversky and Kahneman noted, the “deviations of actual behavior from the normative model are too widespread to be ignored, too systematic to be dismissed as random error, and too fundamental to be accommodated by relaxing the normative system” [37, p. 252].

The findings of our research directly inform practitioners about consumption patterns of digital information goods. One straightforward implication is an observed increased focus on “hits” due to customization. Producers of information goods should be aware of the reduction in subsidies that “hit” information goods provide to the “niche” items when they are sold in fixed information good bundles. These observations have implications for profits, pricing, sales strategies, and scale economics for customizable products. For example, sellers will have to decide how they should price “hits” and “niches” in light of the changes in demand quantities due to customization.

An additional implication of this study is that it provides practitioners some insights pertaining to retail design for customizable bundles of information goods. Although design and search costs increase in the presence of greater product variety, this increased cost may be managed through the use of appropriate technologies such as recommendation systems and reviews [21, 39]. Profit-seeking retailers will need to understand how design costs and compromise effects influence their bottom lines. There is significant opportunity for additional research to design and evaluate sales mechanisms that allow retailers to take advantage of these decision biases. Retailers who profit more from the sale of “hit” items may wish to increase design costs for consumers and try to implement mechanisms that promote compromise effects. Retailers profiting more from the sale of niche goods would be better served to reduce design costs and minimize compromise effects. It is well known that Netflix uses a similar strategy to manage demand for hit movies in its movie rental-by-mail service (e.g., [30]) when customers create their customized movie rental bundle (i.e., the queue). Retailers may be able to adopt similar strategies for maximizing profits by using recommendation systems to analyze bundle contents and to suggest niche or hit items to the consumer. Digital retailers have large product catalogs, and the effects observed in our experimental studies will likely be more pronounced in the real world, which emphasizes the need for retailer interventions to actively manage sales variety and resulting profitability.

## Limitations and Future Research

One of th e limitations of th is study is th at we c onsider only music . As much as music is an appropriate context to test our hypotheses, one might question if the results will hold true for different classes of information goods. Music has numerous traits that make it a suitable context for the manipulation of experimental treatments, which may be less practical for other classes of information goods (e.g., it might be impractical to use movie screenings to prime participants considering the longer durations). However, we believe these differences should be minimal and would expect to observe similar consumption patterns for other information goods. Future research can explore potential differences across information good types (e.g., movies, books, and computer games), possibly in real-life empirical settings.

The scope of this study was to measure the effects of customized bundling on product consumption variety. Therefore, in the design of the experiments, we focused on this key objective and did not consider the moderating effects of recommendation systems, which have been shown to effect consumption variety (e.g., [16]). Although the use of recommendation systems exists in many e-commerce contexts, we believe that there is still a need to understand and quantify the effects of customization on consumption variety first, before moving on to other phenomena in e-commerce settings that might moderate the effects. Therefore, one future area of research is to examine the moderating effects of decision aids, such as recommender systems, on the relationship between design costs, compromise effects, and consumption variety.

## Conclusion

In th is study, we presented two th eoretic al explanations for decreases in consumption variety through the use of customized bundling. In Experiment 1 we demonstrated that customized bundling leads to increased design costs as measured by cognitive effort. In Experiment 2 we isolated the effect of design costs from external search costs and demonstrated that higher design costs result in a significant decrease in consumption variety. In Experiment 3 we demonstrated that higher compromise effects introduced in customization result in a decrease in consumption variety. Analysis of data on music sales from a North American retailer corroborates these findings and points to a general decrease in consumption variety due to customized bundling.
