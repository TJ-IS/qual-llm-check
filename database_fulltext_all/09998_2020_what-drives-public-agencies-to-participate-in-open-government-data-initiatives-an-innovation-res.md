# What drives public agencies to participate in open government data initiatives? an innovation resource perspective

Yang Zhenbin<sup>a</sup>, Atreyi Kankanhalli<sup>b</sup>, Sangwook Ha<sup>b,</sup>⁎, Giri Kumar Tayi<sup>c</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Open government data Public agency External innovators Resource dependence Conformity need Sensitivity of function

## A B S T R A C T

While the potential benefits of open government data (OGD) initiatives are significant, there has often been a lack of participation by public agencies in these eforts. Motivated by this challenge and a corresponding research gap, we develop a theoretically grounded model to explain what drives public agencies to share their data on OGD platforms. Model testing with survey and objective data from 102 public agencies indicates that agencies’ resource dependence on external innovators significantly impacts their data sharing behavior. Furthermore, conformity need and the sensitivity of their function also influence agencies’ data sharing beha vior. Contributions toward research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Governments around the world are increasingly implementing open government data (OGD) initiatives that aim to share data from public agencies through web portals or platforms. Open data in the government context refers to data that are produced through public funds and made available without restrictions on its usage or distribution [1]. Indeed, government entities are often the largest creators and collectors of data in multiple domains, such as transport, health, and environment [2]. The sharing of such data through OGD initiatives allows for new services to be created by external innovators, i.e., external entities including individual developers and firms that are interested in utilizing public agencies’ data for developing new products and services [3]. For example, CVS health launched a service called myHealthFinder that uses public data and API from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) to provide personalized recommendations for pre ventive healthcare services.<sup>1</sup> Another example is the Climate Corporation’s Climate FieldView™ platform that helps farmers to make decisions related to agricultural activities, based on data from the National Weather Service, U.S. Geological Survey, and National Aeronautics and Space Administration.<sup>2</sup>

It is seen that OGD initiatives ofer multiple benefits if successfully implemented. For instance, sharing pubic agency data through OGD initiatives can lead to higher transparency and accountability of government operations by providing an efective way for the public to monitor their activities [4]. In addition, sharing free, reusable public agency data is expected to stimulate public service innovation by lowering the cost of entry for individual developers and corporations to create new services with the data [5].

Despite these potential benefits, many public agencies do not proactively participate in OGD initiatives. For example, interviews of oficials in New York, London, and Berlin found that public agencies in these cities tend to share their data with a small number of groups and organizations rather than participating in OGD initiatives [6]. Similarly, an analysis on www.data.gov portal found that only 5% of all US federal agencies followed government guidelines for disclosing their data [7]. The quality of data and the sensitivity of agency function are suggested as barriers related to such behaviors [8], but little is known about what drives public agencies’ participation or data sharing in OGD initiatives.

In this regard, a few studies have attempted to explore the factors that afect public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives (e.g., [9,10]) – a more detailed review is provided in the next section. However, robust theoretically grounded and empirically validated in vestigations on the phenomenon are scarce [11]. Our review surfaced several gaps in this area. First, there is a lack of investigation of the role of external innovators and their relationship with public agencies in OGD initiatives, although prior research has suggested that these innovators may play an important role in such initiatives [12,23]. In addition, little attention has been paid towards understanding how public agencies’ needs for enhancing transparency as well as stimulating innovations in public services influence their participation in OGD initiatives [8,13]. Moreover, prior research on this topic has used self-reported survey measures to assess the outcome i.e., public agencies’ participation or data sharing in OGD initiatives, which may not reflect their actual participation [14] that our study measures.

Motivated thus, we seek to answer the following research question: What are the needs and constraints of public agencies’ that drive their data sharing behavior in OGD initiatives? To address this research question, we propose a theoretical model to explain the drivers of public agencies open data sharing behavior based on resource dependence theory [15] and related literature. Our model posits that public agencies’ open data sharing behavior is influenced by their resource constraints, which lead them to depend on external innovators to fulfil their need for innovation of public services. We also propose alternative explanations i.e., other needs (need for transparency, conformity need) and constraints (sensitivity of function), for open data sharing by public agencies. The model was validated through survey and objective data from 102 public agencies in Singapore, which has been a leader in e-government [16]. Yet, there are still barriers to OGD participation by public agencies<sup>3</sup> .

The results of our model test indicate that public agencies’ resource dependence on external innovators is determined by the relative magnitude, criticality, substitutability, and discretion of the innovation re sources, which in turn positively afects agencies’ open data sharing behavior. We also found that public agencies’ need for conforming to open data policies from higher authorities, and the sensitivity of their function influence their open data sharing behavior.

Overall, this study contributes to research in this area by developing and empirically validating a theoretical model of public agencies’ open data sharing behavior using objective assessment of the outcome. We integrate antecedents (needs and constraints) of public agencies’ open data sharing behavior from related literature (i.e., need for transparency, conformity need, and sensitivity of function) and expand them by introducing the novel perspective of resource dependence that adds to the explanations beyond existing research. We also contribute to the literature on resource dependence by identifying and testing the efect of the dependence of three types of innovation resources (IT, financial, and human) on public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives.

In terms of practical implications, the results of our study indicate that OGD initiatives are considered as an efective means of acquiring external innovators’ resources for public agencies’ service innovation activities. Thus, public agencies need to proactively engage external innovators e.g., independent developers and companies, in order to reap the benefits of OGD initiatives. Furthermore, the findings also suggest that public agencies should balance their conformity needs (that drive them to share data) against the sensitivity of their function (that hinders their data sharing).

## 2. Conceptual background

In this section, we first review the literature on public agency participation or data sharing in OGD initiatives to highlight the gaps and position our study. Subsequently, we introduce the resource dependence theory and propose it as a suitable perspective for examining public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives, because agencies can use these initiatives to manage their need for acquiring innovation resources from external innovators. Public agencies may lack the in-house resources and expertise to efectively deliver new public services because of the multifaceted nature of services [17]. To alleviate this si. tuation, public agencies can decide to rely on external innovators to meet their resource needs by engaging in formal and informal collaborations with them [18]. In the context of our study, public agencies could agree to share their data via OGD initiatives in order to harness the collective resources of external innovators to innovate public services [8]. For instance, the city of Seattle released a wide variety of datasets to the public in order to develop new citizen-sourced services by using external developers’ resources [19].

## 2.1. OGD initiatives and public agency participation

In recent years, governments worldwide have been launching open government data (OGD) initiatives for the public and organizations to access and use data collected or generated by public agencies. Starting with the data.gov portal initiated by the US federal government in 2009, according to Open Knowledge International’s Global Open Data Index<sup>4</sup>, 94 countries had at least one OGD initiative established by their national or local governments in 2016. Yet, the success of OGD in itiatives crucially depends on public agencies’ participation by sharing their data on such platforms, which has often not been forthcoming [6,7], and requires research attention. In this regard, a few studies have examined the factors that afect public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives (see Table 1). According to the focus of our study, we only include conceptual, review, and empirical studies that specifically cover the antecedents of OGD participation. It should be noted that other studies used the terms degree of data openness [20], adoption of OGD [9], open data publication [21], and OGD capacity [10] that are synonymous to OGD participation or open data sharing by public agencies in our study.

Particularly, some studies have identified potential benefits and barriers for public agencies’ to participate in OGD initiatives. This was done through conducting exploratory interviews and workshop [8], and reviewing open data literature [13,22]. These studies suggested that public agencies expect potential benefits (e.g., enhancing transparency) and barriers (e.g., ensuring data quality) with respect to their participation in OGD initiatives. However, this stream of research did not theoretically and empirically investigate whether these potential benefits and barriers influence public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives.

More recently, a few empirical studies have been conducted to examine the antecedents of public agencies’ OGD participation or open data sharing. Specifically, Wang and Lo [9] used the technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework to assess factors afecting adoption of OGD initiatives by Taiwanese government agencies, defined in terms of their extent of data sharing. They found that perceived benefits, organizational readiness, and external pressures positively influence government agencies’ adoption of OGD (measured through survey). Yang and Wu [21] used many theories to examine the sociotechnical determinants of open data publication among Taiwanese government agencies. They reported that perceived usefulness, organizational capability, external influence, and facilitating conditions positively afect government agencies’ intention to publish open data and their subsequent behavior (both measured through survey), while perceived risk negatively influences the intention. Zhao and Fan [10] utilized resource-based theory to model and assess how diferent types of organizational resources (tangible, human, and intangible) influence public agencies’ OGD capacity (similar to sharing behavior, but also measured through survey) based on survey data from agencies in Shanghai. They found that several tangible, human, and intangible resources positively influence public agencies’ OGD capacity.

## 2.2. Resource dependence theory

Resource dependence theory (RDT) seeks to explain why and how the environment of organizations shapes their behaviors [15]. The theory proposes organizations as a part of open systems embedded in their environment. Organizations depend on others in their environment to acquire critical resources for continuous and successful orga nizational activities. Because this interdependent relationship with others (or resource dependence) can be disrupted by uncertainties in their environment, organizations attempt to manage this relationship through various organizational behaviors [24].

RDT states that an organization’s dependence on specific resources can be determined by their three characteristics, namely the importance, source substitutability, and level of discretion over the resource [15]. The importance of a resource is defined by two dimensions, i.e., the relative magnitude of the resource and its criticality [25]. The relative magnitude of an external resource is defined as the amount of resources derived from a source as a proportion of the total input [26]. The larger the proportion, the more important the resource is perceived by organizations. Resource criticality is defined as the extent to which a resource is necessary for organizational activities [15]. The level of resource dependence is also determined by the number of available alternative sources i.e., source substitutability, with a lower number of alternatives implying that the current source of resources will have more power [27]. In other words, source substitutability refers to the degree to which an organization has alternative sources when a present source is unable or unwilling to continue the supply of resources or reduces the scope of supply [28]. Finally, when an organization is unable to control the allocation or use of an external resource i.e., it has low discretion, its dependence on the resource will correspondingly be high [29]. Discretion over a resource is the organization’s capacity to determine its allocation or use, or the level of control over the resource [15]. Discretion is a major source of power, as having access to or being able to regulate resources’ deployment, reduces dependence on others.

A prerequisite for examining the resource dependence of public agencies on external innovators is an identification of the types of resources that they require for performing service innovation activities [15]. To investigate the relationship between public agencies’ resource dependence and their open data sharing behavior, we propose three types of resources for external innovation (IT, financial, and human resources) based on prior literature.

## 2.2.1. Information technology (IT) resources

IT resources, such as information systems (IS), are considered as a major driver of innovation activities in public agencies [30]. The increased scanning, coordination, and collaboration capabilities of public agencies due to the adoption of IS are seen to facilitate the introduction of new policy and public service innovations [31]. At the same time, researchers have also stressed that public agencies face challenges to meet diverse demands from the public, as they possess limited technical resources regarding their innovation activities [8]. For instance, the breakdown of innovative US public services, such as healthcare.gov, are often attributed to the US government’s use of decades-old IT systems and its inadequate IT procurement process [32]. To address this issue and promote public service innovation, some studies have proposed a “platform” strategy, i.e., relying on external innovators’ IT resources for their innovation [33], which is what OGD initiatives can ofer.

## 2.2.2. Financial resources

Another major barrier to implementing and fostering service innovation for public agencies are financial constraints [34]. This is be cause the regulations and bureaucratic structures of public agencies limit their power and control on their financial resources for innovation [35]. For example, a study of OECD countries found that removing constraints in the budget process and introducing financial incentives are crucial for stimulating innovation in public agencies [36]. To overcome this issue, public agencies can collaborate with external innovators in private organizations and individuals to utilize their financial resources, which could have less restrictions and can therefore be allocated more flexibly than their own resources [37]. Such intersector collaborations can assist public agencies in providing better public services by overcoming financial impediments, particularly in the case of public service innovation [38].

## 2.2.3. Human resources

Beyond IT and financial resources, human resources have been considered as a significant input for conducting public agencies’ service innovation activities [39]. However, insuficient incentive systems in government organizations and the lack of empowerment among public servants are seen as hindering public agencies in the recruitment and allocation of adequate human resources for their innovation activities [40]. For instance, a large-scale survey found that only 5.7% of re spondents selected working for the US federal government as an ideal career because of its low salary compared to the private sector [41]. Introducing open innovation i.e., encouraging the participation of external innovators in public agencies’ innovation activities, would be one approach to resolve this issue [42]. In this approach, external innovators are motivated to contribute their human resources (e.g., external knowledge and expertise) to public agencies’ innovation activities, thus alleviating incentive and empowerment issues.

## 2.3. Transparency and accountability

Another cited reason behind OGD initiatives is that they are moti vated by the need to enhance the transparency and accountability of government operations [43]. This view is linked to the concept of Freedom of Information (FOI) (or the Right to Information) that the disclosure of government information will improve the transparency and accountability of governance by allowing the public to monitor government activities [44]. This line of research suggests that the adoption of information systems in the public sector in recent times has not only changed how government agencies operate, but has also opened a new era of FOI [45]. Specifically, the transition to e-government has enabled public agencies to collect, store, and process in formation, which was not possible in the past, and these agencies use OGDIs as one of their tools for disclosing their information to improve FOI-related government services [46].

These linkages among FOI, e-government, and OGDIs suggest several potential factors that may drive public agencies participation in OGDIs. First, public agency may participate OGDIs to satisfy their need for transparency and accountability [3]. Case studies on the US and UK OGDIs have reported anecdotal evidence on this argument by explaining how the presidential actions and regulations related to FOI resulted in public agencies’ participation in OGDIs [19,47]. Thus, we include need for transparency as an antecedent of public agencies’ data sharing in our model.

Second, public agencies may also have mandates or pressures from higher authorities e.g., federal government or ministries-in-charge, to release their data on OGD platforms [48] in addition to their own need for improving transparency and accountability. This is because OGDIs are often a part of the larger political agenda that is proposed or initiated by the head of administration such as a president [46]. Under this kind of situations, public agencies may try participating in OGDIs to conform to formal and informal demands from their higher authorities.

Thus, we include conformity need as an antecedent of public agencies open data sharing in our model.

Third, as a flip side to the need for transparency and conformance, some public agencies and departments e.g., defense-related, may pos sess sensitive data that is inappropriate to reveal through OGDIs. Hence, the sensitivity of the agency function could act as a constraint to their data sharing [8]. Thus, we include sensitivity of agency function as an antecedent of public agencies’ open data sharing in our model.

## 3. Model and hypotheses

As discussed above, we build on the resource dependence theory (RDT) and related literature to propose our theoretical model on the needs and constraints that influence public agencies’ data sharing in OGD initiatives. Specifically, we posit that the need for innovation resources reflected in the resource dependence dimensions, i.e., relative magnitude of resource, resource criticality, source substitutability, and discretion over external resources, will influence a public agency’s dependence on external innovators and thereby its open data sharing behavior (i.e., the extent of the data shared by the agency through OGD initiatives). The model also proposes that alternative needs i.e., the public agency’s need to conform to higher authorities (conformity need), and need for transparency, as well as constraints i.e., sensitivity of function, will afect its open data sharing behavior. ## 3.1. Relative magnitude of resource

IT, human, and financial resources from external innovators can account for a major proportion of public agencies’ service innovation activities. In terms of IT resources, the inadequacy of IT infrastructure has been observed as a major barrier for e-government [49] and, more recently, for smart city services [50]. For example, public agencies often have inadequate IT infrastructure in terms of servers or networks to deploy new online services [51]. In view of these limitations, public agencies may have to rely on external innovators to provide IT resources for service innovation, hence leading to greater dependence.

Human resources include the manpower, ideas, knowledge, and skills required to meet the objectives of the organization. Prior e-gov ernment literature has highlighted that the failure to harness the ideas, knowledge, and skills of the public has stifled the ability of public agencies to innovate their services [52]. In recent times, citizen sourcing eforts are seen as a solution to the limited internal human resources for innovation of public services [53]. Public agencies may need to increasingly rely on external innovators’ expertise for service innovation.

Financial resources here refer to the monetary capital required by public agencies to meet their goals of service innovation. As public agencies often have limited financial resources with keen competition among diferent priorities [36], it can be challenging for them to invest in developing services that meet the diverse needs of the public. Moreover, as public agencies are often risk-averse [51], they may be constrained financially to innovate services that use recent technology or are seen to have few apparent benefits. Thus, public agencies may depend on external innovators to contribute financial, human, and IT resources, with the dependence being more when the innovation needs are more resource intensive e.g., for many smart city services in recent times [54]. ## 3.2. Resource criticality

IT, human, and financial resources from external innovators can be crucial for public agencies’ service innovation activities. For instance, IT resources such as sensors and servers are essential for agencies seeking to innovate smart city public services e.g., energy monitoring services based on IoT. Moreover, owing to the rapid pace at which technology advances, IT resources can become obsolete quickly, which is particularly salient for public sector organizations [55]. For example, with the rapid increase in mobile penetration rates globally, there is now a spike in demand for mobile public services as compared to earlier web-based services. As inadequate internal IT resources can restrict the provision of new (e.g., mobile) services by the public sector, we expect that the criticality of external innovators’ IT resource contributions will increase public agencies’ dependence on them.

Other than IT resources, external human resources, such as expertise and ideas, are critical for public agencies to achieve continual service innovation [56]. In addition to IT (e.g., data analytics) expertise, new service ideas from external innovators are of increasing importance to public agencies. Similar to the arguments supporting open innovation in firms to harness ideas and solutions from their customers [57], public agencies look to businesses and individuals for ideas to contribute to public service innovation. Moreover, because the normative interests of public agencies are to improve service performance and enhance value to the public [58], higher criticality of the resource will lead to higher dependence on external human resources.

The lack of financial resources is commonly suggested as a major challenge for the innovation of public services [34]. Without suficient financial capital to invest in innovation, public agencies are likely to face dificulties in developing new services e.g., smart city services [54]. With external financial investments made towards utilizing open data, these constraints can be alleviated to some extent, and yet enable agencies to pursue public goals. The extent to which the resources are needed, yet lacking internally, and are made available by external in novators i.e., the resource criticality, will increase the dependence of public agencies on external innovators. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2. Resource criticality (from external innovators) is positively related to a public agency’s dependence on external innovators

## 3.3. Source substitutability

When a public agency wants to promote innovation of public ser vices, it may have to rely on other parties to provide the necessary resources for innovation. Synthesizing stakeholder categories from various e-government studies, Rowley [59] identified several of these parties, such as other public agencies or their employees as relevant stakeholders. For example, in Singapore, the Land Transport Authority could rely on national R&D agencies, such as AStar, to develop parti cular trafic-related applications/services instead of external innovators (individuals or businesses). Indeed, if there are other groups of stakeholders (e.g., other agencies) who are viable alternatives, public agencies will have more avenues to obtain necessary resources for service innovation than relying only on external innovators. Therefore, based on RDT, we expect that the availability of alternatives sources of innovation resources will result in lower dependence on external in novators. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H3. Source substitutability (of external innovators) is negatively related to a public agency’s dependence on external innovators

## 3.4. Discretion over external resources

In the public sector, government agencies’ discretion over the three types of external resources (IT, human, and financial) is expected to vary with diferent innovation initiatives, depending on their ability to restructure their dependency. For example, if public agencies can ne. gotiate with external innovators to provide necessary IT resources (e.g., network infrastructure) to support service innovation over the long term, their dependence will be reduced together with the uncertainty of stable access to these resources.

In the same vein, public agencies may perceive that they are able to control the provision of human resources, such as software development expertise, for service innovation from external innovators. For example, if the public agency can regulate the salaries and employment terms of the external innovators [60], then it will have greater discre tion and lesser dependence on the external innovators. This could be possible if there are employment contracts, whereby the external innovators will then be required to provide manpower for the service innovation according to the contract. In contrast, when there are no binding contracts, the dependence on external innovators will be higher.

In addition, it is sometimes possible for public agencies to have high control over the amount of external financial contributions for service innovation. An example is the use of the public-private partnership (PPP) collaboration approach where both parties can be contractually bound to commit certain levels of financial resources to the service innovation project [18]. However, organizing ad-hoc open data contests (e.g., Apps for Democracy<sup>5</sup>) is a common approach to encourage innovation in OGD initiatives, rather than PPP. Due to the voluntary nature of external innovator participation in such contests, and where open data use is typically governed by license agreements than contracts, public agencies are expected to have less discretion over external financial resources in such cases. The lower control is expected to lead to higher dependence on external innovators. Therefore,

H4. Discretion over external resources is negatively related to a public agency’s dependence on external innovators.

## 3.5. Dependence on external innovators

Dependence refers to an organization’s need to maintain a relationship with other parties to achieve its desired goals [24]. The RDT posits that when an organization is highly dependent on others for resources for attaining its goal, its actions will be influenced by the desire to gain access to these resources [15]. In the context of OGD initiatives, one of the major goals of public agencies is to create socio-economic value through public service innovation that results from their shared information [8]. To achieve this goal, public agencies need to encourage more external innovators to participate in innovation activities by utilizing their data shared through OGD initiatives. This will allow them to gain better access to innovation resources as compared to that when there are only a few external innovators. However, more external innovators will be motivated to take part in service innovation only if there are suficient data provided by the public agency [1].

Thus, we propose that when public agencies’ dependence on external innovators for service innovation is high, their extent of open data sharing will correspondingly be high. Open data can empower and attract greater numbers of external innovators to easily retrieve raw data to innovate on behalf of public agencies [61]. Thereby, public agencies would be able to reap the benefits from external innovations, such as an increase in value for the public, which can be substantial compared to the investments required by agencies to process raw data for publication [62]. Thus, we hypothesize

H5. Public agency’s dependence on external innovators is positively related to its open data sharing behavior

## 3.6. Need for transparency, conformity need, and sensitivity of function

In addition to RDT factors, we also included alternative explanations i.e., needs and constraints, of OGD participation in our research model. First, we expect that public agencies’ need for transparency will influence their open data sharing behaviors. This is related to the principle of freedom of information (FOI), which the United Nations has recognized as a fundamental human right [5]. Subsequently, many countries around the globe have enacted laws supporting FOI for governments to comply with. At the same time, the increasing use of IS in government activities e.g., e-government, has generated large amounts of information not hitherto disclosed to the public [46]. To address this issue, it has been suggested that public agencies use OGD initiatives as a means to disclose government information to the public [44]. When public agencies share their data with the public through OGD initiatives, citizens and interest groups can monitor their activities, thereby, satisfying their need for transparency [4]. Following these arguments, a greater need for transparency will enhance public agency’s open data sharing behavior. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H6. Public agency’s need for transparency is positively related to its open data sharing behavior

A major player related to public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives are higher authorities (e.g., local and national governments) that introduce formal and informal policies to influence agencies to share their data. This could be due to the FOI principle [5] or in order to obtain other benefits accruing from OGD initiatives, such as public goodwill [8]. Either way, prior studies have suggested that such regulations or even informal polices from higher authorities’ generate institutional pressures on public agencies that shape their behavior [48]. Particularly, coercive and normative pressures could be created that drive public agencies to conform with such regulations and policies. Accordingly, we suggest that the need to conform to such pressures from higher authorities will increase public agencies’ open data sharing behavior. Thus, we hypothesize:

H7. Public agency’s conformity need is positively related to its open data sharing behavior

Finally, we expect that a public agency’s specific function will in fluence its open data sharing behavior. Particularly, some public agencies possess and process sensitive information because of their function in the government, which can constrain their OGD participation. For instance, healthcare-related public agencies need to handle private information such as patients’ medical records and their background information [63]. Similarly, public agencies working for na tional security, such as defense agencies, deal with restricted information [64]. The data from these agencies, alone or combined with other data, may reveal individual’s private information [65] or enable people to trace agency operations, which may harm national interests [66]. Indeed, such functions are typically exempt from FOI laws [44] or have their own laws, such as HIPAA. Accordingly, higher sensitivity of function of agencies will lead to their lesser data sharing via OGD initiatives. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H8. Public agency’s sensitivity of function is negatively related to its open data sharing behavior

## 3.7. Control variables

We include two control variables that could afect a public agency’s open data sharing behavior, i.e., data quality and agency size. For instance, if data quality of a public agency is poor, this would prevent them from sharing their data via OGD initiatives, as significant investments would be needed to cleanse the data [67]. We also controlled for agency size because the size of a public agency reflects the expertise and resources that it possesses, whereby the size can influence its open data sharing behavior [4].

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Study context

Singapore’s public agencies and its OGD initiative served as the context for validating our theoretical model. Driven by the Ministry of Finance, the Infocomm Development Authority (IDA), the Land Transport Authority, and the Department of Statistics, Singapore’s OGD initiative<sup>6</sup> was launched in June 2011 [68]. To date, it contains more than 1200 datasets from public agencies and provides government data for applications to be developed by individual and non-government organizations.<sup>7</sup> Initially, data.gov.sg only provided machine-readable datasets from government agencies in Singapore, but more recently the government has revamped the site to implement application programming interfaces (APIs) to support public access government data on a real-time basis [69].

The Singapore context is useful to study because it is considered as one of the exemplary cases of OGD initiatives in the public sector [1]. Singapore has been a leader in e-government (ranked #2 by the 2018 Waseda-IAC Digital Government Rankings<sup>8</sup>) and has a large proportion (10%) of people accessing OGD regularly [16]. The history of Singapore’s OGD initiative, and the role of the Singapore government in implementing it, resemble many developed countries’ approaches for their OGD initiative [70]. Although there are a large number of dataset downloads and API calls from data.gov.sg [71], a number of challenges are also seen, such as improving the data literacy of users, and pro moting more innovative applications [69]. Singapore also does not yet have an FOI Act, though there have been discussions about it.<sup>9</sup>

To gain further insights on the validity of our research model in the Singapore context, two of the authors conducted interviews with the relevant managers from two Singapore public agencies (referred to as A and B here) well-informed about the OGD initiative (not identified for anonymity), to better understand the objectives of the OGD initiative. The interview sessions ranging from 1 to 1.5 hours were audio-recorded with the permission of the interviewees, which were transcribed afterwards. Interviewees stated their purposes for the OGD initiative and motivations for sharing data on it.

First, the agencies’ objective of creating socio-economic value through innovation with the public, surfaced in the first interview. The relevant manager of founding Agency A mentioned:

“We looked at our role as a facilitator on two sides. One is the demand for data, which is (from) the general public. The other is the supply of data that is from the agencies. So our role is to facilitate the agencies and public to collaborate and co-create. The agencies’ role is as the provider of data and the public (role) in providing ideas, solutions, application, technical know-how, creativity, etc. By marrying these two together, what we have is a richer set of tools and applications for citizens which benefit the general public and as a result, leads to greater value creation and more efective people in their work and everyday life.”

Second, both interviewees pointed out the constraints in terms of resources for conducting service innovation activities. Regarding IT resources, the manager from Agency A noted:

“There would also be some developers who have the resources to provide the technological hardware for example the servers. Let’s say if they need to host the data somewhere, whether their own local server or cloud, so they have to pay for the hosting. So there will be some resource required as well.”

while the manager from Agency B observed:

“Along the way we realized we cannot satisfy the needs of all our customers… technology have moved so fast and with so many diferent platforms, some of things that we do are not so nimble like the industry players. By providing data, we can provide services to the community and when they develop new services, they can satisfy bigger segments of our customers.”

Regarding human resources, the manager from Agency A noted:

“[Application developers] can contribute a lot. They have the technical skills, they have the creativity, ideas, and I think they have the time and efort. So with all that they are able to do, they will be able to generate applications that the general public can use.”

Regarding financial resources, the manager from Agency B men tioned:

“[We do face] some [financial] constraints, although not to the extent that we can’t do anything. [To innovate], you need resources such as manpower, technology, the right platform to support subsequently. In terms of skill sets, you also need [them] to manage the diferent platforms you are serving.”

In summary, the responses from these interview sessions conformed to and provided initial support for the resource dependence on the three types of identified external resources for public agencies’ service innovation activities.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs

To develop the survey instrument, we followed the procedures as elaborated in Devellis [72]. First, as far as possible, we adapted measures that had been validated in prior research to fit our study context. Additional items were created where necessary, based on the construct’s conceptualization. Second, for conceptual validation, we performed the two-stage sorting procedure recommended by Moore and Benbasat [73] for the refinement of survey items. For both rounds, the hit ratio, raw agreement, and Kappa scores exceeded the recommended score of 0.65 [73].

The dependent variable (DV), open data sharing behavior was assessed by a cross-validation of both objective and subjective measures. For the public agencies that did not share their data, a value of 0 was assigned to the DV. For the public agencies that shared their data, we assigned the value of the DV based on the extent of data shared reported in the survey, ranging from 1 (very little extent) to 7 (very large extent). We verified these values based on the information available at the public agency’s website as well as on the Singapore government’s OGD platform. Specifically, we collected information on the number of datasets shared by the public agencies in our sample in both venues. We also collected information on the total number of datasets these public agencies have as appeared on their annual reports / press releases. From these two sources of data, we calculated the percentage of their datasets each public agency shared on the OGD platform or agency website. We converted these percentages into a 7-point scale (100 / 7 ≈ 14.3% per each point; 1: 0∼less than 14.3%, 2: 14.3%∼less than 28.6%, 3: 28.6%∼less than 42.9%, 4: 42.9%∼less than 57.2%, 5: 57.2%∼less than 71.5%, 6: 71.5%∼less than 85.8%, 7: 85.8%∼100%). Then we compared this value for each public agency with their survey data to assess the validity of the survey response. The values matched other than 6 public agencies that responded as “Very little extent” and “Little extent” data sharing, as they were planning to share their data in the near future. Thus, the data from both measures was in agreement.

For other constructs, we employed both formative and reflective constructs to ensure methodological soundness [74]. The reflective constructs in our model are as follows: source substitutability, dependence on external innovators, need for transparency, conformity need, sensitivity of function, data quality, and open data sharing behavior (subjective measure). The formative constructs are: relative magnitude of resource, resource criticality, and discretion over external resources, each with three first-order reflective components (i.e., IT, financial, and human). We operationalized these RDT constructs as first-order reflective and second-order formative constructs, which is in agreement with their conceptualization [15]. Items for these measures were rated on sevenpoint Likert scales anchored from Strongly disagree (1) to Strongly agree (7). Finally, we measured agency size by the number of employees working at the agency assessed in terms of 8 diferent categories (see Table 2). A full list of the survey items and their references are provided in Appendix A. The key terms used, e.g., external innovators, were defined in the survey for the benefit of respondents.

## 4.3. Data collection procedure

We collected primary survey data from public agencies in Singapore as follows. We identified them from 2 main lists: 1) the government ministry directory and 2) the government statutory boards directory. A survey questionnaire accompanied by a cover letter explaining the purpose and significance of the study and a postage-paid reply envelope were mailed to the managers who were responsible for each public agency’s open data efort. To improve participation, we sent email reminders to the non-respondents four weeks after the survey was posted. In addition, we followed up with a phone reminder for the remaining agencies another two weeks later. Among the 384 survey questionnaires sent out, 102 complete responses were received, yielding a response rate of 26.6%. This is similar to the response rate ranging from 13 to 27% reported in prior e-government studies [75–77]. A test of non-response bias indicated no significant diferences between early and late respondents in terms of agency characteristics i.e., agency size, position of respondent, and open data sharing behavior, based on ttests. A majority of the respondents were IT managers (51%), while some were assistant IT directors (12.7%), or senior IT directors (12.7%). Most of the public organizations in our sample had sizes of 200–599 employees (25.5%), 10–49 employees (23.5%), or 50–199 employees (21.6%). ## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

To validate our measurement model, we evaluated the survey items by assessing their reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity as suggested in Gefen and Straub [78]. For the reflective constructs, reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s Alpha and compo site reliability. The Cronbach’s Alpha (CA) and composite reliability (CR) for each construct (see Table 3) exceeded the recommended level of 0.70 [79], indicating that all constructs had adequate reliability. 10

Moreover, all item loadings were significant (see Appendix B). Convergent validity was assessed using the average variance extracted (AVE) and factor analysis with principal components analysis and varimax rotation. As desired, all items loaded highly on their intended construct instead of on other constructs (see Appendix C), and their AVEs were above 0.5 [80]. Therefore, convergent validity was considered adequate.

Discriminant validity was evaluated by verifying that items did not load on unintended constructs. Based on the factor analysis results (see Appendix C), the cross-loadings of items was not an issue (maximum value of 0.35). The discriminant validity of constructs was also assessed by comparing AVEs and construct correlations [78]. Our results indicate that the square root of the AVE for each construct (diagonal terms in Table 3) exceeds its correlations with all other constructs. Therefore, discriminant validity was found to be adequate.

Next, we assessed the validity of formative constructs by following recommendations from Petter et al. [81]. We first examined the variance inflation factor (VIF) calculated from measures for each formative construct. The VIFs (maximum value of 1.66) were below the recommended ceiling of 3.3 [82], suggesting that multicollinearity is not an issue. In addition, we examined the statistical significance of the weights of the first-order constructs on their respective second-order construct. Our results (see Table 4) indicate that all the first-order components of their respective second-order formative constructs had positive and significant weights, with the exception of financial resource discretion. Because removing this construct may afect the theoretical validity of the second-order formative construct [81], we decided to retain financial resource discretion for our analysis.

Finally, we conducted a test to assess common method bias. As our survey employed perceptual measures with a single data collection method, we tested for common method bias that may inflate correla tions and create collinearity concerns [83]. Our test results presented in Appendix D indicate that common method bias was not a concern in our study.

## 5.2. Hypotheses tests

To test our hypotheses, partial least squares (PLS) analysis was performed with SmartPLS 3.2.8 as it is a suitable structural equation modelling (SEM) technique for incorporating both formative and reflective constructs jointly [81] as seen in our model. A repeated-indicator approach was used for estimating the structural model for testing our research hypotheses, and the significance levels were estimated using the bootstrap resampling method [84]. The results indicate that relative magnitude of resource, resource criticality, source substitutability, and discretion over external resources, had significant relationships with public agency’s dependence on external innovators in the expected directions (H1, H2, H3, and H4 were supported). Finally, open data sharing behavior was also influenced by public agency’s conformity need and the sensitivity of function, as hypothesized (H7 and H8 were supported), but was not afected by the need for transparency (H6 was not supported). In sum, all the hypotheses were supported except hypothesis H6. Furthermore, the model explained 49.4% of the variance in the dependent variable. ## 5.3. Mediation tests

Following the guidelines in Nitzl et al. [85], we also conducted mediation tests to evaluate whether public agency’s dependence on external innovators mediated the efects of the antecedents (relative magnitude of resource, resource criticality, source substitutability, and discretion over external resources) on the dependent variable (open data sharing behavior). Our mediation test results (see Table 6) revealed that the efects of relative magnitude of resource and resource criticality on the dependent variable were fully mediated by public agency’s dependence on external innovators. In addition, the efect of source substitutability and discretion over external resources were partially mediated by public agency’s dependence on external innovators i.e., the direct efect of these antecedents on open data sharing behavior was still significant after controlling for the mediation efect (= c’ path is significant).

We also found that public agencies’ dependence on external innovators was influenced by the discretion over IT and human resources, but not over financial resources (see Table 4). This indicates that the agencies in our sample did not feel as constrained by their discretion over financial resources as compared to the other two types of resources. A possible explanation for this result could be due to the country where the study was conducted. In Singapore, the government has structured public agencies as state-funded autonomous entities [86]. Once the overall budget plan is approved by the parliament, public agencies can exercise considerable flexibility in managing their financial resources. For instance, they are allowed to determine the allocation of financial resources in their operations. Furthermore, they can shift financial resources between their programs, if necessary. Hence, such flexibility of Singapore public agencies may partially explain why the discretion over financial resources was less important – as also reflected in the quote on financial resources from our interview data. Nevertheless, our model is largely supported and ofers significant contributions to research and practice.

## 6.2. Contributions to research

Our study contributes to extant research in several ways by examining the phenomenon of OGD initiatives from the perspective of public agencies as providers of data. First, although researchers and practitioners have begun to examine OGD-related phenomena, there has been limited theoretically grounded and empirically validated research on the needs and constraints behind public organizations’ open data sharing behavior. This study takes an important step in this direction by proposing a model based on RDT and alternative explanations of OGD sharing, which was empirically validated using survey and objective data from public agencies. Interview data also helped to throw light on our model.

Second, our study is anchored on the resource dependence theory (RDT), a view that is novel and unexplored in explaining public agencies’ dependence on external innovators i.e., innovators in OGD initiatives in this case. Our findings indicate that public agencies depend on external innovators to provide various resources for service innovation, which determines their open data sharing behavior. By extension, as e-government eforts mature and more engagement is sought between public agencies and their external innovators, the RDT can serve as an important theoretical foundation to examine these relationships.

Third, we empirically investigated multiple antecedents of public agencies’ open data sharing behavior. While prior conceptual studies have suggested several benefits (e.g., transparency) and barriers (e.g., sensitivity of function) for public agencies’ open data sharing behavior, empirical tests of the needs and constraints, and that too in conjunction, remain scarce. Through our theoretical modelling and robust empirical analysis, we could reveal and quantify the salient needs and constraints that determine public agencies’ open data sharing behavior. Particularly, dependence on external innovators and conformity need are almost equally important drivers of agencies’ open data sharing behavior, while sensitivity of function is a significant barrier. However, further investigations are required for an expected antecedent (i.e., need for transparency) that was not found significant in our study.

Fourth, our post-hoc analyses ofered a further contribution. Particularly, we found good support for the mediating efect of de pendence on external innovators for the resource dependence dimensions. Finally, we observed robust results while utilizing an objective measure of the dependent variable, i.e., extent of open data sharing behavior.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Combined with existing OGD literature, our findings suggest that a way for public agencies to reach out to their respective external innovators is by sharing data that they possess for public consumption. With an increased disclosure of government data, external innovators will be better empowered to experiment with diferent ways to enhance public value [87], which resonates with Gov 2.0 trends of government seeking to expand its reach and engage the public beyond traditional means [17]. In particular, our findings suggest that open data may be an efective way for public agencies to obtain IT, financial, and human resources. Thus, the managements of public agencies are recommended to consider participating in OGD initiatives as a means to overcome the lack of internal resources. Our findings also suggest that potential barriers to open data sharing should be considered. For example, the sensitivity of agency functions can restrict the extent of data sharing. Therefore, public agencies with sensitive data, may consider other methods to tap the resources of external innovators (e.g., through public-private partnerships).

We also observed that when public agencies have a high dependence on external resources for innovation, they release data to attract external innovators to innovate with the shared data. However, this may open up the possibilities for abuse such as a misrepresentation of data or breach of privacy. Indeed, the management of public agencies must exercise caution when deciding on the types of data to release [88]. For example, raw data that can be aggregated or combined to accurately identify an individual will not be suitable for disclosure due to privacy concerns. In addition, although public agencies may have less control over the types of resources that external innovators provide, it is recommended to have mechanisms in place to withdraw the rights of use of open data use, should the need arise e.g., cases of data misuse [89].

We also found that conformity pressures from upstream authorities can be an efective approach of encouraging public agencies to share more data through OGD initiatives. For policymakers, the development of OGD directions can provide guidance to public agencies on the sharing of datasets, and also motivate them to embrace open data. In addition, this finding has implications for public agencies, as they may have to find ways to overcome the barriers of data release, in the event that they have to comply with mandates from upstream authorities that they report to. For example, if they face dificulties in making investments for data cleansing before release, they may have to request funding for this purpose.

Finally, the overall results of this study in conjunction with prior studies (e.g., [90]) suggest that public agencies may need to proactively search/receive feedback about the kinds of data that external in novators seek. For instance, both our interviews and survey showed that public agencies share their data through OGD initiatives because they expect that this will stimulate a higher level of participation from external innovators (with resources). However, the case of Taiwan’s OGD initiative indicated that creating this “virtuous cycle” requires mutual understanding between public agencies and external innovators, particularly regarding the way government datasets were built and distributed to the public [90]. Hence, we suggest that public agencies wanting to reap the optimal benefits of OGD initiatives need to actively seek opinions and feedback from external innovators who use or are planning to use their data.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of its lim itations. First, as our data were collected through a cross-sectional survey and objective data, causality could not be inferred. The collec tion of longitudinal data in future, with appropriate interventions, could help to assess the causal relationships. Nevertheless, our model is based on well-accepted theory and literature, which adds confidence in our results. Second, our study was conducted in a single country. While this helps to control for contextual and institutional conditions across agencies, it limits the generalizability to other parts of the world. Hence, future research could validate this model in other nations to

extend its generalizability.

Third, although we measured our DV objectively, the other model variables were measured by self-report survey items that may sufer from self-assessment bias. Therefore, future research can attempt to measure some of the other (non-perceptual) variables objectively, such as the diferent types of resources, if data are available. Fourth, our study focused on investigating the need for innovation resources from all external innovators that drives public agencies’ participation in OGD initiatives in aggregate. However, public agencies may have additional motivations for a specific type/group of external innovators based on their expected contributions to public service innovation. Hence, future research could attempt to identify and understand these additional motivations for public agencies from specific groups of external innovators.

## 7. Conclusion

The objective of this study was to develop and test a theoretical model that reveals the types of resources that public agencies need from external innovators and depend on them for, and the efects of public agencies’ resource dependence on their open data sharing behavior, in addition to alternative explanations (other needs and constraints). Drawing on the resource dependence theory and prior literature, we identified the dimensions of public agencies’ dependence and the types of external resources relevant to public service innovation. Besides showing that relative magnitude, resource criticality, source substitutability, and discretion over resources contribute to public organizations’ dependence on external innovators, we validate that the dependence is positively related to the extent of open data sharing by the agencies, in addition to several other antecedents. Overall, the model contributes to theory building in the area of OGD, while also ofering guidance to practitioners in fostering open data sharing.

## Appendix D. Common method bias analysis

We used a full collinearity test [91] to assess common method bias by following the procedure suggested in Kock and Lynn [92]. First, we created a random variable that contains random values ranging from 0 to 1. Second, we analyzed a PLS-SEM model where all our first-order constructs are connected to a construct with the random variable. Third, we calculated VIF (Variance inflation factor) values for these first-order constructs based on the result of the PLS-SEM model. If any of the calculated VIF value is equal to or greater than 3.3, this is considered as a sign of common method bias. Based on our results, none of the first-order constructs showed a VIF value greater than 3.3. From our analysis, we concluded that common method bias is not a concern in our study.
