## Abstract

## Purpose

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the impact of Supply Chain Information Integration (SCII) on the Operational Performance of manufacturing firms in Malaysia considering the role of information leakage.

## Design/methodology/approach

To test the model developed, we conducted an online questionnaire survey with Malaysian manufacturing companies drawn from the Federation of Malaysian Manufacturers directory of 2018. Out of the 400 questionnaires sent out to the manufacturing companies, 144 useable responses were received giving a response rate of 36%. The data were analyzed using SmartPLS, a second-generation statistical tool.

## Findings

The findings of this study showed that information quality, information security, and information technology (IT) had a positive effect on SCII with an explanatory power of 47.2% while SCII, in turn, had a positive effect on operational performance explaining 17% of the variance. Intentional information leakage (IIL) moderated the relationship between SCII and operational performance, whereas accidental information leakage did not moderate the same relationship.

## Practical implications

This study provides insights into difficulties faced when implementing SCII, particularly by medium and large manufacturing companies in Malaysia. It helps identify appropriate strategies that can guide the management in its effort to improve performance by SCII.

## Originality/value

This research is arguably the first study that simultaneously investigates the effect of information quality, IT, and information security on SCII and the moderating effect of information leakage on the relationships between SCII and operational performance. The results of this study indicate that information security has the largest impact on SCII, followed by IT, and information quality. Furthermore, IIL as a negative aspect of information integration may deprive the strength of the relationship between SCII and operational performance.

Keywords:

Supply chain information integration, information sharing, information leakage, Operational performance, manufacturing firms, Malaysia.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s globalized business, supply chain information integration (SCII) is an essential requirement for achieving competitive advantage, adapting to market changes, effective coordination of supply chain activities, facilitating cooperation in the supply chain (SC), as well as effective and efficient forecasting and inventory replenishment [1]. In this research, SCII is defined as the ability of the organization to share information along the SC through electronic linkages [2]. Electronic connectivity is increasingly developed for sharing and receiving information across intra-organization functions and SC partners to address the complexity of various business activities along SCs [3]. The electronic connectivity helps an organization to enhance information integration and improve accuracy in performing business activities. SCII has enabled organizations to integrate production planning, perform joint decision-making, synchronize activities along the SC, which consequently increases the organization’s overall operational performance [4].

However, despite the advantages of SCII, it may pose various risks to organizations in the SC from unlawful utilization of information because information that flows within the SC is associated with material, operation, and finance, which is often sensitive and confidential [2,5]. Information leakage as a negative aspect of information integration may deprive the positive effect of SCII on operational performance [5–7]. Firms need to protect their confidential information related to the products, services, or operations carefully, when pursuing SCII with trading partners [8,10].

In the first half of 2017, 94.3 per cent of information leakage reported in the world is caused by the external violator (43.7%) and employee (50.6%) [11]. Moreover, 67.4% of the information leakages are through the network channel indicating the high risk of electronic connectivity. Knowledge, customer details, transportation specifics, and pricing are constantly pivotal for the manufacturing sector. Such leakages not only lead directly to a diminishment in income and an increase in the total cost of products for customers but do pose a serious threat to sensitive information, damage the organization’s brand reputation, and force unexpected financial obligations [12]. Thus, although SCII leads to increased end product quality and production efficiency, this integration creates new channels for leakages of SC sensitive information and facilitating the ascent of additional information security breaches [2]. Therefore, many firms are still hesitant to share information with other partners across SCs [8,9]. Meticulous considerations of the risk of information leakage, in particular, ought to be paid within manufacturing organizations that have been expanding the SC boundaries [2, 8].

While organization’s performance improvement arising from SCII has been well discussed in the SC management literature, little is known about how the leakage of information in a SC impacts the results of information integration across SC partners [12]. Furthermore, previous studies on SCII have been rather fragmented on their research focus. For instance, some studies investigate the effect of SCII on performance through the use of information technology (IT) [2,13,14], while some focus on SC integration as a key competitive differentiator of operational performance improvement [e.g.,15], and others assess the impact of information quality on SC information sharing [e.g., 16]. Another stream of research has focused on information leakage in SC integration [e.g., 12,17].

These previous studies have shed light on the role of SCII in improving performance and the effect of information leakage on SCII. However, there is no comprehensive study that assesses the effect of information quality, IT, and information security on SCII simultaneously, and how information leakage affects the expected impact of SCII on the operational performance of an organization. Such a study that considers various determinants of SCII, taking into account the moderating effect of information leakage, could enhance the current understanding of the factors influencing SCII and the significance of information leakage.

Therefore, the study aims to simultaneously investigate key factors influencing the SCII and how information leakage affects the relationship of SCII and operational performance. The research questions addressed are:

1. What is the impact of information quality, IT, and information security on SCII?

2. What is the impact of SCII on an organization’s operational performance?

3. How does information leakage affect the impact of SCII on an organization’s operational performance?

To address the research questions, we develop a conceptual model based on resource orchestration theory to justify the selected constructs in the proposed model and their relationships. We then conducted a survey study involving 144 manufacturing firms in Malaysia to test the model. This study findings complement the findings of previous studies on SCII and offer contributions to both research and practice.

In the next section, we present the development of the model and hypotheses. Then, we describe the research methodology and present the findings, followed by a discussion. Based on reflections of the study finding on the previous studies, we highlight theoretical and practical contributions of the study. Finally, we conclude the paper, outline study limitations and propose future research.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical framework development

## 2.1. Overview of previous studies

SCII has been broadly investigated in the management domain because of its significant benefits for both practitioners and academicians [26–28]. Based on a systematic literature review, we found several studies (Table 1) that investigate the relationship between SCII and multiple dimensions of performance.

A few studies [27–29] indicated that SCII was effective for operational and firm performance in general. Lu et al. [30] examined the impact of SC integration on operational performance considering the moderating effect of market uncertainty and they confirmed the positive effect of SC integration on operational performance. Moreover, Yu et al. [28] acknowledged that SCII had a positive effect on flexibility and enhanced the firm’s operational performance.

However, many studies have shown inconsistent results regarding the relationship between SCII and operational performance. For example, Zhao et al. [31] investigated the relationship among supplier integration, customer integration, internal integration, and firm performance. The results of their study indicated that inappropriate (too much or too little) SC integration could weaken a firm’s financial performance. This finding highlighted the importance of managerial orchestration in SCII.

Moreover, Turkulainen and Ketokivi’s [32] study on 266 manufacturing companies in nine countries indicated that the positive effect of integration on operational performance was contingent. In addition, Flynn et al. [33] argued that the relationship between supplier integration and operational performance is not significant when internal integration is high.

Therefore, to enhance the existing knowledge on the relationship between SCII and operational performance, our study employs orchestration theory and proposes a novel framework that considers the moderating role of information leakage on this relationship.

## 2.2. Resource orchestration theory and the proposed framework

The resource-based view (RBV) presents the theoretical basis of comprehension when resources bolster up an organization’s competitive advantages and, thereby, its performance. RBV posits that organization performance can be described by heterogeneity in having resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable [18,19]. However, without managerial acumen, possessing such resources alone does not ensure excellent performance [20]. Therefore, resource orchestration theory addresses this limitation of RBV as it is “concerned with the actions leaders take to facilitate efforts to effectively manage the firm’s resources” [21]. As such, the results of resource usage are determined by joint impacts ending from the mixture of different resources that are coordinated with the focal resource instead of the independent impacts of the sole resources [22]. Improvements in IT give numerous chances to organizations to accomplish seamless integration with their SC partners [23].

Moreover, the advantages of information integration in the SC can be acknowledged if the shared information has a good quality [24]. Information security is one of the main issues in information integration [25]. However, based on resource orchestration theory, what makes a difference is not the individual effect of resources such as information quality, IT, and information security but the fit or alignment of these three independent variables that further create SCII and OP. In this view, resource orchestration theory contention could be extended by theorizing that the capability of organizations to lead SCII into operational performance is subject to their ability to leverage information quality, IT, and information security that can viably be utilized in conjunction with SCII (Figure 1).

## 2.3. Development of hypotheses

## 2.3.1. Information Quality

Information quality has been defined as the timeliness, accuracy, adequacy, and credibility of information exchange [26]. Information quality alludes to information richness, instead of the quantity, and therefore it underscores the quality and nature of shared information among SC partners [27]. Hence, the significance of information sharing in SC relies on what, when, how, and with whom information is shared [28]. All these aspects of information quality must be available to completely understand the potential of information sharing in SCs [24]. Information quality has been identified in the literature as a mechanism, which can generally specify the success of SCII effort [29,30]. The real value of information integration is dependent on how information is shared. Low-quality information has less value. Therefore, SC members must perceive that information from other partners is timely, accurate, and correctly organized to streamline the utilization of shared information. If the quality of information declines, fewer information will be shared. Subsequently, high-quality information provides the organizations with a prominent ability to share information among organizational borders [31].

Various researchers have provided empirical evidence regarding the significance of information quality in SC management. Previous studies in the SC have indicated that mutilated and postponed information makes serious issues in SCs [32]. This mutilated information can be harmful when it moves up the SC [16]. Li and Lin [31] demonstrated that information sharing between organizations is influenced by the quality of shared information. Hartono et al. [34] argued that high quality shared information will result in better information assimilation and better SC performance. Information quality is an integrative strategic tool that enables the SC to obtain a competitive advantage [35]. Thus, the above arguments lead to our first hypothesis.

H1: Information quality has a positive effect on SCII.

## 2.3.2. Information Technology

IT encompasses the information that organizations create and use as well as an extensive variety of increasingly convergent information processing technologies [36]. Advances in IT have dramatically affected the capacity of organizations in a SC to integrate information, and various organizations have taken advantage of these advances. In the interorganizational systems literature, IT is one of the most cited antecedents of success of interorganizational systems use [34]. Moreover, IT is one of the major concerns of integration in the literature [37]. IT plays a significant role in an organization’s collaboration and information sharing both within and between organizations [38]. Therefore, technological independence is necessary for the highly integrated SC [39]. Parallel to the development of IT, companies have become more integrated. Hence, for improving SC performance, effective SC practices with efficient information sharing need to be integrated [6]. IT in SC management involves facilities that enhance the processing of complex information, provide real-time SC information, and improve interfirm coordination [40]. Therefore, with the improvement of IT, companies create greater information systems to facilitate information integration [41].

The literature on the use of IT in the organization proposes that the capability of information integration in the SC can transform the value of IT into performance [42]. Previous literature indicates a positive impact of the use of IT on SC performance [43]. Conversely, different studies have discovered no or even negative correlation between IT and performance [44]. A plausible explanation is that IT resources are neither uncommon nor hard to imitate and, as such, are not likely to be connected directly with high level of operational performance [2]. SC integration is necessary to simplify processes [45]. The utilization of internet will simplify these processes and benefits organizations through smoother information flows [46]. The impact of IT on performance relies on other mechanisms of the SC, such as information integration [47]. However, IT use reinforces the information integration in the SC by more proficient procedures and therefore can decrease lead time [48]. The above arguments lead to the second hypothesis.

H2: IT has a positive effect on SCII.

## 2.3.3. Information Security

In this research, information security refers to secure usage, and the integration of information through structured and standardized processes. Sufficient information security is important for organizations to protect its strategic proprietary information [49]. From a SC perspective, information integration and security are two main challenges [10,50]. Information security plays an important role in information exchange [25]. Therefore, many organizations give considerable attention to SC security and incline to extend their security measures [51]. However, previous studies indicated that security is a challenge for information integration [52]. Qin and Fan [25] further indicate that information security significantly affected information sharing. Hence, secure collaboration between the SC partners will increase information integration. As organizations depend on and secure their important resources to create competitive advantage, they are likely to exercise caution in integrating their resources with other partners [53]. However, unsecured information integration may lead to a unidirectional flow of information. The third hypothesis is devised below based on the abovementioned arguments.

H3: Information security has a positive effect on SCII.

## 2.3.4. Supply Chain Information Integration (SCII)

In this study, SC integration refers to sharing information across SC through electronic connectivity. Many previous studies have generally indicated that having close and integrated SC collaborations is a way to achieve better operational performance [54–58]. However, the literature shows that the relationship between SC integration and operational performance are not always consistent and depends on the configuration of interconnected elements in the organizations [59].

SCII is helpful to the organizations by reducing the task uncertainty and idle time in almost all phases of SC activities [5]. Functions, resources and capabilities can be leveraged by internal integration to design products, reduce duplicated tasks, and ensure product quality [60]. Information might be shared at all pertinent levels, for example, in planning, forecasting, and execution or replenishment [61]. Some studies emphasize substantial advantages such as diminished inventory levels [62], lead time [63], and competitive advantages between partners [64], while others emphasize the limitations of accrued risks and costs of sharing information between SC partners [65,66]. The literature demonstrates that the effectiveness of SCs are significantly influenced by the information integration among SC members and within the organization [64].

An empirical study has indicated that SC integration in which partners share relevant and accurate information with each other is more effective than a collaboration that does not have this characteristic [66]. At the point when an organization receives information regarding its customers’ or suppliers’ production forecasts and plans, for instance, it can enhance its own planning. This leads in lower inventories, followed by lower inventory costs, lower transportation costs, and consequently improved operational performance. Therefore, improvement in the performance and changes in the SC operation may result from information integration [67]. Operational performance is an outcome construct for this research for two reasons. First, the literature indicates that SCII is the main enabler of operational performance [68,69]. Second, OP is a measurable variable, which could be impacted by the level of SC integration [59]. Moreover, there is little uncertainty that operational performance is an essential and vital element of numerous performance measurement models identified in the present literature [70]. Therefore, the fourth hypothesis of this study is proposed to investigate the relationship between SCII and OP.

H4: SCII has a positive effect on operational performance.

## 2.3.5. Information Leakage

Sharing information is in most cases an expected and favorable conduct for an organization to obtain some strategic objectives (e.g., improved organizational performance (OP)) through collaboration with trading partners. While information integration includes many potential advantages for an organization’s SC activities, information may likewise flow beyond organizational boundaries in an undesirable, uncontrollable, and even destructive way. This phenomenon is referred to as information leakage. Information leakage is thus undesirable behavior, which may happen accidentally or intentionally to an unauthorized party.

Accidental information leaks occur unintentionally because of employees’ negligence about what information they should or should not share with partners [71]. Moreover, Jiang et al., [72] argued that because of a lack of organizational control on the collaborative environment, employees may disclose more than what is necessary to the employees from the partner organizations during meetings, exhibitions, professional fairs, or other formal or informal events.

On the other hand, IIL occurs when an employee intentionally exposes the firm’s confidential information to the unauthorized parties. IIL could be more harmful to the organizations. Because in this type of leakage, more critical and confidential information will be targeted, and the information will be on the hand of the unauthorized parties who have the skills and capabilities of using it against the owner of the information. IIL could be associated with employees dissatisfaction with the organization with regard to the lack of trust, organizational barriers, politics [73,74], individual advantage through achieving “master status” among colleagues, and friends [75], and the inadvertence of protection responsibility due to overenthusiasm regarding a new innovation or idea [76]. Therefore, if business-critical information leaks to the collaborative partners, they may use it to their benefit and at the cost of the information owner. Therefore, when an organization’s information, which is inimitable, rare, and non-substitutable is leaked, recovery from such leakage can be extremely challenging. With a higher level of information integration among organizations, the amount of organization sensitive information streaming across SCs increases, which consequently would bring about the higher risk of information leakage [12]. However, information leakage is considered as a type of service failure, which can affect the firms’ relationship with their clients and increase their operational and relational cost [17].

Therefore, leaking business-critical information while sharing with other SC partners may have a negative impact on operational performance. As most previous studies merely investigated the direct effects of SCII on performance, without examining the moderating effect of accidental and IIL, the understanding obtained is incomplete. In view of the above arguments, information leakage through employees may place the firms in an unfavorable situation of losing the benefits of information integration and consequently lower operationa performance. Therefore, we propose the fifth and sixth hypotheses below.

H5: The positive effect of SCII on operational performance will be stronger when accidental information leakage is low.

H6: The positive effect of SCII on operational performance will be stronger when IIL is low.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Sampling

To test the hypotheses, we conducted an online questionnaire survey for Malaysian manufacturing companies. The list of companies was drawn from the Federation of Malaysian Manufacturers directory, 2018. Target respondents for the survey were SC managers or general managers who were knowledgeable in answering the questions on SC integration and performance. This study focuses on the manufacturing industry as it is one of the major victims of information leakage [77]. This study used nonprobability sampling by employing the purposive sampling approach. In this research, manufacturing companies were handpicked based on two criteria. First, those manufacturing companies who have a SC department were selected so that results will be more accurate when the SC manager answers the questionnaire. Second, only medium and large companies were sampled because the more resources that the larger companies may have will result in a greater level of SCII and performance as compared to small companies [78]. Hence, the criteria have been filtered by using two screening questions in the questionnaire.

A prior power analysis [79] is suggested as an efficient technique for controlling factual power before conducting a study [80]. Therefore, this study employed the G\*Power program to conduct power analyses specific to model setups. As the model had a maximum of five predictors, this study needed 138 respondents to test the proposition that the path coefficients we report represent significant effects. However, we set out to collect data slightly larger than the required number. The size of manufacturing companies measured using the SME Corporation Malaysia guideline.

Before conducting the survey, the questionnaire was pretested with 10 industry practitioners who were experts in SC management. The justification behind the pretesting was to ensure that the questionnaire items were clearly understandable for the potential respondents. After pretesting, minor changes were applied to the questionnaires to get more clarity. The final version of questionnaires together with the cover letter was sent by e-mail to the SC managers asking for their participation in the study. Out of 400 questionnaires emailed to the manufacturing companies, 144 usable responds were received within three months for an effective response rate of 36%. Such a response rate could be attributed to our short survey questionnaire, follow up calls, and emails.

## 3.2. Non-response bias and common method bias

There is no selection error, when the entire sample frame responds to a survey. However, it is usually impossible to have a census in a research context. Therefore, it is suggested to have a high response rate (above 20%) [81] and an estimation of nonresponse bias [82]. There are two types of companies in the population of this study: medium and large. Hence, the nonresponse bias was evaluated by comparing the company’s size. A chi-square test $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 1 3 )$ showed no significant difference between medium and large companies, proposing that the sample was not biased toward companies’ size.

Besides, Harman’s single factor score was used to find out the existence of common method bias (CMB) [83,84]. By constraining (unrotated) factor extraction to one factor, the CMB is said to be present if the variance accounted for by a single factor is higher than 50% [84,85]. In this study, the results showed a single factor solution accounted for only 26.04% of the total variance, which suggests that CMB was not a concern. The unmeasured latent marker variable approach [86] was also used to test the $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ changes. After adding marker variable to the endogenous variables, $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ changes were less than 3%; therefore, there is no CMB issue.

## 3.3. Measures

In this study, all constructs contain a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) except SC performance, which has a five-point Likert scale. A different Likert scale is suggested by Podsakoff et al., [83] to reduce CMB. Based on a broad literature review, the measurement items of seven variables for information quality, IT, information security, SCII, and operational performance, as well as items for accidental and IIL, were adapted from the previous studies summarized in Appendix A. The measurement items for information quality were adapted from the instrument developed by Li and Lin [87]. The measurement items for IT were adapted from the instrument developed by Prajogo and Olhager [40], while the measurement items for information security were adapted from the instrument developed by Qin and Fan [25]. Items for SCII were adapted from the instrument developed by Wong and Bernroider [5]. Operational performance measured with the items were adopted from Yu and Huo [58]. Finally, the measurement items for accidental and IIL were adapted from Ritala et al. [76]. For a better understanding of the respondents, we provided them the definition of the business-critical information as the type of information that significantly impacts the company’s operational performance. Then we used the term businesscritical information in the items used for measuring both accidental and IIL.

## 3.4. The ethics of data collection

Several ethical issues were considered while collecting data. First, we assured the respondent that the information provided by them would be strictly confidential. To secure the respondent's identification, the information that could lead to identification were not being asked in the survey. Therefore, respondents’ privacy is one of the primary responsibilities of this study. Second, to avoid any misunderstanding, we explained the purpose of the study briefly on the cover page of the questionnaire. Third, if someone was reluctant to answer the questionnaire, no force was exerted and the individual’s decision was respected. We also selected secure storage and considered security measures for online data collection and storage. Hence, we believed that respondents were truthful and honest in their responses.

## 4. Data analyses and results

## 4.1. Data analysis

To test whether a specified set of variables impact the responses in a predicted way, confirmatory factor analysis was applied by using structural equation modelling with partial least squares (PLS-SEM) version 3.2.7 [88]. The path coefficients and other model parameters were evaluated by the procedures of the PLS-SEM algorithm in such a way that enlarges the explained variance of the dependent construct(s) [89]. To determine individual item reliability, the items were analyzed. Chin [90] suggested that standardized loadings need to be higher than 0.707. The average variance extracted (AVE) is a summary indicator of the convergent validity of constructs. AVE, for all constructs, needs to be higher than 0.50. An AVE value of 0.50 or higher indicates that latent constructs can account for at least half of the variance in items, and the measurement scale has sufficient convergent validity [89]. Henseler et al., [91] suggested the hetrotriat-monotriat ratio of correlations (HTMT) as a new method to deal with assessing discriminant validity in variance-based SEM. The HTMT is an estimate for the factor correlation and should be significantly smaller than one [92]. If the HTMT value is lower than

The second important item for operationa performance is SCII1 (0.032) (Our organization exchanges information with our trading partners electronically) followed by SCII3 (0.031) (Electronic information shared between our organization and trading partners is accurate), SCII5 (0.031) (Electronic information shared between our organization and trading partners is standardized), and SCII4 (0.030) (Electronic information shared between our organization and trading partners is timely).

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Relationship between information quality and SCII

The findings of this research indicate that IQ has a positive impact on SCII, with a small effect size (f<sup>2</sup>= 0.038). This result is consistent with previous literature on operations and production management [16,95]. By definition, IQ covers five quality dimensions—timeliness, accuracy, completeness, adequacy, and reliability. Our findings reveal that accurate information is vital for building up the credibility of organizations and strengthening their professional image. This is because incomplete information can delay or stop the processing of SC transactions, which in turn can cause mistrust among the trading partners.

The findings of existing studies highlight the fact that the image and credibility of organizations can be greatly affected by the presence of high-quality information. If the provided information is sufficient, the supervision of SC management services is heavily facilitated. This, in turn, throws a positive light on the value of the institution. Among the factors that primarily affect the value of institutions, timeliness comes first [87]. The other four influencing factors are accuracy, completeness, adequacy, and reliability in order of importance. It should be noted that the attributes of IQ follow this same order of significance in terms of their strategic benefits. Consequently, the results of this research indicate that as SC partners experience a higher level of information quality, information integration is improved. Therefore, information integration is perceived to be effective when the information is high quality, readily accessible, accurate, and relevant.

## 5.2. Relationship between information technology and SCII

The impact of IT on performance relies on other mechanisms of the SC, such as information integration [47]. Various studies in the existing literature suggest that the use of IT is a key enabler for SCII [47,96]. Thus, we have formulated the second hypothesis, suggesting the positive association between IT and SCII. IT is the heart of every SC integration system. IT could help SCs on enabling interoperability among the partners for the information to be accessible across the SCs.

The findings indicate a positive and significant influence with a medium effect size $( \mathbf { f } ^ { 2 } =$ 0.183). IT is crucial to various aspects of supply chain management, such as communication with partners, providing real-time information including delivery status, production planning, scheduling, and the level of inventory. Before introducing internet, inventory visibility and real-time demand information were almost impossible. Therefore, IT permits solid SCII for order scheduling, demand forecasting, inventory planning, and customer relationship management. In recent years, IT has also been assisting managers to forecast production and operation, which also includes intra- and interfirm coordination.

## 5.3. Relationship between information security and SCII

Sufficient information security is important for organizations to protect their strategic proprietary information [49]. The SC environment is different from the overall environment within any organization. For interconnection and networking among partners and traders, there is always a flow of information among them at a certain level. To analyze this issue, we have formulated the third hypothesis, which suggests that a higher level of information security leads to a higher level of SCII. The result of the analysis shows that there is a significant impact of information security on SCII, with a large effect (f<sup>2</sup>= 0.384).

As observed by Safa et al. [97], the issue of information security is not merely technical; it is a discipline that has several dimensions, ranging from social and cultural, technological, educational, managerial, human, and psychological to law and enforcement. Not only must organizations recognize any possible risk in their SCs, employees must also act in accordance with their organizations’ policies and procedures pertaining to information security. The chances of violation of information security are greatly reduced if employees are aware of the issue of information security and practice regular control and surveillance.

## 5.4. Relationship between SCII and organizational performance

Hypothesis four focuses on the examination of the relationship between SCII and OP. The result is supported with a small effect size $( \mathrm { f } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { = } ~ . 0 3 6 )$ . This finding is consistent with previous empirical surveys by Van der Vaart and Sheu et al. [98,99]. The hypothesis suggests that the integration of information in the SC acts to facilitate the performance of the organization. However, it should be noted that such information integration is based on the elemental ideas of firm-wide electronically aided information-sharing setup. Such a setup would support the intra-functional exchange of information with adequate accuracy, timeliness, and standardization. Therefore, SC management is enhanced when there is a dynamic relationship between the partners and information integration across the whole SC. SC integration could reduce the logistics costs, which would eventually result in increased profitability for the organizations and enhance the flexibility of the firms to react to the market changes. However, SCII needs proactive collaboration among cross-functional departments, buyers, and suppliers, which are connected and coordinated by the flow of information.

## 5.5. Moderation effects of accidental information leakage and intentional information leakage

As the level of information integration rises in organizations, so does the risk of information leakage. This is because a much greater volume of sensitive information is flowing across the SC, thus increasing the chance of a leak [12]. Such a leak in information is no less than a service failure and may have adverse consequences on customer relationships, as well as operational and relational costs [17]. Hypotheses five and six are formulated based on these considerations. Regarding the moderation effect of information leakage on the relationship between SCII and OP, H5 is not supported, whereas H6 is supported.

Apart from accidental leaks, information may also be leaked maliciously by disgruntled employees. As such leaks may damage the organization irreparably, the management should keep in mind the emotional nature of employees, who are after all human and may discount ethical and legal restrictions when it comes to carrying out a grudge. Another form of intentional leakage is when an employee, out of excessive zeal, takes his own decisions with regard to sharing information and ends up with leakage. Whether out of disloyalty or negligence, intentional leakage can be argued to have a negative moderation effect on the relationship between SCII and OP. In other words, organizations that face the issue of intentional leakage of critical information are less likely to benefit from external information sharing than that of organizations that are protected from this problem.

## 6. Study Contributions

## 6.1 Theoretical contribution

##  Enhancing SCII research through the use of resource orchestration theory

Table 1 shows that single theory or a combination of some theories could determine the range of the SC integration. The organizational information processing theory, RBV, transaction cost theory, resource dependency theory, and agency theory are some of the theories that dominate the SC integration literature, either individually or in combination with other theories. The results reported in Table 1 further indicate that RBV is the most dominated SC integration theory. RBV analyses the SC uniquely and examines the activities along the SC collectively and individually [100]. Every activity along the SC needs specific resources and capabilities to perform the task and contribute to creating a competitive advantage.

Nevertheless, it is substantial, and more useful to integrate the existing resources across the SC and leverage them adequately, to create a competitive advantage. In this way, manufacturing companies can realize greater operational performance with the assistance of their SC partners. The capabilities given by each partner along the SC can be integrated in such a way that the focal firm can gain greater and better operational performance. However, the orchestration theory argues that orchestrating capabilities along the SC, such as leveraging the aggregate capabilities, generates not only synergistic and stronger outcomes, but also produces causal ambiguity that creates barriers to entry for competitors to follow [13]. Consequently, leveraging strategy and collective capability could lead to sustainable higher operational performance and higher value for the customers. Hence, without managerial orchestration, resources may not simply lead to excellent operational performance. Therefore, we use the resource orchestration theory to address this limitation of the RBV. As a result, although RBV is a dominated paradigm in the SCII, however, this study suggests that the resource orchestration theory is a more suitable theory for conducting research on SCII.

##  Providing a comprehensive study of SCII by taking into account the effect of information leakage

This research is the first empirical study that explicitly investigates the relationship between SCII and operational performance considering the moderating effect of accidental and IIL. Prior studies in this field used conceptual and qualitative methods to investigate the role of information leakage and the risk associated with information sharing in the SC [12,101,102]. Therefore, the results of this study are important because many of the existing SCII studies do not consider the moderating impact of information leakage. Moreover, this research split information leakage into accidental and intentional, whereas previous studies investigated information leakage without considering accidental or intentional leakage.

Furthermore, although the resource orchestration theory has been used in prior studies to test the relationship between the supply chain integration and firm performance [13,103], the studies did not consider the moderating effect of information leakage on this relationship. We thereby contribute to the resource orchestration theory literature by conducting an empirical research to test the moderating role of accidental and IIL on the relationship between SCII and operational performance.

In addition, by highlighting the moderating effects of accidental and IIL, this study findings indicate that SCII enhances operational performance when IIL is low. This finding is important because many of the existing SC integration literature did not consider the moderating role of IIL on the relationship between SCII and OP. Therefore, the finding of this research suggests that testing the impact of SCII on OP without considering the moderating effect of IIL is incomplete and even dubious.

## 6.2 Practical contribution

The relationship between SCII, information leakage, and operational performance is a relevant topic not only in information and operation management but also in industry practice. This study is the first study that investigates SCII in Malaysian medium and large manufacturing companies. It excludes small manufacturing companies because of their low level of integration [78]. It focuses on manufacturing companies, as this industry is the second highest affected industry in terms of information leakage in 2018 [110]. Therefore, the results of this research offer useful contributions to this industry.

Specifically, this research has four major practical implications. First, this research provides insights into difficulties faced when implementing SCII, particularly by medium and large manufacturing companies in Malaysia. It offers understandings into strategies that the management may want to follow in its effort to improve operational performance by SCII. However, the result of this research demonstrates that the harmful impact of information leakage may outbalance the advantages of information integration in the SC.

Second, this research shows that the loss associated with IIL may weaken the effect of SCII on the operational performance. Firms and their executives that reciprocally share information with external partners for their own operational performance must be aware of the risk associated with IIL and the harmful effect of such leakage. The risk of information leakage to outside the firm’s boundaries increases when the firms are engaging with information integration. Therefore, the more information exchanged externally, the more important it is to pay attention to the leakage problems. Consequently, the result of this study is applicable for firm’s SC managers as they are the ones commonly responsible for collaboration with external SC partners and for employees who are actively involved in external collaboration on behalf of their organizations.

Third, the results of this research have a significant implication to those organizations that do not have sufficient experience in collaborating with external partners. Organizations can handle these matters by securing and postulating prescribed outlines within the organization, aggregate responsiveness of employees, conduct training to increase the awareness of employees about information leakage, tightening and determining the authoritative structure in collaborations and implement a strict data encryption policy. These steps from the organization could reduce the threat of information leakage.

Fourth, based on IMPA results (Figure 3), information security is the most important factor that influences SCII. Therefore, the results of this study suggest that to achieve a higher level of information security, manufacturing companies are suggested to have a standardized and structured information sharing process to guarantee the security of information flow. For this purpose, manufacturing companies may employ ANSIISA-95 standard. This standard can create a structure on how information can be used and modeled for all participants in a production process [104]. However, to define the structure of the shared information, manufacturing companies may need business-to-manufacturing markup language to implement the ISA-95 standard.

Moreover, companies need to understand clearly the source and use of shared information in their operational processes to safeguard the information flow. They need to ensure the information security of their partners in the SC. Figure 4 shows that SCII2 is the most important item that influences operational performance. Therefore, manufacturing companies should work with their trading partners electronically on cross-organizational business activities to have a higher operational performance. Manufacturing companies should take note that sharing information with their trading partners through an electronic linkage (SCII1) is the second important item to have a higher operational performance. For this purpose, a modernized business-to-business information exchange solution is required to perform information exchange activities in real time that benefits the manufacturing industry. In addition, manufacturing companies may consider using blockchain technology, Internet-of-Things (IoT), and radio-frequency identification (RFID) for a better information integration across the SC. Although blockchain technology is currently not widely implemented within SCs, however, those manufacturing companies looking to enhance end-to-end visibility should consider this technology. IoT could empower SCII and provide managers a window into

customer needs by gathering information from a large number of devices, including interior organization sensors and associated products in the possession of end users. RFID could help manufacturing companies to increase the SC transparency.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper aims to examine the antecedents of SCII, its impact on OP, and the moderating effect of information leakage between SCII and OP. Toward that goal, resource orchestration theory has been deployed as a theoretical lens to develop a research model and propose six hypotheses. The results partially support the hypotheses. It is observed that the IQ, IT, and information security strongly influence the SCII, which in turn impacts OP. Accidental information leakage has no moderation effect on the relationship between SCII and OP, whereas IIL significantly and negatively moderates the relationship. The results of the research reveal a new comprehension of the proactive connection between SCII, OP, and information leakage, indicating that there are underlying complexities that should be considered.

Limitations are an inseparable part of any research. Therefore, the present research has its own limitations. First, the results of this study may be limited to Malaysian manufacturing companies, in that the information sharing, IT, and information security culture in Malaysia may be dissimilar from that of other nations. Therefore, future researchers may consider different industries such as the service industry or high-tech companies in other countries.

Second, it should be noted that the present research framework might be affected by contextual factors, for example, industries type, companies’ size, an organizations’ situation in the SC, the length of the SC, and the type of SC, which are excluded in this research. Therefore, future studies may consider these contextual factors to further enhance the findings of our study.

Third, different countries may have different legal systems. The legal system may affect employees’ responsibilities in terms of information sharing and minimize the risk of IIL. Therefore, the results of this study may be different from other countries with a different legal system.

Fourth, this study did not cover any integration barriers. Hence, future studies could enhance our study findings by focusing on some of the integration barriers such as trust, cost, risk, bureaucracy, and culture.
