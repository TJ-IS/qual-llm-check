# The linkage between reporting quality and performance in IS projects<sup>§</sup>

Ronald L. Thompson <sup>a,\*</sup>, H. Jeff Smith <sup>b</sup>, Charalambos L. Iacovou

<sup>a</sup> Babcock Graduate School of Management, Wake Forest University, Winston-Salem, NC 27109, USA <sup>b</sup> Richard T. Farmer School of Business, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA

Received 3 November 2005; received in revised form 9 September 2006; accepted 29 December 2006 Available online 12 January 2007

## Abstract

Recent research has suggested that flawed status reporting is a serious concern in IS projects. However, the linkage between reporting quality and project performance has not been empirically confirmed. Our investigation consisted of two complementary studies, both of which employed previously validated measures of reporting quality and project outcomes. The first considered the perceptions of 210 IS project members of the quality of status reports they submitted to their project managers. The second considered the perceptions of 485 IS project managers of the quality of project reports they received from project members reporting to them. Both showed that the perceived quality of project reporting was less than perfect and was significantly associated with project task and psychological outcomes. Moreover, the second study results suggested that reporting quality was also related to organizational outcomes. We offer recommendations for project managers.

Keywords: Information systems development; Project management; Status reporting; Project outcomes; Communication quality

## 1. Introduction

The quality of status reporting of IS projects has been a topic of concern. Case studies [25], experiments [30], surveys of IS auditors, and simulations [31] have discussed status misreporting. When bearing bad news [21], communicating with overcommitted executives [17], or furthering their own interests [12], individuals reporting on the progress of project related activities may provide overly optimistic status information [26].

Although grounded in different methodologies, all these studies have two important characteristics: (1) they have not confronted this problem in a large-scale, rigorous empirical survey of status reporters and project managers, and (2) virtually all of the investigations sought to understand the factors that lead project reporters to engage in misreporting behavior assuming that inaccurate reporting had a significant, negative impact on project performance. Some researchers [29] provided theoretical arguments supporting such an impact, but there is little empirical evidence to prove it. Thus it is clear that the full linkage between reporting quality and project performance has still to be empirically confirmed [33].

To address these gaps in the literature, we conducted an empirical investigation, involving two surveys. Our primary goal was to address the question:

Is reporting quality associated with project outcomes?

## 2. Literature review—project status reporting

Project reporting involves the communication of information about the status of the project to a manager. The status-related material may be in either verbal or written form, and the reporters usually compare the state of the project versus the expectations in the project plan, and identify issues that may cause problems in its completion.

While IS project reporting usually represents a sequential chain of communication up the organization, our work focuses only on the ‘‘team member to project manager’’ communication link. We focused on this because of the critical role it plays in monitoring and managing the project: team members are normally the source of key status information, while project managers tend to be the key decision-makers in running day-to-day project activities. Reporting quality refers to the degree that the IS status reports give a complete and accurate representation of the project’s state.

The generation and transmission of reliable status updates can be a challenging task for three reasons:

\- IS development is a social endeavor that involves developing intangible assets and interacting with specialists. Thus assessing the status of the project is difficult and involves subjective judgment.

\- Inherent cognitive and processing limitations make it virtually impossible for reporters to obtain and transmit a complete and accurate picture of a project [4].

\- Human biases and social pressures exert an influence on the accuracy of project reports: deliberate distortion of status information by reporters can occur by alteration or withholding of relevant information.

Given the complicated nature of IS projects [35] and reporter errors and biases, it is not surprising that status reporting is imperfect.

## 2.1. Reporting quality and project performance— hypotheses

As IS projects are undertakings that aim to develop a technological system for an organization, assessing their performance can be complex. From a technological perspective, one would expect that a successful project would produce usable systems in an efficient manner; from a social perspective, the process should result in satisfied project members; and from an organizational perspective, they should result in net benefits to the firm. Given their multifaceted nature, a comprehensive evaluation of an IS project should therefore include assessment of three key components: task, psychological and organizational outcomes.

Task outcomes refer to the capability of a project to produce high quality deliverables in an efficient way [15]. From an information processing perspective [13], accurate information must be provided to decisionmakers by those specialists who perform the sub-tasks. By receiving this information, decision-makers can integrate the information and make any necessary adjustments to align resource allocations, schedules and priorities to the task. This, in turn, should result in enhanced task efficiency and higher-quality outputs.

As uncertainty is a prominent trait of most IS projects [8], the information processing perspective is especially relevant to status reporting. Indeed, researchers of IS project communications have asserted repeatedly that misreporting is likely to have a negative impact on project task outcome, especially efficiency in terms of budget and schedule overruns [16]. Despite extensive theoretical arguments, empirical evidence for this linkage has been sparse. In their work, Keil et al. [20] found that information asymmetry was often observed in projects that experienced escalation.

We therefore anticipated that accurate, timely reporting by project team members was likely to enhance task outcomes. Project managers who received reliable status reports from their team would be better equipped to allocate resources efficiently and initiate appropriate corrective actions as needed. Accurate status updates would allow project managers to organize their project more effectively, and this should be self-reinforcing; projects that enjoyed high quality communications would tend to be well-managed and organized and thus be less likely to fail [18]. Misreporting, on the other hand, would be likely to lead to misunderstanding, mismanagement, overruns, and delays. Such a task environment could encourage misreporting, since the communication of bad news is uncomfortable or worrisome to the reporter. Given the bidirectional impact between reporting quality and project efficiency and effectiveness, we thus proposed that:

H1. Reporting quality will be positively related to project task outcomes.

Psychological outcomes involve the level of team members’ satisfaction with their work. When project managers receive more complete and accurate information about the status of a project, they are more likely to manage the project team effectively. Moreover, better informed managers are more likely to engage in evenhanded decisions. Without accurate information, the behavior of project managers is likely to affect the morale of the project team negatively. Good, open communication should therefore lead to equitable resource allocation, improved interactions and higher team performance, creating better relationships and a more comfortable team environment [10] that is less conducive to intentional misreporting [9]. Inaccurate reporting, on the other hand, can lead to poor judgment, misallocation of resources, and the development of a team culture in which each member primarily looks after his or her own interests [34]. Thus, we expected that:

H2. Reporting quality will be positively related to project psychological outcomes.

Organizational outcomes are the business values that the operational systems add to the organization. We proposed that more precise status reporting was likely to lead to the development of better systems. Clear signals from the system users about the delivered system (their level of involvement, feeling about the quality of intermediate deliverables, and their early feedback during testing) provide vital cues to project managers, who can use this information to guide the team and to make any changes needed to help produce a usable system with strong acceptance. Lacking such important information, project managers may not be aware of key issues, and the eventual acceptance and use of the system may be jeopardized. We anticipated that:

H3. Reporting quality will be positively related to project organizational outcomes.

## 3. Research methodology

To examine the hypotheses, we conducted two surveys. In Study 1, project team members were asked to provide information about their reporting as report senders. In Study 2, we used a complementary perspective: project managers were asked to provide an assessment of the reports that they received from their teams as report receivers. In both studies, the reporting quality measures were correlated with the assessment of project outcomes.

## 3.1. Measures

We utilized previously developed and validated measures to assess the constructs. The same measures were used in both studies: we used Mohr et al.’s communication quality scale [22], which measured the effect of communication timeliness, accuracy, adequacy, completeness and credibility. To evaluate project performance, we used an 11-item, multidimensional scale developed and empirically validated by Aladwani [1]. To avoid the possibility of artificially high correlations resulting from items being placed closely together on a questionnaire [19], we separated the items and mixed them with other construct measures (not used in this study). The questions from Study 2 (along with descriptive statistics) are shown in Appendix A.

The survey instrument was pilot tested by ten individuals from the Study 1 population and by seven individuals from the Study 2 population. Based on their feedback, the wording of some of the questions was modified to reflect their professional environment. For example, the term ‘‘company’’ was replaced with ‘‘organization’’ to account for the fact that the Study 1 population included many employees from governmental agencies.

## 3.2. Respondents

## 3.2.1. Study 1

The sample for Study 1 consisted of people who were team members in state governmental projects in an Eastern US state. They included users, IS professionals, and vendor representatives. We adopted stratified sampling to select a diverse subset of projects based on several dimensions (project duration, budget, and vendor involvement) from a listing of on-going projects. Surveys were sent to all non-executive participants in each project. The cover letter that accompanied the survey was signed by the researchers, the state auditor, and an executive from the respondent’s organization. 500 surveys were mailed, and 21 were returned as undeliverable. Two reminders were emailed to each subject. Ultimately 264 participants responded, yielding a gross response rate of 55.1%. Of these, 54 cases were removed because of missing data.

We embraced an intentionally conservative algorithm in deciding which cases should be removed: a questionnaire was removed if the respondent did not complete two or more of the items for any construct. In addition, a case was removed if the respondent had not answered the single item for ‘‘organizational outcome.’’ This left 210 usable responses, with a net response rate of 43.8%. We believe that the endorsement of the survey by government executives contributed positively to the response rate. Approximately 43% of the respondents were IS professionals, 30% were business users, and the remainder was made up of vendor representatives or consultants.

The respondents were instructed to answer the questions within the context of a specific IS project (the project on which the participant was currently spending most of his or her time). One hundred and forty-four (68.5%) indicated the project for which they were answering was either in the implementation phase or had been just completed; 45 (21%) indicated that the project was in the design phase, and the remainder either specified the analysis phase or did not respond.

## 3.2.2. Study 2

The sample for the second survey consisted of project managers who were members of the Project Management Institute (PMI). Three thousand surveys were mailed to randomly selected PMI IS project managers in the Eastern United States, and 52 were returned as undeliverable. The cover letter was signed by the researchers and the PMI Research Director. One reminder was mailed to each subject. Five hundred ninety-nine responses were received (representing a response rate of 20.3%). Respondents were given the option of using online or paper surveys; we received 502 hardcopy responses, and 97 online ones. As with Study 1, we embraced an intentionally conservative algorithm in deciding which cases should be removed. A case was removed if the respondent did not complete two or more of the items for any construct. It was also removed if the respondent had not answered the single item for ‘‘organizational outcome.’’ Seventeen hardcopy cases were removed for one of these reasons. In addition, the 97 responses that had been submitted on-line were missing values for the ‘‘organizational outcome’’ question. It was later found that there had been an error in coding the online survey, and the responses had not been captured in our database. We decided to disqualify these 97 online cases, leaving a usable sample of 485, and a net response rate of 17.3%. A test of means for differences between the online and paper-based respondent groups showed no differences. Of the 16 measurement items, only one showed a difference in mean responses that was statistically significant at $p < 0 . 0 5$ , which is roughly what would be expected from chance alone. The response rate for the second study is comparable to that of prior studies focusing on sensitive IS issues; e.g., [32].

Three hundred and eleven of Study 2 participants (64.1%) indicated they were an employee of the organization that completed the project. One hundred and thirty-three (27.4%) said they were an employee of a third party (a vendor or consulting firm), and the remainder stated they were independent consultants. The majority (308 or 63.5%) reported having over ten years of project management experience. Seventy percent of the respondents were male.

In both studies, respondents were guaranteed anonymity in their responses. To assess the presence of non-response bias, we compared late responses (last 20% received) to early ones (the first 20% received) in each study. In Study 1, one item (out of the 16 that were included in our analysis) was found to be different across the sample segments (at the 0.05 level of significance), and none of the items in Study 2 were - this is less than one would expect by chance alone. ## 4. Results

We utilized the Partial Least Squares (PLS) technique: specifically, PLS-Graph Version 3.0 [5], to analyze the data. Unlike traditional statistical methods, it allows the simultaneous assessment of the measurement and the structural models and is considered especially useful in the early stages of theory testing, emphasizing model prediction rather than model fit. In addition, PLS is less restrictive than covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) techniques such as LISREL, AMOS, or EQS in terms of sample size and distributional requirements [6]. For example, these techniques generally require large sample sizes and their fit indices tend to over-reject models at sample sizes of 250 or less. Given that our study represented an initial attempt to explore the linkage between reporting quality and project performance, the sample size for one of our studies was below 250, and our primary emphasis was on model prediction rather than model fit, the use of PLS seemed appropriate.

## 4.1. Measurement model assessment

We conducted several tests of convergent and discriminant validity. For convergent validity, we assessed individual and construct reliability. All multi-item constructs were modeled as reflective, rather than formative, since the measurement items were viewed as being influenced or caused by the underlying latent variable. For item reliability, we examined the item-to-construct loadings (see Table 2) for all multiitem variables (i.e., all except organizational outcomes that utilized a single-item scale). As project outcome was a multidimensional construct, each of its dimensions was assessed separately.

In order for the shared variance between each item and its construct to exceed the error variance, standardized loadings of 0.707 or greater are needed; however, loadings of 0.6–0.7 are acceptable if the loadings of other items within the same construct are high. All items in Study 1 exhibited loadings over 0.7; in Study 2, two task outcomes items (T3 and T6)

had loadings below 0.7 (see Table 2). Given that the two task outcome items had loadings over 0.60 and the remaining items for this construct had strong loadings (over 0.74), this was not a major a concern. Thus, we concluded that all items exhibited adequate reliability.

To assess construct reliability, we considered two internal consistency indicators: composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE) scores. All composite reliability indicators for the multi-item measures in our surveys were higher than 0.85; internal consistency scores of 0.8 or higher provide exemplary evidence of reliability [3]. Fornell et al. [11] suggested that an AVE value of 0.5 is required to provide evidence of satisfactory construct reliability, and all of our scores met this standard (the lowest was 0.55 for Task in Study 2). Thus, we concluded that the reliability of our construct measures was more than adequate.

We conducted two tests to assess the discriminant validity of the measures. First, we calculated each item’s loading on its own construct and its crossloadings on all other constructs. As Table 2 shows, each item’s loading on its intended construct was higher than its cross-loading on other constructs. As a second part of this test, we also compared the lowest loading of the intended items for each construct to the cross-loadings of other items on it. The Study 2 data did reveal one minor challenge: item P1 cross-loaded at a slightly higher level on the task outcomes construct [T] than one of the Task items (T6).

## 4.2. Structural model assessment

Fig. 1 shows the effects for each hypothesized path, as well as the amount of variance explained $( R ^ { 2 } )$ for all endogenous factors in Study 1. In terms of explanatory power, our model indicated that 8% of the variance of task outcomes, 6% of the variance of psychological outcomes, and 1% of the organizational outcomes was explained by reporting quality. The small variance explained values suggested, not surprisingly, that other factors besides the quality of reporting influenced project performance.

The path coefficients were 0.29 for task outcomes, 0.25 for psychological outcomes, and 0.12 for organizational outcomes. In terms of practical significance, the effect of reporting quality on task and psychological outcomes appeared to be moderate, while the effect on organizational outcomes seemed to be weak [7]. To assess statistical significance, the bootstrapping method (using 500 resamples) was used. The first two paths (to task and psychological outcomes) were significant at the 0.01 level; the third one (to organizational outcomes) was significant at $p < 0 . 1 0 .$

In sum, the results suggested that the task and psychological dimensions of project performance had a significant association with reporting quality, providing strong support for hypotheses H1 and H2. Given that the evidence for H3 was marginal in terms of statistical significance (at $p < 0 . 1 0 )$ ) and its practical importance was very low $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 1 )$ ), we concluded that the results did not provide adequate support for the hypothesis.

While these findings are enlightening, it should be noted that there are two limitations in Study 1’s design—both of which were addressed by a slightly different design for Study 2: they relate to the measures of organizational outcomes and reporting accuracy. In Study 1 we asked respondents to estimate the probable organizational impact of the system before the completion of their project, while in Study 2 we were able to ask respondents to assess organizational outcomes based on the actual use of the system following its completion.

Thus, in Study 1 we had to use each respondent’s own assessment of the communicated project status as a proxy for the project report that was received by their project manager. While we believe that this proxy was an adequate measure (representing the average quality of the report received by the project manager factored across all the respondents in the sample), the respondents in Study 2 were able to provide a direct assessment of their teams’ reporting quality. In addition, Study 2 allowed us to examine the linkage between reporting accuracy and project outcomes from the perspective of report receivers.

In terms of explanatory power, our model indicated that 26%, 32% and 13% of the variance $( R ^ { 2 } )$ of the three project performance dimensions was explained by reporting quality in Study 2 (see Fig. 2). All relationships between reporting quality and outcomes (path coefficients) were strong in terms of magnitude. All three paths were statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , providing strong support for all hypotheses.

## 5. Discussion

As the empirical results showed, our first two propositions received considerable support in both studies. The third was supported in Study 2 but not in Study 1. Overall, these results indicated that the quality of status reporting on IS projects did have a significant relationship with performance, especially as it related to task and psychological outcomes. This link was confirmed by the perceptions of both report senders and receivers. Also our set of large-scale surveys provided an assessment of status reporters’ (in Study 1) and project managers’ (in Study 2) perceptions of report quality.

Although all of the mean responses to the ‘‘Quality of reporting’’ items (see Appendix A) exceeded the midpoint of the scale, they were significantly below 7 at a level of $p < 0 . 0 1$ . Thus, both reporters (in Study 1) and receivers (in Study 2) agreed that the reports are not completely accurate, timely, adequate, complete, or credible. Because the reporters and receivers were not paired in our study, it is inappropriate to compare their perceptions in a statistical sense.

In examining the relationship between status reporting quality and performance, the findings suggested that the linkages between reporting quality and task and psychological project outcomes were significant, while the association between reporting and organizational outcomes seemed to be less concrete, at least in Study 1. Obviously, the business impact of a system would be affected by many usage-related factors that were outside the scope of our model and may not have been clear to participants until long after the system’s acceptance into production mode.

Study 1’s sample was drawn from individuals working for or in a set of state government IS projects, whereas Study 2’s sample was drawn from a database of IS project managers in which the majority worked in for-profit firms. In many respects, the pressures associated with IS projects – to deliver on time, within budget, and with promised functionality – are similar in both domains, especially since the state in Study 1 was experiencing severe cost-cutting pressure at the time of the survey. However, it is possible that the profit motive of most Study 2 respondents’ organizations might cause additional pressures, and some of the checks-andbalances of the state government (a part of the Study 1 respondents’ lives) might not be observed in for-profit enterprises. Also the projects in Study 1 were in process at the time of the study, whereas those in Study 2 had been completed and it seems possible that completed projects were more conducive to accurate, comprehensive project outcome assessments.

## 5.1. Limitations

Our study was dependent on self-reported perceptions. Although they were subjective, they nevertheless shed light on the phenomenon under investigation. In addition, the use of a single item to measure organizational outcomes may account partly for the low variance explained for the factor in both studies. We took this approach because this item was part of a previously validated instrument.

Our sampling embraced a cross-sectional rather than longitudinal approach. While both of our studies focused on different project lifecycle stages (Study 1 for ongoing projects and Study 2 on completed ones), the lack of longitudinal data did not allow us to assess the dynamic nature of the bidirectional influence between reporting and project performance. Also, our cross-sectional approach did not allow us to explore possible causal relationships between the three performance dimensions.

In addition, while the sample for the second study was drawn from a cross-section of diverse organizations and industries, the sample for the first was selected from state governmental projects. This could limit the generalizability of the Study 1 findings; it is possible that the impact of reporting quality on organizational outcomes would be stronger in a for-profit environment. This is consistent with the findings of Study 2, which enjoys higher external validity due to the diverse nature of its sample. Finally, we used a single reporting source for each project in order to protect the anonymity of the respondents and to minimize their personal risk in participating. A paired sample design might have enhanced the validity of the findings.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our study revealed that both project managers and team members acknowledged that reporting about IS projects was less than perfect. This is troubling, as the need for effective reporting in IS projects is obviously important. Virtually all major IS project management approaches posit that the transmission of clear information is an essential element of effective IS project management [23,24]. Further, extensive empirical evidence supports the positive effects of communication in IS projects [2,14,27].

Project executives should establish communication environments that are likely to nurture open, complete reporting. To achieve this, managers should take steps to identify and minimize situations that are likely to lead to misreporting: troubled or escalated development initiatives should be noted and resolved or discouraged [28].

A periodic audit of a project’s communication environment could help managers identify potential reporting problems. Moreover, project executives who establish and maintain unguarded, trust-based communication channels with multiple sources in a project team are more likely to detect inaccurate reports and improve the likelihood of project success.

In addition to creating an environment in which highquality reporting is more likely, senior managers can also take steps to spot the occasional misreporting that does occur. One reasonable approach is to use multiple sources for status information – sometimes termed ‘‘triangulation’’ – and to make sure that the sources represent different project populations.

## 6. Conclusion

Our work offered evidence to support a linkage between reporting quality and three types of project outcomes in both the perceptions of project report senders and receivers. Our study also showed that the quality of the reports was often imperfect. Given the damaging effects of misreporting, we contend that there is a need to understand both the factors and the management practices that impact reporting quality.
