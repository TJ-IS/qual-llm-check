# Adoption of Open Source Software: The role of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation

Kholekile L. Gwebu, Jing Wang ⁎

Department of Decision Sciences, Whittemore School of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire, NH, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 21 January 2010 Received in revised form 15 December 2010 Accepted 19 December 2010 Available online 24 December 2010

Keywords: Open Source Software Social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Personal innovativeness Adoption decisions

## a b s t r a c t

While the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of incorporating Open Source Software (OSS) into personal and organizational systems have been widely touted, OSS must be adopted and used by end users before these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be realized. Drawing on research in information systems and sociology, this study develops and evaluates an integrated model for the acceptance of OSS. In addition to the traditional technology adoption variables the <sup>fi</sup>ndings stress the importance of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as a key driver of OSS adoption. The proposed model provides a useful decision support tool for assessing and proactively designing interventions targeted at successful OSS adoption and diffusion.

## 1. Introduction

According to a recent study by the International Data Corporation (IDC), the market for Open Source Software (OSS) will grow at an annual rate of 22.4% to reach \$8.1 billion by 2013 [37]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports Gartner's projections that by 2012, at least 80% of all commercial software solutions will include substantive open source components [25]. Despite the promising picture painted by these forecasts and the potential technological superiority of OSS applications i.e., high quality, security, reliability, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, low acquisition cost, and no vendor lock-ins [21,24], low acceptance rates continue to hinder their dominance in the marketplace [27,28]. Although the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of successful OSS incorporation can be enticing, they cannot be realized unless it is accepted and used by the intended mainstream software users. Further, OSS also has disadvantages including limited documentation and training support, overwhelmingly frequent releases of patches and software version upgrades, and constraints imposed by the various OSS license terms [26,28,49,50]. Thus, understanding and creating the conditions under which OSS applications will be used remains a real challenge and a high-priority for researchers and practitioners alike [27,28]. To this end, the current research aims to identify factors that facilitate the voluntary acceptance of OSS applications by mainstream users.

OSS applications certainly share many characteristics with other types of software, thus, factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as important in technology adoption and diffusion may play an equally important role in the adoption and use of OSS. Nevertheless, OSS applications also have certain key unique features. For instance, they are more than just computer programs; they are community-based innovations [23,59]. Hence, an important question is whether individuals would think and act differently in their decisions to adopt OSS. Since its inception the OSS culture has been shaped by a strong sense of community [43]. However, research investigating the link between the OSS community and individuals' adoption behavior is scarce. Under the theory of social identity [52,53], OSS can confer its users with a distinct collective identity which in turn creates a sense of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community. Empirical work from sociology and marketing demonstrates that social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation positively impacts member behavior, including product evaluation, adoption, purchasing, word-of-mouth marketing, and member participation and engagement [4,20]. Thus, we can develop a parallel argument that strong identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community may contribute favorably to individuals' decision to adopt OSS applications.

Given this, OSS adoption should be viewed as an instance of software acceptance within a setting that combines technology adoption with a social identity element. It therefore requires distinct theorizing that integrates literature from the <sup>fi</sup>elds of information systems (IS) and sociology. Consequently, this study draws from established bases of research in IS and sociology to develop an integrated model for the acceptance of OSS in a voluntary adoption setting. While IS research offers models of technology adoption, we expect the factors affecting OSS adoption decisions to be somewhat different due, in part, to the unique social identity OSS confers to its users. Issues of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and behavior are addressed in the sociology literature. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine how individuals' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community could in<sup>fl</sup>uence their beliefs related to OSS applications (usefulness and ease of use), and how identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and beliefs could together contribute to OSS adoption in a voluntary adoption setting.

This study has direct implications for research. Recently, <sup>fi</sup>rms have become increasingly interested in making signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investments in building and facilitating communities for innovation and new product development [23]. This continuing phenomenon of community based innovation underscores the importance of adapting the technology acceptance theories to this trend and understanding how to use individuals' relationships with the community as a lever for increasing adoption and usage. This research explores how individuals' social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community contributes to their adoption intention, representing one of the few efforts toward understanding technology acceptance and usage behavior in the context of community-based innovations. From a practical perspective, factors similar to those important in the adoption of OSS applications may be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the adoption and usage of other types of community-based innovations. Thus, understanding the adoption patterns of OSS could provide profound implications for organizations' community-based innovation initiatives.

This research should also be of practical importance to <sup>fi</sup>rms trying to push for cost saving through OSS adoption. The proposed model provides a useful decision support tool for <sup>fi</sup>rms needing to assess the likelihood of success for new OSS implementations. It also helps them to understand the drivers of voluntary OSS acceptance in order to proactively design interventions targeted at user groups that may be less inclined to adopt and use OSS. The OSS community and <sup>fi</sup>rms that seek to establish lucrative services using OSS could also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research and increase the market share of OSS applications in the software industry.

The paper begins with a brief review of the communal nature of OSS and the work on OSS acceptance. Next, we draw on the technology acceptance literature and the theory of social identity to develop an integrative model of OSS adoption. We then empirically evaluate the model using survey data. We conclude with a discussion of the results and their research and practical implications.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Why is OSS a community driven innovation?

OSS refers to any computer software whose source code is made publically available under OSS licensing formats. The idea of open source software development can be traced back to 1960s when scientists and researchers (also known as hackers) relied on free and openly shared software code for their work due to the unavailability of commercial software solutions [29,61]. In the 1980s when software vendors began to control source code, some hackers became offended by the loss of access to the source code, particularly to the code that they had been involved in developing. They were also distressed by a general trend towards the development of proprietary software released under licenses that prevented free access to the source code. To <sup>fi</sup>ght against closed source/proprietary software, a practice they deemed immoral, the Free Software Foundation (FSF) was created and various copyleft licensing was developed to preserve free access for all to the software developed by hackers [61]. The goal of these initiatives was to promote ideologies such as 1) information should be free and 2) making the source code of computer programs freely available is more valuable to users and the society because it enables them to adapt, learn from, or base new work on the source code. Despite its appeal, the free software idea remained non-mainstream and the software industry was especially weary of it. To enhance its acceptance, prominent hackers such as Bruce Perens and Eric Raymond founded the OSS movement in the 1990s, which incorporates similar licensing practices as those pioneered by the FSF, but deemphasizes ideological concepts with regard to morality [61]. Today, the OSS phenomenon has evolved into a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant force in the software landscape.

In addition to the free access to source code, another unique feature of OSS is its communal nature. Since its inception, the hacker culture, which later evolved into the OSS movement, has differed substantially from the proprietary software culture. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in extremely distributed environments enabled by the internet, individuals from geographically dispersed locations form various OSS user groups. Through these groups individuals collaborate and interact online (occasionally face-to-face), and a wide variety of OSS products and user support are developed and offered [29]. Further, OSS development is marked by shared ideologies and values. In addition to the ones mentioned above, other well-developed and repeatedly cited OSS ideologies and values include: 1) sharing, cooperating, and helping should be valued and 2) OSS development model should produce higher quality software than the closed source software development model [7,29]. Third, a strong sense of duty and obligation permeates many OSS development and user groups. This is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the reciprocal behavior widely observed in OSS groups and the member-funded promotion for various OSS projects [7]. These characteristics–the establishment of social relationship through interaction and cooperation, innovation by and for the users, shared ideologies and values, and the strong sense of duty and obligation–epitomize the fundamental aspects of a community and distinctively mark OSS as a community-based innovation [11].

## 2.2. Previous work on OSS adoption

While opinion leaders, professionals, and agencies alike opine that OSS is ready for mainstream adoption [21,25,37], the dilemma of its low acceptance rate and underutilization persists [27,28]. In general, the issue associated with OSS adoption has received little attention in the academic literature [19]. The few exceptions focus on OSS adoption at the organizational level and minimal work exists at the individual level [19,28]. However, OSS applications that are intended to be incorporated into organizational work processes are of little value and can eventually be discontinued unless the intended users accept and use them. Hence, it is imperative to go beyond examining the acceptance of OSS at the organizational level and understand OSS adoption from the individuals' perspective.

To our best knowledge, there exists only one other study which seeks to understand OSS adoption at the individual level. Using Davis' technology acceptance model (TAM) as a theoretical backdrop, Gallegoa et al., [24] propose and empirically examine a model of user acceptance towards OSS applications. Based on a survey of 347 Linux operation systems users, their <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use exert a strong positive impact on intention to use Linux, which subsequently predicts usage behavior. Perceived ease of use is also found to positively impact perceived usefulness. User perceptions of the technological characteristics (<sup>fl</sup>exibility, quality, capability) are found to positively impact perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use while no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship is found between social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and users' beliefs on ease of use and usefulness.

While the study by Gallegoa et al. [24] has made important and unique contributions, the understanding of user acceptance of OSS can be deepened through the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinants that are unique to OSS adoption and use. Although the strength of TAM lies in its parsimony, it may lack adequate speci<sup>fi</sup>city to explain and enunciate OSS adoption given that OSS has some notable differences compared to traditional proprietary software. For instance, OSS adoption is often a voluntary decision and is hence less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by subjective norm pressure [7]. This may explain why Gallegoa et al. [24] did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any relationship between social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, measured as subjective norm pressure, and users' beliefs on ease of use and usefulness. On the other hand, the strong community-oriented OSS culture highlighted in the previous section implies that the effect of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on OSS adoption is more likely to manifest in the form of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community. In the marketing and sociology literature, social identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation with a brand community or a social movement has been shown to in<sup>fl</sup>uence participation, word-of-mouth behavior, purchasing intention, and adoption behavior [4,48,55]. This implies that understanding individuals' OSS adoption decisions needs to be expanded from its current focus on technology acceptance variables (i.e., usefulness, ease of use, and subjective norm) to encompass the social in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the community.

## 3. Theoretical underpinning and hypotheses development

User technology acceptance has received extensive attention from IS researchers and practitioners and through a variety of theoretical perspectives. Of the models that have been proposed and examined, TAM, developed by Davis [18], is arguably the most parsimonious and widely accepted [54]. It is an intention-based model adapted from the Theory of Reasoned Action [3]. TAM suggests that two behavioral beliefs, perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU), determine an individual's behavioral intention to use a technology, which predicts subsequent behavior. TAM has been used as the theoretical basis for numerous empirical studies of user technology acceptance and the results suggest that it is robust across a broad range of technologies, user populations, and settings.

Although TAM seems to be a natural starting point, the communal nature of OSS could be an important boundary condition to the validity and applicability of TAM for understanding of Open Source Adoption. Research in various disciplines has recurrently documented the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of groups on member behavior [48,55,57]. Yet TAM excludes social in<sup>fl</sup>uence from its formulation and a fuller understanding of OSS adoption thus requires the incorporation of sociology theories that encompass the effect of the community.

The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the community on OSS adoption may manifest via the psychological pathway of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the community. This is because favorable conditions are pervasive in the OSS community for identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to occur. First, the OSS community has its distinctive values, ideologies, and software development practices. According to the Social Identity Theory (SIT), individual searching for identity tend to classify themselves and others into various social categories [52,53]. Thus, the OSS community's distinctiveness serves to increase the tendency of individuals to identify with the community. Further, with its quality <sup>fl</sup>agship products such as Linux and Apache and its widely publicized software development practice, i.e. “given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow [43]”, the OSS community is often associated with the prestige of unbreakable products. Based on SIT, this perceived prestige could further strengthen individuals' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community through the enhancement of their self-esteem [52,53]. The SIT literature also suggests that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is likely to be associated with strong awareness of out-group(s) as it reinforces the awareness of one's in-group [52,53]. The salience of out-group is evident in OSS members' strong sentiments against <sup>fi</sup>rms selling proprietary software. The strong awareness of the proprietary <sup>fi</sup>rms increases ingroup favoritism and leads to a stronger identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community. Finally, like-minded individuals with shared values and ideologies interact and cooperate online and through their collective efforts, they produce various OSS applications. According to the SIT literature, social interaction, similarity, and shared goals could all favorably affect the extent to which one identi<sup>fi</sup>es with the OSS community.

Taken together, the pervasiveness of the set of favorable conditions– community distinctiveness and prestige, out-group salience, social interaction, similarity, and shared goals–suggests that social identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community is likely to be prevalent. Given the importance of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in shaping individual behavior [48,55,57], it stands to reason that the understanding of OSS adoption can be deepened through the integration of TAM and SIT.

Finally, migrating towards OSS implies a steep learning curve and a shift away from something (proprietary software) that most end users have invested a substantial amount of time learning and have subsequently become relatively comfortable with. Thus, it is highly likely that only a subgroup of individuals, by their very nature, will readily accept OSS while others will resist. Among other individual propensity variables, personal innovativeness (PI) has been recurrently found in the technology acceptance literature to be an important concept for examining technology acceptance [1,45,63], and is hence likely to impact OSS adoption.

Given the above reasons, we draw upon the technology acceptance literature and SIT to propose an integrated model for the acceptance of OSS (see Fig. 1).The proposed model contends that behavioral intention is a key variable in determining future behavior and is a function of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, personal innovativeness, perceived usefulness and ease of use.

## 3.1. Perceived usefulness (PU)

PU is de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as the extent to which one believes that using an OSS application will enhance his or her job performance. There is strong empirical evidence supporting the relationship between PU and intention to adopt a technology [18,58]. In many ways, OSS applications have the potential to enhance end users' job performance over proprietary alternatives. The close to zero initial licensing fees for OSS have made a wide variety of quality software products affordable. The <sup>fl</sup>exibility of free access to OSS source code provides technically skilled users the opportunity to audit and/or customize the software to meet the speci<sup>fi</sup>c functional and security requirements of their jobs [33,42]. The improvement of OSS adopters' job performance could also stem from the availability of many reliable and high-quality OSS products. Nevertheless, OSS applications also have disadvantages. Many OSS applications are distributed under very restrictive license terms that limit users' ability to commercialize the software (i.e., copyleft provision) or to combine the software with other OSS applications distributed under less restrictive licenses (i.e., viral provision) [35]. Such restrictions could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce users perceived usefulness of OSS [49,50]. Thus, adopting OSS applications has become a volitional behavior that calls for a rational, deliberate assessment of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and disadvantages of the applications, as such the more the perceived usefulness outweighs the perceived disadvantages, the more likely that OSS applications will be adopted. Thus, consistent with the theoretical arguments underlying TAM, we anticipate a direct and positive impact of PU on the intention to adopt OSS applications.

H1. Perceived usefulness is positively related to the intention to adopt OSS applications.

## 3.2. Perceived ease of use (PEOU)

PEOU is de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as the extent to which a person believes that using an OSS application will be free of effort. There is extensive empirical evidence supporting that PEOU is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly linked to adoption intention, both directly and indirectly via its impact on PU [18]. Thus, consistent with TAM, we hypothesize that PEOU is a determinant of PU since ceteris paribus, the less effort required to use an OSS application, the more using it is likely to improve job performance. PEOU is also expected to act as a direct determinant of behavioral intention to adopt an OSS application. This is because many OSS applications are released at the pre-alpha, alpha or beta development stage and are packaged inconveniently [19,26,28]. Such applications tend to be unstable and lack an intuitive design [60]. Thus, using them could require a fair amount of cognitive effort. In the worst case scenario, OSS users need to have the technical know-how to download, compile, con<sup>fi</sup>gure, and/or install the software before use. As formal training and support are often lacking [19,26], OSS users have to learn to maneuver through interfaces and functionalities, troubleshoot when necessary, and follow the support and documentation materials by themselves. The frequent releases of patches and software versions could also overwhelm some users and make the maintenance of some OSS applications extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Given these challenges, the easier software users perceive using an OSS application, the more likely they will be to adopt the application.

## 3.3. Personal Innovativeness in Information Technology (PIIT)

Since OSS is an instance of information technology (IT), here we employ a domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c construct of PI developed by Agarwal & Prasad [1] known as personal innovativeness in information technology (PIIT). PIIT is expected to better predict individual behavior towards an information technology than PI, a global construct of individual innovativeness.

PIIT is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the willingness of an individual to try out any new IT [1]. While PIIT was initially theorized as a moderator of the effects of PU and PEOU on intention to use a new IT [1], consistent with more recent studies [36,63], we conceptualize and evaluate PIIT as a direct determinant of PU and PEOU and a direct determinant of intention to adopt OSS. According to the innovation diffusion theory, people demonstrate different characteristics and react differently to an innovation due to their differences in innovativeness. Early adopters with high innovativeness can more easily envision the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts associated with an innovation than later adopters [40,45], resulting in them being more likely to view OSS applications as useful. They tend to score high on technical competence and aspiration [40,45] and are more likely to be able to tolerate the risks of using OSS without considerable training, documentation, and/or support [28]. Hence, relative to those who adopt later, they should consider OSS applications as easier to use. More recently, many OSS applications have undergone a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant transformation to improve usability, maintainability, and ease of use [21]. Firms have emerged building lucrative businesses on top of successful OSS products, selling conveniently bundled and packaged OSS products together with professional support, training, consultancy, and certi<sup>fi</sup>cation services [21,62]. Individuals high on PIIT are more likely to be aware of this new development and are hence more likely to view OSS as easy to use. Further, earlier adopters are more cognizant of the image they project by adopting an innovation relatively earlier than others and are likely to demonstrate higher self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy about their ability to cope with uncertainty and control their future [40,45]. Image and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in turn will positively impact PU and PEOU respectively [36,58].

As a persistent and general predisposition in trying out and accepting new technologies and innovations, PIIT is also expected to have a direct effect on one's intention to adopt OSS applications. This conjecture is consistent with attitude theories that assert that over and above attitudes, behavior is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by habits [56]. Thus, individuals high on PIIT will be more habituated to using OSS applications, be more skilled at using them and be better at realizing their usefulness. Yi et al. [63] found that the effect of PIIT on adoption intention was only partially mediated by PU and PEOU and PIIT exerted a strong and direct impact on adoption intention. Hence, on the basis of previous theories and empirical work, we hypothesize the following:

${ \bf H 3 _ { a } . }$ PIIT is positively related to the perceived usefulness of OSS applications.

$\mathbf { H 3 _ { b } . }$ PIIT is positively related to the perceived ease of use of OSS applications.

$\mathbf { H 3 _ { c } } .$ PIIT is positively related to the intention to adopt OSS applications.

## 3.4. Social identification with OSS

OSS applications are developed, and possibly used, by like-minded individuals who congregate under the label loosely termed “the OSS community”. Hence we de<sup>fi</sup>ne social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (SI) with OSS here as the degree to which the user construes himself or herself to be a member—that is, as “belonging” to the OSS community. To some this is a community that serves a common software interest: providing open-shared software solutions to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll speci<sup>fi</sup>c software needs that cannot be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by the current proprietary software solutions [29]. To some, this is a community that represents a social movement with certain social and political ideologies and agendas [7,29]. And still for some, this is a community that stands for the open source brand (unbreakable Linux) [21]. Being an interest group, or a movement, or a brand, as previously discussed, favorable conditions permeate the OSS community for social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to prevail. These conditions include community distinctiveness and prestige, out-group salience, social interaction, and shared goals. Most importantly, the distinctive values, ideologies, and software development practices observed in the OSS community could 1) confer its members with a unique collective identity, 2) appear appealing to many software users, and 3) draw them towards developing a social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the community and subsequently towards learning and using the software produced by this community.

Social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation involves cognitive, affective, and evaluative components [20]. Cognitively, social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation manifests through mechanisms of categorization. In this case, individuals formulate a self-awareness of membership in the OSS community that emphasizes one's perceived similarities (e.g., shared software interests and ideologies) with other OSS members and perceived dissimilarities with out-groups (e.g., proprietary software users). The affective component implies a sense of emotional and affective commitment to the OSS community [10,20]. Due to this emotion and affection, individuals who strongly identify with OSS could develop a preference and favoritism towards the OSS community and its software and could evaluate OSS applications more positively (useful and easy to use) than others. This affective commitment can also manifest as loyalty towards the OSS community and its software and as strong sentiment against proprietary software <sup>fi</sup>rms and applications [7]. Finally, the evaluative component positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences OSS users' sense of self-worth as OSS membership is a source of group pride to them [17]. Under social identity theory, individuals, motivated by the need for positive self-esteem, develop identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with their group(s), and strong identi<sup>fi</sup>cation could make them strive to maintain and enhance their groups' reputation against outgroups [52]. Thus, individuals who strongly identify with OSS would make an effort to improve the standing of OSS by adopting OSS applications themselves or acting as diffusion agents to persuade others to adopt. A good case in point can be demonstrated by Mozilla Firefox users who contributed their own money to run a print advertisement in The New York Times in late 2004 [38]. Thus, we expect that software users' beliefs (e.g., usefulness and ease of use) and adoption intention for OSS may be created and enhanced by the sense of belongingness to the OSS community. Supporting this conjecture, there is emerging evidence from the marketing and sociology literature that a member's social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with a brand, a brand community, or a social movement manifests itself in favorable product evaluation, word-of-mouth, re-patronage, purchasing, adoption, and participation and engagement behaviors [13,48,55].

H4 . SI with OSS is positively related to the perceived usefulness of OSS applications.

H4<sub>b</sub>. SI with OSS is positively related to the perceived ease of use of OSS applications.

${ \bf H 4 } _ { \bf c } .$ SI with OSS is positively related to the intention to adopt OSS applications.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data collection, participants' characteristics, and instruments

A survey of undergraduate students, graduate students, and working professionals was used to collect the data. A URL to the online survey was distributed electronically to a sample of potential respondents and made available online for sixty days. A total of 280 usable responses were received. Table 1 shows the demographics of these respondents.

The questionnaire items used in this study were adapted from previous studies and reworded for the context of OSS adoption (Appendix A). Measures for PU and PEOU were based on Davis' [18] study, PIIT on the work of Agarwal and Prasad [1], and SI with OSS on Tyler's [57] work. Respondents were asked to indicate their degree of agreement or disagreement with each item on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale ranging from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree).

## 4.2. Survey bias

We assessed the survey for two potential sources of bias—nonresponse bias and common method bias. Non-response bias arises when the respondents to a survey signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ from individuals who do not respond while common method bias results from systematic measurement error due to the use of a single method. To test the possible effects of non-response bias, we followed a technique proposed by Jiang et al. [31] where the sample of respondents was split in two (early and late responses). The late responses served as surrogates for non-responses and were compared to the early responses for differences. A t-test found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between these two categories of responses, suggesting that nonresponse bias was not a major concern. To assess the possible effects of common method bias we performed Harman's single factor test. Using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), this test checks 1) whether a single factor emerges when all the variables under consideration are entered into the analysis and 2) whether a single factor accounts for the majority of the variance. If these conditions are true then common method bias is a potential problem. In this study multiple factors emerged from the EFA and none of the factors accounted for the majority of the variance. Thus common method bias is unlikely to be a major concern.

## 5. Analysis and results

Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression was used to test the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the relationships in the proposed model. This technique has been extensively used in IS research because it is able to handle complex predictive models [6] such as the one considered in this study. Moreover, PLS makes minimal demands on sample's size and the distribution of the data [32], making it well suited for exploratory research [9,34]. Using the software SmartPLS [44], it was possible to develop and assess the measurement model and the structural model proposed in this study.

## 5.1. Measurement model assessment

We evaluated the internal consistency of the scales using three different approaches—Cronbach's Alpha, Composite Reliability and Average Variance Extracted (AVE). Table 2 shows that all the scales exceeded the minimum acceptable threshold value (0.7) required for Cronbach's Alpha and Composite Reliability [41]. Fornell and Larcker [22] suggest that convergent validity can be demonstrated if the AVE is greater than 0.5. As shown in Table 2, the AVE of all the scales exceeded 0.5, indicating that the scales exhibited adequate internal consistency.

We used two approaches to assess whether the scales demonstrate suf<sup>fi</sup>cient discriminant validity. With the <sup>fi</sup>rst approach, we employed factor analysis and the loadings of each of the items were evaluated. Discriminant validity can be demonstrated if item loadings are high on their intended constructs and cross loadings with other constructs are lower [30]. For each of the items the results reveal high loadings on their intended constructs and lower cross loadings (see Table 3). With the second approach, we adopted a technique advocated by Chin [14] where the square root of AVE was placed on the diagonal of the latent variable correlation matrix and compared to the absolute value of the standardized correlations. Discriminant validity can be demonstrated if the square root of AVE is larger than the absolute value of the standardized correlations. Table 4 shows that the cross diagonal values are greater than the absolute values of the standardized correlations, suggesting that the scales exhibited adequate discriminant validity.

## 5.2.3. Post hoc analysis

It is important to note that even though many software users develop social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with OSS by joining OSS social network groups and engaging in a variety of social or OSS related activities [7], many may perceive OSS as what Anderson [5] termed, “an imagined community existing in their minds” (p.419). Individuals in the latter case may perceive a psychological sense of community with other OSS users, yet do not join membership in any OSS social network groups or engage in any social interaction with other members. Therefore, there are two interesting questions to ask 1) does social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with OSS play an equally important role in shaping OSS adoption decisions between individuals developing a psychological sense of community and those joining OSS social network groups; and 2) can joining OSS social network groups strengthen individuals' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community and their intention to adopt OSS. Research indicates that social interaction between individuals is a conducive condition for the development of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [39], and behaviors and intentions are equally in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the psychological sense of community [13]. Based on these studies, it can be expected that joining OSS social network groups would enhance social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, but would not strengthen the effect of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on adoption intention.

## 6. Discussions, implications, and future research

## 6.1. Discussion

Consistent with TAM we found PU and PEOU to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on intention to adopt an OSS application. However, we found PU to be relatively weak at explaining individuals' OSS adoption intention compared with PEOU. One possible explanation could be that the majority of the mainstream software users may have already perceived current proprietary software solutions to be useful enough in serving their needs [12] and may hence forgo OSS applications even though they may perceive OSS applications somewhat more useful than their proprietary counterparts. This is especially true given the stereotypical assumption that OSS applications are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to learn and use and are beyond the technical capability of ordinary users [19,26,28]. In other words, the explanatory power of the perceived usefulness factor may weaken as the merits of the proprietary software increases. The importance of PEOU over PU could also be magni<sup>fi</sup>ed by the drawbacks of OSS such as the lack of training and formal support and the velocity of software version upgrades. Lending support to this conjecture are prior studies that have documented substantial learning costs and concerns over on-going training and support as two major barriers to OSS adoption [26,28]. The relatively low explanatory power of PU may also underscore the importance of including unique context speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors such as PIIT and SI in the research model. PEOU was not found to impact PU. This might be because numerous survey respondents have been using some OSS applications and thus PEOU becomes less important overall in determining PU after the actual use [51]. Similar to the results of other work [46], we found a positive relationship between PIIT and OSS adoption intention and PEOU, but no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between PIIT and PU. One possible explanation for the lack of a positive relationship between PIIT and PU lies in the restrictions imposed by some OSS licenses. The constraints dictated by the copyleft and /or viral provisions may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the perceived usefulness of OSS among one particular subgroup of innovative users, those who have the interest to try out the opportunity of OSS commercialization and crossapplication combination [49,50]. Finally, supporting our contention surrounding the in<sup>fl</sup>uential and cohesive nature of the OSS community, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that individuals' social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with OSS positively impacts OSS adoption intention as well as perceptions (PU and PEOU). This result holds true for individuals who are OSS network members as well as those who are not members but psychologically identify with the OSS community. Joining OSS social network groups was found to strengthen individuals' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the OSS community and their intention to adopt OSS.

## 6.2. Implications for practice and research

Overall, this pattern of results yields several practical implications. First, given the strong and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of PEOU on OSS adoption intention, it is critical to change users' presumptions that all OSS applications are extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use and that they are only for tech-savvy users. Providing proper user training and support is also essential for directing and solidifying individuals' perceptions of the ease of using the applications. For organizations trying to make decisions on how to best increase OSS use, mandatory approaches could prove to be less effective than the use of training to target positive changes in PEOU. Second, since we found that PIIT has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on PEOU and adoption intention, cultivating individual innovativeness becomes crucial in promoting OSS adoption. PIIT can also be used to meaningfully classify potential users with different levels of innovativeness into distinct groups. Organizations can <sup>fi</sup>rst focus their OSS incorporation efforts on adopters who are more likely to readily embrace the intended OSS applications and then use them as change agents and opinion leaders to facilitate further diffusion. In addition, customized training and support programs can also be designed to meet the different needs of each adopter group, thereby effectively reducing employee resistance and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently utilizing organizational resources. Finally, the dual nature of OSS users as software users and as users of a community-based innovation means that community building may be just as important to attract users as an easy-to-use interface, navigational structure, and other elements of human–computer interaction. The OSS community can take advantage of its in<sup>fl</sup>uential impact and use its user groups as important conduits for fostering loyalty and promoting adoption. Organizations are advised to promote the OSS ideology and encourage their employees to join OSS user groups to facilitate OSS adoption initiatives.

From a research perspective, the proposed model extends TAM studies by showing that social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on usage intentions over and above PU and PEOU. While the current work examines OSS adoption, the results can shed light on the adoption of community-based innovations in general. As more <sup>fi</sup>rms embark on initiatives to learn, organize, and nurture communities for innovation and new product development [23], more research is needed to understand how different aspects of individuals' relationships with such communities in<sup>fl</sup>uence their intentions and behaviors.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

The results should be viewed with the limitations of the study in mind. First, we should note that the current study focuses on voluntary OSS adoption although OSS adoption can occur in voluntary or mandatory settings. Past literature suggests that factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing IT adoption differ from mandatory to voluntary settings [58], thus caution must be exercised when attempting to extend the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study to the mandatory setting. Research in sociology has found that the effects of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on behavioral intention manifest through different routes [8]. Subjective norm re<sup>fl</sup>ects the route characterized by compliance and coercive power while social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation re<sup>fl</sup>ects the route of self-de<sup>fi</sup>ning relationship with a group and its members [8]. In the IS literature, scholars have largely focused on the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of subjective norm and have suggested that the effect of subjective norm on intention is only detected in mandatory and not in voluntary system usage settings [58]. This is because the compliance-based effect of subjective norm on intention is more likely to occur in mandatory settings where authorities have the coercive power to reward the adoption behavior or punish nonadoption behavior. Conversely, it is possible that the impact of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on adoption will only be present in a voluntary setting, but not in a mandatory setting. Thus, future research comparing the effects of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on OSS adoption in mandatory versus voluntary settings is needed in order to offer more comprehensive implications for using individuals' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with OSS as a lever for increasing OSS adoption and usage.

Second, adoption intention as opposed to actual behavior was used as the primary dependent variable. Despite the substantial empirical support for the intention–behavior link, future research may consider measuring the actual adoption behavior and using longitudinal data to establish causal links.

Finally, this study focuses on the effect of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on OSS adoption. Future research may consider exploring other aspects of individuals' relationships with the OSS community (e.g., community engagement, community pressure, and relationship quality etc.) and their impacts on various intentions and behaviors (e.g., loyalty intention, recommendation intention, and usage behavior).
