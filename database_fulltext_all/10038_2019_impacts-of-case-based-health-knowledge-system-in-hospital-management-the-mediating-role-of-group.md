# Impacts of case-based health knowledge system in hospital management: The mediating role of group efectiveness

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Case-based systems Group effectiveness Hospital management performance Knowledge management Hospital Intelligent health information management

## A B S T R A C T

With the rise of artificial intelligence, case-based health knowledge management systems (CBHKS) have been widely adopted in hospitals. CBHKS are data-driven intelligent platforms that integrate latest technologies, such as artificial intelligence and cloud computing. As an integral part of smart hospitals, CBHKS can support decision processes at diferent levels in hospitals. However, researchers have not yet clearly addressed how CBHBKS improves hospital management outcomes. Based on group efectiveness and leadership performance-maintenance theories, we develop a conceptual model to explain the role of CBHKS in hospital management. To test the research hypotheses in the conceptual model, we collected survey data from 214 doctors, and performed data analysis using partial least squares (PLS)-based structural equation modeling. The empirical testing results show that the CBHKS implementation significantly and positively influences group performance, group members’ satisfaction, group learning, and external satisfaction; and group members’ satisfaction and external satisfaction significantly and positively afect management performance and maintenance.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid development of modern information technology (IT) and the application of artificial intelligence, intelligent knowledgebased information systems (IS) have been universally implemented throughout the world. The healthcare industry is no exception. In fact, many large hospitals in China have adopted case-based health knowledge management systems (CBHKS). For instance, the CBHKS developed by Winning Health Technology Group has been used in more than 1500 hospitals in China, supporting more than 200,000 doctors [1]. CBHKS are comprehensive, intelligent software platforms that integrate a variety of latest information technologies such as artificial intelligence and cloud computing. It utilizes large volumes of historical cases, to support decision processes in hospitals. In particular, the system can provide decision makers with similar historical cases (i.e., issues and their solutions). As a result, CBHKS efectively facilitate the utilization of healthcare information resources, knowledge management, and management decision making in hospitals. Practically, CBHKS are beneficial for many functional areas in a hospital, such as medical diagnoses and prognoses, drug inventory management, nursing management, medical equipment management, patient relationship management, and human resources. Because of their comprehensiveness and eficiency in knowledge reasoning, CBHKS are playing an increasingly important role in improving hospital management, becoming an integral part of “smart hospitals.”

Prior research has explored the positive impact of hospital information systems (HIS) on organizational performance [2–4]. Fur thermore, scholars have discussed the influence path of hospital IT application on organizational performance, through factors such as the breadth and depth of information sharing [5], patients’ length of stay [6], and patient satisfaction [7].

This study examines the post adoption use of CBHKS, which is closely associated with the long-term performance of hospital management. Using expectation confirmation theory, Bhattacherjee [8] was among the first to investigate the continued use of IT. His work was followed by an array of studies that focused on information systems post adoption behavior (ISPAB) [9–11]. ISPAB explains why individuals and organizations continue to use and expand IS functions and proposed the factors afecting the continuing utilization of IS and IS expansion among individuals and organizations. This stream of research forms the theoretical foundation for the continued use of CBHKS in hospitals. The theoretical basis of ISPAB mainly involves difusion of innovations theory, organizational learning theory, and revolutionary management theory [12–14]. Earlier, ISPAB research has focused on certain cognitive factors, such as perceived service attributes (usefulness and compatibility) and perceived service utilization and network externality (complementary product usage) [15]. When studying the impact of IS on hospitals’ management performance, prior research has mainly focused on constructs such as clinical supervision, the hierarchy and complementarity of organizational structure, and hospital governing boards [16–18]. Few studies have comprehensively examined the impact of group efectiveness on management performance, which has been concerned by some scholars in the past [19,20]. Hence, in the efect evaluation of the post-adoption use of CBHKS, the indirect influence of CBHKS on management output through the mediating efect of group efectiveness should also be considered.

Group efectiveness is the degree to which a team has accomplished the goals or objectives administered by some authorized personnel or the organization [21]. Sundstrom and Mclntyre [22] believed that group eficiency has four aspects: group performance, group learning, group satisfaction, and the degree of external satisfaction. These four aspects may influence post adoption attitudes and behaviors of individuals within a group and organizational performance. Hospitals have various departments, institutes, centers, etc. These group-level units are afected by group cohesiveness and teamwork within the confines of processes and research [23]. Group efectiveness is an im portant manifestation of an organization’s efectiveness as well as a sign of management competency in hospitals. Examining continued use of IS through studying the mediating efect of group efectiveness will fill an important gap in ISPAB research. Additionally, studying the influence path of CBHKS on hospital performance through all four dimensions of group efectiveness may further enrich the literature on the organizational impact of HIS. Therefore, it is of practical significance to understand how CBHKS improve management performance and maintenance in hospitals through group efectiveness.

When measuring management outcomes, previous IS studies often used management performance as the key indicator [24,25]. According to Misumi’s [26] performance-maintenance (PM) theory of leadership, the performance leadership function (P) is oriented toward goal achievement and problem solving, and the maintenance leadership function (M) involves preserving group social stability. In PM theory, P and M are considered to be two axes on which the level of each factor can be measured [27]. In general, high performance and high maintenance leadership will bring about ideal management efect. Although PM theory is widely used to study leadership behavior, P and M es sentially reflect the main concerns of organizational managers and are important aspects of management objectives. Therefore, it is necessary to consider both P and M when studying the management output of an organization. Prior research has studied the efects of these two aspects, respectively. Some study has proved the positive relationship between IS application and employee satisfaction [28–30], which is closely related to the group maintenance of the organization. At the same time, a similar conclusion has been drawn on the relationship between IS application and organizational performance [31,32]. Hence, both P and M are vital to the improvement of hospital management. However, when evaluating management outcomes, management maintenance has been seldom taken into consideration. Practically, the loss of talents is an critical issue for managers [33,34]. Hence, typically considered human capital-intensive, hospitals should regard the maintenance of talents as one of the most important management objectives. It is necessary to include management maintenance in the assessment of management results [26]. This will also enrich the research on evaluating the man. agement outcomes resulted from CBHKS implementation.

Based on the frameworks of the group efectiveness and PM theory of leadership, this study investigates the impact of CBHKS on hospital management outcomes by means of the mediating efect of group effectiveness. Particularly, we reveal how group efectiveness mediates the efect of CBHKS implementation on management performance and maintenance. To achieve this, we adopt the four-dimension measures of group efectiveness proposed by Sundstrom and Mclntyre [22], namely group performance, group members’ satisfaction, external satisfaction, and group learning. Our study addresses the following research questions:

(1) Does the implementation of CBHKS improve hospital management performance and maintenance?

(2) How does group efectiveness mediate the impact of CBHKS on management performance and maintenance in hospitals?

Answering these questions is important for developing a holistic view on how CBHKS impact management outcomes. From a practical perspective, the potential findings will promote the CBHKS implementation and help hospital management use such systems more efectively.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review related research on IS post adoption use, group efectiveness, and the PM theory of leadership, which provides the theoretical foundation for our study. Then, in Section 3, we develop the research model and propose the research hypotheses. We discuss in Section 4 the research metho dology used to test the hypotheses. In Section 5, we present our results and further test the mediating efect of group efectiveness between CBHKS and management performance. The last section concludes this paper with a discussion of findings, implications for theory and practice, limitations, and future directions.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Group efectiveness

Prior research has examined the influence of IS implementation on hospital performance from the perspective of technology, patient experience, and satisfaction [4–6]. Little research paid attention to the role of group efectiveness in the relationship between HIS and management outcomes. Group, including the formal or informal team, is an important part of organization, and group behavior is an important research aspect in organization. McGrath [35] posited that a group (or team, as in management literature) is a social collection of existing mutual understanding and potential interaction between members. If a group can complete a given task and meet its members' individual needs, it is considered highly efective. Russell et al. [36] believed that group efectiveness is an important result and embodiment of organization or group behavior [37]. In the IS research community, scholars also have studied the impact of IS implementation on group eficiency, and provided empirical evidence to support positive efect of IS on group eficiency [38,39]. Hence, it is reasonable to believe that group efectiveness may play an important role in the relationship between CBHKS and hospital management outcomes.

Hackman [40] proposed the earliest definition of group efectiveness. In his study, group efectiveness has been defined from three perspectives: work output, impact on group members (including satisfaction), and the improvement of group problem-solving skills for the future. Later research supplemented and perfected the definition of group efectiveness. Sundstrom and Mclntyre [22] used member satisfaction, performance, external satisfaction, and team learning to measure group efectiveness. Cohen and Baily [41] further noted that the three aspects of group efectiveness are: (1) group performance, namely eficiency, productivity, response speed, quality, service customers’ satisfaction, and innovation; (2) members of the group, namely satisfaction of the members, commitment, and trust in management; and (3) members’ behavior, namely the absence of members, turnover, and safety. Lurey and Raisinghani [42] believed that member satisfaction and performance could be used to evaluate group efectiveness.

Based on the definitions of group efectiveness in prior research and the four dimensions of group efectiveness proposed by Sundstrom and Mclntyre [22], we measure group efectiveness using the following: group performance, group member satisfaction, group learning, and external satisfaction. Most studies on group efectiveness have focused mostly on constructing a reasonable group management system that enhances group efectiveness. The influence of group efectiveness is relevant to many levels in a management hierarchy, such as individual, team, business unit, and organization [41,43,44]. In this study, we focus on the impact of group efectiveness on organizational performance, explaining why the adoption of CBHKS in hospitals afects group work and influences management performance through group efectiveness. Measuring these four dimensions of group efectiveness as intermediary variables, we develop our research model to explain the impact of CBHKS on hospital performance and maintenance.

## 2.2. Performance-maintenance (PM) theory of leadership

Misumi [45] proposed the PM theory of leadership in the 1970s. This theory divides leadership behavior into two categories, i.e., Performance (P) and Maintenance (M). The performance function means that a leader must make detailed work plans, require and strictly monitor a staf to carry out the plans to ensure that a group’s specific goals can be achieved. The maintenance function means that a leader should be concerned about staf welfare, try to build a harmonious relationship with its members, carefully encourage them, and pay attention to proper authorization to ensure ongoing improvement in the organization’s normal operation.

In PM theory, leaders have two main management objectives: organizational goals and organizational relationships. Organizational goals focus on how to improve management performance; organizational relationship focuses on how to establish a stable and harmonious inner environment that maintains the organization. Echoing PM theory, we establish management efectiveness and management maintenance as the dual goals of adopting CBHKS in hospitals to capture the impact of CBHKS on hospital management.

## 3. Conceptual models and hypothesis development

## 3.1. CBHKS implementation and group efectiveness

## 3.1.1. CBHKS implementation and group performance

According to the definition by Cohen and Baily [41], group performance is mainly reflected in the eficiency, productivity, responsiveness, quality of service, customers’ satisfaction, and innovation during healthcare services. A rich literature exists on the relationship between IT investment and performance. Most related studies consider the investment and implementation of IT as a positive factor in group and organizational performance [46,47]. The use of IT can improve the efectiveness and eficiency [47] and the quality of products and services [48].

In the healthcare industry, some scholars have researched the in fluence of IS on hospital management performance. For example, Menachemi et al. [49] discussed the positive impact of hospital IT on financial performance. Cui et al. [50] empirically showed that a performance management IS could improve the performance of clinical departments — such as in the quality and eficiency of healthcare services in Shanghai’s Ninth People’s Hospital — through a real-time query platform for the staf that could help its members monitor and analyze key performance indexes and solve problems.

CBHKS are one type of IT service platform specifically used within the healthcare arena that is also likely to improve group performance. CBHKS provide an information integration platform that supports synchronous sharing of medical information between multiple depart ments. For example, patients’ test results can be quickly shared with medical group members in diferent departments to accelerate diagnosis and treatment. According to Media Synchronicity Theory, an improved capability of media to support information synchronicity could enhance communication performance and then promote the collaborative behaviors of staf members [51]. As an important media in hospital management, CBHKS may strengthen two aspects of a medical staf’s information synchronicity: (1) reductions in the time needed to query patients’ information and improve the eficiency of treatment; and (2) promotion of communication and sharing of knowledge among doctors, which will lead to improvement in the quality of healthcare service. Improving the eficiency and quality of healthcare services will enhance the group performance of hospital staf by attracting more patients. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1a. CBHKS Implementation positively influences group performance.

## 3.1.2. CBHKS implementation and group members’ satisfaction

Another dimension of group efectiveness is group members’ satisfaction. This refers to the satisfaction of internal members and to their commitment and trust in management [41]. In the context of a hospital, it represents the work attitudes of doctors, nurses, and other medical staf. Considerable research has been done on the relationships between the implementation of IS and group member satisfaction. For example, Ammenwerth et al. [52] believe that IT could be used to improve the sense of responsibility in nursing care, thus, improving the eficiency of nurses as well as increasing the satisfaction of patients and other members of the hospital staf. Langfred [53] posited that team performance depends on an individual’s and a team’s autonomy, which could both be improved by the implementation of IS. This further encourages users’ initiative and adds to their satisfaction.

Media Synchronicity Theory posits that communication efectiveness may be enhanced by faster information conveyance and higher information convergence [51], which, in turn, will improve users’ satisfaction with access to information and its use. We believe that CBHKS implementation improves doctors’ autonomy. Doctors can conveniently query medical records using CBHKS. This makes their work less dependent on IT staf. Moreover, the query and report features of CBHKS cost doctors less time to check information of patients and prior treatment, shortening the cycle of creating medical records and improving the eficiency of diagnosis and treatment. The improvement of eficiency is even more pronounced in medical group collaboration. In addition, the data analytics component of CBHKS also provides a powerful tool for doctors to conduct medical research, which is of positive significance for improving doctors' professional knowledge and ability. All of these improve the satisfaction of members of the medical staf, facilitating their completion of treatment processes. More eficient treatment processes will improve patients’ satisfaction and, in turn, improve their relationship with the medical staf. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. CBHKS Implementation positively influences group members satisfaction.

## 3.1.3. CBHKS implementation and group learning

Some scholars who study group learning define it from the perspective of a learning process. For instance, Edmondson et al. [54] regarded it as “an ongoing process of reflection and action, characterized by asking questions, seeking feedback, experimenting, reflecting on results, and discussing errors or unexpected outcomes of actions.” Other researchers define it from the perspective of group learning results. London et al. [55] consider group learning as “the extent to which members seek opportunities to develop new skills and knowledge, welcome challenging assignments, were willing to take risks on new ideas, and work on tasks that require considerable skill and knowledge.”

The relationship between IT implementation and group learning has received a lot of attention in the literature [56]. Scholars believe that IT plays an important role in the process of shifting to a “learning organization” by providing technical support for efectively storing, organizing, and modifying information and knowledge. Furthermore, some scholars believe that IT could facilitate organizational learning [57]. Related studies suggested that the implementation of new tech nologies often requires employees to update their skills [58,59]. Sharma [60] proposed that learning should take place in a space that permits convenient cooperation and exchanges with others and not be confined to closed spaces for independent study.

In hospitals, the use of CBHKS also promotes group learning among the staf. In particular, the implementation of such a system requires doctors to learn how to use it correctly, which can be regarded as a technical learning process for a group. Moreover, CBHKS can support hospital decision processes by analyzing large volumes of historical cases, identifying similar cases, and generating solutions. Hence, CBHKS implementation will promote the information and knowledge sharing among doctors and enhance their treatment skills. Last but not least, the CBHKS implementation can encourage doctors to cooperate in the treatment process. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1c. CBHKS Implementation positively influences group learning.

## 3.1.4. CBHKS Implementation and external satisfaction

External satisfaction is a consequence of group behaviors and a dimension of group efectiveness. According to Sundstrom and Mclntyre [22], higher group efectiveness leads to higher consumer satisfaction. Information conveyance and information convergence through media could not only afect a staf’s work eficiency but also improve a customer’s perceived information transparency [51], which promotes trust among contracting parties and improves external satisfaction.

The implementation of IS is likely to improve patient satisfaction and hospital management and reduce administrative costs. Amin et al. [61] posited that the utilization of IS can optimize inspection processes, standardize operating procedures, and improve patient and staf satisfaction. Aiken [62] concluded that the application of IS in hospitals optimizes the treatment process by shortening treatment time and improving service quality, thus enhancing patient communication and improving patient satisfaction with his or her medical treatment.

CBHKS are the integrated IS not only for medical staf but also for patients. On the one hand, CBHKS improve the eficiency and speed of medical information transmission. On the other hand, CBHKS increase the transparency and sharing of information between doctors and patients. Because of the improved eficiency, doctors will have more time to discuss treatment with other doctors and rethink their work, making treatment more efective. This is likely to enhance patients’ satisfaction. Moreover, CBHKS implementation will show a hospital’s IT capability and commitment. This will create a better image of the hospital, potentially improving external satisfaction. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1d. CBHKS Implementation positively influences external satisfaction.

## 3.2. Group efectiveness and hospital management

According to PM theory, the outcome of hospital management can be divided into management performance and management main tenance. The CBHKS implementation may improve group efectiveness. As a consequence, higher group efectiveness may also lead to the im provement of hospital management (including performance and main tenance).

## 3.2.1. Group performance and hospital management

Only a few studies have analyzed the relationships between hospital management and group efectiveness. Besstremyannaya et al. [63] believed that hospital management performance was a kind of output that reflected hospital input as represented by cost eficiency. CBHKS implementation, as a kind of IT input, may improve the eficiency, productivity, and responsiveness of a group of doctors [41] and, as a result, improve patients’ satisfaction with hospital services. High patient satisfaction leads to more use of healthcare services from the same hospital, increasing its financial income, an important indicator of management performance. Hence, the usage of CBHKS has a significant impact on management performance. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a. Group performance positively influences hospital management performance.

Hospital management maintenance means keeping the relationship in a hospital stable and harmonious through appropriate communication while supporting employees' autonomy. It preserves a hospital’s social stability. High group performance will improve financial performance [64], which could improve the income of individuals and groups under the generally accepted performance appraisal system and enhance the stability of the organization. Furthermore, high group performance implies harmonious interpersonal relationships and efi cient communication in organizations, which would foster group members’ job satisfaction and improve management maintenance. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2b. Group performance positively influences hospital management maintenance.

## 3.2.2. Group members’ satisfaction and hospital management

Group members’ satisfaction is related to the recognition, responsibilities, supervision, and opportunities ofered during their work [65]. A number of researchers have empirically found that satisfaction is related to performance, such as organizations’ outcomes and management efectiveness [66]. For example, Sweeney [67] described the links between satisfaction and organizational outcomes. Koys [68] found that employee satisfaction had positive efects on organizational levels of efectiveness, which actually is a manifestation of management levels. Judge et al. [69] performed a comprehensive quantitative review of research on the satisfaction–performance relationship and concluded that these two variables were at least moderately correlated. Other scholars suggested that team job satisfaction is related to new product performance and commercial success [70]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H3a. Group members’ satisfaction positively influences hospital management performance.

In the context of hospital management, many scholars have studied the relationships between group members’ satisfaction and hospital performance. Student et al. [71] tested the positive efect of nurses’ satisfaction with communication on performance. Mascia et al. [72] studied how hospital restructuring influences the job satisfaction of physicians. The use of CBHKS is likely to improve communication efficiency and accelerate the eficiency of searches for knowledge. It is also likely to trigger the restructuring of a hospital’s business processes and shorten diagnosis time. Thus, the presence of CBHKS may improve both the staf’s job satisfaction and the hospital’s performance. This is an important indicator of management performance and maintenance. Hence, we hypothesize:

H3b. Group members’ satisfaction positively influences hospital management maintenance.

## 3.2.3. Group learning and hospital management

Synergy from group learning will foster higher levels of creativity and productivity than any single individual can create [73]. This sy nergy could eventually result in innovative solutions to management problems and to the improvement of organizational management. Group learning will be helpful for better communication and coordination in teams. It has been shown to have a positive influence on new product quality through the improvement of management [74]. Hassan et al. [75] suggested that team learning behaviors play a significant role in ensuring the success of marketing teams. Argote [76] posited that the occurrence of team learning was mainly manifested in the relatively enduring change in the knowledge and performance of team members. He also used organizational and team learning curves to measure the performance of team learning. Chen and Zheng [77] found a significant positive relationship between a team’s learning ability and team performance. Pagell [78] examined the adaptability between employee skills and work environment and suggested that it directly afects organizational performance.

In hospitals, leaders, department directors, managers, and medical staf can use various functions of CBHKS to conduct group learning and acquire information and knowledge for management decision making. All of these could improve the professional skills of a medical group and deliver a high quality of healthcare service to patients. These gains also could lead to more eficiency that would permit treatment of more patients as well as earn their trust, which eventually will improve hospital management performance. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4a. Group learning positively influences hospital management performance.

In addition, CBHKS could also help improve the efectiveness of group learning and increase the communication behaviors of group members, which, in turn, could increase team cohesion and promote the hospital’s management maintenance. Jahani et al. [79] proposed that the learning levels promoted the job satisfaction of hospital em ployees. Through information sharing and intelligent mining, CBHKS could improve the eficiency of communication among medical staf, increase the depth and breadth of communication information content, and thus improve the learning level of medical staf. Hence, CBHKS could increase the job satisfaction of medical staf and further improve hospital management maintenance. We propose the following hypoth esis:

H4b. Group learning positively influences hospital management maintenance.

## 3.2.4. External satisfaction and hospital management

Previous studies have investigated the relationships between external satisfaction and management performance and maintenance [80]. External satisfaction is a kind of work attitude from external stakeholders, while management maintenance directly embodies organizational efectiveness [81]. Terziovski [82] investigated the relationships among quality management practices, customer satisfaction, and productivity improvement. He suggested that improvement of customers’ satisfaction correlates positively with productivity improvement.

External satisfaction in hospitals mainly refers to patients’ satisfaction. The CBHKS implementation improves treatment efectiveness and eficiency, saves patients’ time, and then improves external satisfaction. Patients’ attitudes are vital in determining whether patients will choose a hospital again. Moreover, patients’ satisfaction influences the service choices of potential customers through word-of-mouth. As one dimension of group efectiveness, external satisfaction is an important reflection of whether the work of medical groups is efective. As a result, external satisfaction can directly influence hospital performance. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5a. External satisfaction has a significantly positive efect on hospital management performance.

In addition, CBHKS can promote healthcare service efectiveness, which also improves the satisfaction of patients. This improves doctor–patient relationships, which indirectly enhance the stability of medical groups [83]. High external satisfaction can reduce the doctor–patient conflict, increase the medical group members’ sense of security, and promote the stability of medical working group. Hence, external satisfaction influences the hospital’s continuing development and maintenance. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5b. External satisfaction has a significant positive efect on hospital management maintenance.

## 4. Methodology

To test our research model. a study was conducted at one of the biggest hospitals in East China. This hospital has successfully implemented CBHKS. The CBHKS implemented at the hospital are easy to use and useful for doctors, nurses, and managers. The hospital started to adopt IS in 1996. The doctors and nurses in this hospital were among the first to promote the use of HIS. The implementation of CBHKS in the hospital started in 2012, mainly providing management and decision support for medical staf and hospital managers. The CBHKS include functions such as cases organizing and maintenance, collaborative casebased diagnosis and nursing support for doctors and nurses, case-based management and decision support for hospital managers, online members’ communications, and group learning. At present, this sstem has become a widely used popular support tool for healthcare decision making and hospital management in this hospital.

## 4.1. Measurements

The measurements for most of the variables were adapted from previous studies. Seven constructs are used in this study: CBHKS implementation, Group Performance, Group Members’ Satisfaction, Group Learning, External Satisfaction, Management Performance, and Management Maintenance. All of the items were measured with a seven-point Likert scale [84,85], ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). CBHKS implementation was measured based on the items from Angst and Agarwal [86] and Tippins and Sohi [87]. The measurements for performance and satisfaction were adopted from Lurey and Raisinghani [42] in 2001. The measurements of group learning are mainly based on the team learning research of Huang et al. [88].

## 4.2. Data collection

We used a multistage iterative process to collect the data. We con ducted a pilot study using 85 respondents to test for any ambiguou expressions, awkward wordings, or distortions of the original meanings. On the basis of data and respondents’ suggestions in the pilot study, we modified the questionnaire, which was then used to collect data from the medical staf in the hospital. This study had the full support of the hospital’s management. A small gift with a value of approximately \$10 was provided to each respondent who completed and returned the questionnaire. The survey lasted for two and a half months.

The participants were randomly from the group leaders (such as department heads and ofice directors). Then, with the assistance of these group leaders, we informed the selected participants and explained the purpose of the study. Based on the questionnaire filling rule, we informed the group leaders in advance, they should organize at least two in-group discussions and extensively solicit opinions and suggestions from personnel within the group. Based on full communication and consultation with colleagues within groups, the leaders filled in the questionnaires on behalf of their groups. Although the questionnaires were completed by the leaders, obviously the scores represent common understanding of groups, not only individual opinions of leaders.

They were also informed that participation was voluntary as well as anonymous. We distributed 250 questionnaires and got 225 returned ones. The response rate is 90%. Among the 225 questionnaires returned, 11 were discarded as incomplete. Hence, we used 214 responses in the analysis. The sample demographics were shown in Table 1. In this table, we only counted the people who finally filled in the questionnaires. Table 1 shows the demographic data.

## 4.3. Data analysis technique

PLS-based structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to perform a simultaneous evaluation of both measurement quality (measurement model) and construct interrelationship (structural model) [9]. Prior studies have indicated that PLS-SEM overcame problematic model identification issues and that it is a powerful method to analyze complex models by using smaller samples [89,90]. Covariance-based SEM cannot provide robust results because our sample size is limited. Thus, in this study, we used SmartPLS 2 to evaluate measurement quality and test hypotheses.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement model

Analysis of the measurement model mainly involves the assessment of reliability and validity. Among the reliability metrics, Cronbach’s α reliability coeficient has been most commonly used. A factor’s Cronbach’s α evaluates the internal consistency of the measurements. The threshold value of Cronbach’s α recommended by Hair et al. [91] is 0.5. Another metric for internal consistency is composite reliability, which is not sensitive to the number of items in the scale [91]. Hair et al. [91] considered composite reliability of 0.7 or more to be very credible. Both Cronbach's α and composite reliability were used for a comprehensive analysis of the reliability of the questionnaires.

The validity of constructs is commonly measured from two aspects, i.e., convergent validity and discriminant validity. Convergent validity can be assessed by using average variance extracted (AVE). AVE measures the level of variance captured by a construct versus the level because of measurement error. AVE values higher than 0.5 are considered very good. According to Fornell and Larcker [92], to ensure the existence of discriminant validity among the factors, the square root of a construct’s AVE value needs to exceed its correlation with other constructs.

Table 2 presents the assessment of reliability and validity. All of the Cronbach’s α and composite reliability values exceed 0.8, indicating very good reliability. All of the AVE values exceed 0.5, suggesting good convergent validity. The right part of Table 2 shows the correlations among the constructs; the square roots of the AVE values are on the diagonal. The square root of each construct’s AVE value exceeds its correlations with other constructs, indicating strong discriminant validity. Table 3 shows the mean, standard deviation, and factor loadings of each measurement. All of the factor loadings exceed $0 . 7 ,$ providing additional evidence for good discriminant validity.

We followed Kock and Lynn [93] and Kock [94] to assess potential common method bias using a full collinearity test. Specifically, we created a dummy construct (with a single indicator containing random values between 0 and 1) and used all constructs to predict it through a PLS regression. The variance inflation indicator (VIF) values for each of the construct are reported in Table 4. All of the VIF values are below the recommended threshold, i.e., 3.3. This suggests that our data analysis is not threatened by common method bias or multicollinearity.

## 5.3. Test of mediation

In our research model, we hypothesized that group performance, group members’ satisfaction, group learning, and external satisfaction mediate the efect of CBHKS implementation on management performance and maintenance. Thus, we further tested the mediation efects. For this purpose, we followed the procedure for testing mediation effects of a PLS model in Rai et al. [95]. First, we added a direct path between CBHKS implementation and management performance. The Rsquared for management performance is 0.5930. The R-squared for management performance in the original model (full mediation) is 0.5928. We calculated pseudo F statistics following Rai et al. [95] to test if this diference is significant. The pseudo F statistic is 0.1007 with (1, 206) degrees of freedom. The p value is not significant, suggesting that adding the direct efect does not improve the model fit. Moreover, the path coeficient of the direct efect is not significant, either. This suggests that the four dimensions of group efectiveness fully mediate the efect of CBHKS implementation on management performance.

In the same fashion, we added a direct path between CBHKS im plementation and management maintenance. The R-squared for management maintenance is 0.6201. The R-squared for this construct in the original model is 0.6046. The pseudo F statistic is 8.3640 with (1, 206) degrees of freedom, which is statistically significant. The path coefi cient of the direct efect is -0.1610, which is also statistically significant. Meanwhile, the path coeficients of the four dimensions of group effectiveness have increased. This suggests that the direct efect may have a suppression efect on the relationship between CBHKS implementation and the four dimensions of group efectiveness. Specifically, CBHKS implementation has a positive correlation (0.34) with management maintenance. It is also positively correlated with external satisfaction (0.51), group learning (0.54), group members’ satisfaction (0.47), and group performance (0.57). However, the direct efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance has a negative path coeficient when it is included and the “predictive validity” of the four dimension of group efectiveness has increased. By definition [96,97], CBHKS implementation is likely to be a negative suppressor in our research model.

It is considered as an inconsistent mediation when the mediation efect has a diferent sign than the direct efect and one of the independent variables is a negative suppressor [98]. This is common in situations where the direct efect may be counterproductive [98]. In our research context, implementation of a new system may bring changes that immediately lower the stability of relationships in a hospital. It may also reduce employees’ perception of autonomy because they are mandated to use the new system. This is a plausible explanation of the negative direct efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance. Nonetheless, in the partial mediation model, all coefi cients in the four mediation paths are positive and statistically significant. The total efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance is 0.34. This satisfies the condition of mediation with the presence of a negative suppressor [99]. Thus, our results support the role of group efectiveness in mediating the positive but indirect efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

In this study, the relationships among CBHKS implementation, group efectiveness, and the improvement of management performance and management maintenance in hospital management are thoroughly investigated. The results show that CBHKS implementation has a sig nificant, positive efect on group efectiveness. Moreover, all of the four dimensions of group efectiveness have significant, positive efects on hospital management performance. Group members’ satisfaction and external satisfaction also have significant, positive efects on hospital management maintenance. But the efects of group performance and group learning on management maintenance are not significant.

We also tested the mediating efect of group efectiveness between CBHKS implementation and hospital management output. According to the result of empirical test, group efectiveness fully mediates the efect of CBHKS implementation on hospital management performance and partially mediates the efect on hospital management maintenance. In addition, we find that CBHKS implementation has the negative direct efect on hospital management maintenance.

## 6.1. Implications

## 6.1.1. Implications for research

This study has important implications for IS research. First, we in vestigated how CBHKS implementation afects the two diferent dimensions of hospital management: management performance and management maintenance. Drawing on the PM theory, we introduce two aspects of leadership behavior into our research as two dimensions of management output. P and M have been studied as the management output in some prior researches, respectively [28–32]. Our study considers both dimensions in our research context and shows that CBHKS implementation impacts management performance and maintenance diferently. This is not only new but also important in the literature on the organizational impact of IS. Prior related studies paid more attention to organizational performance, which is one of the most important aspects in evaluating management outcomes [24,25,63]. However, the importance of management maintenance cannot be neglected because it concerns the stability of an organization [26]. Our findings show that CBHKS implementation indeed afects both management performance and maintenance through group efectiveness. Future IS research may consider examining both management performance and maintenance when studying the impact of IS implementation. This will provide us with a holistic view on how it impacts management outcomes.

Second, we introduced the four dimensions of group efectiveness to our research model and demonstrated their mediating roles in the relationship between CBHKS implementation and management outcomes. Group efectiveness is a meso-level construct, while management output is an organizational level construct. In our study, we established the influence path of CBHKS on the organizational level construct by virtue of the meso-level construct of group efectiveness, which is seldom discussed in previous literature. Our study further enriches the literature on the path of IT's impact on organizational output. From the empirical results, the mediating efect of group efectiveness shows internal diferences. Among the four dimensions, group performance and group learning capture the improvements of group-level business processes while group members’ satisfaction and external satisfaction reflect the emotional consequences. Our findings show that group effectiveness fully mediates the efect of CBHKS implementation and management performance. This suggests that both business process improvements and emotional factors contribute to management performance and can fully capture the efect of CBHKS implementation. However, in the relationship between CBHKS implementation and management maintenance, only group members’ satisfaction and external satisfaction are significant mediators. This implies that organizational stability may have a stronger association with emotional factors than economic factors. Merely improving group-level business processes is not efective in enhancing organizational stability. Rather, the emotional consequences brought by IS implementation are more significant. These findings have provided a foundation for future research that aims at gaining deep insights when studying the organiza tional impact of IS.

## 6.1.2. Implications for practice

The systems we investigated in this study, CBHKS, are used in the healthcare industry, a high-impact area where intelligent systems are being widely deployed. Our research findings have important implications for doctors and other decision makers in hospitals. On the one hand, our data analysis shows that CBHKS implementation has positive (but indirect) efects on management performance and maintenance. This provides new empirical evidence for the usefulness of CBHKS. Such evidence is crucial in initiating CBHKS projects in hospitals that have not implemented such a system yet. On the other hand, our findings show that group efectiveness fully mediates the positive efects. This calls for decision makers’ attention on the group level outcomes when considering the organizational impact of CBHKS. In particular, when studying the potential benefits of CBHKS implementation in the project planning phase, the four dimensions of group efectiveness can provide important indicators for intangible returns.

Based on the results from our data analysis, the roles of CBHKS seem to be complex in influencing the relationship between CBHKS im plementation and management maintenance. Although the positive efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance is mediated by group efectiveness, the direct efect is negative. This implies that CBHKS implementation may immediately decrease organizational stability because of the required change to the habits and culture in a hospital. Thus, the positive impact of CBHKS on management maintenance may only be observed in the long horizon. Moreover, only the emotional aspect of group efectiveness (i.e., group members satisfaction and external satisfaction) is significant in mediating the positive efect of CBHKS implementation on management maintenance. As a result, hospital management is advised to focus more on emotional dimensions to mitigate the potential negative impact on organizational stability when implementing CBHKS. This is important for both internal and external stakeholders.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

This study is not without limitations. First, we measured external satisfaction based on medical staf’s perception. Although the medical staf is frequently updated with patient’s feedback, our instruments did not directly collect this information from patients. However, external satisfaction intends to capture the change in satisfaction after the CBHKS implementation. Most patients do not use the hospital’s service long enough to experience such a change. Yet, many hospital employees worked for the hospital both before and after the CBHKS implementation. Thus, their perception of external satisfaction can better capture the “change” aspect of the construct. Future research may validate our results by measuring external satisfaction directly by conducting patient surveys.

In this study, we focused on the indirect (mediated) efect of CBHKS implementation on management outcomes rather than the direct efect. The results from our data analysis suggest that CBHKS implementation may be a negative suppressor. Its direct efect on management maintenance is negative, but its mediated efect is positive. Although we provided a plausible explanation for it, this interesting mechanism needs to be further examined and empirically tested. Further research may work in this direction.

Moreover, CBHKS have been implemented in a large number of hospitals. These hospitals may difer in their processes and management. In this study, we only used data collected from one hospital. Thus, the generalizability of our findings should be interpreted with cautions. Future studies may validate our findings using data from multiple hospitals.

Additionally, future research can also focus on studying how the CBHKS implementation enhances diferent dimensions of healthcare service quality. Aagja and Garg [100] developed a scale to measure perceived service quality for public hospitals from a user’s perspective. The scale they developed is called public hospital service quality (PubHosQual), which measures the five dimensions of hospital service quality: admission, healthcare service, overall service, discharge process, and social responsibility. Based on our current study, future work can address how CBHKS afect improvement in the diferent dimensions of hospital service quality, and further examine which specific modules or functions of CBHKS work most efectively. This will be helpful in identifying areas where specific modifications are needed so that hospital management can focus on these areas during the implementations of healthcare IT.
