# Sympathy or strategy: social capital drivers for collaborative contributions to the IS community

Matthias Trier<sup>1</sup> and Judith Molka-Danielsen<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Denmark; <sup>2</sup>Department of Economy, Informatics and Social Sciences, Molde University College, Norway

## Abstract

Despite growing interest in delineating the social identity of Information Systems (IS) research and the network structures of its scholarly community, little is known about how the IS community network is shaped by individual conceptions and what motivates IS researchers to engage in research collaboration. Using an exploratory theoretical framework that is based on three dimensions of social capital theory, we examined 32 years of scientific co-authorship in an international IS researcher community. We formulated propositions to empirically examine the multi-level relationships between personal drivers and the resulting complex network organization of the IS community. Our propositions are refined with qualitative interviews and tested using a survey. This process revealed a collaborative research culture with several individual dispositions, including a strategic structural focus, a cognitive focus and a relational focus. These exist among actors displaying a range of differing behaviours such as active engagement and passive serendipity. Our study indicates individual differences at the conception stage of engaging in academic collaboration impact on the resulting network-level configuration. We identified that regional preference, maturity life cycles and lack of smallworld properties highlight the important role of senior members as structural backbones and brokers within the IS community. ## Introduction

The intellectual identity of the Information Systems (IS) scholarly community is delineated in an ongoing academic debate (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). This debate includes the analysis and comparison of different styles and profiles of research across continents and nations (Avgerou et al, 1999; Galliers & Whitley, 2007; Lyytinen et al, 2007). It includes a main focus on different citation and publication preferences. These citation preferences give rise to network representations, which can uncover the central importance of different IS journal outlets (Polites & Watson, 2008).

As one approach, DeSanctis (2003, p. 362) argued for a social action perspective on IS research whereby the discipline is acknowledged as a community. The discourse of this community takes place in conferences and journals and reflects both the output and the progress of the field of IS as a whole. DeSanctis proposed that network analysis can help to understand and develop social identity among IS researchers. Social identity is defined as self-awareness of an individual’s membership in a group and the emotional and evaluative significance of this membership (Tajfel, 1978).

The importance of studying academics’ social identity is further emphasized by the growing relevance of coauthored papers. Among IS researchers, their share has significantly increased from 40% in 1978 to more than 80% of all journal publications (Oh et al, 2005; Galliers & Whitley, 2007). This increase may reflect a higher difficulty and pressure to publish in times of globalization (Loos et al, 2010). On the other hand, Beaver (2006) presents evidence that co-authored contributions yield higher citation impact (yet there are inconclusive results; see the discussion in Mingers & Xu, 2010).

Consequently, we studied the researchers’ structural patterns of academic collaboration via their co-authorships using a social network perspective.

Several studies have focused on the relationships of IS researchers. Vidgen et al (2007) presented the most central actors of the European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS) conference and measured this community’s network properties. Xu & Chau (2006) measured the co-authorship structure of contributions to the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) to illustrate DeSanctis’ (2003) concept of social identity. Oh et al (2005) demonstrated that low redundancy among researchers’ network contacts was related to a significant increase in levels of citation impact for IS journal articles, whereas dense and rich network relationships did not show a significant effect.

These studies contribute to our understanding of the social identity of the discipline. Nevertheless, the grounding of the overall culture and performance of the IS community in the individual collaboration activities of its participants remains poorly understood. For example, prior research does not indicate the individual factors that lead researchers to participate in large inter-organizational networks. Consequently, little is known about how the observed network structure developed. We argue that researchers involved in networks are likely to have a considerable impact on the community because they enjoy high levels of autonomy in managing their collaboration in the absence of a central force or authority. Therefore, their interactions in network structures provide the main mechanism for knowledge integration performance (Baldwin et al, 1997). Furthermore, the current lack of understanding academic collaboration as the basis of the research community can be viewed as a gap in stark contrast with the non-linear growth of scientific collaboration (Acedo et al, 2006).

This gap invites research into the social identity of the IS research community that evolves from our beliefs and attitudes about academic collaboration. To our knowledge to date, there has been no study that links actors’ attitudes and beliefs about collaborating in a multi-level approach to their position in their research community’s network. Yet such a study would highlight the motivations and drivers of individuals to engage in collaborative scholarly production. Some individuals may prefer to work with fellow researchers of like mindedness. On the other hand, a strategic individual might prefer collaborations that will lead to an improved position on their career path. At the network level, it is important to recognize and understand how collaboration activities shape the IS community and impact the culture and performance of IS research.

We seek to understand the individual drivers and their roles for a scientific community network in information systems. Specifically, we examine the network of coauthors who contributed scientific articles to the Information Systems Researchers Seminar in Scandinavia (IRIS). IRIS is one of the oldest annual IS conferences (Galliers & Whitley, 2007); in 2011 it had convened for 34 years. This gives us not only an insight into how collaborations are formed within this community but also a better understanding of how an international IS research community has emerged and changed over time.

In this paper, we introduce related theories that informed our exploratory research design. Second, we refine the exploratory theoretical framework and adapt it to our domain of analysis using a small-scale study of qualitative interviews. Third, we propose five actor dispositions, as sets of beliefs that constitute a characteristic approach of an IS scholar to engage in research collaboration with other fellow academics. Fourth, we inform how the exploratory theoretical framework was surveyed using an online questionnaire. Fifth, an analysis of the community applying social network analysis (SNA) at the network level is given. We studied the structural configuration of the collaboration network over 32 years using SNA. We then combined both levels to report on the multi-level relationships between the individual dispositions and network embeddedness that resulted from a correlation analysis. Finally, we present the main conclusions of our research.

In brief, this paper gives rise to a new analytical app roach that can aid the IS community to better understand how we can progress as a large complex network of researchers, a response to the challenge identified in Lyytinen et al (2007). By specializing on co-authorships in the academic arena we shed light on a major aspect of the collective research practice about which we still know surprisingly little, despite its importance for the social identity of IS research.

## General background

Various relevant theories have been proposed to explain network effects (Monge & Contractor, 2003). We respond to this breadth of scope by adopting a broad exploratory approach that addressed multiple theories. In this section, we review existing literature in order to consider related impact factors that are likely to relate to the emergent structure of complex co-authorship networks of IS researchers. We have based our discussion on social capital theory that conceptualizes the resources and benefits individuals or groups can derive based on their embeddedness in a social network (Bourdieu, 1986). Social capital can be defined as ‘resources embedded in social networks accessed and used by actors for action’ (Lin, 2001, p. 25). We assume that the actions of individual researchers are likely to be motivated by beliefs and attitudes about advantages gained through increased social capital. Nahapiet & Ghoshal (1998) proposed three main dimensions: structural, relational and cognitive. Each dimension ‘facilitates the creation and exchange of knowledge’ (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 243). This widely used conceptualization has also been adopted to IS research (Xu & Chau, 2006; Robert et al, 2008).

## Impact of network structures

The structural dimension captures the overall patterns of ties among the actors (i.e. the network structure). It reflects the ‘who knows whom’ as a potential resource available to the actors (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). The benefits of embeddedness in social network structures have been conceptualized by Burt (1995), who proposed the theory of structural holes. According to this theory, an individual might be motivated to establish a broker position between dense regions of relationships that have only sparse connections between them, in other words, there is a structural hole. Actors from different groups are not linked directly and depend on the broker as their connector and match maker. Based on this theory, we derived the following proposition: A brokering focus has impact on the final co-authorship network embeddedness of the researcher. Henceforth, we refer to our propositions in an abbreviated form, only stating the various independent theoretical constructs of the propositions in parenthesis (e.g. brokering focus). Due to the time required to establish a broker position, we only expected senior researchers to yield measurable network impacts.

In order to benefit from structural holes, individuals need to strive for an overview of others and their collaborations (overview focus). Such a preference favours building up loose, large networks as these increase the chances of mediating between different dense regions (loose, large network focus). Burt also argued that network positions are especially beneficial if the linked regions have a high diversity. Therefore, we proposed that the researchers’ focus on heterogeneous networks influences the embeddedness of the network (heterogeneous network focus). This diversity requires learning about other researchers’ topics of interest (topic focus). Burt’s perspective assumed a high level of self-interest (Walker et al, 1997) that may find its expression in proactive development and strategic selection of mutually beneficial co-authorship opportunities. This leads to the expectation that the individual’s perceived importance of active relationship development (as a focus) influences network embeddedness. Furthermore, we proposed that the active strategist is likely to link up with other academics or co-authors higher up the hierarchy in order to utilize their reach to extend his/her own influence (hierarchy focus). A similar, and possibly more convenient enhancement in embeddedness is likely to result if the researcher is (or was) supported by a more senior mentor or advisor (mentor support).

A similar motivation to engage in collaborative networks is implied by Granovetter (1973) in the theory of the strength of weak ties. He argued that strong links will lead to similarity among actors. Consequently, the corresponding information and views obtained by such close contacts are unlikely to be novel. On the other hand, weak ties to others who are diverse and distant are less redundant and thus may make these links information rich. This theory suggests a focus on active relationship management as well as large, loose and heterogeneous networks. Further, the individual is motivated to learn about others in order to engage in forming weak ties with diverse complementary contacts. This addresses the topic focus and suggests a preference for heterogeneous networks as drivers of collaboration.

## Impact of relationship quality

The above two theories (Granovetter, 1973; Burt, 1995) seem to favour loose large networks (i.e. a mass approach with a structural focus) over strong intimate and rich relationships. However, there is a second and alternative strand of theory that emphasizes the perceived importance of relational qualities between actors (Burt, 1995; Oh et al, 2005). This relational dimension results from the history of the relationship (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). It fosters solidarity, generalized reciprocity and reduces free-rider behaviour (Takahashi, 2000). This dimension relates to enhanced knowledge exchange among scientists (Bouty, 2000).

Homophily theory (Monge & Contractor, 2003, p. 223) proposes that individuals prefer to interact with others that have similar properties in order to reduce psychological discomfort and foster reciprocity. Individuals actively seek to reduce dissonance and build an emotional balance with the partners in their network (Heider, 1946). If we apply homophily theory to a co-authorship network, we can expect that actors will prefer to establish links with people of a similar culture such as found in the same country, same university or with similar personal values. This gives rise to the expectancy that affective relational aspects such as a focus on sympathy and positive experience can also be important drivers for engaging in research co-authorships. We are sympathetic to the self-conscious desire for emotional harmony and sharing emotions, especially among equals (McDougall, 1912).

As another proponent of the relational view, Feld (1981) found that actors maintain a social activity focus. In other words, the connections they establish prior to entering a new network will largely define where actors enter the network and how well they perform in the network once they are members. Correspondingly, individuals who have established a strong relationship by working together, sharing the same topics or having previously collaborated in projects, are likely to maintain these links in the collaboration network (focus on maintaining existing ties). Simultaneously, this theory implies that actors who have had multiple affiliations in the past (i.e. working at various institutes) will have several social circles that would bring them into an advantageous position in the network. Additionally, social circles encourage the role of a mentor as an important catalyst in a researcher’s existing network (mentor support).

## Impact of the cognitive dimension

The third aspect of social capital is the cognitive dimension (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). This aspect recognizes the important role of the actual contents of the interaction, the common understandings and concepts. Such ‘shared mental models can be developed when members have similar experiences’ (Robert et al, 2008, p. 321). If such a cognitive fit exists, members can better anticipate what others need to accomplish their tasks without much communication activity. Actors have a better cognitive map (of others) helping them to organize information to accomplish tasks more effectively (Robert et al, 2008). In the research collaboration context, the cognitive perspective suggests several theoretical drivers. The strategic actor could use knowledge about other actors’ research domains to yield high levels of collaboration effectiveness (motivating an overview focus). Further, actors may strive for sharing common ideas and high levels of ‘cognitive fit’. This results in a topic-oriented focus when it comes to selecting collaboration partners. We include also the notion of feedback and discussion of work in the cognitive category (improve work focus), as the contents and concepts of the relationship are emphasized during these two processes.

In conclusion, we structured our exploratory theoretical framework by integrating and categorizing most of our identified co-authorship drivers with Nahapiet’s and Ghoshal’s concept of the three social capital dimensions. This approach gives an integrated structure of different dispositions of individuals’ networking behaviour. Examples of such behaviour are the management of active strategic relationships to influence the structural embeddedness or heterogeneity of new contacts; or a preference for developing the cognitive dimension by linking up with experts in similar topic fields vs a tendency towards nurturing relational capital by investing in trustful relationships.

## Proactivity vs passive serendipity

A fourth dimension is the notion of proactivity vs passivity as they relate to networking activity (Walker et al, 1997). In the proactivity view, actors are assumed to actively decide and strategically act as entrepreneurs to advance their network relationships (Burt, 1995). The other passive view, which we call passive serendipity (because social interactions are left to chance), implies more passive actors who are subject to the network influences of their pre-existing close ties (Bourdieu, 1986). These two opposing positions may relate to or explain different network embeddedness. We therefore included this aspect of social capital in our initial exploratory research framework.

Lastly, we consider the researchers’ duration of participation in the community (tenure). In line with Oh et al (2005), we expected that with increasing length of participation actors are able to increase or improve their ties with other researchers, which results in a more embedded position in the overall co-authorship network.

## Refining the exploratory theoretical framework

To assess the applicability of the general theories to the domain of research collaboration and to refine our framework, we conducted a small-scale interview study. Based on this qualitative inquiry, we assessed how the primarily analytical dimensions suggested by social capital theory can be rephrased and conceptualized as a more illustrative and practical set of behavioural dispositions that specifically address research collaborations.

## Identifying drivers in interviews

During semi-structured interviews, we asked the respondents to describe their concepts of co-authorship. We used a qualitative content analysis with the theoretical drivers as the initial codes. We added new codes as they emerged from the data and compared our results with our initial theoretical framework.

Our small set of four network actors was recruited using a theory-driven maximal variation sampling strategy (Miles & Huberman, 1994) in order to capture the diverse contexts and perspectives at different stages of the research career. We recruited one full professor (A), two associate professors (B, C) and one postgraduate student near completion of a doctoral degree programme (D). Our interest was in the main constructs that the participants related to their authoring collaboration in an inter-organizational community with different researchers. We used open-ended questions to encourage the participants to reflect on their personal drivers and motivations to engage in joint work with others. Their perceptions could be coded and categorized within our theoretical social capital dimensions, but often the participants touched on a variety of drivers simultaneously. For example, the following quote from an associate professor emphasized both a relational and cognitive focus:

I think the main reason is that for me, the driving force is always to do interesting things with interesting people first – sometimes the topic comes first and sometimes the relation to the people makes me interested in their fields. (B)

## Aggregating co-authorship dispositions

We have adopted a theoretical framework that is based on social capital theory, however, we must emphasize the somewhat generic and analytical differentiation proposed in this approach: Nahapiet & Ghoshal (1998, p. 243) noted that the three dimensions of social capital theory ‘are, in fact, highly interrelated’. In the interviews, we uncovered evidence for the role of passive serendipity – a notion not covered by previous social capital studies. Based on the insights from our interviews, we propose a set of five theoretical dispositions to aggregate different beliefs and attitudes of actors likely to occur among researchers. These dispositions are conceptualized in line with the social capital dimensions:

Disposition 1 (relational focus): Actors seek actively to reinforce their established relationship links. Such links endure over long periods of time. Such long-lasting relationships have value for individuals in both getting an overview of work and discussing it. Topics and co-authorships evolve via pre-existing established links. This is an example of sympathy as an important driver.

Disposition 2 (cognitive focus): Individual participants are receptive when presented with trend topics that can initiate future collaborations. Relationships form as a result of good topic matches (cognitive capital). Co-authorships are actively forged. Strategic-thinking appears as a dominating driver in the selection of new links.

Disposition 3 (structural focus): In line with Burt’s structural hole theory, we expected that researchers might actively and strategically achieve well-embedded network positions, build large, loose networks and reach out to prominent (i.e. senior, central) authors.

Disposition 4 (structural tenured broker focus): Seniors advisors take an active role in embedding junior researchers in scientific networks. To do so, seniors broker their disciples into embedded structures often by contri buting directly to inter-organizational collaborations. This disposition indicates that the main driver can be strongly influenced by the researcher’s level of career.

Disposition 5 (serendipity focus): We further expected that researchers might have a preference for passive serendipity, in other words, not actively seek out new co-authorships.

The proposed dispositions translate and adapt the aggregate factors of social capital theory to the concrete field of research collaboration in an attempt to yield question items and insights that can be applied to the field of IS research networks.

## Methodology

To improve our understanding of the complex link between individual preferences for engaging in research collaborations and their impact on the evolving network structure, we adopt a multi-method (Mingers, 2003) and multi-level research approach. Multi-level research addresses the relationship between different levels of abstraction such as the actor and the network (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007). As discussed already, we first refined our exploratory theoretical framework with qualitative interviews at the actor level. Then, we administered an exploratory survey to measure the impact of the individual drivers for collaboration in co-authorships. As a second level in our multi-level approach, we studied and measured the network level of the co-authorship network using dynamic network visualization and analysis. We used the event-driven dynamic network analytical approach proposed by Trier (2008) to take a longitudinal perspective on the network-level evolution of the IRIS research community over 32 years. The quantitative surveyed constructs measured at the individual level were finally related to network-level constructs such as network centrality (see Appendix A for definitions) to study the interrelationships using a non-parametric correlation analysis.

## The Scandinavian-centred IS community

We used our framework to study the Information Systems Researchers in Scandinavia (IRIS). The collaborative output of this community emphasizes younger researchers’ steps in reaching out and connecting. However, senior researchers have been equally active in collaborating on the papers. Our study considered both age-groups equally and allowed for comparisons between junior and senior researchers.

The network examined is a large international collaborative network among 1435 Scandinavian IS researchers. The work conducted in the community is represented in this study as co-authorship on articles presented at the conference. These co-authorships constitute the ties between researchers.

While it might be argued that IRIS is a special subcommunity of IS research, we note that it connects 33 countries worldwide. In our network analysis, we compare further structural properties to existing studies of ICIS (Xu & Chau, 2006) and ECIS (Vidgen et al, 2007). The results of this comparison are summarized in Table 3 in the discussion section.

## Survey items and constructs

Based on our exploratory theory framework, we conceived an initial set of unidimensional candidate constructs to study personal preferences for engaging in research collaboration. This list was then adapted to our research context and substantiated using qualitative interviews. Guided by the scale development process of MacKenzie et al (2011), Table 1 provides a brief description for each proposed construct, summarizes its sources and illustrates its relationship to the social capital dimensions (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998) and to the proposed co-authorship dispositions.

To the structural aspect of social capital, we related (1) the formation of large but rather loose networks, (2) the desire to form a very heterogeneous network, (3) the perceived importance of acting as a broker in the network, (4) the degree support from seniors/mentors, and (5) the importance of linking with individuals at higher hierarchy levels. The relational aspect of social capital is emphasized in (6) the role of active relationship management, (7) the perceived importance of sympathy with the contact, and (8) the maintenance of existing contacts. The cognitive dimension is related to (9) the preference for topic specialists, (10) the importance of focusing on new topics, (11) researchers’ intention to improve their work through exchange with others, or (12) their attempts to create an overview of the work of others. Finally, we assessed the level of activity in proactively reaching out to develop co-authorships by asking about the occurrence of (13) passive serendipity. We also measured the number of institutions, ranked coauthored papers and, to assess the tenure of researchers, the time span of collaborative publications in the conference.

In the absence of existing measurement instruments for co-authorship dispositions, our exploratory scale development process (MacKenzie et al, 2011) continued with a conceptualization of an initial item pool of reflective indicators (see Appendix B). The preliminary step of translating the general social capital dimensions to more operational co-authorship dispositions simplified the development of items. The instrument is a preliminary exploration of the domain and we wished to include diverse constructs. Consequently, we kept the survey short by minimizing the number of items per construct. New items were formulated as statements using terms known to researchers. We used a five-point Likert scale to measure survey respondents. Due to the relatively small size of the active community and the exploratory nature of our inquiry, we administered the survey with no pre-test. Nevertheless, we collected feedback about the survey from respondents. The feedback did not identify any substantial misinterpretation of the items.

The survey was offered to a total of 110 active IRIS community members. Conference participants were invited to complete a paper format during a general session at the 2009 IRIS Conference. Later, a link to an identical electronic version was sent via email to active members of IRIS who were not present at that session. We received 40 usable responses in paper format and three additional responses through the interactive survey form. In total, we received 43 usable responses or a response rate of 39%.

After data collection, we evaluated the validity of all multi-item constructs by computing Cronbach’s alpha coefficients (summarized in Table 2). The constructs ‘active relationship management’ and ‘improvement of work’ did not exhibit high levels of reliability. These two rather complex behavioural constructs might consist of several underlying dimensions and this suggests further inquiry could be a promising and necessary research objective. In our study, our goal was not to develop an instrument with a maximum of scale reliability, but rather to broadly identify and explore the diverse influences that may drive the social structures of the IS research community.

## Network data items and data collection

To study structural social capital and network-related factors, we used SNA with researchers as the nodes. We conceptualized interpersonal links or ties (i.e. edges) as representing the existence of at least two authors on any one article contributed to the IRIS conferences. Following Rulke & Galaskiewicz (2000), we measured the strength of the ties among group members.

We used additional common metrics of SNA to examine the individuals in the network. These are defined as direct contacts (i.e. direct co-authors are first degree authors, the second degree co-author is an indirect link. i.e. the co-author of the co-authors) or the betweenness centrality (see Appendix C for expanded definitions). For example, Burt (1995) described the betweenness centrality as a measure of interpersonal influence that an actor has on others and that arises from the actor’s network position between other actors. Direct contacts are a measure of the activity of an individual actor. This, then, is the number of direct co-authorships with another person. The count of papers indicates how many papers a researcher contributed as an author or co-author.

Our approach emphasizes the contribution to the community structure rather than individual efforts. Hence, we increased the interpersonal relationship strength by one for all participants of a joint paper, regardless of the number of co-authors. As our study focus was on collaboration, individually authored papers were not considered as they did not add to a person’s network embeddedness. Researchers who never have engaged in co-authorship of papers are maintained as isolated nodes in our network and are not linked to other authors. Single authors contribute to the value of the IRIS community, but not to our unit of analysis, the structural properties of the community network. Since all our survey respondents co-authored papers, the isolation of authors did not affect our quantitative research.

Further measures undertaken included as an indicator for relationship strength the average number of coauthored papers (link events in our network-analytical terminology) of a researcher and his/her contacts. The duration of participation quantifies the time in years from first to last participation and indicates how long the researcher was contributing to the observed IRIS community structure. For each act of co-authorship, the contributors’ countries of residence were manually coded based on the author information reported in the paper. Researchers may relocate in the course of their careers, for example when they transition from being a Ph.D. student to a research position. In the quantitative analysis, we cross-checked the coded origin of the country affiliation with the one reported by the survey respondents and found no inconsistencies. Respondents chose to be affiliated with their most recent place of work. To arrive at one country per researcher in our visualizations, we used the latest country for authors who had worked in multiple countries.

The data were collected through a variety of sources. These included the IRIS-History web pages that contained data from 1978 to 1998 (accessed in 2006: http://www .cs.utu.fi/IRIS/hist.htm). From 1999 to 2009 we obtained PDF files of the proceedings on CD, available on the IRIS Conference website: (http://www.scandinavian-iris .org). Finally, Markku Nurminen, one of the conference founders from Finland, provided us with ‘missing pieces’ from his personal archives.

## Statistical methods

Network measurement was based on the event-driven dynamic network analysis method and the corresponding software, Commetrix (Trier, 2008). After coding the network data according to the software’s format requirements, we used the system to compute and export a large array of different network properties for each survey respondent, including several specific measures that required dynamic network analysis and storage of actor attributes in the network data (see Appendix C). The exported network data were imported into SPSS along with the data from the surveys in order to develop one consistent data set linking the authors’ perceptions with their network variables. All data were anonymized. In addition, we used Commetrix to produce and visually explore animations of the evolution of the complete network.

The correlation analysis used at this stage of the research was the most appropriate strategy to uncover the multiple unknown dependencies between individual actors’ beliefs and the co-authorship network. As our data on individual networking preferences were collected based on an ordinal Likert-scale and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated the absence of normal distribution, we applied the nonparametric Spearman’s Rank Correlation. This method is particularly appropriate as it does not assume a cause-effect direction and hence addresses the general difficulty of defining dependent and independent variables in multilevel research that relates the individual actor level to a structural network level. It also lends itself to an exploratory approach, which aims to cover different relationships rather an a priori reduction of our theoretical model to only a few impact factors. Finally, non-parametric methods are also used with smaller sample sizes (Groebner et al, 2005), which enables us to explore some contrasts between senior and junior researchers.

## Results

Our study included two levels of analysis. First, an exploration at the network level of inter-organizational collaboration among researchers. By drawing upon our data of 32 years, we were able to visualize and discuss the evolutionary process of the community. Second, the results of our correlation analysis of the relationship between individual dispositions towards collaborating in co-authorships illustrate our unit of collaboration and the corresponding network position.

Within the central component, the network appears to be dominated by a few prominent researchers who have established densely connected sections around them. Such patterning suggests that link formation may not be efficient in structural terms, and implies the difficulty of transcending the researcher network to quickly form smaller networks. This also emphasizes the important role senior researchers play in actively integrating others into the network and introducing them to each other. A likely explanation of the long paths is the diversity of the researchers’ backgrounds and dispositions. There seems to be a low probability of compatibility with many other researchers, this makes linking to multiple authors less likely. This high diversity points to a reduced potential of co-authorship strategies that only focus on building multiple contacts.

Network visualization and analysis further helped us to identify structural patterns of international and national collaboration. Supporting Feld’s social circle theory, most researchers were primarily connected with co-authors from their own country (see the thin edges and the clear national clusters in Figure 1). Each Scandinavian country has its own cluster in the community, connected by national relationship links. The national clusters, however, all have some participants in the middle of the network who are connected to a core axis of international collaboration (Figure 1, thick edges). Seniority appears to drive the international scope and hence creates a hierarchical structure of national vs international relationships in the network.

## Individual drivers for collaboration networks

After analysing the network, we studied the impact factors identified for our five individual dispositions and their relationship with the corresponding network position. We aimed to present the richness of the interdependencies in Table 2. In this section, we report the core findings.

Looking at the descriptive statistics in column 2 of Table 2, we find that, in the observed community, researchers perceived not only the existing relationship links (m ¼ 3.8, SD ¼ 0.75) and the sympathy (m ¼ 3.7, $\mathrm { S D } = 0 . 9 1 ) $ , but also the creation of loose, large networks (m ¼ 3.6, SD ¼ 0.77) as most important. Hence, the relational and the structural dispositions are both relevant. Heterogeneity (m ¼ 2.8, SD ¼ 1.2) and topic focus (m ¼ 2.6, SD ¼ 0.96) were perceived as least important, indicating researchers gave low priority to the cognitive aspect of engaging in collaboration.

When we analysed the multi-level relationships of individual researchers and network-level measures, career level was significantly correlated with a central position in the network marked by many direct contacts (r ¼ 0.69 with Po0.01, henceforth indicated by ‘\*\*’, n ¼ 29) and central relationships, or betweenness centrality (r ¼ 0.63\*\*, n ¼ 29). A similar impact can be observed for the number of previous affiliations (institutions) that the researcher reported (degree centrality, $r = 0 . 5 3 ^ { \star \star } , n = 2 2 )$ This emphasized the prominent role of the author’s history for network measures (Trier, 2008), which is plausible, as networks represent the accumulated history of activity (Bourdieu, 1986).

Motivated by the important role of experience and tenure, we contrast the dispositions of junior and senior community members by differentiating the results of our non-parametric correlation analysis into the career categories of pre-Ph.D. (junior) and post-Ph.D. (senior). Due to the low sample size, this investigation is exploratory to support theory development. Interpretation focusses on the effect size (i.e. the size of the correlation).

At the junior researcher level, only a hierarchy focus, as a structural factor, related to elevated network positions: Researchers who prefer to link up with people from higher hierarchical levels also extended their reach or access to immediate and indirect collaborators significantly (r ¼ 0.47 with Po0.05, henceforth indicated by ‘\*’, n ¼ 15). However, such actors’ contacts were significantly less international. A relational disposition for the most part yielded lower network centrality, in other words, a more peripheral position in the community, as evidenced by the negative impact of the disposition to actively manage network links. This relationship management preference further coincided with a focus on maintaining existing ties, which related to reduced levels of centrality. Researchers who perceive sympathy as a driver of collaboration had significantly stronger relationships, as measured via link events per contact (r ¼ 0.48\*, n ¼ 14). They also assumed significantly less central network positions between other collaborators, i.e. a lower betweenness centrality $( r = - 0 . 5 7 ^ { \star } , ~ n = 1 4 ) .$ Also notable was the absence of impact of (a) a heterogeneous focus, (b) seeking feedback and (c) focusing new topics, as examples of cognitive dispositions. The focus on new topics, however, was related to a reduced number of peers and a less dense collaboration network around the researcher. Passive serendipity, or open-mindedly waiting for lucky incidents to improve the network position, appears to yield significantly more co-authorship activity (r ¼ 0.48\*, measured via link events, n ¼ 14) and a more central position in the community, betweenness centrality (r ¼ 0.57\*, n ¼ 14).

At the senior level, there were several apparent relationships, but only few exhibit significant correlations. For example, a topic focus and a preference for passive serendipity both have a negative correlation with the network position, whereas the maintenance of existing links and emphasis on getting an overview about others have a somewhat positive relation with centrality. Furthermore, maintaining existing co-authorships is related to a situation where researchers can reach many indirect contacts via their relatively few direct relationships (pulsetaker role, r ¼ 0.57\*, n ¼ 14).

The results suggest some differences between juniors and seniors. For example, high levels of engagement in active relationship management (e.g. with many physical meetings) showed a significant negative relationship to high levels of network centrality only for junior researchers (degree centrality, r ¼ -0.75\*\*, n ¼ 14). The significantly higher publication output of these researchers (r ¼ 0.69\*, n ¼ 26) and their lower participation duration suggested that the researcher may look beyond the community’s boundaries. A further difference existed for a passive serendipity, which was only positive for juniors (r ¼ 0.57\*, n ¼ 14). This suggests that, with increasing tenure, a passive approach is of less use or occurs less, for networking.

Table 2 also reports interdependencies between the various factors and largely supports our proposed dispositions. Our insights are summarized as follows:

Disposition 1 (relational focus): The proposed disposition with a relational focus exhibits no statistically significant correlations among the corresponding relational constructs.

Disposition 2 (cognitive focus): The disposition is more salient for junior researchers. The correlation between topic specialization and scouting the network for new topics (r ¼ 0.31\*, n ¼ 34) gives rise to the assumption of a broader cognitive focus in line with our expected disposition.

Disposition 3 (structural focus): Diverse interrelationships of structural constructs suggest the existence of a broader structural disposition in line with Burt (1995). The data indicate the disposition is perceived primarily at the junior level. For example, gearing towards large networks is related to mentor support (r ¼ 0.57\*, n ¼ 13). The disposition further appears to include a focus on people higher up in the hierarchy (r ¼ 0.41\*, n ¼ 32).

Disposition 4 (structural tenured broker focus): The negative correlation (r ¼ -0.50\*, n ¼ 15) between brokering and hierarchy focus can be explained by the fact that the design of our broker variable targeted only senior researchers who by virtue of their seniority cannot link to others higher up in the hierarchy. Finally, in line with Nahapiet & Ghoshal’s (1998) expectations about the co-occurrence of different aspects of social capital, we noted a range of significant interrelations between the structural and the relational focus, indicating that both dimensions may coexist.

Disposition 5 (serendipity focus): Finally, we identified the expected differentiation of active and passive networking. The role of serendipity is supported by the finding that high levels of serendipity focus coincide positively with a preference for sympathy (r ¼ 0.30\*, n ¼ 36), and negatively with active relationship development (r ¼ -0.40\*, n ¼ 19).

## Discussion and interpretation

Our network-level analysis indicates that the IRIS network exhibits structural properties similar to the structures of ICIS (Xu & Chau, 2006) and ECIS (Vidgen et al, 2007). An overview about these properties is presented in Table 3.

For example, a large central component dominates the network structure of the studied IS research community. In the large component, inter-organizational relationships form a central backbone, whereas at the periphery national relationships dominate. Consistent with Oh et al (2005) or Xu & Chau (2006), this suggests a hierarchical structure that exhibits higher-level network bridges, and indicates the importance of actors’ network tenure and position for spanning the scientific field with crossregional collaboration.

We found that structural and relational social capital dimensions were perceived as most important. The correlation between the proposed impact factors further suggests the likely existence of different general actor dispositions. They include, as sets of related beliefs, a relational focus, a cognitive focus, a structural focus, a structural broker focus and passive waiting for chance (serendipity).

Repeated participation in the community, related to seniority and career level, appears as a dominant source of network centrality in our IS research community. With increasing tenure, a focus on brokering between other, possibly more junior members of the community significantly contributes to the actors’ reach in the network, by access to directly or indirectly linked researchers.

The strong influence of this history effect of repeated contributions is also corroborated by the notable negative relationship between a strategic structural focus and network embeddedness. For example, a preference to establish loose and large networks related to lower levels of degree centrality. Hence, strategically growing the co-authorship network appears to be ineffective in such a large and complex network. The ‘accumulated history effect stated by Bourdieu (1986, p. 241) appears to be more prevalent than individual instrumental network strategies in larger networks in the long run. This indicates a reduced applicability of ego-centric social capital theories such as Burt’s (1995) structural holes concept, which implies strategic individuals who are actively striving for critical network positions. However, for junior members of a network, a structural disposition corresponded with a higher paper output, suggesting that these researchers also went beyond the observed community’s boundaries.

A topic-oriented disposition corresponds with reduced co-authorship activity or reduced levels of network centrality. It can be assumed that the positive effect of topic leadership is offset by the lower number of available specialists in that topic domain. This results in less central structural positions. However, despite the lack of network centrality effects, we noted that researchers who focus on topic aspects show significantly raised levels of co-authored papers as a quantitative output. Thus, they are likely to shape the IS research identity in other ways.

The relational disposition was only salient for junior researchers who appeared to perceive a close link between maintaining existing contacts, sympathy and relationship management. As far as the impact on network embeddedness is concerned, active relationship management related with lower degree centrality but appeared to improve the output quality through ranked papers. A similar negative relation to network embeddedness can be found for sympathy but it appears to yield stronger relationships, measured as papers per contact, for junior members. This suggests that a sympathy disposition and the resulting strong links do not necessarily connect the individual to very central actors. This conclusion is also indicated by lower closeness.

As an addition to existing social capital research, our data demonstrate the important role of a passive networking approach. For juniors, readiness for serendipity relates positively with network centrality, whereas for seniors there was no effect. It appears that serendipity preference relates to increased levels of openness for other researchers, which benefits younger researchers. This is supported by higher activity, indicated by number of co-authorships, which can bring about more contacts and results in higher network centrality. In contrast, for senior researchers, passive serendipity appears as an ineffective disposition, a possible indication of increased instrumentality and increased saturation with existing co-authorship contacts at that experience level.

The identified relationships invite the following recommendation to IS researchers:

For junior researchers, openness for new contacts – as indicated by an emphasis on passive serendipity, a low tendency to remain with existing networks and a professional attitude of collaboration and work development that persists even without requiring high levels of sympathy – relates to elevated network embeddedness in the researcher community.

For seniors, passive serendipity and quick building of a large contact network are not related to centrality. In addition to repeated participation, senior researchers need to develop an overview and active relationship management with a large existing network accompanied by a broker focus that brings them to the centre of the research community.

At a more general level, the relative importance of the different dispositional factors might reflect the social identity and research culture in the IS community. For example, in our predominantly Scandinavian community, factors such as sympathy and maintenance of existing ties were rated relatively high. These collaboration preferences seem to indicate a low pressure to publish in the studied community (Lyytinen et al, 2007) and a low perception of competition. Comparing this preference configuration to other research communities could provide an explanation for the observed differences in the cohesiveness and productivity of large multi-national research cultures (Avgerou et al, 1999; Galliers & Whitley, 2007). For example, the attributed importance of serendipity or sympathy across different research communities could serve as a useful indicator of the degree of instrumental and professional networking in order to increase research outcomes.

Finally, we note an inverse relationship between low social network centrality and high numbers of publi cations. Therefore, when network measures are evaluated, we should be careful not to confuse low centrality with low performance. People can have important achievements with very few colleagues or as a single author. A network perspective does not focus on the evaluation of such intellectual output but emphasizes an author’s contribution to the social identity of the IS research community.

## Limitations

In this complex setting of collaboration, the multitude of simultaneously operating influence factors imposes restrictions for the analysis of the specific role of individual factors. We addressed this issue by adopting an exploratory approach rather than focusing on only a few factors. A targeted analysis of individual relationships indicated by our study needs to be the subject of future research.

The exploratory character of our measurement instrument expresses only a first step towards a robust conceptualization, as evidenced in some constructs with lower values of Cronbach’s alpha. We preferred the inclusion of further relevant factors over measuring a particular factor with additional items, resulting in different numbers of items to measure the various constructs.

Our sample covers the core of currently active researchers, but in the light of studying three decades of scientific collaboration it is difficult, if not impossible, to gather data about other past activities to arrive at a higher sample size. Small samples are an inherent limitation when comparing network data of long periods of interactivity against current researcher’s perceptions, with the result that no existing study of co-authorship other than ours relates the actor to the network level. We further addressed this issue by restricting ourselves to exploratory insights. Finally, we note that a co-authorship network perspective attenuates the role of single authors. This invites further research on motives and situations that may explain when researchers decide to author a paper alone and what this implies for the community.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, our study sheds light on individual differences in the conceptualization of engaging in academic collaboration and the resulting impacts on the emerging network-level configuration of a large international community of IS researchers. The adopted network perspective accentuates the strong link between interrelated individual activities and the evolving social identity and output on the community level.

Our results suggest the existence of five different highlevel actor dispositions to engage in co-authorship with other researchers. These dispositions derive from and extend the three dimensions of social capital theory as proposed by Nahapiet & Ghoshal (1998). Our empirical exploration uncovered the important role of serendipity and sympathy for shaping the observed research community. Differentiating junior and senior researchers, we found that such a collaboration preference was only effective for junior academics. This and other differences between junior and senior researchers suggest a maturation process in the networking behaviour.

We further conclude that social network embeddedness in the IS community is primarily an accumulated history effect that is hard to influence via instrumental personal strategies alone. The results hence support the notion of network embeddedness as a valuable resource: The benefits of deriving value from such a position can be conceived as a complex capability that is hard to copy and thus provides sustainable competitive advantages (e.g. Kogut & Zander, 1992). Actors with high embeddedness also contributed with higher-level links that connected different national social circles, constituting the international backbone of the IRIS community.
