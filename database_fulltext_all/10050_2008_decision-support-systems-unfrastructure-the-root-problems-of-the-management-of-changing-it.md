## a b s t r a c t

Rapidly emerging IT underpins decision support systems, but research has yet to explain the IT management challenges of such rapid change. This study sought to understand the problems of rapid IT change and the interrelationships between them. It used structured interviews with 16 IT professionals and 246 useable responses from a survey of 1000 such professionals. After identifying <sup>fi</sup>ve problem categories, it proposed and tested theory that Vendor Competitiveness leads to Poor Quality, Incompatibility, and Management Confusion, and these increase Training Demands. Besides the theory, the research contributes a survey instrument and focal points to help IT managers better provide the infrastructure for DSS.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology is increasing in capability and decreasing in cost. At the same time, organizations are deploying new IT at a greater rate [17] and applying new IT at the center of more areas of the business to improve management decision making [65]. This state of continuous IT change is not only producing ways to use new IT to compete [25], but also elevating the importance of IT infrastructures [124]. Emerging technologies, such as enterprise systems, provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant decision support bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [58]. As a result, how well the introduction of new IT is managed determines its strategic impact [110]. Success in this turbulent environment requires change-ready IT capability, i.e. the ability to deliver strategic IT solutions quickly [30,91].

Because “the IT industry is complex, uncertain, and ever changing [102, p. 45],” the problems of managing IT today are growing more and more complicated. Moreover, complex interrelationships between IT management problems can exacerbate matters. Thus more needs to be known about the IT management problems that arise speci<sup>fi</sup>cally from rapid IT change and the interrelationships between them [12].

At the same time, combined with a corporate culture more aware of, accepting of, and most importantly, dependent on IT, such change has propelled IT into an increasingly visible role in more and more organizations. This has heightened the importance of managing IT in these organizations, and thus of understanding the challenge of managing today's rapidly changing IT. An understanding of the challenge of managing today's rapidly changing IT might enable management to increase the value IT delivers, an established concern of IT executives [79].

Information technology as de<sup>fi</sup>ned herein is any hardware or software used to build, operate, or maintain an organization's IS applications including decision support tools and technologies as well as the IT infrastructure of transaction processing systems, ERP systems, servers, networks, and B2C and B2B websites that enable those applications to function. IT thus comprises not only the decision support tools themselves, but also the tools for developing the underlying functional aspects of a DSS [18]. The study investigated the recent problems of the major IT projects in organizations.

The goals of this research were to better understand the problems of rapid ITchange and the interrelationships between them in order to improve the acquisition and implementation of new IT that provides the infrastructure for decision support systems. The research employed qualitative and quantitative data collection in two separate phases. It used the qualitative data from the <sup>fi</sup>rst to generate an instrument to gather the quantitative data in the second. It then applied both exploratory and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory data analyses as well as structural equation modeling to the quantitative to generate and test new theory to explain the intricacies of the challenges of rapid IT change. In doing so, it attempted to answer two research questions:

What problems from rapid ITchange affect IT management? How are the problems from rapid IT change interrelated?

## 2. Practical basis of the research

“One of the greatest dif<sup>fi</sup>culties confronting IT managers is adapting to rapid change [47, p. 1].” The challenge is not new. In recent years, the world has been changing so rapidly that managing in a steady state is no longer possible. “The need to adapt and change continuously has become a given in managerial life. In no other domain has this observation been more relevant than the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information technology [81, p. 109].” Such ITchange is replete with “startling innovations and drastic, unpredictable shifts in technology's direction [108, p. 62].” The change is not only rapid, but also is expected to continue into the future; in fact, the pace of change is accelerating [8,16,59,60,67,70,113].

Industry observers have suggested that this swiftly changing IT causes dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for IT management [24]. Traditional IT structures cannot cope with continual change and the rapid introduction and utilization of information technologies [21]. IT methodologies and processes appear not to keep pace with technology change [127]. New IT must be absorbed, mastered, and controlled. This demands the ability to apply new ideas and practices that in turn require abstract thinking, problem solving, and inference [85].

As a result, new IT is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage [19]. Realizing the potential of new IT while avoiding associated risks can pose a complex challenge to IT management. Continuous upgrading might keep companies constantly on the cutting edge, but such upgrading is fraught with dif<sup>fi</sup>culties [86]. Mistakes can be costly and IT managers cannot be experts on all emerging ITs. Because of the lengthy duration of IT acquisition and implementation processes combined with the uncertain economic life of a new technology [99], a new IT can even become obsolete before its initial use. This vulnerability to obsolescence is especially disconcerting [108], and can be exacerbated by the time required to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from new IT [82]. Indeed, surveys of executives have repeatedly con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the fact that changing IT is a major challenge to many organizations [37,39,94,109,123].

## 3. Theoretical basis: uncertainty caused by environmental change

Studies have long documented the problems associated with an uncertain environment. As uncertainty increases, problems occur [46]. For example, the uncertainty of emerging technologies has disrupted organizations and industries [36]. Researchers have portrayed a changing environment as causing problems to which organizations adapt [23,73,95,118,126]. Organizations try to adapt by ameliorating the problems through the reduction of the uncertainty [46].

IT is a dimension of the environment [80]. Rapid IT change brings uncertainty about the implementation, application, and support of the new IT itself. New IT – through its uncertainty - thus produces such problems as delays, cost overruns, and unexpected work in IT projects. In the context of such problems, organizations try to adapt through uncertainty reduction by, for example, training IT staff to be better prepared to apply new IT [3,111] and effectively timing the acquisition of new IT [99].

With this theoretical basis (stated once again more succinctly, that the uncertainty of environmental change causes problems to which organizations adapt), research has laid the foundation to address the problems of the management of IT in a changing technological environment. For example, Lewin [74] investigated change itself. He recognized the manner in which organizations change by proposing that they unfreeze from, move to, and refreeze in states of equilibrium.

In addition, research has considered how IT has affected the overall organization [4,66,92,112]. For example, it has examined the role of IT in change to the entire organization, but without a focus on the effect of IT change on IT management [92].

Research has also investigated organizational issues caused by a changing environment [35,69]. One study suggested a contingency theory where different environmental conditions (analogous to IT change) require different organizational designs [69]. Another viewed an organization as a hierarchical set of systems where changes in higher level systems (e.g., IT change) lead to changes in lower level ones [35]. However, neither of these studies speci<sup>fi</sup>cally considered IT change.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>c to this research, one study proposed that changing dimensions of the environment cause various IT management problems [71]. The study was based on structured interviews with 20 IS executives and on organizational research [40,42,64,66,97,98,118]. IT was one of <sup>fi</sup>ve environmental dimensions uncovered in that study. Several illustrative IT management problems were provided.

Subsequent research con<sup>fi</sup>rmed a subset of proposed categories of problems of new IT [12]. However, the initial, proposed categorization in that study (based on the qualitative assessment of interview comments from only sixteen subjects) and the subsequent reduction of those categories raised doubts about the resulting categorization. Moreover, the con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory analysis was initially unsolvable and required model re-speci<sup>fi</sup>cation merely to run. These problems suggested that an exploratory analysis might more meaningfully categorize the problems.

In summary, rapid IT change has been problematic to IT managers. However, it needs more direct attention in research. This study makes an early effort to directly examine the IT management problems caused by rapid IT change. Thus, it seeks to better understand the problems from rapid IT change that affect IT management. Under the presumption that some of the problems contribute to the severity of others, with one or more as root causes, it also seeks to understand how they might be interrelated.

## 4. Overview of research approach

The research applied accepted techniques for generating grounded theory by collecting a vast number of qualitative facts to achieve generality and by using diverse data from interviews to make the theory representative of everyday realities [49]. The research thus began with structured interviews to identify the problems from rapid IT change [29]. The problems formed the basis of a postal survey. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) reduced the number of items into a theoretically sound set of problem constructs [55], an accepted approach in DSS research [120]. This factor analysis thus answered the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, What problems from rapid IT change affect IT management?

Prior literature suggested a theory describing the relationships among the factors. Partial Least Squares (PLS) Graph version 3.0 was used to validate the measurement model and test the theory. The structural equation model in the PLS run thus answered the second question, How are the problems from rapid IT change interrelated?

A more detailed description of this approach follows. After elucidating the data collection and demographics, the analysis to answer each research question is explained.

## 4.1. Structured Interviews

This study began with the development of a <sup>fi</sup>eld survey based on structured interviews [29]. It followed a three step procedure of clarifying the concepts, developing initial indicators, and evaluating the indicators [38].

Sixteen subjects for the interviews were chosen from a list of IT professionals who were alumni of the business college of a major midwestern university. They were selected to represent diverse industries, IT organizations of different sizes, and various levels within their IT organizations to ensure a broad view of the effects of changing IT.

Interviews averaged 90 minutes. At the beginning of each interview, subjects received a description of the background and objectives of the research to help focus on projects that used IT new to the organization. The researchers explained the document orally to the subjects.

They then asked the subjects to identify the three or four most recently considered or implemented projects of which they had fairly detailed knowledge. For each project, they asked subjects to list any new ITs that were investigated for use in the project. Subjects were then asked to describe any problems the IT organization had with this new technology. The researchers characterized problems as anything that caused unexpected work or delay on a project. They frequently asked for elucidation to ensure they understood completely.

The same two researchers were present for all interviews. They took extensive notes, and reconciled and transcribed them shortly thereafter. With the subjects' permission, the researchers also recorded all of the interviews. They used the recordings to resolve any discrepancies during transcription. The transcribed notes included 142 problems.

No information was initially combined across interviews. In fact, diligent effort was made to collect a comprehensive set of problems.

## 4.2. Survey Generation

The researchers then analyzed these problems to produce the survey items. First, each problem was compared to all others and obvious duplicates were removed. For example, fourteen different problems in some way included user experience or learning time. These were all combined into the item, Time required to become proficient with new IT. (Survey items appear hereafter in italic.)

Second, the researchers compared each remaining item to all others, and looked for common themes. In other words, they sought pairs of items that included a similar problem. When they found such similarity, a single item was created to represent the common theme and thus further reduce obvious redundancy.

For example, one subject described how a technology did not work as advertised. A subject at another organization described the inability of a product to perform the anticipated functions. These two very similar actions resulted in Overselling of new IT capabilities by a vendor.

This iterative comparison of resulting items continued until no common themes remained. Thus the items were reduced from 142 to 45. The items were placed in a survey that asked subjects to consider the major development, implementation, or support efforts using new IT underway during the last three years about which they had fairly detailed knowledge. It then asked them to what extent each item was a problem attributable to new IT during these last few major development, implementation, and support efforts. The questions used a 1 to 7 scale where 1 meant "to no extent" and 7 meant "to a very great extent."

A pilot study was then conducted with <sup>fi</sup>ve IT professionals to validate the survey. The pilot was to ensure that the survey was clear and concise, and that items portrayed their intended meaning. Feedback was also sought on the length of the survey, its overall appearance, and how each participant would react to receiving it in the mail. Comments and suggestions were used iteratively to revise the survey and its cover letter.

During each pilot, the senior author met with each of the participants individually and brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviewed the purpose of the survey. The subjects were then asked to read the cover letter and complete the survey. They were also asked to write suggestions on the survey for improvement or to identify unclear meanings. The senior author took notes about the actions of the participants. After completion of the survey, that author obtained their feedback about it. All subjects found the time needed to complete the survey acceptable.

The comments of each participant were incorporated before meeting with the next. In addition to substantial improvement in the clarity of the survey, several other major contributions resulted from the pilot. For example, the items were further consolidated from 45 to 39.

## 5. Data analysis for question 1: What problems from rapid IT change affect it management?

The data analysis began with EFA to answer the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question (What problems from rapid IT change affect IT management?). The answers to this <sup>fi</sup>rst question would then be a set of constructs applied to resolve the second question (How are the problems resulting from rapid IT change interrelated?). Thirty-nine items were too many to make sense of, but a few categories could facilitate a clear understanding.

The EFA used the principle factor method with promax oblique rotation on the problem items. Oblique rotation is suggested for correlated factors [56]. Because the authors anticipated interrelationships among the problems, any resulting factors would be correlated. A suggested sample size for an EFA is a minimum of <sup>fi</sup>ve responses for each item in the analysis [56], and hence 246 responses were adequate for the EFA.

The EFA followed the eigenvalue-one [63] and percent of variance criteria [56] with a 5% cutoff to initially determine the number of factors to retain. Five factors initially met both criteria, and were retained. According to Hatcher [56], the proportion of variance accounted for is more appropriate than the eigenvalue-one rule for deciding the number of factors to retain in a factor analysis (whereas the eigenvalue-one rule is more appropriate in principle component analysis). The decision was therefore made to continue to retain factors that accounted for at least 5% of the variance.

## 6. Question 1 discussion: problem categories

The EFA produced <sup>fi</sup>ve IT problem categories: Training Demands, Incompatibility, Poor Quality, Management Confusion, and Vendor Competitiveness. These empirically answer the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, What problems from rapid IT change affect IT management? Each category of problem can produce delays, cost overruns, and unexpected work in IT efforts. They provide a succinct basis for an explanation of IT management problems due to rapid IT change.

The problems have some foundation in prior research and much support in the popular IT press. The current empirical research further establishes their relevance to the context of rapidly changing IT. Each is now brie<sup>fl</sup>y elucidated in terms of its connection to such change.

Training Demands, the most experienced problem category according to paired t-test comparisons (i.e., at pN.001, its mean of 5.07 was greater than the means of all of the other problem categories), illustrates the challenges of becoming pro<sup>fi</sup>cient with new IT. Indeed, organizations must increasingly cultivate desired IT skills [30]. However, “IT skills requirements represent a moving target… [111, p. 84]” Additionally, rapid ITchange and the growing number of emerging ITs require that IT professionals have a wider and wider variety of skills and knowledge [72,105]. The level of technical knowledge required has also increased for programmers, analysts and IT managers [119]. These increased knowledge requirements are attributable to the new IT options available to organizations.

Learning IT in today's time conscious environment is not easy [93] and existing IT professionals are not always positively inclined to reskill to newer technologies [3]. The need for differing reskilling strategies for varying employees [47] exacerbates the training problem. In addition, hiring staff with necessary skills is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult due to the ongoing shortage of necessary IT skills in the workforce [22,50,87,129] thus making the inability to obtain suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently quali<sup>fi</sup>ed personnel a major IT planning problem [116]. The challenges of keeping an IT staff current and productive when faced with constant rapid change are indeed formidable.

Incompatibility, the second most experienced problem (pN.01), demonstrates the need for IT to work together. IT organizations are no longer single vendor shops, but instead rely heavily on a combination of products from as many as 50 to 100 vendors [102]. Incompatibility in those products challenges these organizations, and may become increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult as more and more organizations connect electronically. Creating and maintaining adequate interfaces, in other words creating compatibility, between multiple IT is not easy. Industry standards are often lacking [53]. The IT organization must create or acquire software and hardware for such interfaces. This increases an already large workload in most IT organizations. Poor Quality includes new IT failure to perform properly (both explainable and unexplainable) as well as inadequate IT documentation. Poor quality can render new IT useless. Products too often make it to market with serious <sup>fl</sup>aws [100,106,107,121]. In 1998, poor quality software led to 85 billion dollars in lost productivity [51]. Poor IT quality challenges IT management.

Management Confusion about how to exploit new IT's potential [68] and manage new IT implementation efforts [19] poses another problem. IT managers have long struggled with the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and assessment of the best emerging IT among the growing number of choices [28,41,61,80]. They realize the importance of staying informed about the many new ITs [101], but struggle to do so. The rapidity of IT change exacerbates the problem.

The ongoing prominence of IT change in IT management surveys further supports the assertion that it confuses management. A survey of 50 CIOs in the U.S. indicated that rapidly changing technologies was their second biggest issue [6]. Another of 364 North American CIOs showed that capitalizing on advances in IT was a top management issue [109]. A third survey of 43 senior U.S. executives revealed that keeping pace with changing technologies was currently a major issue [94], and a fourth of 409 such executives found that 78% projected that doing so will be one of their biggest challenges in the future [35]. More recent surveys consistently identify complexity reduction as a top ten, key issue for IT executives [78,77,79].

Finally, Vendor Competitiveness poses a problem for IT organizations. Just as in other industries, competition among IT vendors has stiffened [7,48,103]. In their zeal for market share, vendors seem often to release their products prematurely. They may also market their products with excessive enthusiasm. Some vendor product changes “appear to be just for the sake of change and to increase vendor revenues [78, p. 275]” and serve little business purpose.

Vaporware – de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Webster as “a new computerrelated product that has been widely advertised but is not yet available” – illustrates this over enthusiasm. It causes problems for IT organizations when actual delivery is delayed or never happens as promised [9,45,62]. Despite the confusion vaporware causes, some software <sup>fi</sup>rms have used it in a strategic manner [11].

Simply overselling of capabilities is also common. For example, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission sued IT vendors, claiming that the <sup>fi</sup>rms misrepresented the capabilities of their IT [104]. An IT executive in another organization summarized the situation by bluntly asserting, “They [IT vendors] lie to us!” [83, p. 15].

Some IT vendors lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient support for their own products. Lack of adequate support hampers IT organizations [114,123]. IT vendors have a strong interest in selling products [84] and hence, some pressure IT managers to upgrade too often.

## 7. A framework for question 2: How are the problems from rapid it change interrelated?

This research sought to understand the interrelationships among the problem categories under the presumption that some of them contribute to the severity of others, with the possibility of one or more as root causes. A closer look at the problems suggests a root problem and their potential relationships. The relationships are expressed as six propositions.

First, the rush to make products available may confuse the vendors' customers, namely IT managers [34]. The shear numbers of new IT increase complexity, and alone can confuse IT management [28,41,61,77,80]. “Many previous technologies have been so oversold that it is now sometimes dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to tell legitimate enthusiasm from marketing hype” [43, p. 8). The lack of unbiased information about new products also makes staying informed about and choosing the products very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult [43]. Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>rst proposition asserts:

## P1. As Vendor Competitiveness increases, Management Confusion increases

In rapidly evolving markets such as IT, competition to be <sup>fi</sup>rst to market leads to a premature market entry; <sup>fi</sup>rms cannot wait for the optimal time to launch new products [89,90]. This rush to market with its focus on features may give compatibility with others' IT a lower priority among vendors. Despite the growing popularity of open standards and interoperability, proprietary control over products can still be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of vendors' competitive strategies [26,57]. To help lock customers into their products or delay the adoption of competitive ones, vendors increase incompatibility through proprietary enhancements or extensions to existing standards, and even create perceptions of incompatibility [26] as they oversell their products. Incompatibility problems are exacerbated, especially during adaptation, when vendors rush to market without adequate support or expertise on their own new products [115]. Hence, the proposition:

## P2. As Vendor Competitiveness increases, Incompatibility increases

Similarly, in their haste companies may sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce product quality by speeding products to market with little testing to adequately <sup>fi</sup>nd and remove errors [34]. Although <sup>fi</sup>fty percent of the underlying cause of software bugs is simply carelessness [88], IT vendors still believe there are diminishing returns on testing [117] and still rush products to market. Thus vendors focus on new features at the expense of quality [20,96].

In the rush to be <sup>fi</sup>rst to market, early entries are often inferior to later products [1]. Economists have even asserted “The preemptive aspect of the competition induces <sup>fi</sup>rms to ‘spoil’ the market by introducing inferior innovations” [76, p. 106]. In summary, these assertions suggest the proposition:

## P3. As Vendor Competitiveness increases, Poor Quality IT increases

Because Management Confusion involves choosing and staying informed about numerous emerging ITs “to recognize opportunities to apply new technologies as they become available” [105, p. 33], greater knowledge is required. Training to develop that knowledge would be expected to reduce uncertainty [46,111], and therefore address this problem. However, meeting the demand for this training is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly, and thus is viewed as a problem by IT management. Hence, the proposition:

P4. As Management Confusion increases, Training Demands increase

Furthermore, Incompatibility requires that IT professionals understand IT at a deeper level than they do when products integrate smoothly. Developer skills are thus an important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the success of implementing new technology [52]. “Rapidly changing technologies that are powerful but immature have generated a renewed emphasis on the technical quali<sup>fi</sup>cations of IT staff…”’ and thus Incompatibility requires increased “technical skills to build bridges between old systems and new [105, p. 33]” to enable them to work together. When it invests in a new IT, “the organization may end up locked into a stranded technology, <sup>fi</sup>nding it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to hire experienced staff” [43, p. 20], thus leaving it little choice but to train staff to understand the new IT in order to create the interfaces between multiple ITs. Hence:

## P5. As Incompatibility increases, Training Demands increase

Poor Quality requires a thorough understanding of the new IT to enable debugging and correcting errors. In the presence of both explainable and unexplainable errors in new IT, IT specialists need to better understand the IT in order to make it work. Hence more and better training is demanded. Inadequate documentation, a form of Poor Quality, exacerbates this demand because IT specialists typically rely on such written information to <sup>fi</sup>nd and resolve errors. In fact, in the absence of training, IT specialists may waste valuable time muddling through a problem [75]. In one study, learning IT informally without classes was the third (of 34) most used way of coping with the problems of rapid ITchange although it was only the 27th most effective [13], thus further suggesting the demand for formal training. A <sup>fi</sup>nal proposition states:

## P6. As Poor Quality increases, Training Demands increase

Fig. 1 illustrates the above discussion. The combination of the problems and their proposed relationships thus represents new theory. These problem relationships illustrate the presumption that some problems contribute to the severity of others in that Vendor Competitiveness (with the lowest mean at 3.96, a value lower than all others at pb.05 or better) is expected to contribute to Poor Quality, Incompatibility, and Management Confusion (with higher means of 4.21, 4.52, and 4.19 respectively) which in turn would exacerbate Training Demands (with the highest mean at 5.07).

## 8. Model analysis

PLS Graph version 3.00 was applied to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the measurement model and test the path model. PLS Graph is a component-based, structured equation modeling tool that employs least squares estimation in contrast to the covariancebased approach used by such tools as LISREL, EQS, and AMOS. PLS is often used because it places fewer demands on sample size, scales, and data distribution assumptions [27,44,125].

The <sup>fi</sup>ve problem categories, with their manifest variables loading on them in the EFA, served as latent variables. A sample size of 246 meets the criterion of ten times the largest number of re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators for any latent variable in the model [27].

The measurement model was <sup>fi</sup>rst con<sup>fi</sup>rmed through PLS. Composite reliability, as well as convergent and discriminant validity were tested [10,27,128]. Composite reliabilities are similar to Cronbach's alpha, and ranged from .85 to .94, thus exceeding the accepted cutoff of .70 [2,32,128].

## 9. Question 2 discussion: problem category interrelationships

The data analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the causal model and answered the second research question, How are the problems from rapid IT change interrelated? The results were thus consistent with the expectation that Vendor Competitiveness, as root problem, increases Management Confusion (P1), Incompatibility (P2), and Poor Quality (P3). The rush to move products rapidly into the market would seem to increase the complexity of IT management, reduce their ability to work together, and reduce the quality of the many new IT products. Interestingly, Vendor Competitiveness had a very strong impact on all three dependent variables with pb.001.

The results were also consistent with the expectation that Management Confusion(P4), Incompatibility (P5) and Poor Quality(P6) increase Training Demands. The increased complexity of IT management, the inability of diverse new IT products to function smoothly together, and insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently effective products would seem to increase the need for education and training. Management Confusion had a much stronger impact on Training Demands than did Incompatibility and Poor Quality with pb.001 versus pb.05. Perhaps this highlights a greater managerial than technical role in the building of information systems.

## 10. Implications for research

The goals of this research were to better understand the problems of rapid IT change and the interrelationships between them in order to improve the acquisition and implementation of new IT that provides the infrastructure for decision support systems. The EFA answered the question, What problems from rapid IT change affect IT management? The causal model analysis answered the second question, How are the problems from rapid IT change interrelated? Thus, the research met its goals of better understanding the problems of rapid IT change and interrelationships between them.

By doing so, it provides a foundation for the further study of how rapid IT change affects IT management. More focused examination of each individual problem and interrelationship could be useful to facilitate a better understanding of the challenge. Comparisons of problems across industries, managerial level or experience, and even speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies might also be useful.

This study considered the perceptions of experienced IT professionals. Validation studies should use other samples. Users, user managers, and IT managers would be appropriate subjects. Future researchers should also use multiple respondents in each organization. Also, future studies should seek to develop and employ more objective measures of problems.

The current study investigated recent problems with any new IT, including decision support tools and enabling infrastructure. Future research might more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine the problems of decision support systems, and compare and contrast them with those of the infrastructure technologies that enable them.

Research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed coping mechanisms by which IT managers deal with the problems of rapid IT change [13–15]. Future research should show how such strategies are related to and work in resolving the problems described here. Additional research could determine which responses are applied to particular problems and which responses are most successful at addressing particular problems. Studies could also examine particular responses in more detail.

A limitation of factor analysis is that it can eliminate potentially important items – in this case, especially severe problems – simply because they do not combine with other items and thus load on a particular factor. The current study followed a conventional approach for survey development and data analysis [29,55], but better measures might also be needed. Different wording and collapsing of redundant items might have resulted in the emergence of different factors from the analysis. Furthermore, the items might have been well constructed, but simply unrelated to other items. Hence, additional items might be necessary to identify additional factors. Future research might thus more closely consider the items dropped from the factor analysis in this study.

Another stream of research should investigate the change in IT itself. The current study used existing literature to establish the prevalence of IT change. However, change can take place in many forms and all ITs are not the same. Changes in different ITs may have diverse effects. Research could investigate the nature of changes taking place and their effect on IT management problems and responses. Sample research questions might be:

What is the nature of the change in IT experienced by IT management?

What is the pace of this change in IT and does the pace affect the problems encountered?

Does the nature of the change have an effect on the problems experienced and the responses used to address them?

Finally, all of these research efforts could also consider different organizational characteristics. Various sizes and types of overall organizations, industries, or IT organizations themselves could be independent variables used to determine if the problems and their interrelationships differ based on organizational characteristics.

## 11. Implications for practice

This research identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve problem categories - Vendor Competitiveness, Poor Quality, Incompatibility, Management Confusion and Training Demands. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide focal points for IT managers for examining their own organizational problems. Such managers should understand the extent to which they encounter them and how they respond to them. Such examination could help them avoid or reduce the impact of the problems.

In addition to the problem categories, the individual items provide the opportunity for similar introspection. Table 2 shows the extent that organizations in the current study experienced each item. IT managers should assess the extent they encounter each in order to benchmark their organizations against others. The managers should also assess their response to the items. Both assessments might stimulate better ways to reduce or avoid problems.

Of special concern was Vendor Competitiveness because it was the root cause in the model. Thus, IT managers should pay close attention to interaction with vendors. They should be more wary of vendor marketing claims and pressures to adopt new products prematurely. Prior to product acquisition, they should verify the level of support that vendors actually provide.

They should also recognize the importance of Training Demands, the most experienced problem from rapid IT change. They might try to be more careful not to underestimate the training required about new IT and the time required to become pro<sup>fi</sup>cient at implementing the new IT. Responding to such Training Demands is essential for maintaining a skilled workforce.

To deal with Management Confusion, IT managers should recognize the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty staying informed about emerging ITs as well as the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty choosing among them. They could implement more careful strategies to assess the increasing number of such ITs. They might also try to prevent fading enthusiasm for recent IT investments from causing them to jump prematurely to newer ITs when they become popular.

To deal with Incompatibility, managers could strive to implement new IT more compatible with their existing IT. They might also seek tools to better handle inadequate interfaces and incompatibilities between multiple ITs. Finally, to address Poor Quality, IT managers should demand fewer errors in and better documentation of new IT.

This research also has implications for IT vendors. It suggests that they be sensitive to IT managers' perceptions of them. The current study implies unfavorable perceptions. To be better business partners, vendors should both reassess their marketing strategies and increase efforts to reduce the problems of Poor Quality, Incompatibility, Management Confusion, and Training Demands.

## 12. Conclusion

This paper makes a variety of contributions to DSS research and practice. First, it raises researchers' and practitioners' awareness of the challenges of rapidly changing technology. It does so by identifying <sup>fi</sup>ve categories of the problems of such change: Vendor Competitiveness,Poor Quality, Incompatibility, Management Confusion, and Training Demands.

The paper contributes by generating new theory, as well as by demonstrating how to combine qualitative and quantitative data collection efforts, along with published research, in order to do so. The new theory asserted that increased Vendor Competitiveness leads to Poor Quality, Incompatibility, and Management Confusion, and that these three increase Training Demands, and the analysis supported the six propositions.

The research further contributes by stimulating future investigation of the problems of rapid IT change and of the ways by which organizations can react to them. It furnishes a new instrument with which to conduct further study of rapid IT change. Finally, it provides focal points to help IT managers examine their own problems, and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on how to respond to them.

In conclusion, successful decision support systems depend on an IT infrastructure that gathers, stores, and provides appropriate information. However, IT is changing rapidly, and IT managers struggle with the resulting uncertainty. They need to understand the problems of such change and their interrelationships. Little past research has directly addressed this issue but this study does so. IT change is unlikely to decrease in the near future. More likely, it will increase. Perhaps by understanding it, IT managers will deal with it better.
