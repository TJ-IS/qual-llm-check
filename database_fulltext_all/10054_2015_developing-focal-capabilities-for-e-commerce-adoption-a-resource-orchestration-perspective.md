## A B S T R A C T

Although an increasing number of manufacturing enterprises have engaged in e-commerce activities in recent years, there are few successful examples. We utilize the notion of resource orchestration as a theoretical lens through which to investigate the nature of the e-commerce adoption process. The findings generate a process model that can be used to explain how resources are orchestrated in the successful adoption of e-commerce by manufacturers: the predominant competitive environment of each phase gives rise to a specific focal capability that is developed through resource-focused actions. This research benefits both academics and practitioners by contributing to cumulative theoretical developments and by offering practical insights.

## 1. Introduction

E-commerce is essential for enterprises because it improves their understanding of customer needs and of the products and services available in the market [6,55,69]. Since 2011, an increasing number of traditional manufacturers have engaged in such initiatives. By conducting e-commerce, manufacturers can eliminate distributors and wholesalers; optimize internal processes; reduce the production period, inventory and circulation [8]; and thus obtain higher margins [33]. However, although some manufacturers, such as Dell, General Electric and Estee Lauder [25,67], have achieved tangible improvements in their operational efficiency and revenue generation by adopting e-commerce, some firms have been less successful. Researchers and practitioners have struggled to determine the optimal conditions for the adoption of e-commerce by manufacturers [25,26,42].

Existing studies have focused considerable attention on the factors that facilitate or inhibit e-commerce adoption [18,25,41]. The findings generally fall into two categories – the strategic value of certain information technologies to top managers and the factors that influence the adoption of information technology (IT) [19], emphasizing the importance of the perceived benefits, strategic commitment to e-commerce, external pressures, organizational readiness, information system (IS) characteristics and e-commerce capabilities, among others [19,20,25]. Such studies reveal why enterprises adopt e-commerce and recognize the fundamental role of resources. For example, Grandon and Pearson [19] note the importance of financial and technological resources, Zhu et al. [70] find that large firms enjoy ‘‘resource advantages’’ in e-commerce adoption, and Huy et al. [28] observe that the scarcity of necessary resources is the major challenge in the implementation of e-commerce.

Although the ‘‘why’’ question has been investigated in many studies, the ‘‘how’’ question (i.e., ‘‘How do manufacturers adopt e-commerce?’’) has yet to be fully explored because of a lack of attention to the e-commerce adoption process [27,41]. Process theories focus on sequences of activities to explain how particular outcomes evolve over time [49]. Process models can enable scholars and practitioners to understand the underlying mechanisms involved in such processes [22].

Several scholars have recognized this problem and have adopted the process perspective to investigate e-commerce adoption [36,40,41]. Such studies have adopted two alternative perspectives: a factor-oriented perspective [31,40,61] and a stageoriented perspective [12,48,66]. Factor-oriented studies segment the adoption process into several stages, such as the initiation, decision and implementation stages, and investigate the facilitators and inhibitors in each stage. Stage-oriented studies, by contrast, focus on the adoption stages per se, such as the presence, transaction portal integration and enterprise integration stages, and investigate the features of each stage, such as communication channels and e-commerce functions. Both types of studies acknowledge the fundamental role of resources in the adoption process [31,40,41] and help answer the ‘‘how’’ question to some extent. Nonetheless, the precise manner in which manufacturers adopt e-commerce remains unknown.

Although resource-based theory (RBT)<sup>2</sup> provides a foundation for investigations of how manufacturers adopt e-commerce [47,50,58], the most recent developments in RBT suggest a new direction. Specifically, recent studies observe that managers’ resource-focused actions are more important than resources per se in achieving competitive advantages [24,32,52], given that enterprises exhibit different levels of performance even when they own similar resources [53]. Thus, Sirmon et al. [52] use the term ‘‘resource orchestration’’ to describe how managers achieve resource-based competitive advantages.

In this paper, we adopt both RBT and a resource orchestration perspective to explore how manufacturers adopt e-commerce. RBT is recognized as the fundamental theory in e-commerce research [47,69,72], whereas the resource orchestration concept provides an additional theoretical lens through which to discuss resource management actions that reflect the dynamic nature of resources [2]. By investigating the e-commerce adoption processes of manufacturers, this study not only helps reveal the nature of manufacturers’ e-commerce adoption but also adds to the cumulative theoretical development of the resource orchestration perspective. We address this research goal by analyzing the successful e-commerce adoption process of China’s Haier Group, one of the largest white goods manufacturers in the world.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. RBT: from resources to capabilities

A firm is basically a collection of resources that prompt or constrain firm growth [46]. Generally, a firm’s salient resources consist of physical, human and organizational capital, and those that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable create competitive advantages [3]. Hence, resources are one of the most important themes for both enterprises and scholars, and RBT is formed and adapted to dozens of research fields.

Nonetheless, the static nature of RBT is highly controversial, as the dynamic nature of the environment and its effect on resources have attracted increasing attention. Consequently, RBT has been supplemented by a dynamic capability perspective to address the realities of a high-velocity market, rapid technological change and other environmental changes [57,59,71]. Dynamic capability refers to the ability of a firm to achieve new forms of competitive advantage by renewing technological, organizational and managerial resources to achieve congruence within the changing business environment [14,45,59]. This capability emphasizes the importance of the continuous reconfiguration of resources based on environmental changes and emphasizes that resource reconfiguration enables distinct capabilities within a firm [44,59]. Notably, the transformation from RBT to dynamic capability allows for an enhanced explanation of how improved organizational performance may be achieved by firms that can perform an activity more effectively than their competitors with otherwise similar resources [5]. For example, Samsung began with few resources with which to develop semiconductor products. However, through the license of a 64K DRAM design from Micron and the purchase of a design from Zytrex, Samsung assimilated and imitated the technology and ultimately gained the ability to develop innovative products [34]. By contrast, Sony, a former technology giant, lost its leading position because of its choice of BETA rather than VHS technology.

Given the importance of dynamic capabilities, some studies have further discussed its structure and agreed that there are different levels of dynamic capabilities [1]. The focal dynamic capability is the firm-level value-creating exploratory capability that leads to superior performance from a radical innovation perspective [35]. Additionally, complementary dynamic capabilities are basic-level capabilities, such as the resource base itself and the creation and extension of the resource base [10,35].

## 2.2. Toward a resource orchestration view of e-commerce adoption

In addition to its failure to consider the dynamic environment, RBT is criticized for overlooking resource management actions. RBT posits the static possession of resources, whereas some recent studies focus on managers’ actions with respect to resources and suggest that only when resources are managed effectively can competitive advantages materialize [23,52,65]. Consistent with this logic, the work of Sirmon et al. [52] develops the concept of ‘‘resource orchestration,’’ which refers to how managers achieve resource-based competitive advantages. Sirmon et al. [54] also propose a framework to describe resource orchestration actions: structuring, bundling and leveraging. Structuring refers to the structuring of a resource portfolio, including acquiring, accumulating and divesting; bundling is the process of using resources to build capabilities, including stabilizing, enriching and pioneering; and leveraging emphasizes leveraging capabilities in the marketplace to create value, including mobilizing, coordinating and deploying. Thus, resource orchestration provides a bridge between uncertain environments and capabilities (Fig. 1).

The external environment is continuously changing. To cope with this dynamic environment, enterprises must reconfigure their resources [44,59], i.e., orchestrate their resources by acquiring, renewing, structuring, bundling and conducting other resourcefocused actions [41,52,59]. Enterprises can thus generate new capabilities [44,59]. These changes in resources, resource orchestration and capabilities in prominent enterprises, especially when the number of the enterprises reaches a certain level, will result in changes in the environment. Thus, enterprises are forced to become involved in the subsequent round of resource reconfigu ration and capability building.

Indeed, the resource orchestration concept has already been incorporated into the study of e-commerce [41] and e-government [56]. Although relevant studies remain scarce, extant studies cover all three salient aspects of resource orchestration: resources, the environment and action. Taher [56] identifies three types of organizational resources involved in resource orchestration – precedency resources, dependent resources and upshot resources – and investigates the relationships among them in accordance with the impressionability perspective. Wang et al. [64] find that resource structuring plays a more important role in the business value of information technology creation in a stable environmental context, whereas capability building is more important in dynamic environments. Montealegre [41] identify specific resource orchestration actions in the three phases of capability development in support of an e-commerce strategy: (1) developing the capability to strategize through global benchmarking and training, learning from past experience and history, and absorbing knowledge as a unified group at the top of the organization; (2) developing greater flexibility through the integration of resources into core activities and experimentation as well as the investment in, leveraging and co-option of resources; and (3) integrating and engendering trust through internal commitment, investment in complementary infrastructure and the strengthening of external relationships. However, although these studies discussed the relationships among the environment, resources and capabilities from different perspectives, none has simultaneously focused on all three constructs to reveal their relationships with one another. Furthermore, although Montealegre’s work [41] presented an indepth analysis of the resource orchestration-capability relationship, the sample used was a service enterprise, and the ways in which manufacturers conduct e-commerce adoption remains unknown.

## 3. Research methodology

The case research methodology is particularly appropriate for this study for several reasons. First, this paper aims to answer the ‘‘how’’ question (i.e., ‘‘How do manufacturers adopt e-commerce?’’), and the case study method is particularly useful for addressing such a question [7]. Second, the adoption process concerns the ‘‘how’’ question, and the case study method is suitable for a process-based analysis [21].

Given the research question, the case selected was subject to conditions. First, the case organization should be a manufacturing enterprise, which means that its core competences are R&D, production, marketing and service. Second, the sample enterprise must have adopted an e-commerce business model that can be used to investigate manufacturers’ e-commerce adoption processes. Third, the sample enterprise should be reasonably large and sufficiently complex in terms of its operational activities to allow for a substantive set of resources and capabilities to be studied. The case of Haier is particularly appropriate for our purpose, as Haier is a large-scale manufacturing enterprise with appropriate R&D, production, marketing and service competences, and it successfully adopted e-commerce after a series of complex changes.

Data were collected in two steps. Research access was first negotiated and granted in September 2010. To accurately determine the appropriate direction of data collection and to facilitate on-site interviews, we systematically collected secondary data from a variety of sources, including newspapers, magazines, books and the Internet. Meanwhile, we selected and confirmed the theoretical lens [43] that we would employ in the study and read both the classic and current literature. The collection of secondary data, and the adoption of the RBT approach and the resource orchestration perspective enabled us to construct a preliminary theoretical model [43], which we used as a guide for subsequent on-site data collection and analysis.

On-site data collection was then conducted at Haier’s headquarters in September 2010 and January 2013. We applied the topdown interview method [43], interviewing a total of 46 informants (Table 1), including the senior vice president, the CIO, and department directors, among others. Each interview lasted approximately 60–90 min, and the questions were customized to the informants. Each digitally recorded interview was then transcribed. In total, the interviews formed a document of more than 200 pages. The transcript, along with the secondary data that we collected before the interview, allowed for triangulation, which enabled greater substantiation of the constructs and hypotheses [15].

To organize the large volume of data, we focused on two themes: the history of Haier and its e-commerce adoption process. The exhibition of the history of Haier could facilitate the development of people’s familiarity with the company, thus allowing them to become acquainted with its e-commerce adoption context, which is important to the understanding of adoption practices and theories [13].

Focusing on the three constructs – the environment, resource orchestration and capabilities – in Fig. 1, we temporally organized the data to completely depict Haier’s e-commerce adoption process and identified three important substages: the introduction of a call center, the introduction of an online sales channel and the integration of online and offline networks. The first substage enabled Haier to introduce and deploy a basic IT system, which was then continuously extended and supported the adoption of e-commerce as well as instilled the notion of ‘‘e-thinking’’ into Haier’s people, culture and strategy. The importance of the second substage is that the online sales channel is the prototype for its e-commerce, and thus, the introduction of the online sales channel marked the beginning of the e-commerce adoption process. Additionally, the integration substage indicated how online and offline networks were integrated and finally achieved success.

Data analysis was performed concurrently with data collection to take advantage of the flexibility that the case study research methodology affords [15]. Based on the emerging data, to facilitate the examination of e-commerce adoption in different competitive conditions, we divided events and activities into three distinct phases: the introduction of the call center, the introduction of the online sales channel and the integration of online and offline networks. The data collected from each interview were sorted according to a pre-identified research theme and were coded using ATLAS.ti, a type of qualitative data analysis software. Finally, we identified the relevant features of the environment, resource orchestration actions and capabilities of each stage. The preliminary theoretical model was then validated and revised accordingly. The data analysis followed an iterative process of shifting our focus among the empirical data, the relevant literature and the emerging model until theoretical saturation was achieved [43].

## 4. Case description

## 4.1. Organizational background

Headquartered in Tsingtao, China, Haier was established in 1984, when it was merely a small-scale refrigerator manufacturer. After 30 years of operation, however, Haier has become the world’s first white goods brand [17], and in 2012, it was ranked as one of the 50 most innovative companies in the world [62]. The company has more than 80,000 employees in 17 countries. In 2012, its turnover and profits reached \$27 billion and \$1.5 billion, respectively. Haier’s development can be divided into five stages: brand building, diversification, globalization, global branding and networking.

Brand building (1984–1990). In the 1980s, when the Chinese home appliance market was still small and many other companies were rushing to increase production, Haier did not blindly pursue production but focused on improving product quality. This effort made Haier famous for its product quality, and the company achieved a competitive advantage when the market was oversupplied.

Diversification (1991–1997). In 1991, competition became extremely fierce because of the increasing number of home appliance manufacturers, leading to large-scale mergers and acquisitions (M&As). Haier, similar to other powerful enterprises, initiated M&As and implemented a diversification strategy. By acquiring 18 enterprises, the company expanded its business scope from the manufacture of refrigerators to that of various types of white goods. Meanwhile, to cope with fierce competition, Haier began to focus on building its service system, which in turn helped the company gain competitive advantages over rivals and avoid price wars.

Globalization (1998–2004). In 1998, Haier adopted a globalization strategy and became committed to creating its own brand. To fulfill this goal, the company first entered the German, American and other developed markets to establish brand recognition and then entered the markets of developing countries through use of this brand. Meanwhile, Haier gradually established its design, manufacturing and marketing facilities and teams overseas.

Global branding (2005–2011). In 2005, Haier upgraded its globalization strategy and implemented a global branding strategy. The prevalence of e-commerce in China compelled Haier and other manufacturers to pay closer attention to user demand, enhancing the central position of users. To accomplish this goal, Haier not only leveraged its own resources to serve users but also made use of resources scattered around the world and integrated global R&D, manufacturing and marketing resources to create a global brand.

Networking (2012–present). In December 2012, to more effectively adapt to the e-commerce era, Haier implemented a networking strategy to direct and encourage employees. This strategy had two aspects: building network-based marketing, logistics and service systems to quickly and accurately meet customer requirements and building a nodular enterprise to cross corporate boundaries and utilize superior resources beyond those of Haier.

## 4.2. The introduction of a call center (1997–2007)

In the late 1990s, competition in the Chinese home appliance market was extremely fierce because of the increasing number of manufacturers. To gain a greater market share, manufacturers not only focused intensively on product quality but also engaged in price wars. However, at the time, services were overlooked. A senior manager in the marketing department recalled the following:

Although at that time, most consumers emphasized prices, we knew their attention would divert to services.

Therefore, to overcome the competition and not become involved in price wars, Haier, unlike other manufacturers, sought to develop and improve its services. To evaluate service quality, Haier first used telephone interviews and, in 1997, introduced a call center. A senior manager in the service department explained the introduction of the call center:

Consumer feedback was not limited to service quality but also included suggestions for product design, function and quality, etc. This information was valuable, and the better use of this information could help Haier improve its extant products and services and develop new items. . . In addition, many potential consumers required assistance and might provide valuable information.

By leveraging the call center, Haier was able to effectively manage (potential) consumers’ feedback regarding products and services. Information was then categorized and transferred to the R&D, production and service departments. The senior vice president commented as follows:

The call center replaced marketing employees and became the most important information source for Haier in understanding consumers’ needs. And we knew what to do next. Consequently, Haier was able to change its production mode from ‘‘products to consumers’’ to ‘‘consumers’ demands to products’’. . .In 2000, Haier developed the website to exhibit and promote new products and services.

## 4.3. The introduction of an online sales channel (2008–2010)

Since 2000, Gome, Suning and other retail chain enterprises have undergone rapid development and have come to control the home appliance sales channels. Manufacturers had no choice but to unconditionally cooperate with any promotions from retail chain enterprises. The popularity of e-commerce, especially with the emergence of bulky goods transactions on the Internet, offered manufacturers the opportunity to overcome the control of home appliance channels by retail chain enterprises. A senior manager at E-Haier, Haier’s official online store, described the situation as follows:

Although the Chinese home appliance market was dominated by offline sales channels at the time, the annual growth rate of online sales reached 85%. Furthermore, consumer groups are changing, and post-1980s and -1990s online consumers have come to constitute the main consumers.

Therefore, in 2008, Haier established E-Haier by enabling the website to be used to conduct online transactions. To create an online payment function, Haier worked with the China Construction Bank, the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and other commercial banks, as well as with Alipay, the most frequently used third-party payment platform in China.

## 4.4. The integration of online and offline networks (2011–present)

Since 2010, the home appliance online market has undergone rapid development, as major B2C websites have attached importance to this type of business. Consequently, a growing number of consumers currently prefer to shop for home appliance products online. Thus, E-Haier’s sales have continuously increased, which in turn threatened the sales of offline channels. Haier’s community stores, run by franchisees, complained about losing customers. A franchisee stated as follows:

Suddenly, the sales of my store fell sharply. At that time, I was aware of the power of online channels.

Meanwhile, Haier became entangled in a price war as e-commerce websites including E-Haier sold the same products and competed to obtain larger market shares. To reverse this situation, Haier supplied different products to different B2C websites and sold mass-customized products on E-Haier.

Additionally, to increase the efficiency of resource utilization, Haier integrated its online and offline networks. The senior vice president explained as follows:

The integration of online and offline networks coordinates the operation of E-Haier and Haier’s self-owned marketing, logistics and service networks.

To integrate its online and offline networks, Haier extended the role of community stores. Previously, community stores were merely responsible for serving store visitors; however, following integration, community stores additionally provided delivery and installation services for products sold online.

The change not only generated revenue for community stores but also benefited E-Haier’s operations. A senior manager in the IS department noted as follows:

When consumers order products on E-Haier, automatically generated orders are simultaneously transferred to the R&D, production, logistics and service departments. These departments are responsible for the corresponding work and are connected with each other through IS to enable cooperation. . .Consider the cooperation between the logistics and service departments, for example. The logistics department is responsible for transporting products to the closest community stores with the assistance of a four-dimensional map, and the community store is responsible for providing the so-called ‘‘last mile service,’’ including the delivery and installation of products.

To support this change in the process, Haier improved its IS. The R&D, inventory, marketing, production, logistics and service systems were connected with one another, replacing the previous linear model, such that order details could be simultaneously transferred to each department to aid them in arranging for the necessary resources in advance. Furthermore, to flexibly respond to consumer demands, Haier not only relied on its own R&D resources but also invited external R&D teams to participate in product development and improvement. Additionally, to ensure smooth adoption, Haier introduced corresponding managerial measures: the community of interest (COI), which is consumercentric. In the COI philosophy, an organization is viewed as a network of large and small nodes. A node could be an individual or a team. From the receipt of an order through the product’s installation, the process of providing a product is controlled by a COI, and Haier can trace strong points and weak points in the process. The senior vice president emphasized the following:

The integration between the online and offline networks required Haier to implement comprehensive changes. We did not have any references and had to explore everything. Now, Haier can provide customized products with 24-h delivery and simultaneous delivery and installation services to consumers due to the successful integration between the online and offline networks.

## 5.1. Developing focal capability

The case analysis revealed that a focal capability was developed in accordance with the predominant competitive environment in each phase.

During the phase in which the call center was introduced, Haier’s adoption of e-commerce occurred within a horizontal competitive environment, which is an environment that features fierce competition among rival firms. Because of the increasing number of manufacturers, the home appliance market was oversupplied; thus, to seize a greater market share, most manufacturers engaged in price wars. To overcome the competition in such an environment, a manufacturer was required to meet consumers’ needs more effectively than its competitors. Haier, to maintain its leading position in the market and to implement its differentiation strategy, was compelled to gain a better understanding of consumer needs and thereby provide suitable products and services. Haier thus needed to develop a greater capability for sensing and responding to consumer needs. For instance, Haier introduced the call center to collect consumer feedback on products and services. In addition, by transmitting relevant information to the appropriate departments, Haier was able to improve product design and quality, develop new products and improve services.

During the phase in which the online sales channel was introduced, the competitive environment was predominantly one of crisscrossed competition characterized by competition within the supply chain and especially between manufacturers and sales channels, which competed to gain greater shares of company profits, as well as between manufacturers. As a result of their lowcost strategies, retail chain enterprises were welcomed by consumers, enabling them to outperform other sales channels, which forced Haier to rely on such enterprises. The prevalence of ecommerce provided Haier with an opportunity to eliminate its dependence on these alternative channels. Given the independence of its operations and its lack of relevant resources to implement online transactions, Haier needed to integrate external resources into its operations. Therefore, Haier needed to develop a greater capability for cooperating. For example, Haier cooperated with the China Construction Bank, other commercial banks and Alipay to support online transactions. The company also outsourced logistics and services to deliver and install products.

In the phase involving the integration of online and offline networks, Haier’s e-commerce adoption environment was dominated by self-competition, i.e., competition within the enterprise, especially between or among two or more teams with the same or similar functions. Increasing the online sales of home appliance products threatened the sales of Haier’s community stores, and the competition between the two channels was harmful to Haier’s development and interests. To resolve this situation and to foster the development of both channels, Haier was forced to rely on its capability for innovating, given the absence of a reference example. For instance, the company repositioned its community stores, which, in addition to an offline sales channel, became the ‘‘last mile services’’ provider of E-Haier. ## 5.2. Developing focal capabilities through resource orchestration

The case analysis of Haier also reveals that the focal capability in each phase of the company’s e-commerce adoption was developed through resource orchestration. This orchestration occurs when a company purposefully creates, extends or modifies its resource base and employs organizational processes in the most general sense or routines [73], which, embedded in the firm, lead to the creation of corresponding capabilities [23]. While resource orchestration emphasizes resource-focused actions [52], actual actions are mediated through organizational resources [4]. Therefore, such resource orchestration is characterized by resourcefocused actions along with focal resources. In addition, different actions result in different configurations of resources [51,68]. Viewed longitudinally across the three phases, the case analysis yielded a process model of the role of resource orchestration in manufacturers’ e-commerce adoption. This model is illustrated in Fig. 2.

In the phase in which the call center was introduced, the capability for sensing and responding to consumer demands was developed through the resource-focused actions of acquisition and deployment. Acquiring resources contributed to improving the organizational resource portfolio and providing the foundation for the development of corresponding capabilities [52]. In Haier’s case, the resource acquired was the call center, an externally connected information system that enabled the company to better determine customer needs [11] by collecting and analyzing consumer feedback on products and services. Moreover, understanding consumers’ needs enabled Haier to more effectively respond to these needs by deploying related information to improve product and service design and quality and to develop new products and services. For example, the call center collaborated with and transferred valuable information to the R&D, production and service departments.

In the phase in which the online sales channel was introduced, the capability for cooperating was achieved through the resourcefocused actions of enrichment and integration. Enriching resources helps organizations extend their current capabilities [52]. In the case of Haier, the enriched resource was the company’s website – an IS resource. By enriching this IS resource, Haier expanded its capabilities for sensing and responding to consumer needs to encompass cooperative capabilities. The company enriched the functionality of its website from merely exhibiting products to conducting online transactions, which required Haier to cooperate with banks and other third-party payment platforms. Furthermore, these enrichment efforts were coupled with integration measures. When resources are scattered across different organizations, different resource owners must cooperate with one another [16,37]. The subjective resources integrated by Haier to operate E-Haier included IS, external logistics and service resources. The last two types of resources were used to deliver and install products, respectively, and belonged to external stakeholders. Therefore, Haier needed to cooperate with external logistics and service resource owners.

As the adoption of e-commerce proceeded into the phase of the integration of online and offline networks, Haier’s innovative capability was developed through various resource-focused actions, namely, converting, pioneering and coordinating. First, resource conversion was catalyzed by managerial resources [38]. Hence, to convert resources, organizations should introduce new managerial measures, which requires organizations to be innovative. In the case of Haier, to resolve the conflicts between community stores and E-Haier, new functions – including the ‘‘last mile service’’ – were added to community stores. Thus, community stores played a key role in both online and offline channels. This conversion was initiated through managerial measures under Haier’s networking strategy. Because of a lack of references, Haier had to find its own path. Second, pioneering, which typically involves capturing new and novel knowledge from several sources, generates a capability for radical innovation [52,54]. In the case of Haier, the company innovatively changed its IS from a linear model to a networked model to facilitate customized product and service transactions online. ## 5.3. Evolution of the relationship among the environment, resource orchestration and focal capabilities

Given a panoramic view of the entire process, the number of manufacturers grew sharply during the first phase because of the rapid growth of the Chinese home appliance industry, and thus, the competition between the manufacturers became increasingly fierce (i.e., horizontal competition was the prominent environmental condition during that phase). To address the competition between manufacturers, competent enterprises such as Haier were forced to change. Therefore, Haier acquired and deployed a call center by connecting internal R&D, production and service resources to improve its service system and to conduct a differentiated competition strategy. The emphasis on service led Haier to generate and continuously improve its capability for sensing and responding.

As the competition in the home appliance industry became increasingly fierce, all manufacturers began to attach more importance to sales channels. Consequently, this development prompted the emergence of sales channel controllers – retail chain enterprises – that transformed the horizontal competition environment into the crisscrossed competition environment as manufacturers competed with other manufacturers and retail chain enterprises for profit. To alleviate the competition, some large-scale manufacturers enriched the functions of their websites from merely exhibiting products to providing online transaction channels, which required the further integration of IS and internal R&D, production and service resources with internal logistics resources and external platform and service resources. These cooperative practices further facilitated the development of cooperation capabilities.

Hereafter, following the rapid development of e-commerce in China, an increasing number of manufacturers initiated online sales channels and cooperated with third-party e-commerce platforms, which in turn caused a conflict between traditional channels and the emerging online sales channels. Therefore, selfcompetition was the dominant environment in the industry. To eliminate the competition between online and offline sales channels and to coordinate these channels, manufacturers must convert and pioneer some existing resources to give them new meanings and functions and to further coordinate them to target the same goal. These innovative actions will help to develop and improve manufacturers’ innovation capabilities.

## 6. Discussion

In recent years, the prevalence of e-commerce has motivated an increasing number of manufacturers to launch online transaction services [29]. However, successful online transaction services among manufacturers are limited, largely because of conflicts between online and offline channels [60]. Therefore, it is valuable to study successful examples to illuminate the ways in which manufacturers can successfully adopt e-commerce [25,26,42], focusing on the key factors that facilitate e-commerce success [18,20,25]. Although some studies investigate the process of e-commerce adoption, they generally apply either a factor-oriented perspective [31,40,61] or a stage-oriented perspective. Thus, such studies focus, for example, on the communication channel and the e-commerce functions of each stage [12,48,66] rather than on the nature of the process (i.e., on the development of the resources and capabilities required in each phase of the process).

This paper has investigated the e-commerce adoption process of a manufacturing enterprise and revealed the nature of e-commerce adoption by focusing not only on the manufacturer’s resources and the development of its capabilities but also on the resource-focused actions that it undertakes and the competitive environments that it faces. The findings indicate that focal capabilities must be developed to achieve the adoption of e-commerce. The development of capabilities requires the orchestration of resources according to the changing environment. Consequently, our study responds to the need to examine e-commerce adoption as a dynamic process [22,27], avoiding the danger of oversimplifying manufacturers’ e-commerce adoption as merely the introduction of online sales technologies.

Furthermore, this paper has theoretical and practical implications, as scholars have struggled to investigate the optimal conditions for the adoption of e-commerce by manufacturing enterprises [25,26,42] and have accordingly produced only a limited number of successful studies. By examining the adoption process, this study extracted the characteristics and nature of each phase of the process and provided insight into the overall process.

First, our research reveals that to successfully adopt e-com merce, manufacturers must develop corresponding capabilities for success according to the changes in the predominant environment by orchestrating their resources (as shown in Fig. 2). On the one hand, as dynamic capabilities are the capacities to renew competencies to achieve congruence with the changing business environment [1,59], manufacturing enterprises should design their capabilities according to the specific environment. In the case of the e-commerce adoption of manufacturing enterprises, because the predominant environment changes typically follow the sequence of horizontal, crisscrossed and self-competition, these firms must develop the capabilities for sensing and responding, cooperating, and innovating to gain competitive advantages or adapt to a new environment. On the other hand, to develop these capabilities, manufacturing enterprises must orchestrate their resources. To develop these three capabilities, manufacturers need to first acquire and deploy basic IT resources with the support of R&D, production and service resources; second, they must enrich their IT resources and link them to other resources, such as R&D, production, logistics and services; and third, they must further pioneer IT resources, convert offline resources, and coordinate the internal and external resources involved.

Second, resource orchestration plays a critical role in capability development. In this process, resource orchestration involves three subprocesses: acquiring and deploying; enriching and integrating; and conversing, pioneering and coordinating. Each subprocess involves the following: (1) resource structuring – acquiring, enriching, conversing and pioneering – to form a resource portfolio; (2) bundling – deploying, integrating and coordinating – to generate capabilities; and (3) capability leverage – to exploit existing capabilities [52]. This process is iterative rather than linear, as noted by Sirmon et al. [52]. Only through this iterative process can manufacturing enterprises develop corresponding capabilities by continuously structuring and bundling resources and responding to changes in the environment.

Third, resource orchestration bridges resources and dynamic capabilities. RBT argues that simultaneously valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable resources are the sources of superior performance and enable firms to achieve competitive advantages [3]. However, opponents believe that the environment is continuously changing and that static resources will not ensure sustainable competitive advantages. Firms that develop corresponding capabilities only to respond to the changing environment could encompass all competitive advantages [59]. However, we

## 7. Conclusion

Because an increasing number of manufacturers are launching e-commerce while only a few are successful, there is a need to improve both the research and practice in this field; thus, the e-commerce adoption by one Chinese manufacturing enterprise was investigated in the present study. Employing RBT and the resource orchestration perspective as a theoretical lens, this paper provides a process model to help explain how organizational resources can be orchestrated to develop capabilities in accordance with the changing environment for the successful adoption of e-commerce by manufacturers. Specifically, the process model demonstrates that the predominant competitive environment at each phase of e-commerce adoption – horizontal, crisscrossed and self-competition – requires a particular focal capability: sensing and responding, cooperating and innovating, respectively. Moreover, the focal capabilities were developed through resource-focused actions with respect to specific resources: acquiring and deploying basic IT resources with the support of R&D, production and service resources; enriching IT resources to link them to other resources; and further pioneering IT resources, converting offline resources, and coordinating the internal and external resources involved. This research generates theoretical and empirical insights into e-commerce adoption and thus has important implications for both scholars and practitioners in the field.
