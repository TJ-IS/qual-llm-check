# Falling from Grace: Understanding Successful Crowdfunding Experiences in Serial Entrepreneurship

Lusi Yang,<sup>1</sup> David W. Lehman,<sup>2</sup> Zhiyi Wang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, USA, lusiyang@gsu.edu <sup>2</sup>McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, USA, lehman@virginia.edu <sup>3</sup>Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder, USA, zhiyi.wang@colorado.edu

## Abstract

Crowdfunding has become an important fundraising method for entrepreneurs. In this study, we examine the role of successful crowdfunding experiences in serial entrepreneurship. Although serial entrepreneurs are typically more mature and achieve higher success rates, it remains unclear how their success affects their subsequent crowdfunding performance, given the extant literature’s mixed views of crowdfunding experiences. To resolve this tension regarding the effects of crowdfunding success, we integrate insights from the crowdfunding literature with organizational learning theory to propose a research framework with competing hypotheses. On the one hand, the crowdfunding literature suggests a positive effect arising from the signaling of legitimacy and credibility from entrepreneurs’ prior success. On the other hand, organizational learning theory suggests the potential of a superstitious learning effect wherein entrepreneurs erroneously associate success with specific actions that may not contribute to success, thereby resulting in reduced subsequent performance. We further identify several moderators related to both theoretical perspectives. Empirical analysis of 10,795 crowdfunding campaigns from 3,978 serial entrepreneurs on Kickstarter suggests that successful experiences, on average, have a detrimental effect on the performance of subsequent campaigns. The results also suggest that experience relatedness, backing experience, and prior blockbuster success can mitigate this negative effect, whereas goal overshooting accentuates it. Supplementary analysis provides insights into the mechanisms at play, specifically, how the positive and negative aspects of success manifest. This study provides theoretical implications for the crowdfunding literature by addressing the tension inherent to crowdfunding success and offers guidance for crowdfunding entrepreneurs and platform operators.

Keywords: Crowdfunding Experience, Serial Entrepreneurs, Successful Experiences, Superstitious Learning, Signaling, Experience Characteristics

## 1 Introduction

Crowdfunding has become a valuable fundraising method for entrepreneurs in recent years. Crowdfunding platforms allow entrepreneurs to directly reach potential funders on an unprecedented scale. However, entrepreneurs still face significant challenges when soliciting funding on such platforms. For example, around 60% of campaigns on Kickstarter—one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms—fail to reach their funding goals.

To better understand how to overcome these challenges, scholars have increasingly focused on serial entrepreneurs: those who launch several crowdfunding campaigns in sequence (Kuppuswamy & Mollick, 2016; Sewaid et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017). Crowdfunding is typically characterized by low entry barriers; many novice entrepreneurs thus lack sufficient capabilities and join crowdfunding with the simple intention of gaining support for rough ideas. In contrast, serial entrepreneurs often develop platformspecific skills and generally create more welldeveloped campaigns. They also seek to continually improve their campaigns and bring new creative ideas to the platform. Evidence suggests that serial entrepreneurs tend to achieve significantly higher success rates and higher funding amounts than novice entrepreneurs (Butticè et al., 2017). On Kickstarter, serial entrepreneurs account for more than one-third of all funds raised.

On the journey of serial entrepreneurship, successful experiences are of particular importance. Crowdfunding typically abides by an all-or-nothing fundraising policy such that entrepreneurs only receive pledged funds upon reaching their funding target. Successful fundraising thus allows entrepreneurs to build their capital and bring their ideas into the real world (Butticè et al., 2017; Peterson & Wu, 2021). However, it is not clear how successful experiences influence the subsequent campaigns of serial entrepreneurs. On the one hand, the prominent view within the crowdfunding literature suggests that success serves as a signal of credibility and quality to overcome information asymmetry, positively affecting subsequent fundraising performance (Courtney et al., 2017). On the other hand, some studies suggest that successful experiences are likely to be followed by unfavorable outcomes; entrepreneurs may increase complexities and coast on their reputation in subsequent campaigns without continuous improvement or adaptation, resulting in decreased campaign performance (Peterson & Wu, 2021; Sewaid et al., 2021).

Anecdotal evidence also points to divergent pathways for serial entrepreneurs. For example, the entrepreneurial team of The Ridge developed innovative eyewear in its first campaign and then introduced a slim, minimalist wallet in its second campaign, achieving successful fundraising in both campaigns.<sup>1</sup> In contrast, Wild Iron, Inc., a prototyping company founded by Robert Stone, experienced success in its first crowdfunding campaign for a camera stabilizer but failed in its second campaign for a flexible foam phone stand.<sup>2</sup> Psychic Bunny, a content production company, successfully met its fundraising goal in its first campaign, which produced a narrative film, but it, too, failed in its second campaign, which sought to develop a video game.<sup>3</sup>

Given the mixed views from prior literature and anecdotal evidence, it is necessary to further examine the tension of crowdfunding success in serial entrepreneurship and explore the contingencies behind this tension. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to (1) investigate how successful experiences influence subsequent performances for serial entrepreneurs in crowdfunding, and (2) identify under what conditions the effect of successful experiences are strengthened or weakened.

To resolve this tension regarding the effects of success, we integrate insights from the crowdfunding literature and organizational learning theory to propose competing hypotheses. On the positive side, prior crowdfunding success can signal the legitimacy of early-stage ventures such that entrepreneurs who achieve campaign success are deemed to have the credibility and capability to launch quality campaigns (Brüntje & Gajda, 2016; Courtney et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). On the negative side, we posit that the characteristics of the crowdfunding context impose significant challenges for entrepreneurs in effectively learning from their prior success such that superstitious learning occurs—that is, entrepreneurs mistakenly associate their success with specific actions that may not be related to success, thereby resulting in decreased performance in subsequent campaigns (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014; Zollo, 2009). In addition to these alternative possible main effects, we identify several moderators from the theoretical mechanisms of signaling and superstitious learning particularly relevant to the features of crowdfunding.

To test the proposed theoretical framework, we analyzed 10,795 Kickstarter campaigns from 3,978 serial entrepreneurs. Using a Heckman selection model to account for the selection process of serial entrepreneurship, we found that successful experiences, on average, have a detrimental effect on the performance of subsequent campaigns. This suggests that the negative effect of superstitious learning on crowdfunding success is more prominent than the positive impact of signaling. Regarding the moderators of superstitious learning, our findings suggest that the detrimental effect of success can be attenuated with experience relatedness and backing experience, whereas it is intensified when entrepreneurs overestimate their capabilities of realizing the campaign, as reflected by the overshooting of the funding goal. Regarding the moderator of signaling, the analysis suggests that having a strong signal of past success, manifested as blockbuster success, can mitigate the negative main effect.

Supplementary analyses validated the mechanisms at play, including both the positive and negative mechanisms. Our analysis suggests that signaling only materializes for repeat backers or when campaign creators achieve blockbuster success. Our analysis of behavioral adaptations across campaigns provides empirical evidence of superstitious learning and what drives the negative effect of success. Furthermore, multiple robustness checks ensure the reliability of the results by considering alternative model specifications, measures, and explanations, and by using an instrumental variable approach to address potential endogeneity issues.

This study provides several theoretical implications for the information systems (IS) literature on crowdfunding and the management literature on organizational learning. First, we attend to the mixed views of successful experiences in crowdfunding by highlighting the occurrence of superstitious learning in this phenomenon. We document that successful experiences precede a decrease in subsequent campaign performance. Second, we complement prior research by showing how crowdfunding success influences future performance differently when considering the experience accumulation process for the same serial entrepreneur (i.e., under a panel fixed-effects model that captures within-entrepreneur changes). Third, we contribute to the organizational learning literature by extending the discussion of learning challenges and unfavorable learning outcomes to the context of crowdfunding, which typically involves task rarity and task complexities. Beyond theory, this study yields important implications for crowdfunding entrepreneurs and platform operators to overcome the challenges that follow success.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 The Mixed Effects of Crowdfunding Experience

In recent years, there has been a notable surge in crowdfunding research. Table 1 summarizes particularly relevant studies that have delved into entrepreneurs’ crowdfunding experience. Prior research has documented that signaling is crucial in crowdfunding because it helps reduce the information asymmetry between entrepreneurs (i.e., campaign creators) and backers (Scheaf et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021). That is, by communicating the positive attributes of their campaigns, entrepreneurs foster trust and perceptions of credibility among potential backers. Recent studies have applied the signaling perspective to crowdfunding experience, indicating that entrepreneur crowdfunding experience may be considered a form of founders’ human (or social) capital (Beckman et al., 2007; Butticè et al., 2017; Courtney et al., 2017; Gompers et al., 2008). This means that campaigns from experienced, successful founders are more likely to succeed than those launched by novice entrepreneurs. In short, past success is often seen as a signal of campaign quality and founder credibility, thereby breeding further success.

Apart from signaling, the literature suggests that crowdfunding experience can be acquired through different sources—i.e., entrepreneurs can launch their own campaigns as founders or participate in others campaigns as backers (Kunz et al., 2017). Research has recognized that backing activities can expand an entrepreneur’s experience portfolio. For example, Gong et al. (2021) incorporated both founding and backing activities into their empirical models as proxies for founders’ crowdfunding experience and demonstrated that both experiences are helpful. By and large, the central theme of these studies is that entrepreneurs gain appropriate knowledge by accumulating founding and backing experiences (Minniti & Bygrave, 2001; Parker, 2006).

This body of literature also highlights the importance of experience outcomes in shaping the subsequent actions of entrepreneurs. Depending on whether the funding goal was achieved, a crowdfunding experience can be deemed a success or failure. Examining entrepreneurs behavioral responses to failures, Piening et al. (2021) theorized and empirically tested how past crowdfunding failures can influence the search distance of entrepreneurs (i.e., entrepreneurs’ search efforts move away from their current activities) such that repeated failures motivate engagement in problemistic search that explores potential solutions to remedy performance shortfalls. Meanwhile, repeated failures may actually reduce search distance via anxiety, stress, and threat rigidity. It has been consistently documented that after a failure, problemistic search is conducted to identify potential solutions (Greve, 2003; Iyer et al., 2019; Piening et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, other studies have shown that crowdfunding success may not always yield additional positive outcomes in the future. Examining entrepreneurial learning across campaigns, Peterson and Wu (2021) recognized that entrepreneurs struggle to predict potential issues that can arise when executing a campaign following a previous successful fundraising effort. As a result, they are less likely to fulfill subsequent campaigns. This is because entrepreneurs learn of the opportunities to increase complexities to augment their next campaign, unwittingly introducing unforeseen interdependencies between project components. Similarly, Sewaid et al. (2021) found that serial entrepreneurs’ performance over successive crowdfunding campaigns follows a cyclical pattern: An above-average outcome precedes a below-average performance because entrepreneurs coast on the good reputation established but exert less effort in the next campaign.

In summary, prior crowdfunding literature yields mixed findings regarding the downstream effect of crowdfunding success. On the one hand, when applying the signaling rationale to crowdfunding experiences, this implies that success can signal quality and help build trust; on the other hand, when examining learning across campaigns, success may not motivate entrepreneurs to continue their efforts in crafting subsequent campaigns, potentially diminishing subsequent performances.

Motivated by these mixed findings, this study intends to resolve this tension by uncovering the effect of successful experiences on subsequent campaign performance and by examining the conditions under which the different mechanisms materialize. We posit that entrepreneurs behaviors and motivations evolve with the accumulation of experiences, making it important to move beyond the examination of success factors for a single campaign by focusing on successive campaigns by serial entrepreneurs (Ucbasaran et al., 2008). Therefore, to answer our research question, this study focuses on serial entrepreneurs and seeks to understand their experiences across multiple crowdfunding attempts.

## 2.2 Entrepreneurial Learning: Unpacking the Experience-Performance Relationship

Learning definition and the conventional view: This study is related to the broader literature on the experience-performance relationship, particularly in entrepreneurship, that examines tasks involving problem-solving and innovation. Conventional wisdom posits that entrepreneurs with more experience tend to create ventures that achieve better performance, a relationship consistent with the learning curve theory (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011; Argote & Todorova, 2007). This literature defines learning as “a change in behavior or performance that occurs as a function of experience” (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014, p. 455). The positive effect of experience typically relies on the assumption that individuals or organizations cumulatively extract useful lessons and inferences from prior experiences and then use these lessons to inform future actions (Argote et al., 2021). This typically occurs in highly repetitive operating tasks common to the manufacturing (e.g., Argote et al., 1990) and service industries (e.g., Reagans et al., 2005).

Negative effect of prior experiences from superstitious learning: This literature also suggests that while experiences have the potential to generate useful learning, they can lead to harmful learning outcomes that diminish performance (Kim et al., 2009; Musaji et al., 2020; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). The organizational learning literature refers to this situation as “superstitious learning,” which occurs when entrepreneurs draw incorrect inferences from experiences. Superstitious learning was first introduced by Levitt and March (1988), who defined it as a situation where “the subjective experience of learning is compelling, but the connections between actions and outcomes are misspecified” (p. 325). As individuals acquire experience, they may learn inappropriately such that they mistakenly attribute success to a specific action or behavior when, in reality, the success might have happened due to other factors or even random chance (Denrell et al., 2004; March, 2010). As a consequence, this misattribution can have harmful effects on future performance (Kim et al., 2009).

Several conditions have been shown to be particularly conducive to superstitious learning (Miller, 2012). First, low or moderate levels of experience do not allow individuals to generalize their experiences correctly to future events, and “unfavorable outcomes are common” (Musaji et al., 2020, p. 205; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). With limited experience, entrepreneurs and innovators are not able to gain a comprehensive understanding of a broad range of challenges and accompanying solutions (Simon, 1978). Instead, they may draw incorrect inferences regarding what will lead to success. For example, Musaji et al. (2020) provided empirical evidence that when experience is limited in the strategic decision-making process of selecting franchisee applicants, the effect of experience on decision outcomes is negative. Similarly, experiencing rare events as wide ranging as founding a business, developing a crowdfunding campaign, taking in mergers and acquisitions, and establishing a partnership also tends to result in inaccurate inferences (Anderson, 1990; Zollo, 2009; Zollo & Meier, 2008; Zollo & Singh, 2004).

Second, experiences involving complex strategic decisions can engender superstitious learning, resulting in unintended negative consequences (Zollo, 2009). An implicit assumption in the learning curve literature is the repetition of relatively homogeneous, operational tasks (Argote, 2013). Tasks of relatively limited complexity, such as making pizzas (Darr et al., 1995), make it easier to draw useful inferences regarding the associations between actions and outcomes. However, highcomplexity tasks (e.g., introducing new products) may create causal ambiguity such that it is difficult to distinguish which decision or action leads to which outcome (Levitt & March, 1988; Lippman & Rumelt, 1982; Zollo, 2009). In this sense, inaccurate inferences are likely to be drawn from experience. As a result, prior experience may lead to unfavorable outcomes. For example, Zollo (2009) argued and empirically demonstrated that in the context of corporate acquisitions, which are characterized by complex and rare strategic decisions, there is a negative relationship between the perceived success of past acquisition experiences and subsequent performance.

Third, cognitive biases also play an important role in superstitious learning. Certain biases—such as overconfidence (Chen et al., 2018; Hayward et al., 2006) and cognitive fixation (Bayus, 2013)—can alter how individuals interpret and draw inferences from experiences. Overconfidence, in particular, may result in misattribution and in turn, exacerbate superstitious learning via strategic decisions made based on incorrect links between actions and outcomes (Heimeriks, 2010). Furthermore, entrepreneurs tend to exhibit higher levels of bias than managers in well-established organizations (Zhang & Cueto, 2017). It has been suggested that overconfidence shapes how entrepreneurs interpret and respond to different outcomes of their experiences. For example, overconfidence can stimulate entrepreneurs to take excessive risks after experiencing success because they may falsely believe that their past success is indicative of future outcomes (Hayward et al., 2006). Although overconfidence may positively affect some entrepreneurial outcomes, such as new venture creation and innovativeness, poorer venture performance typically follows (Kraft et al., 2022).

Taken together, the extant literature indicates that entrepreneurs face several challenges when leveraging past experiences to improve performance, including limited experiences, high task complexity, and cognitive biases. By integrating these insights with the crowdfunding literature and the characteristics of crowdfunding, we develop a theoretical framework to understand how and under what conditions entrepreneurs are affected by successful past experiences.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Main Effect of Successful Experiences on Subsequent Performance

Our first hypothesis concerns the effect of prior successful campaigns on subsequent crowdfunding performance for serial entrepreneurs. We discuss the main effect from the two perspectives outlined above: signaling and superstitious learning.

On the one hand, it is imperative for entrepreneurs to establish the legitimacy of their early-stage ventures to potential investors and customers. As such, entrepreneurs use signals to build legitimacy in the eyes of stakeholders (Fisher et al., 2017; Svetek, 2022). Signaling plays an important role in entrepreneurial finance, and prior successful founding attempts can be a valuable signal for the human and social capital of the firm to early-stage investors. Specifically, our review of the crowdfunding literature suggests that public information from a campaign can signal the campaign’s credibility and quality (Wang et al., 2021). Drawing on this signaling perspective from the crowdfunding literature, prior campaign success can reduce information asymmetry by signaling to potential backers that the campaign creators can steer a crowdfunding initiative and that their ideas are well-received (Brüntje & Gajda, 2016; Courtney et al., 2017). Such signaling helps to establish a foundation of trust with potential backers, especially backers of the creators’ past campaigns (Bafera & Kleinert, 2022). Furthermore, according to the learning curve literature, success indicates effectiveness; following successful crowdfunding campaigns, entrepreneurs are more likely to adopt similar strategies for their next campaign rather than exploring something substantially different (Audia & Goncalo, 2007; Baum & Dahlin, 2007). In this regard, success gives entrepreneurs firsthand knowledge about creating and running a crowdfunding campaign. As a result, a positive effect of success may be expected, which can manifest as improved performance for serial entrepreneurs.

On the other hand, successful crowdfunding experiences can negatively affect performance due to superstitious learning, where entrepreneurs draw inaccurate inferences from prior experiences (Levitt & March, 1988; Zollo, 2009); in such cases, they falsely attribute success to certain actions or behaviors that may not actually relate to their positive outcomes, even though their subjective learning experience may be compelling (Argote et al., 2021). This can lead to the formation of superstitious beliefs that certain actions are responsible for success, even if these actions do not truly drive the outcomes. Superstitious learning is possible, even likely, in crowdfunding due to several contextual characteristics. First, launching a crowdfunding campaign—similar to starting a business or introducing a new product—is a rare event; it is unlikely that entrepreneurs will obtain new crowdfunding campaign experiences on a daily, monthly, or otherwise regular basis. In this context, with limited campaign launch experience, it is more challenging for entrepreneurs to develop a thorough understanding of what leads to campaign success. A recent study provides evidence that to successfully process franchisee applications, it takes around 19 prior experiences for the positive effect of experience to materialize; before that, decision makers learn inappropriately and experience attainment is harmful to performance (Musaji et al., 2020). In a similar vein, it is unlikely that an entrepreneur will accumulate sufficient experience for it to result in positive outcomes. Second, crowdfunding tasks are characterized by complexity, involving a range of intricate interdependencies among the elements during a campaign’s execution (Peterson & Wu, 2021; Simon, 1962), such as reward layout, video preparation, and story crafting. This high level of complexity incurs causal ambiguity (Levitt & March, 1988), making it challenging for entrepreneurs to derive accurate inferences from their experience. Finally, entrepreneurs and innovators are more subject to overconfidence and cognitive fixation (Bayus, 2013) than managers in wellestablished organizations (Zhang & Cueto, 2017). This tendency could be heightened by successful experiences, which discourage deep reflection on prior experiences, thereby preventing consideration of a wide range of perspectives and circumstances and limiting the accuracy of the lessons learned from these experiences. As previously discussed, these conditions could lead to superstitious learning whereby success is followed by negative performance.

Taken together, these arguments suggest the multifaceted nature of crowdfunding success. It can have a positive effect by signaling credibility, thereby fostering trust and mitigating information asymmetry. Meanwhile, the unique environment of crowdfunding can engender a negative effect due to superstitious learning. Therefore, we propose the following competing hypotheses:<sup>4</sup>

H1a (signaling): For serial entrepreneurs, the number of successful crowdfunding experiences, on average, is positively associated with subsequent crowdfunding performance.

H1b (superstitious learning): For serial entrepreneurs, the number of successful crowdfunding experiences, on average, is negatively associated with subsequent crowdfunding performance.

## 3.2 Moderating Effects

Having proposed the main effect of crowdfunding success on subsequent campaign performance, we build on the arguments about signaling and superstitious learning to delve into the potential conditions that may tilt the main effect either positively or negatively. We identify contingencies by jointly considering the discussed theoretical mechanisms and features of crowdfunding. These include heterogeneity of the stock of prior experiences in the form of relatedness of campaigns (H2) and backing experience (H3), the role of overconfidence in the form of campaign goal overshooting (H4), and the strength of signals in the form of blockbuster successes (H5). Below, we discuss how each aspect manifests in crowdfunding. <sup>5</sup>

## 3.2.1 Experience Heterogeneity: Relatedness and Backing Experience

We have argued that entrepreneurs can draw incorrect inferences (i.e., superstitious beliefs that certain actions drive success) from prior crowdfunding success. Contextual similarity (or dissimilarity) is considered to be an important factor in the formation of superstitious learning (Mulotte et al., 2013). Toft-Kehler et al. (2014) indicated that contextual similarity could significantly reduce the tendency of inappropriate learning and decrease the number of inaccurate inferences. Therefore, the propensity of superstitious learning can be mitigated with high experience relatedness (Schilling et al., 2003; Zollo, 2009), specifically for serial crowdfunding entrepreneurs, when their prior campaigns are closely relevant to the focal campaign. When past campaigns have a higher degree of relatedness to the subsequent campaign, it is more likely that the lessons drawn from prior experiences remain relevant and applicable to the context at hand. Thus, the tendency to generalize prior experiences inappropriately may attenuate while the tendency to generalize appropriately increases (Lai et al., 2019).

If we consider a campaign for a stacked portable laptop monitor, for example, prior campaign management for a dual-screen laptop monitor will be more helpful in communicating product functions and specifications to backers, compared to campaign management for a portable sit-to-stand laptop stand. All other things being equal, the former experience better helps the creator understand the issues associated with using multiple monitors, such as the pain of toggling between multiple windows and applications. However, the latter experience may not be directly beneficial, despite providing insight into ergonomic laptop stand design. In this context, having related experience can help entrepreneurs comprehend what it takes to promote a laptop monitor product more accurately, and they can apply lessons learned to their management of the current campaign.

Building on the above arguments, we propose the moderating role of experience relatedness. Relatedness helps to reduce the tendency to make inaccurate associations between unrelated factors and campaign outcomes. We anticipate that having more relevant campaign experiences could drive the effect of successful experiences in a positive direction. We thus propose the following hypothesis:

H2 (experience relatedness): Experience relatedness positively moderates the relationship between successful crowdfunding experiences and subsequent performance.

As previously discussed, one of the cognitive biases faced by entrepreneurs and innovators is cognitive fixation; entrepreneurs may inadvertently become fixated on the strategies used in prior successful campaigns and have difficulty in deviating from them even if a new campaign requires a different approach (Bayus, 2013; Burroughs et al., 2018). Our review of the crowdfunding literature suggests that entrepreneurs can accumulate experiences from different sources by not only launching their own campaigns as founders but also by backing others’ campaigns as backers (Gong et al., 2021; Kunz et al., 2017; Mollick, 2014). This indicates that campaign creators’ experience portfolios can be expanded by engaging in other campaigns as backers. We thus suggest that backing activities function as a contingent factor.

Specifically, we propose that backing experiences can help entrepreneurs overcome the cognitive fixation associated with prior crowdfunding success. When backing other campaigns, entrepreneurs are exposed to different approaches and challenges. For example, as a backer, they may have the opportunity to receive “backer-only” updates and receive limited edition rewards (Jiang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2020). Backers can also post comments to interact with other campaign creators. Such activities are typically exclusive to backers rather than campaign observers. This process allows them to learn how other creators effectively manage their campaigns and engage with their backers in various contexts. This means that backing activities can allow focal entrepreneurs to broaden their perspective, overcome their fixation on past success, consider alternative strategies, and adjust solutions according to the campaign at hand (Bayus, 2013; Kohn et al., 2011).

In light of the preceding arguments, we expect that backing experiences expand the knowledge reservoir of entrepreneurs, thereby enabling them to explore and adopt strategies that are suitable for their next campaign instead of fixating on repeating previously employed strategies, which may be ineffective in a new context. We therefore propose the following hypothesis:

H3 (backing experience): Backing experiences positively moderate the relationship between successful crowdfunding experiences and subsequent performance.

## 3.2.2 Overconfidence: Campaign Goal Overshooting

Overconfidence, as discussed above, inhibits entrepreneurs from benefiting from success through an overestimation of their campaign realization (Kraft et al., 2022; Zhang & Cueto, 2017). This overestimation can be “reflected in inflated expectations of the self,” or the overestimation of their ability, performance, and chance of success (Kraft et al., 2022, p. 3; Moore & Healy, 2008). Following this definition, we argue that for serial entrepreneurs in crowdfunding, overconfidence can be reflected in goal overshooting when launching a campaign. In this regard, past performance can serve as a benchmark (Anderson et al., 2010; Hollenbeck & Williams, 1987); specifically, we define goal overshooting as a funding goal that significantly exceeds what the entrepreneur has previously achieved in a prior campaign (Wei et al., 2022). As such, we consider goal overshooting to be another contingent factor.

We posit that goal overshooting exacerbates the role of overconfidence in learning from prior success. That is, entrepreneurs who overestimate their campaign goals are more likely to be overconfident compared to their counterparts who do not engage in goal overshooting. Critically, campaign goals are publicly set, and such public declarations have been shown to elicit greater commitment (e.g., Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004), even in the face of negative feedback (e.g., Brockner, 1992). Setting overly ambitious goals will therefore nudge an entrepreneur to be even more optimistic about their abilities and further underestimate future challenges, owing to their commitment to this public goal. Consequently, they will seek information that confirms these beliefs (that may not be accurate), ignoring contradictory or negative information (Russo & Schoemaker, 1992). For instance, a campaign creator who believes that offering limited rewards led to their prior success may focus solely on the positive outcomes of this practice, such as the attractiveness of limited edition products, while disregarding negative responses from backers who missed out on these rewards (Yang et al., 2020). Selectively disregarding situations in which these actions did not lead to success can result in complacency and inadequate preparation for future challenges. Additionally, campaign creators may underestimate technological or logistical problems that might emerge during campaign management (Cassar & Gibson, 2007). Thus, goal overshooting can reinforce superstitious learning, where inaccurate lessons are drawn from success, ultimately diminishing subsequent performance.

Taken together, we suggest that goal overshooting exacerbates entrepreneurs’ overconfidence, which can moderate the effect of crowdfunding success in a negative direction. We thus propose:

H4 (goal overshooting): Goal overshooting negatively moderates the relationship between successful crowdfunding experiences and subsequent performance.

## 3.2.3 Signal Strength: Blockbuster Success

As noted, the signaling perspective can be applied to understand the effect of crowdfunding success and points to a positive outcome (i.e., an increase in performance). The signaling literature argues that the effectiveness of signaling hinges on the signal’s strength (Bafera & Kleinert, 2022). Following this reasoning, we posit that backers in crowdfunding prefer strong signals with less ambiguity. Not all types of success are created equal. The crowdfunding literature specifically delineates two types of success: blockbuster success and regular success (Soublière & Gehman, 2020; Wang et al., 2023). A blockbuster success is a campaign that gains fundraising that far exceeds its target. Due to the large magnitude of the blockbuster success, we deem it to be a strong signal that can thus positively drive the beneficial effects of success.

However, crowdfunding campaigns mostly “succeed by narrow margins, or else fail by large amounts” (Mollick, 2014, p. 2). It is difficult for campaigns to exceed their target by a large margin, with most successful campaigns only exceeding their goal by a small margin. Put differently, achieving a blockbuster success can be a potent signal because it is rather hard to replicate. In addition, different from regular success, which may create a negative legitimacy spillover, blockbuster success generates a positive legitimacy spillover, as it can lend more credibility to potential backers (Soublière & Gehman, 2020). Furthermore, blockbuster campaigns can enlarge the size of the backer population on the platform, and the salient success strengthens the signal perceived by these backers, which encourages repeated engagement in subsequent campaigns (Wang et al., 2023).

Taken together, we expect that the positive mechanism (i.e., signaling) of successful crowdfunding experiences can be strengthened by a prior blockbuster success. Therefore, experiencing a blockbuster success shifts the main effect in a positive direction. We propose:

H5 (blockbuster success): Experiencing a blockbuster success positively moderates the relationship between successful crowdfunding experiences and subsequent performance.

## 4 Data and Methods

## 4.1 Empirical Context and Sample

We retrieved our data from Kickstarter.com. On this platform, entrepreneurs, known as campaign creators, launch creative campaigns and seek funds from a large population of crowdfunders, known as “backers.” Launching a campaign is a serious endeavor, and campaign creators undergo a rigorous review and verification process on the platform. Each campaign posted is self-managed; creators are given complete control over, responsibility for, and ownership of their campaigns. Creators set funding goals and deadlines for campaigns, and individuals who are interested in a certain campaign pledge money and become backers. A campaign is deemed successful if the fundraising goal is achieved by the deadline. Usually, the success of a crowdfunding campaign involves a substantial investment of entrepreneurs’ effort and time and requires effective design and management strategies (Gerber & Hui, 2013; Kuppuswamy & Mollick, 2016).

The data included relevant information on all crowdfunding campaigns launched between April 2009 (i.e., the platform’s inception) and December 2014. We chose this time span because, since 2015, Kickstarter has maintained hidden campaign backer lists; this period thus allowed us to observe both launching and backing activities from entrepreneurs on the platform. Across all categories, we retrieved 186,174 campaigns from 158,857 unique creators. These campaigns collectively received \$1.4 billion from over 7 million unique backers and achieved a success rate of approximately 40%. Consistent with the literature’s definition, we considered those who launched at least three campaigns during the data collection period as serial entrepreneurs (Parker, 2013). Following prior research in this context, we excluded campaigns aiming to raise less than \$100 (Hong et al., 2018).

## 4.2 Measures

## 4.2.1 Dependent Variable

Because we focused on how successful experiences influence subsequent crowdfunding performance, our dependent variable was measured as the crowdfunding campaign performance. To better capture the extent of the performance, we adopted the total amount pledged (Pledged<sub>ij</sub>, in USD) as the dependent variable, where subscripts i and j denote the $j ^ { \mathrm { { \bar { h } } } }$ campaign launched by the entrepreneur i. This measure aligns with fundraising procedures in crowdfunding, because the campaign’s primary objective is to gain sufficient financial resources. We took the log transformation to account for its skewed distribution. We also used an alternative measure of fundraising success (i.e., a binary variable for whether the campaign achieved its funding goal) to check the robustness of the results (see § 5.3).

## 4.2.2 Independent Variables

Our key independent variable of interest was entrepreneurs’ prior crowdfunding success. For each prior campaign from the same entrepreneur before the focal campaign, we considered it successful if it reached its predetermined funding goal. This allowed each prior campaign to be unambiguously classified as either a success or a failure. We then measured successful crowdfunding experiences as the cumulative number of successful campaigns launched by the entrepreneur i before the focal campaign j (SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed). Meanwhile, because successful experiences can be related to unsuccessful campaign attempts, we included failed experiences together, which was similarly measured as the cumulative number of failed campaigns launched by the entrepreneur i before the focal campaign j (FailureExp<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed). For H1—with its competing hypotheses—the coefficient of SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> could be either positive (H1a) or negative (H1b).

## 4.2.3 Moderator Variables

Experience relatedness: H2 proposed a positive moderating effect of experience relatedness—i.e., higher relatedness between prior successful campaigns, and the focal campaign can mitigate the negative aspect of success (i.e., superstitious learning). We used a text mining technique to quantify the extent of the relatedness of entrepreneurs’ prior successful campaigns to the focal campaign. This approach transcends the category because the content of campaigns belonging to the same category may vary substantially (Soublière et al., 2024). For instance, campaigns in the games category may relate to card games, mobile games, or video games. We measured the relatedness between current and prior successful campaigns using a topic modeling approach (latent Dirichlet allocation, LDA; see details in Appendix A), and we averaged the relatedness between the focal campaign j and each prior successful campaign from the same entrepreneur i (SuccessRelatedness<sub>ij</sub>).<sup>6</sup> For H2, we expected the coefficient of the interaction term between SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> and SuccessRelatedness<sub>ij</sub> to be positive.

Backing experiences: H3 theorized a positive moderating effect of backing experiences such that having more backing experiences could mitigate the negative aspect of successful crowdfunding experiences. We measured backing experiences as the number of backed campaigns from the entrepreneur i before the focal campaign j (BackingExp<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed). For H3, we expected the interaction effect between SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> and BackingExp<sub>ij</sub> to be positive.

Goal overshooting: H4 proposed a negative moderating effect of goal overshooting such that the negative aspect of successful crowdfunding experiences is intensified when an entrepreneur overshoots the funding goal. To measure goal overshooting, we compared the funding goal of the focal campaign and the average pledged amount of all prior successful campaigns from the same entrepreneur. This measure aligns with our theorization that overconfidence reflects inflated self-expectations, with prior performance acting as a benchmark (Hollenbeck & Williams, 1987; Kraft et al., 2022). Notably, the average pledged amount from prior successful campaigns indicates how much the entrepreneur may raise, and its comparison with the focal campaign’s funding goal captures whether the entrepreneur overestimates their capability. Specifically, we measured goal overshooting as whether the funding goal of focal campaign j exceeded 200%<sup>7</sup> of the average pledged amount of prior successful campaigns from the same entrepreneur i (GoalOvershooting<sub>ij</sub>). <sup>8</sup> For H4, we expected the interaction effect between SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> and GoalOvershooting<sub>ij</sub> to be negative.

Blockbuster success: H5 suggested a positive moderating effect of blockbuster success such that the positive aspect of signaling is strengthened when an entrepreneur achieves blockbuster success in prior campaigns. We followed Soublière and Gehman (2020) to define blockbuster success as a successful campaign funded over 150%. This cutoff was deemed reasonable because less than 10% of all campaigns achieve funding performance over 150% of their funding goal. We then measured blockbuster success as whether the entrepreneur i has at least one prior successful campaign funded over 150% before the focal campaign j (BlockbusterSuccess<sub>ij</sub>). For H5, we expected a positive interaction effect between SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> and BlockbusterSuccess<sub>ij</sub>.

## 4.2.4 Control Variables

We included several sets of control variables in the analysis to account for campaign-specific factors, campaign quality indicators, and campaign creators characteristics. For campaign-specific information, we included the funding goal of the campaign in USD (Goal<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed) and the campaign duration in days (CampaignDuration<sub>ij</sub>). For campaign qualityrelated information, we included the number of images in the focal campaign (ImageCount<sub>ij</sub>), whether the campaign included a video clip or not (HasVideo<sub>ij</sub>), the length of the campaign story in words (StoryLength<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed), the number of reward options (NumRewards<sub>ij</sub>) (Wessel et al., 2017), and the update speed (QuickUpdate<sub>ij</sub>, an indicator variable coded as 1 if the entrepreneur posted updates within three days of launch and 0 otherwise) (Mollick, 2014). Because our dependent variable was the total amount pledged, we controlled for the reward prices (MinPrice<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed), captured by the minimum price of reward options. We also controlled for the readability of the campaign story (Readability<sub>ij</sub>), or how difficult the story was to comprehend. Following prior research, we used the Flesch Reading Ease score to measure readability, with higher scores indicating greater ease of reading and better understanding of a story (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017).<sup>9</sup>

For creator characteristics, we controlled for the creator’s pattern of launching campaigns, using the pace of previous campaign creations (PaceCreated<sub>ij</sub>, average days between previously launched campaigns, log transformed) to account for the process of experience accumulation. We further included the tenure of the campaign creator, measured as the number of months since the creator joined the platform, determined by the launch of the focal campaign (Tenure<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed), and the potential preparation efforts associated with launching the campaign, measured as the number of days between the creation and the launch time of the focal campaign (Preparation<sub>ij</sub>, log transformed). Finally, we included the campaign’s domain (Category fixed effects) to capture categoryspecific effects and incorporated year and month fixed effects to account for platform-level and industry-level shocks over time, as well as the influence of market entry timing (Gompers et al., 2010). Table 2 summarizes the research variables and measurements.

The first-stage selection equation models the selection process into serial entrepreneurship with a probit model. Figure 2 illustrates the first-stage model. Given the fundraising outcome, creator characteristics, and other control variables of campaign j-1, we predicted whether an entrepreneur i would launch campaign j. Specifically, we included the fundraising outcome of campaign success (Success<sub>ij-1</sub>, successful or not) for campaign j-1 because the campaign outcome directly influences an entrepreneur’s intention to make a subsequent attempt (Kuppuswamy & Mollick, 2016). On the one hand, it could motivate entrepreneurs to continue their journey in crowdfunding; on the other hand, it allows entrepreneurs to fulfill their ideas so that they may focus on their businesses and stop creating new campaigns. We also included the average funding performance of all prior campaigns by the same entrepreneur including campaign j-1 (AvgPledged<sub>ij-1</sub>). Entrepreneurs are more likely to launch a campaign if they have not raised a substantial amount of funding in prior campaigns. For creator characteristics of campaign j-1, we specifically focused on the number of backing experiences (BackingExp<sub>ij-1</sub>), the creator’s tenure on the platform (Tenure<sub>ij-1</sub>), and the preparation between the creation and the launch time (Preparation<sub>ij-1</sub>) because these factors are more likely to influence the likelihood of launching a subsequent campaign. With more backing experiences and a longer history on the platform, entrepreneurs are more likely to be engaged in crowdfunding and more likely to launch new campaigns. In contrast, when entrepreneurs have extensively prepared their last campaign over a longer period, they will typically be less inclined to launch new campaigns due to the effort spent.

Meanwhile, we kept all other control variables and dummies of campaign j-1 in the selection equation to make sure all potential selection factors were controlled. The first-stage model utilized the nature of the crowdfunding setting because the launch decision of the next campaign is primarily determined by variables measured at campaign j-1 instead of campaign j. This allowed a different set of variables to influence selection and fundraising outcomes in the two stages, meeting the exclusion restrictions criteria (Wooldridge, 2010). The inverse Mills ratio calculated from the first-stage estimation was included in the second-stage model to correct the selection bias. The results of the first-stage model were largely consistent with our expectations. Appendix B presents the results of the selection variables and discusses them in detail.

In the second-stage model, ExperienceVars<sub>ij</sub> represents focal variables that vary across entrepreneur i and campaign j, including SuccessExp<sub>ij</sub> and its interactions with the four moderators (SuccessRelatedness<sub>ij</sub>, BackingExp<sub>ij</sub>, GoalOvershooting<sub>ij,</sub> and BlockbusterSuccess<sub>ij</sub>). Additionally, <sub>i</sub> captures the entrepreneur’s unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., the entrepreneur’s gender, innate talent, and industry experience prior to becoming an entrepreneur), which is invariant over time. The Hausman test (Allison, 2005; Wooldridge, 2012) confirmed that fixed-effects models were preferred over random-effects models for our sample, where the time-invariant entrepreneur fixedeffects <sub>i</sub> were eliminated in our estimations. Notably, the fixed-effects model removed any unobserved, timeinvariant entrepreneur heterogeneity. This allowed entrepreneur-specific effects (e.g., ability and innate talent) to be correlated with the focal variables and teased out in the estimations, making the estimations less likely to be undermined by the bias arising from endogeneity. Furthermore, because the measures may be related within each entrepreneur, all models used heteroskedasticity robust standard errors clustered by entrepreneurs.

With this model setup, the first campaign of each serial entrepreneur was excluded from the fixed-effects model because no prior campaign existed to build the selection process. Furthermore, we removed outliers<sup>10</sup> from the estimation sample, leaving 10,795 entrepreneurial campaigns from 3,978 serial entrepreneurs.

## 5 Results and Discussions

Table 3 presents descriptive statistics for all study measures. Table 4 presents the results of hypothesis testing. We checked variance inflation factors (VIFs) for potential multicollinearity problems and found them below the recommended thresholds (Belsley et al., 2005; Cohen et al., 2013). Among all the estimation models, the maximum VIF value was 3.63, and the average was 1.86, indicating no significant concern of multicollinearity.

We first estimated a model with the main effect of successful crowdfunding experiences (Model 1) and then added interaction terms with the moderators (Models 2 to 5). Model 6 presents a full model with all interactions, and the results are largely consistent. For the control variables, as Model 1 shows, higher funding goals and longer campaign durations were related to better performance. All campaign quality-related variables were positively associated with campaign performance. The minimum price of reward options, the readability of the campaign story, the pace of creating campaigns, the tenure of the creator on the platform, and the potential preparation efforts did not significantly affect performance.

## 5.1 Hypothesis Testing

H1 focused on the main effect of successful crowdfunding experiences. In Model 1, the coefficient for ln(SuccessExp) is negative and significant ( = -0.946, p < 0.001). This result suggests that the negative effect of superstitious learning is dominant compared to the positive effect of signaling. Entrepreneurs with more successful experiences tend to raise fewer funds, supporting competing hypothesis H1b rather than H1a. This result and our theorizing imply that entrepreneurs indeed draw inaccurate inferences from prior success. Additionally, contrasted with the effect of successful experiences, failed experiences positively affected subsequent crowdfunding performance (ln(FailureExp): = 1.097, p < 0.001). Although failures may convey negative signals, entrepreneurs can benefit from their failures and carefully adapt strategies to perform better in subsequent campaigns.

H2 proposed a positive moderating effect of experience relatedness. In Model 2, the coefficient of ln(SuccessExp) × SuccessRelatedness is positive and significant ( = 0.768, p < 0.001). When prior successful campaigns are related to the focal campaign, entrepreneurs are less likely to be affected by the negative influence of success. Even if entrepreneurs draw inaccurate inferences from prior experiences, greater relatedness of prior success allows them to reduce the likelihood of applying wrong strategies. This result supports H2.

H3 proposed that acquiring backing experience positively moderates the effect of success. In Model 3, the interaction effect between ln(SuccessExp) and ln(BackingExp) is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 6 9$ p < 0.01). Based on our theorization, this implies that backing experiences broaden entrepreneurs’ knowledge reservoir, which can reduce cognitive fixation. Backing enables entrepreneurs to better comprehend what makes campaigns successful in various contexts and develop suitable strategies for their next campaign. Thus, H3 is supported.

H5 proposed that entrepreneurs who have achieved a blockbuster success benefit from a greater positive signaling effect of prior success. Taken together, successful crowdfunding experiences exhibit a negative overall effect, suggesting that the negative effect of superstitious learning is dominant over the positive effect of signaling. Furthermore, several factors related to characteristics of experience in crowdfunding can help mitigate the negative effect; entrepreneurs can utilize the contextual similarity through experience relatedness and expand knowledge from backing experiences to reduce the tendency toward inappropriate learning. However, campaign goal overshooting intensifies the negative effect of successful experiences, suggesting the importance of avoiding overconfidence after success. Moreover, the stronger signaling effect from blockbuster success strengthens the positive aspect of success, thus attenuating the negative overall effect of successful experiences.

## 5.2 Identifying the Mechanisms of Successful Experiences

Our main analyses suggest the overall negative downstream effect of crowdfunding success. The negative average effect of successful crowdfunding experiences implies that superstitious learning is more prominent than the signaling effect, with the moderators providing nuanced insights into the mechanisms behind superstitious learning and signaling. To further establish the validity of our proposed mechanisms, we examine here how these mechanisms materialize.

## 5.2.1 Adaptation Behaviors After Success

We first examine how and why superstitious learning happens by identifying behavioral changes that drive subsequent failures after crowdfunding success. As discussed in H1, the negative aspect of superstitious learning is due to biases such as cognitive fixation and overconfidence. Analyzing entrepreneurs’ adaptation behaviors after successful campaigns could offer direct evidence about how such mechanisms materialize.

For each serial entrepreneur in our estimation sample, we compared campaign attributes between the previous and the focal campaign for two scenarios of interest (i.e., from success to failure and from success to success). Aligning with our theoretical arguments regarding superstitious learning, we focused on three measurable sets of campaign attributes: the campaign’s funding goal, the campaign’s quality, and the entrepreneur’s engagement. The funding goal set by the entrepreneur (Goal) captures the entrepreneur’s goal overshooting and overconfidence levels. The campaign quality attributes included the five campaign-level control variables related to quality and positively contributing to performance: number of images (ImageCount), existence of video (HasVideo), number of words in the campaign story (StoryLength), number of reward options (NumRewards), and quick update indicator (QuickUpdate) (Mollick, 2014). We also created a unified measure of quality by performing principle component analysis (PCA) on the five quality-related variables and taking the first component of PCA (Quality PCA) (Geva et al., 2019). We measured the entrepreneur’s engagement as the sum of posting activities to backers (i.e., updates and responses to backer comments) during the campaign’s course of fundraising (Engagement). Table 5 presents the results of attribute comparison across sequential campaigns.

This reveals some notable patterns. Entrepreneurs who succeeded in their most recent campaign and then failed in the focal campaign (Panel 1: Success → Failure) increased their funding goals significantly (Goal from \$4,559 to \$10,655). Additionally, most campaign quality attributes decreased from the previous campaign to the focal campaign, and the overall quality measure also dropped significantly (Quality PCA from 0.54 to 0.33). Furthermore, their engagement with backers decreased dramatically (Engagement from 96.55 to 13.53). In contrast, entrepreneurs who achieved consecutive successes (Panel 2: Success → Success) generally increased their funding goals more cautiously at small margins (Goal from \$6,126 to \$6,771). Meanwhile, they kept similar levels of campaign quality attributes and slightly increased their overall campaign quality (Quality PCA from 0.74 to 0.84). Although their engagement with backers decreased, they continued to maintain a high level of overall engagement (Engagement from 140.6 to 100.7).

## 5.2.2 Signaling Fostering Trust-Building

The potential positive mechanism of successful experiences comes from a signaling effect, whereby prior successful campaigns help to signal credibility and build trust with backers. The test of H5 and the theorization of the research framework suggest the existence of signaling, even though it is weaker compared to superstitious learning. In this section, we report on our further testing of when such signaling materializes.

When the signaling effect manifests, we posit that the signaling effect is more likely to apply to repeated backers who had previously backed successful campaigns by the same entrepreneur such that they are likely to return to the focal entrepreneurs’ subsequent campaign(s) (Butticè et al., 2017). Furthermore, when an entrepreneur achieves blockbuster success, the strong signal strengthens this positive mechanism, further encouraging backers to support the same entrepreneur repeatedly in their subsequent endeavors. This also creates a legitimacy spillover to backers new to the entrepreneurs.

To test this trust-building mechanism of the signaling effect, we analyzed backers in the campaigns of serial entrepreneurs. We constructed two additional dependent variables: RepeatedBackers (the number of backers from prior campaigns of the same entrepreneur supporting the focal campaign) and NewBackers (the number of backers supporting the entrepreneur for the first time in the focal campaign). Following the logic of the proposed mechanism— that is, that signaling fosters trust-building—it is reasonable to expect that the number of repeated backers is less subject to the negative main effect of success, in general, and when blockbuster success exists, repeated and new backers are both less subject to the negative main effect of success.

Table 6 reports the results. As Models 7 and 8 suggest, successful experiences primarily exert a strong negative effect on first-time backers of an entrepreneur rather than on repeated backers. Given that our data indicates that only 20% of backers, on average, are repeated backers from prior campaigns, the aggregated effect on overall campaign performance (in Table 4) is primarily driven by the reduction in the number of new backers. Furthermore, when an entrepreneur has had a prior blockbuster success, Model 9 suggests that trust-building with repeated backers is also more salient. The blockbuster success also engenders strong signals for the credibility and legitimacy of the entrepreneur to firsttime backers, resulting in a relatively weak but significant interaction effect in Model 10. This confirms that trust-building exists, but mainly for repeated backers from prior campaigns and when the campaign creator has achieved a blockbuster success.

## 5.3 Robustness Tests

We conducted several additional robustness tests to further validate our results. Table 7 summarizes these tests. First, we considered alternative model specifications, including panel fixed-effects OLS without Heckman correction (Wolfolds & Siegel, 2019), linear probability model with campaign success as the dependent variable, estimations with campaign delivery outcome controlled, and a regression discontinuity design using campaigns with performance slightly above or below funding goals. We report the robustness analyses of model specifications in Appendix C.

Furthermore, we tested alternative measures and ruled out alternative explanations. We alternatively measured crowdfunding experiences by considering the depreciation effect of less recent experiences (Kim & Miner, 2007) because it is possible that less recent experiences exert less influence than more recent experiences. For successful experiences, we considered the original form, the ratio of successful experiences, and the frequency of prior success as alternative measures. For each of the moderators, we also examined its alternative measures and alternative explanations, including alternative ways of capturing experience relatedness, warm-glow giving (Sutanto et al., 2021) and observational learning from backing experiences, different benchmarks for goal overshooting, <sup>11</sup> and alternative measures and definitions of blockbuster success. Appendix D reports these robustness checks.

Finally, to account for the potential endogeneity problem arising from other time-varying unobserved factors related to successful crowdfunding experiences, we used an instrumental variable approach, with the number of overwhelmingly successful campaigns on the platform as an instrument (Doshi, 2014; Wang et al., 2023). We also instrumented the endogenous moderators in their interaction effects with successful experiences. Appendix E reports the details of instrumental variable estimation.

## 6 Discussion and Implications

This study examines the relationship between successful experiences and campaign performance in crowdfunding. Our review of the literature suggests that there is a lack of consensus regarding the main effects of crowdfunding success. We used panel data involving 3,978 serial entrepreneurs in crowdfunding to analyze this tension. The empirical results suggest that for serial entrepreneurs, successful campaigns are, on average, followed by a decrease in crowdfunding performance. Furthermore, experience relatedness, the acquisition of backing experiences, and blockbuster success can mitigate such a negative effect, whereas goal overshooting can strengthen it. Our theorization, along with the empirical findings, imply that although signaling plays an important role in successful experiences, issues related to superstitious learning hold significant importance in crowdfunding.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

The theoretical implications of this study are threefold. First, we provide insights into the crowdfunding literature by investigating the tension inherent in crowdfunding success. On the one hand, the signaling perspective suggests that successful experiences send positive signals to backers and contribute to trustbuilding (Courtney et al., 2017; Hsu, 2007). On the other hand, when examining serial entrepreneurs’ crosscampaign performance, entrepreneurs may fall into competency traps following a success, leading them to believe that relying on existing strategies will breed further success and that it is therefore not necessary to refine their subsequent campaigns diligently or make better decisions (Peterson & Wu, 2021; Sewaid et al., 2021). Our theorization and empirical findings add to this conversation by suggesting that the negative consequences of experiencing success are more pronounced than the positive consequences in terms of subsequent performance changes. Integrating insights from the organizational learning literature, we elucidate the mechanisms that may contribute to the negative effects of success. When they have experienced success, serial entrepreneurs in crowdfunding are especially likely to draw inaccurate inferences from their prior campaigns and follow previous strategies that seemingly relate to success but may be less effective than other approaches (Levitt & March, 1988; Rhee & Kim, 2015; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014; Zollo, 2009). Additionally, entrepreneurs’ biases, such as cognitive fixation and overconfidence, can further lead to unfavorable outcomes (Bayus, 2013; Zhang & Cueto, 2017).

Second, this study complements the literature’s positioning of experience as a credible signal of campaign quality and founder credibility by providing empirical evidence concerning a negative overall effect of crowdfunding success on subsequent performance. For example, Courtney et al. (2017) employed a cross-sectional analysis of crowdfunding campaigns and incorporated crowdfunding success into their empirical model. Considering campaigns launched by each entrepreneur independently (e.g., cross-sectional analysis fails to consider the experience accumulation process from the current to the subsequent campaign), their findings suggest that compared to entrepreneurs with previous success, campaigns from novice entrepreneurs are less likely to be successful.<sup>12</sup> Complementing this view, the present study focuses on serial entrepreneurship and employs a panel data analysis that captures the withinentrepreneur variations across consecutive campaigns. Our findings suggest that successful crowdfunding experiences do not necessarily breed further success; competency traps—as a result of superstitious learning—appear to be common in crowdfunding in particular and entrepreneurship in general. Therefore, our study, together with the extant literature, suggests that while crowdfunding success can help novice entrepreneurs signal credibility, it does little to help serial entrepreneurs improve subsequent performance. Put differently, more successful experiences in crowdfunding are actually associated with inferior performance because entrepreneurs may not be able to draw appropriate experiential knowledge to apply to new funding attempts (Peterson & Wu, 2021; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014).

Third, beyond the IS literature on crowdfunding, we enrich the organizational learning literature by extending the discussions of unfavorable learning outcomes to the context of entrepreneurship (e.g., entrepreneurial financing in crowdfunding). An emerging topic in organizational learning concerns what contributes to unfavorable outcomes following experience accumulation (Musaji et al., 2020). The traditional learning curve view posits that when performing repetitive, relatively homogeneous tasks (e.g., manufacturing and service industry jobs), individuals can benefit from successful experiences because success helps them develop a richer and more comprehensive repertoire of appropriate strategies (Argote & Epple, 1990; KC et al., 2013; Reagans et al.,

2005; Zollo, 2009). However, in the case of rare events or high-complexity tasks, individuals are likely to learn inappropriately by extracting incorrect inferences from prior experiences, which results in superstitious learning (Zollo, 2009). We add to this conversation by theorizing and empirically demonstrating the negative effects of prior success in the context of crowdfunding, where campaigns are typically rare and complex events that involve causal ambiguity. Furthermore, we respond to the calls for “understanding when different types of experience are complements or substitutes for one another” (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011, p. 1127) by showing that the acquisition of backing experiences can mitigate the negative effect of success.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Beyond theory, this study also yields important implications for practitioners on crowdfunding platforms. Our findings suggest that successful experiences have negative effects on subsequent performance. For successful serial entrepreneurs, one important task is to overcome superstitious learning. They should be mindful of success traps and explore strategies by carefully considering the context of the new campaign, rather than blindly relying on practices used to achieve success in the past (Rhee & Kim, 2015), because they may otherwise be prone to superstitious beliefs that may not be accurate. Simply repeating prior strategies and drawing superficial inferences could inadvertently lead to inferior campaign performance.

Our findings further suggest several courses of action for serial entrepreneurs to overcome superstitious learning. Entrepreneurs can utilize the characteristics of experience and the campaign to mitigate the negative consequences of successful experiences. Specifically, they should consider creating related campaigns to build upon similar content domains instead of exploring largely different domains and benchmarking their prior performance to set more reasonable goals and reduce overconfidence bias. They should also expand their portfolio heterogeneity by backing other campaigns. Learning from other campaigns comes at a relatively low cost and helps broaden one’s set of valuable strategies while reducing cognitive fixation.

Our findings also offer insights for crowdfunding platforms regarding feature design. Because successful experiences engender a negative effect on subsequent campaigns, platforms could design features to better visualize how practices used in previous campaigns led to particular outcomes to dissuade entrepreneurs from drawing inaccurate inferences. They could also conduct more careful campaign screening. For example, when successful serial entrepreneurs return to create new campaigns, more attention should be paid to campaign quality attributes to guarantee the employment of appropriate strategies.

Furthermore, platforms could design algorithms to recognize the relatedness of new campaigns to previous campaigns. When creators launch campaigns that differ significantly from previous successful campaigns, platforms will be able to guide them toward taking an adaptive approach in developing their new campaign. Similarly, when creators set unrealistic funding goals compared to their performance in prior successful campaigns, platforms can respond quickly to remind entrepreneurs to be wary of overconfidence. Finally, platform administrators should encourage entrepreneurs to engage in others’ campaigns and learn from this experience by actively recommending other campaigns to serial entrepreneurs and emphasizing the value of learning about different perspectives through backing activities.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our study has limitations that future research can build on. First, the crowdfunding platform in this study operates on an “all-or-nothing” funding model, meaning that campaigns must reach their fundraising goal by the deadline to receive the pledged funds. This model emphasizes the salience of successful experiences and allows for unambiguous measures of crowdfunding success. In contrast, some crowdfunding platforms adopt the alternative mechanism of “keep-it-all,” where entrepreneurs receive the pledged funds regardless of whether they achieve the funding goal (Burtch et al., 2018). In the “keep-it-all” context, how entrepreneurs perceive the outcome of prior experiences and extract lessons from them can be different. One avenue for future research might involve investigating how provision mechanisms shape the relationship between experience outcomes and subsequent crowdfunding performance. Additionally, it would be valuable to examine the downstream effects of success in different forms of crowdfunding, such as crowdlending (Wang et al., 2024).

Furthermore, when investigating the moderating effects of successful experiences, we primarily focused on the general characteristics of prior experiences, such as relatedness and sources. Other characteristics of or actions taken during prior successful experiences may also weaken or strengthen the effect of crowdfunding success. These might include engagement with backers, reward structure design, and social media activities. Future research could explore more specific actions in prior experiences to understand other important moderators of crowdfunding success.

## 7 Concluding Remarks

Entrepreneurs increasingly rely on crowdfunding platforms as a key method of raising funds. For serial entrepreneurs, it is critical to understand whether or not success breeds further success. Contrary to prior findings and popular belief, our findings suggest that, on average, it does not. In other words, successful entrepreneurs frequently “fall from grace” by following successful campaigns with unsuccessful campaigns. However, our findings point to theoretical and practical ways in which these entrepreneurs might successfully glean lessons from prior successes to perhaps maintain their standing.

## Appendix A: Text Mining Approach to Measure Relatedness Between Campaigns

To measure the relatedness of prior successful experiences and examine the moderating effect of experience relatedness, we adopted a text mining approach. The text mining approach captures the underlying themes in crowdfunding campaigns beyond the predefined categories. In particular, when launching a campaign, entrepreneurs must post a campaign story, depicting the entrepreneurial idea. Therefore, we utilized the textual information provided in the campaign stories to construct the measure of relatedness and to obtain a nuanced measure of experience relatedness (Soublière et al., 2024).

Using a computer-assisted natural language processing technique (latent Dirichlet allocation, LDA), we analyzed the corpus of all campaigns’ stories. LDA is a bag-of-words model that assumes each document (i.e., the text in a campaign story) has a mixture of topics (Griffiths & Steyvers, 2004). By tracing the words that appear in the documents, LDA identifies the topics for each document by associating words with those topics. The topic vector generated by LDA represents the distribution of topics in the document. The output of LDA is a topic distribution vector for each campaign that represents the weights associated with all identified topics.

Procedurally, we used all campaigns in our data as the input for topic modeling. We first conducted pre-processing of the text documents for each text document (i.e., campaign story), including tokenization, lemmatization, removing stop words, and removing words commonly used in Kickstarter campaigns (e.g., “Kickstarter”). Given two campaigns stories (one for the prior successful campaign, and the other one for the focal campaign), a pairwise proximity (i.e., relatedness) value can be computed using the cosine similarity $( \frac { C a m p a i g n _ { a } \cdot \ C a m p a i g n _ { b } } { \| C a m p a i g n _ { a } \| \| C a m p a i g n _ { b } \| } )$ of the extracted topic vectors

between Campaign and Campaign . A similar approach has been adopted in prior literature to determine the similarity between two businesses using textual documents (e.g., Shi et al., 2016) and to measure knowledge distance using descriptions in patent applications (e.g., Kaplan & Vakili, 2015). LDA requires the researcher to predefine the number of output topics. We set the number of topics at 50 for our main results and checked the alternative number of topics (100, 150, and 200) for greater granularity of topics, which produced similar results. We then measured the relatedness of successful experiences by averaging relatedness scores between all prior successful campaigns and the focal campaign.

## Appendix B: First-Stage Selection Model Results

We present and discuss the results of the first-stage selection model in this appendix. In particular, we used the fundraising outcome, creator characteristics, and all other campaign-level controls of campaign j-1 to predict the selection process of launching campaign j in the first-stage selection equation. The selection equation was estimated with a probit model, and the results are presented in Table B1. In the selection model results, entrepreneurs are less likely to launch a subsequent campaign if their last campaign is successful. This is consistent with the nature of crowdfunding because entrepreneurs are less likely to make additional campaign attempts after fulfilling what they want to pursue in crowdfunding (Kuppuswamy & Mollick, 2016). That being said, entrepreneurs are more likely to launch a new campaign when they have not raised a substantial amount of funding in prior campaigns. In addition, entrepreneurs with more backing experiences, longer tenure on the platform, and less preparation for their last campaign are more likely to launch a subsequent campaign. Backing more campaigns allows entrepreneurs to learn from other campaigns and interact with other creators so that they are more ready to launch new campaigns. Similarly, longer tenure implies a greater commitment to the platform to launch new campaigns. However, if entrepreneurs are less prepared in the last campaign, they may feel they could do better in the next campaign and thus are more likely to launch the next campaign; on the contrary, if they spent longer preparing the last campaign, the cost of preparation may prevent them from creating new campaigns. As the selection variables in the selection equation were measured at campaign j-1, these variables were specific to the first-stage model. In contrast, the variables, including the fundraising outcome, in the second stage were measured at campaign j. The selection variables in the first stage thus do not influence the outcome in the second stage, meeting the exclusion restrictions (Wooldridge, 2010).

Furthermore, we explored alternative model specifications with campaign delivery outcomes included. In rewardbased crowdfunding, backers fund campaigns in exchange for rewards, and entrepreneurs deliver promised rewards to backers upon successful fundraising. Appropriate delivery is critical for an entrepreneur to achieve backer satisfaction and continue to be successful. In particular, the delivery of rewards could influence both the selection process (launching the next campaign) and the subsequent fundraising performance. Therefore, we included delivery-related variables in both stages of the Heckman selection model.

To measure delivery outcomes, we relied on the post-campaign updates because entrepreneurs typically stated their fulfillment or shipping status in the updates. Our data included a total of 918,617 updates from all campaigns, and we only focused on updates from successful campaigns after the course of fundraising (379,900 updates), because only successful campaigns attempted to deliver the rewards after fundraising. Among these updates, some mentioned the start of fulfillment (e.g., rewards available to claim) or shipment (e.g., rewards have been shipped), implying the fact that creators have fulfilled the rewards by the time of the update (Tuo et al., 2019). Given the large number of updates to infer such information, we adopted a machine learning approach based on the update texts. For the updates from successful campaigns after fundraising, we randomly selected 33,183 updates from 10% of campaigns in the sample. We then manually coded these campaign updates as delivery-related or not. With the coded subsample, we trained a machine learning model with TF-IDF and support vector machine to predict whether an update was delivery-related. The model utilized the typical words about fulfillment or delivery and achieved a satisfactory performance with a macro F1 score of 0.80. We then used the model to infer whether each of the remaining updates mentioned fulfillment or delivery information.

With each post-campaign update classified as delivery-related or not, we calculated two measures of delivery. First, we measured whether the promised rewards were delivered as the existence of delivery-related updates (Delivery). Because whether an entrepreneur delivered the rewards directly influences the decision to launch the subsequent campaign, we included this measure of campaign j-1 in the first-stage selection model. Second, we measured performance of delivery as the on-time delivery ratio in all prior successful campaigns from the same entrepreneur. To capture on-time delivery, we compared the time of the first available delivery-related update and the (most frequent) estimated delivery time in the reward options. A campaign was labeled as on-time delivery if the month of the first delivery-related update was the same as or earlier than the month of estimated delivery time. We then calculated the on-time delivery ratio of prior successful campaigns before the focal campaign (OnTimeRatio). As this variable may influence subsequent campaign performance, we included it in the second stage model. The results with deliveryrelated variables included are presented in Tables C3 and C4.

In the first-stage selection model in Table C3, delivery has a positive effect on the decision to launch the next campaign. Even though successful entrepreneurs, in general, were less likely to launch subsequent campaigns, those who delivered their campaign rewards may think about the next steps and continue their crowdfunding journey. In Table C4, because only successful campaigns have delivery performance information, we only included entrepreneurs with successful crowdfunding experiences in the estimations. The results show that (1) the findings still hold after controlling the performance of delivery, and (2) the performance of delivery does not significantly influence subsequent crowdfunding performance.

Lastly, to further identify how prior success affects subsequent performance, we examined the effect of successful experiences by focusing on how campaigns with performance slightly above or below their funding goals (i.e., around 100% funded) influence subsequent campaign performance of the same entrepreneurs. Campaigns with funding performance slightly above or below the funding goal achieved similar levels of performance but ended up with different outcomes. Therefore, campaigns with performance slightly above or below 100% funded should have similar qualities, and their outcomes would be determined by the achievement of the funding goal. This is similar to a regression discontinuity design (Angrist & Lavy, 1999), where the selection into treatment group (success) is determined by the cutoff of 100% funded. For these campaigns, as they share similar qualities, their effects on the same entrepreneurs’ subsequent performance are mainly attributed to the campaign outcome (success or failure) rather than other aspects of the campaigns. This allows for a relatively clean setting to identify the effect of prior success. In particular, when a prior campaign’s performance is close to its funding goal, a successful outcome can be a positive signal relative to a failed outcome. In addition, the different outcomes of campaigns drive different motivations in the learning process to reflect on prior campaigns and adapt in subsequent campaigns (Argote et al., 2021). Therefore, superstitious learning can happen because entrepreneurs perceive successful and failed outcomes differently. Taken together, based on our arguments in the paper, we expect that both the positive mechanism (i.e., signaling) and negative mechanism (i.e., superstitious learning) would be stronger for successful campaigns than their failed counterparts around the cutoff. Therefore, we use this design as a robustness test to further identify the relative strength of these competing effects.

We chose campaigns ending up with [90%, 100%) and [100%, 110%] funded as the core set of campaigns with performance slightly above or below their funding goals and identified the next campaigns by the same entrepreneurs as the estimation sample. However, on Kickstarter, it is rare for campaigns to be funded slightly below the funding goal in the range of 90% to 100% (only 128 out of 186,174 campaigns) (Mollick, 2014). This is because campaign typically get momentum to achieve the funding goal once they reach 80% to 90% (Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2017). Therefore, we also considered campaigns with [80%, 100%) and [100%, 110%] funded, and campaigns with [80%, 100%) and [100%, 120%] funded as the alternative sets of campaigns with performance slightly above or below the funding goal and used the next campaigns by the same entrepreneurs as the estimation samples.

Moreover, although campaigns with performance slightly above or below their funding goals are regarded as of comparative quality, their campaign outcomes can still be attributed to the variation in some other factors. To make sure the campaigns with failed and successful outcomes are comparable in quality, we further matched each failed campaign to successful campaigns by Mahalanobis matching on campaign-level covariates. Because failed campaigns slightly below the funding goal are relatively limited, for each slightly failed campaign, we matched three slightly successful campaigns using nearest neighbor matching without replacement. For the matched campaigns, we used the next campaigns by the same entrepreneurs as the estimation sample.

For all estimation samples, we estimated the effect of the last campaign outcome (success or failure) on focal campaign performance. We also included all campaign-level control variables in the estimations. The results are shown in Table C5. For Models 1 to 3, we used the sample of campaigns with their last campaigns’ performance falling into [90%, 110%], [80%, 110%], and [80%, 120%] respectively. For campaigns with their last campaigns under [90%, 110%], the effect of last success is negative but insignificant. It could be because of the limited failed campaigns in this interval, limiting the statistical power. In comparison, for campaigns with their last campaigns under [80%, 110%] or [80%, 120%], the effect of last success is negatively significant, further suggesting that superstitious learning is indeed dominant over the signaling effect. From Models 4 to 6, we used the next campaigns of the matched campaigns under [90%, 110%], [80%, 110%], and [80%, 120%] respectively. The effects of the last success are similar but some of them are only marginally significant, possibly due to a limited sample size after matching. These results with a relatively clean setting of prior success reveal that when the last campaign’s performance is slightly above or below the funding goal, although prior success induces both a positive signaling effect and negative superstitious learning, the consequence of superstitious learning is still dominant.

For backing experiences, we examined the alternative explanations of warm-glow giving and observational learning. First, it is possible that backing experiences could merely indicate financial investment or warm-glow giving rather than a channel for expanding the experience portfolio (Sutanto et al., 2021). To address this concern, we alternatively measured backing experiences by only considering backing activities that involve commenting behaviors (i.e., an entrepreneur posted at least one comment to the campaign she backed) or campaign updates (i.e., the backed campaign posted updates to inform backers of the campaign progress). When entrepreneurs commented on others’ campaigns or received updates from their backed campaigns, they were exposed to more learning opportunities through interactions with other campaign creators. Therefore, these two alternative measures (CommentedBacking and UpdatedBacking) can capture the components in backing experiences that effectively help to broaden the knowledge portfolio of entrepreneurs. The results in Table D4 (Models 1 and 2) remain consistent with these alternative measures.

In addition, because backing experiences capture the activities to learn from other campaigns, an alternative process is observational learning—i.e., entrepreneurs can browse other campaigns to learn from them rather than backing them. To test if such an alternative process exists, we constructed the potential consideration set for entrepreneurs, which includes the campaigns likely observed by the entrepreneur. For each focal campaign, we identified two potential consideration sets for the campaign creator: campaigns in the same category within the past year and campaigns related to the focal campaign (classified as relatedness to the focal campaign over 0.7) within the past year. We then used the number of campaigns in the consideration sets as proxies of observational learning (CategoryCampaign and RelatedCampaign). Models 3 and 4 in Table D4 suggest no observational learning effects. ## Appendix E: Instrumental Variable Estimations

We present the details of instrumental variable estimations in this appendix. Although we employed the Heckman selection model to account for selection and the panel fixed-effects model to remove entrepreneur unobserved heterogeneity, serial entrepreneurs’ successful crowdfunding experiences may still be subject to endogeneity issues such as other time-varying unobserved factors or experiences outside the platform. To formally resolve the endogeneity issues, we adopted an instrumental variable approach by using the number of overwhelmingly successful campaigns on the platform as an instrument for successful experiences. This instrument was operationalized as the number of campaigns achieving \$1 million pledged amount on the platform (i.e., overwhelmingly successful) within the six months before the last campaign (i.e., before the creator of the focal campaign accumulated all prior successful experiences). According to prior research, the number of overwhelmingly successful campaigns can positively affect other campaigns’ performance so that entrepreneurs accumulate more successful experiences at the time of those overwhelmingly successful campaigns, but it should not relate to the performance of the focal campaign (Doshi, 2014; Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, this instrumental variable captures the exogenous component of accumulating successful experiences and is unrelated to other unobserved factors.

In addition to the core variable of successful experiences, some of our moderators may be endogenous, and we applied instrumental variables for these moderators accordingly. For experience relatedness (entrepreneurs may endogenously choose to launch related campaigns), we used the duration (in days) since the last campaign to the focal campaign as an instrument, because a creator with a longer duration from the last campaign paid less attention to prior campaigns and was thus less likely to launch a related campaign (i.e., lower relatedness of successful experience). For backing experiences (entrepreneurs can back other campaigns due to unobserved factors), we instrumented it with the number of backing activities from the campaign creators backed by the focal entrepreneur. That is, for each backing activity, we identified the creator of the backed campaign and counted the creator’s past backing activities. More backing activities from the backed creators motivated the focal entrepreneur to back other campaigns. For goal overshooting (entrepreneurs may overshoot their funding goals endogenously), we used the average pledged amount of all campaigns in the same category in the past year as an instrument, because a higher pledged amount for other campaigns in the past could induce a higher funding goal of the focal campaign and thus increase the chance of overshooting. These instruments all significantly influence the moderators but do not directly affect the focal campaign’s performance.

With these instrumental variables, we estimated panel fixed-effects instrumental variable models (Baum et al., 2003; Wooldridge, 2010). Table E1 presents the results: the number of overwhelming performance projects positively affects successful experiences (Angrist-Pischke test: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 4 2 2 . 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; rejecting the problem of weak instruments), and the effect of successful experiences is consistent. We also applied the instrumental variable for the interaction effects, with the both main variable and moderators instrumented, and the results remained consistent.
