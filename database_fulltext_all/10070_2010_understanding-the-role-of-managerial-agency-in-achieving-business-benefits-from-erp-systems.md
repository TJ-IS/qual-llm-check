# Understanding the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems

Lorraine Staehr

Department of Computer Science & Computer Engineering, La Trobe University, PO Box 199, Bendigo 3552, Australia, email: l.staehr@latrobe.edu.au

Abstract. Previous enterprise resource planning (ERP) research has identified ‘top management support’ as an important factor in implementation success. However, most studies are short on the detail of exactly how and why ‘top management support’ contributes to ERP success. Moreover, although ‘improved management decision-making’ is often claimed as a business benefit of ERP systems, there is little evidence in previous ERP research of it having actually occurred. This paper examines the role of managerial agency at all levels in four Australian manufacturing organizations in achieving business benefits from ERP systems during the post-implementation period. The research contributes to current understanding of the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems by providing theoretically based, detailed and interesting insights from four interpretive case studies.

Keywords: ERP systems, interpretive case study, business benefits, structuration theory

## INTRODUCTION

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems are large, complex software packages that provide an integrated real-time environment based on an enterprise-wide data model with a set of software applications that allow processing of the core transactional data of the organization (Bancroft et al., 1998). Collective investment by organizations worldwide in ERP systems since the early 1990s has been in the order of hundreds of billions of dollars. However, there have been widely varying outcomes from ERP system implementations, with a high degree of risk associated with implementation and use. Some organizations have had very successful ERP implementations (Davenport, 2000), while others, such as FoxMeyer Health Corporation, have suffered disastrous business consequences (Bulkeley, 1996). Despite a large body of ERP research literature from a number of different perspectives, there is not an adequate understanding and explanation as to how and why these varying outcomes occur.

To develop a deeper understanding of these varying outcomes, the research question addressed in this paper is: How and why does managerial agency influence the business benefits achieved from ERP systems during the post-implementation period?

In this research, patterns of benefits realization in the years after ‘go-live’ were explored. This was carried out by conducting interpretive case studies of ERP system use in four Australian manufacturing companies. In each case, achieving business benefits from ERP systems was viewed as an organizational change process, within a specific context, occurring over time.

Although ‘top management support’ is an often-cited factor that contributes to the success of ERP systems, precisely how and why it contributes right across the ERP life cycle are not well understood. In addition, the business benefit of ‘improved management decision-making is an important goal of ERP system use (Davenport, 2000, p. 75; Davenport et al., 2004; Harris & Davenport, 2006). A study by Holsapple & Sena (2003) found that managers in companies that have adopted ERP systems perceive relatively high levels of decision-support characteristics in ERP systems and that they regard these characteristics as important. However, improved management decision-making often fails to materialize during the use of the ERP system (e.g. Ross & Vitale, 2000; Boudreau, 2003). Therefore, this is an important issue for information systems (IS) research and practice.

Using empirical data from four Australian manufacturing companies, this paper contributes to a deeper understanding of the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems. In the context of this work, managerial agency refers to the actions of managers at all levels who had the authority to make decisions regarding ERP planning, implementation and use. This included business staff, IT staff and ERP consultants. For example, decisions made in the planning and implementation phases can impact on the use of the ERP system. During use of the ERP system, the motivation and ability of managers to use the ERP system to improve decision-making can impact on the business benefits achieved from the system. This paper provides a theoretically based understanding and explanation of how and why managerial agency at all levels either enabled or constrained the achievement of business benefits in the four manufacturing organizations.

## BACKGROUND

Four aspects of ERP research form the background to this research. The first is the literature that has proposed ERP life cycles (e.g. Markus & Tanis, 2000; Parr & Shanks, 2000; Ross & Vitale, 2000). This study used the Markus & Tanis (2000) ERP life cycle, which has four phases: ‘Chartering’, ‘Project’, ‘Shakedown’ and ‘Onward and Upward’. The Chartering phase is an initial planning phase, while the Project phase involves getting the system up and running in one or more business units in the organization. The Shakedown phase starts when the system goes ‘live’ and finishes when normal operations are achieved. The final phase, Onward and Upward, starts when normal operations are achieved and continues until the system is replaced.

A second group of ERP literature has concentrated primarily on the Project phase of ERP systems and proposed critical success factors (e.g. Holland & Light, 1999; Parr et al., 1999) or ‘recipes’ for success with ERP implementation (e.g. Markus & Tanis, 2000). Some of these studies have included issues or problems and/or business benefits achieved in the Shakedowr and Onward and Upward phases (e.g. Markus et al., 2000; Chang & Gable, 2002). Many studies identify ‘top management support’ as a critical success factor in ERP implementation (e.g. Bingi et al., 1999; Holland & Light, 1999; Parr et al., 1999; Davenport, 2000, pp. 41–42; Shanks et al., 2000; Sumner, 2000; Willcocks & Sykes, 2000; Somers & Nelson, 2001; Brown & Vessey, 2003). This factor has been shown to be important for IS implementation success in general as well as for ERP systems (e.g. Ginzberg, 1981; Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Duchess et al., 1989). The most detail on top management support requirements is provided by Sumner (2000); however, it is restricted to the Project phase of the ERP life cycle. Both Ross & Vitale (2000) and Willcocks & Sykes (2000) acknowledge the importance of management support during the post-implementation phase, although only in quite general terms.

A third strand of the literature has developed models for assessing the business benefits achieved with ERP systems (e.g. Shang & Seddon, 2000; Gable et al., 2003). Shang & Seddon’s (2000) framework for assessing the business benefits of ERP systems includes five dimensions of benefits: operational, managerial, strategic, IT infrastructure and organizational, with multiple possible benefit categories within each dimension. The business benefits are viewed from a managerial perspective. This benefits framework was used to assess the business benefits achieved during the post-implementation phase in each of the four manu facturing companies studied in this research.

Finally, some studies have looked at the ERP system in use in order to explain how business benefits are achieved from ERP systems. Markus & Tanis (2000) and Markus et al. (2000) related the business consequences to events in the earlier ERP life cycle phases and to external influences because of changing business conditions. In a study of manufacturing organizations, Ross & Vitale (2000) identified four obstacles to achieving business benefits from ERP systems. They were failure to plan and implement performance metrics for the new system, inadequately resourcing the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases, no improved management decision-making and inadequately addressing resistance to change. Of particular interest to this study was the lack of improvement in management decisionmaking, although it should be noted that Ross and Vitale’s study examined organizations that were only 18 months post go-live.

Although the use of an ERP system is mandatory for operational users, the extent of use by managers for decision-making is largely voluntary (Boudreau, 2003). In Boudreau’s investigation in a single organization, she was able to go some way towards explaining the observed limited use of the ERP system for management decision-making. Managers underestimated the complexity of the ERP system and were less likely therefore to learn enough about using the system to fulfil management reporting requirements. In a later paper on this same research, the role of human agency in the use of technology was emphasized (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). However, the lack of reported managerial decision-making benefits may simply be related to the time elapsed after go-live. All of the studies discussed above, i.e. Markus et al.

(2000), Ross & Vitale (2000) and Boudreau (2003) involved organizations that were at most 18 months post go-live with their ERP system.

A study by Shang & Seddon (2003) of organizations 3 years after go-live showed that managerial benefits took about 18 months to start to appear. This finding was supported by Holsapple & Sena (2005) who provide evidence that ERP systems are capable of increasing managerial decision-making benefits. In the Holsapple & Sena (2005) study, 81% of the organizations surveyed had been using the majority of their ERP modules for more than 1 year, and 30% for more than 3 years. They also found significant connections between decisionsupport objectives in the planning phase of the ERP life cycle and decision-support benefits achieved.

Two studies have examined the approaches taken by individual managers in ERP imple mentations. A study by Ward et al. (2005) focused on the management style of the projec team and its influence on business benefits. The managerial competencies required of an ERP project manager during each phase of the ERP life cycle were identified by Kraemmergaard & Rose (2002). However, neither of these studies clearly linked managerial agency to the achievement of business benefits from ERP systems.

Another study by Davenport et al. (2004) looked at the overall business benefits from enterprise systems and the factors that contribute to them. Their statistical model identified three main factors (‘integrate’, ‘optimize’ (i.e. improve business processes) and ‘informate’) that predict perceived business value. The factor ‘informate’ corresponds to ‘improved management decision-making’. Its inclusion in the model indicates its perceived importance in contributing to business benefits from ERP systems.

This study builds on and uses the previous literature in the following ways: It recognizes and adds to the limited research on understanding and explaining the varying outcomes from ERP system use. It focuses on the effect of managerial agency at all levels during the process of ERP implementation and use. In particular, evidence for the influence of ‘top management support’ throughout the entire ERP life cycle and the business benefit of ‘improved management decision-making’ were sought. It is based on empirical data from organizations that had experience with their ERP system for up to 4<sup>1</sup>⁄2 years post go-live, considerably longer than many previous studies. Although the study focused on the business benefits of ERP systems that accrue during use, Markus & Tanis (2000) and Markus et al. (2000) showed that the earlier phases of the ERP life cycle influence each subsequent phase. Therefore, the postimplementation period was considered in relation to the influences of these earlier phases, which formed an important context for ERP system use.

## RESEARCH DESIGN

This section begins with an explanation of the theoretical background to the research and is followed by an outline of the research method. The research method is justified, and site selection is explained, followed by the procedures used for data collection, data analysis and quality control.

## Theoretical background

This research takes the view that ISs are social systems (Land & Hirschheim, 1983). Infor mation technology (IT), in this case the ERP system (i.e. the software and hardware), forms only a part of the IS. ERP systems are implemented and used within a social system. The social theory chosen to analyse ERP implementation and use was structuration theory (Giddens, 1984). Because structuration theory is an emergent process theory (Markus & Robey, 1988; Orlikowski & Robey, 1991; Walsham & Han, 1991) addressing multiple levels of analysis (Jones, 1999), it is a suitable theory to use for an empirical study examining the interaction of context and process over time. In addition, structuration theory emphasizes the importance of human agency in social systems and is therefore an appropriate theory to use when examining the role of managerial agency. Structuration theory was used as a sensitizing device to understand and explain the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems. Using theory to analyse the cases differentiates the research results from mere anecdotes (Klein & Myers, 1999).

The basic elements of structuration theory (Giddens, 1984, p. 29) are shown in Figure 1. The structural properties of social systems both influence and are influenced by human action through the three modalities. Structure has the ability to either enable or constrain human action, and human action can reinforce or change structure. The three dimensions of social structure in social systems are signification, legitimation and domination. It is important to note that this social structure consists of rules and resources that exist only in the human actor’s minds. There are also three dimensions of human action: communication, power and sanctioning of conduct. The dimensions of social structure are produced and reproduced over time by the dimensions of human action. This interaction between structure and human action is called the ‘duality of structure’. Routinization of social activity is an important aspect of human action in structuration theory and describes/explains the difficulty of making people act differently. Walsham & Han (1991, p. 78) call this ‘fixity of social conduct’. In other words, there is a tendency for humans to exhibit habitual behaviour, the behaviour they already know and are comfortable with, and, consequently, they exhibit a natural resistance to change. This propensity for routine behaviour is psychologically linked to reducing anxiety (Giddens, 1984, p. 282).

A number of IS researchers have either used or proposed the use of structuration theory to understand ERP implementation and/or use (e.g. Volkoff, 1999; Chae, 2001; Pozzebon, 2001; Staehr et al., 2004; Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Rose et al., 2005). However, structuration theory is not without its critics. An overview of these criticisms in terms of its relevance to empirical research is provided by Jones (1999). For example, Gregson (1989) questions the use of structuration theory in empirical research because of its high-level concepts, and other ‘authors have suggested that structuration is best considered as a meta-theory, a way of thinking about the world rather than as an empirically testable explanation of social behavior (Jones, 1999, p. 113).

One of the four ways that Jones & Karsten (2008) identify that structuration theory has been applied in IS research is through the use of its key ideas and concepts. This is how structuration theory was used in this research. Both Walsham & Han (1991) and Jones (1999) have expressed concerns that its use in this way may not remain true to Gidden’s (1984) principles. Therefore, care was taken to address these concerns. The use of concepts from structuration theory in this research is consistent with Gidden’s ideas, i.e. the concepts are used in a way that retains the underlying meta-theoretical assumptions of structuration theory. The key ideas and concepts of structuration theory used in this study were the duality of structure, routinization, the unintentional consequences of intentional action and structural contradiction. They provided insights that contributed to a deep understanding of each case and to the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems.

## Research method

An interpretive case study method was used. This research met the conditions for a case study method. The research question asks ‘how’ and ‘why’. The researcher had no control of events within the selected organizations, and the focus, i.e. ERP systems, was on a contemporary phenomenon within a real-life context (Yin, 2003, pp. 5–13). A multiple case study design was chosen because an understanding of the four Australian manufacturing companies studied had the potential to provide multiple sources of evidence and, therefore, a more robust study (Stake, 1994; Yin, 2003, pp. 46–47). In all four manufacturing organizations, SAP (SAP AG, Waldorf, Germany) R/3 software (the client server version of SAP) was implemented, providing some consistency to allow comparison and contrast. As well as similarities, there were differences between the organizations. For example, the organizations were from different manufacturing industries and had different motivations for implementing an ERP system. There were also differences in size, structure and culture. The comparisons made possible by having multiple cases allowed investigation of how variability in context influences managerial agency in the process of organizational change and the eventual outcomes (i.e. business benefits) associated with the planning, implementation and use of the ERP system (Pettigrew, 1985).

Because the time elapsed since going ‘live’ with an ERP system is an important influence on the business benefits achieved (O’Grady, 2002), all organizations in this study had more than 18 months experience with their ERP system, with some as long as 4<sup>1</sup>⁄2 years. The organizations were named ManA, ManB, ManC and ManD to preserve anonymity. Table 1 gives some background information on each of the case study sites. Consistent with an interpretive approach, the primary source of data was face-to-face, in-depth semi-structured interviews. The nature of the research design and its primary dependence on interview data provided the means for rich description of the process within context of achieving business benefits from ERP systems over time in the four manufacturing organizations. As a result, the findings are firmly grounded in the empirical data, one of the advantages of interpretive research.

The role of the researcher in this study was that of an ‘outside observer’ (Walsham, 1995). The main advantage of this role is that the researcher is not seen as having a stake in certain interpretations and outcomes and, therefore, interviewees may be more frank. However a disadvantage is that the researcher does not feel for the organization as an insider would. Also, as was the case in this research, the researcher may be denied access to some data (e.g. documentation about the ERP system planning, implementation and use), because of his or her status as outsider (Walsham, 1995).

As far as possible, the informants were chosen according to their perceived ability to report on the business benefits achieved in particular dimensions of Shang & Seddon’s (2000) ERP benefits framework (see Table 2). That is, the operational, managerial and organizational business benefits were obtained from the perspective of business unit managers, strategic benefits from the perspective of senior management and IT infrastructure benefits from the perspective of the IT manager. However, at one organization (ManC), it was necessary to also interview an implementation partner consultant because the IT staff and all but one of the business representatives on the ERP implementation team had left the organization. There were 9 informants at ManA, there were 5 at ManB, there were 9 at ManC and at ManD there were 5, making a total of 28 informants across the four organizations.

Interviews were conducted at ManA and ManB in late 2001, at ManC in 2002 and at ManD in 2003. Each interview was between 25 minutes and 1<sup>1</sup>⁄2 hours in duration, with most lasting more than 1 hour. Some informants were interviewed a second time or contacted by email o phone to answer follow-up questions. The interviews were tape-recorded, transcribed and returned to informants for checking to ensure accuracy. To provide further information on each case, data were obtained from other sources where possible. This consisted of documentation in the form of internal company documents, company presentations, company newsletters, data from financial databases, company web pages and newspaper articles. The Nudist (N5) software (QSR International Pty. Ltd., Doncaster, Australia) package was used to manage the research data.

Using the Shang & Seddon (2000) ERP benefits framework (shown in Table 2), the business benefits achieved by each organization were scored subjectively to allow comparisons. Both the number of times a benefit was mentioned by different informants and the appropriateness of the background of the informant to comment on the particular business benefit were taken into account in the scoring process. Each organization was classified as achieving limited substantial or extensive business benefits on the basis of the scoring process. The next step in data analysis involved writing a description or ‘story’ about each case (Wolcott, 1994). Then concepts from structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) were used to assist in analysing and interpreting each case. Structuration theory provided a lens through which the differing extent of business benefits achieved by the organizations with ERP systems could be understood and explained through the influence of managerial agency. The seven principles developed by Klein & Myers (1999) for conducting interpretive field studies in ISs were applied in this research to ensure quality control.

## ANALYSIS

The analysis starts with a brief overview of the four cases and is followed by an explanation o how managerial agency influenced the business benefits achieved in each organization.

## Overview of the four cases

The four organizations achieved a different number and extent of business benefits from their ERP systems. ManA and ManC both achieved limited business benefits, ManD achieved substantial business benefits, while ManB achieved the most business benefits of the four, with business benefits gained across all five of the benefit dimensions of the Shang & Seddon (2000) ERP benefits framework. A brief overview of each case indicating the extent of business benefits achieved and the major contributing influences are described in the following sections.

## The ManA case

ManA achieved limited business benefits from its SAP system. The business benefits achieved during the post-implementation period were influenced by the unintended consequences of decisions made in the Chartering and Project phases, e.g. extensive customization in some areas. The situation was exacerbated by an unusual pattern of demand for product as a resul of the introduction of the goods and services tax by the Australian government. Education and training mainly occurred prior to implementation, and there were problems with the level of on-site support provided post implementation. However, the business benefits were assessed at only 21 months after go-live.

## The ManB case

In contrast. ManB achieved extensive business benefits from its SAP system. The SAP implementation at ManB was undertaken as a strategic business initiative proceeding despite a predicted negative return on investment. The ‘small bang’ implementation strategy allowed the project team to learn from the early site implementations, and this knowledge was used to improve subsequent site implementations. However, the use of the same team for both development and training and support post implementation influenced the operational and managerial benefits achieved. There was inefficient and ineffective use of the system by inexperienced users that had a direct impact on productivity. Also, some managers failed to change their routine behaviour and to adopt the new interpretive schemes required to improve management decision-making through the use of SAP.

## The ManC case

ManC achieved only limited business benefits from its SAP system despite a substantia elapsed time since going live with its SAP system. The SAP implementation was motivated by a need to reduce costs and to solve the year 2000 problem. The Project phase was not completed on time. This affected the training schedule, with the training needing to be repeated close to go-live. This was inadequate as a result of IT staff being involved in data conversion and testing at the same time. There were issues of software fit because of some complex manufacturing processes at ManC. A lack of financial resources pervaded the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases at ManC. There was a lack of people resources resulting from redundancies, departure of all but one member of the SAP project team within 6 months of going live and managers and users not having the required skills and abilities to use SAP well. This resulted in an ongoing dependence on the implementation partner. The financial con straints meant that SAP was not upgraded.

## The ManD case

ManD achieved substantial business benefits from its SAP system. Change management started in the Chartering phase with the importance of having business representatives on the project team recognized from the beginning. The staged implementation (i.e. module by module) was a low-risk option. There was no performance dip in the Shakedown period at ManD, as business managers found from the start that SAP was an improvement compared with the legacy systems. Business process improvement was driven by headquarters that expected more accomplishment with less financial resources. It was also driven by employee incentive schemes for improved business processes.

## How and why managerial agency influenced business benefits

Structuration theory provided a theoretical basis for identifying and explaining how and why managerial agency influenced the extent of business benefits achieved by the four organizations. In the context of this work, managerial agency refers to the actions of managers at all levels who had the authority to make decisions regarding ERP planning, implementation and use. This included business staff, IT staff and ERP consultants. Examples from each of the fou organizations of how managerial agency influenced the process of achieving business benefits are shown in Table 3 (which extends across two pages). This study was primarily focused on the achievement of business benefits in the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases, i.e. post implementation. However, decisions made in earlier phases can influence the duration of the Shakedown phase and the subsequent business benefits achieved (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Markus et al., 2000). Two perspectives on the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits from ERP systems were therefore examined in this research. The first involved the decisions that were made during the early phases of the ERP life cycle that influenced the extent of business benefits achieved during post implementation. These can be thought of as indirect influences because, although they occurred during the Chartering and Project phases, their influence extended to the post-implementation period. The second perspective involved the more direct influence of managerial agency in enabling or constraining the achievement of business benefits during the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases, i.e. the post-implementation period. Overlap in the dimensions of the duality of structure can be illustrated by considering the role of managerial agency in supporting change management at ManB prior to go-live (see number 6 in Table 3). To facilitate the change to SAP, those in authority drew on the existing informa norms of communication. That is, the influential people in the organization were identified and approached and convinced of the need to understand and communicate to others the need for change. This example showed all three dimensions in use. Authority (power) and knowledge and understanding of current norms for the most efficient modes of communication were al used to achieve the end of successfully promoting the change to SAP.

More details on the results presented in Table 3 follow along with supporting empirica evidence. The numbers in the text refer to the numbered examples of the nature of manageria agency in Table 3.

In the Chartering phase, senior managers drew on structures of domination to influence (using authoritative resources) the ERP software package (allocative resources) chosen at ManA and ManD (1). In these two organizations, the decision to choose SAP was made by head office based on its successful use in other parts of the organization. However the choice of SAP was greeted quite differently in the two organizations as the following quotes indicate. There was evidence to suggest that SAP was not the best choice from the point of view of manufacturing at ManA:

. . . early in 97 my team recognized that the software that we had wasn’t going to get us to class A in the MRP II<sup>1</sup> project and we looked at five different software alternatives of which SAP was one and I’ve got to say that it wasn’t my preferred option. That was my recommendation to the executive at the time but [the corporate entity] as we were then, had previously made the decision to go to SAP. . . . (Logistics and Planning Manager, Business B, ManA)

In contrast, at ManD, the perception was that they benefitted from the decision made by head office:

So as a smaller company I think we were the benefactors of getting such a system [SAP] because you can imagine on our own, company size, we wouldn’t, the return on investment probably wouldn’t be here. (Section Manager, Electronics Final Assembly, ManD)

This is an example of the same decision having quite different consequences in differen organizations (contexts). At ManA, there were problems with production planning that can be linked to the decision to implement SAP. These problems contributed to a longer-than-normal Shakedown period and delayed the achievement of business benefits. In direct contrast, at ManD, there was no Shakedown period. This was claimed to have occurred because SAP was so much better than the legacy systems.

Although not influencing the choice of a specific software package, at ManC, a lack of allocative resources heavily influenced management’s decision to implement a software package in the first place because it was believed that a package would provide the cheapest option:

. . . they wanted to really have an off the shelf package sort of thing which we wouldn’t do any modifications to, and really just let the system run off the shelf and have no cost of programmers or people like that. (Commercial Manager – Logistics, ManC)

This limited availability of allocative resources for implementation and use at ManC affected the business benefits achieved. It resulted in limited training and change management after go-live and indefinitely delayed the second stage of the implementation and the implementa tion of upgrades. Obviously, management did not foresee the effect of this attitude, which limited the business benefits achievable from the ERP system.

At ManB, managerial agency (authoritative resources) influenced the choice of business representatives on the ERP implementation team, resulting in the best people being chosen (2). Although the implementation was a system replacement, the differences between manufacturing plants were accommodated by using different templates, contributing to the achievement of operational business benefits. In contrast, at both ManA and ManC, second- and third-level people were chosen for the project team. There were two main reasons for not choosing the best people in these organizations. One reason was to maintain, as far as possible, ‘business as usual’ throughout the Project phase. On the face of it, this makes sense but the risks were recognized by this manager:

. . . by taking people from the business and putting them there, yes, you share a bit of knowledge but you also carry across bad practices because if a person is doing a job today and is comfortable with the way they are doing it and you then go and configure your SAP to mirror what you’ve done, what you’ve done is you’ve taken an existing process without reengineering, without doing a BPR, and therefore your bad habits get configured and personally I’ve actually seen this happen. (Business Improvement Manager, Corporate Financial Services, ManA)

The SAP software is the filter through which understanding of the business and its processes must be viewed by users and managers post implementation. The way the SAP software is configured can require users and managers to comprehend new interpretive schemes and shape new structures of signification. Old ways of viewing the business can impact on how the SAP system is configured in the implementation process and therefore ultimately affect the business benefits from its use. The SAP software is interpretively flexible; that is, it is possible to configure the software to suit the existing business processes of the organization, or alternatively. the claimed best practices' of the software can be used. At ManA. there was no re-engineering, and the best practices of the existing businesses within ManA were implemented. This meant that business processes were standardized across ManA. However, the consequences of implementing the existing business processes limited the benefits obtained from the software as the preceding and following quotes testify:

What we did was we said, no, no, no the software has to change to fit the current process. Whether our process is right or not that’s what we know so that’s what we are going to do. So in essence if the process was wrong all that SAP enabled us to do is do the wrong things more quickly, which was, it’s a bit of a cynical view I know, but ideally when you are implementing software you get your processes right before you get the software put in place so that you can take advantage of the software early on. (Logistics and Planning Manager, Business B, ManA)

At ManA, implementing the existing business processes made the changes for users less dramatic but affected the configuration of the software and limited the business benefits achieved. The other reason these organizations chose second- and third-level business representatives was to assist in downsizing the workforce when the implementation was finished. This meant that staff with SAP expertise were lost. This SAP expertise was sorely needed in the post-implementation period limiting the business benefits achieved. This is an example of a decision made at one point in time having unintended consequences at another point in time (unintentional consequences of intentional action).

Managerial agency in the form of authoritative resources through the existing structures of domination influenced the type and quality of ERP expertise used for the implementation at ManA (3). At ManA, the decision to use internal ERP expertise proved problematic. Some of the existing knowledge and assumptions (norms) the internal SAP consultants had about the nature of the ManA businesses from previous SAP implementations in the parent company were not appropriate. This was caused by the ManA businesses being so different from the businesses in the other divisions of the parent company:

. . . the consumer business is very, very different to the rest of [the parent company]. It is a totally different business because it’s a consumer product business and it’s got retail, it’s just the sheer size and volume of its customer base, its supplier base and its manufacturing processes are so different to the rest of the organization. (Business Improvement Manager, Corporate Financial Services, ManA)

## and

. . . the number of SKUs, stock keeping units, in our range was enormous and we found that, I understood from talking to people that SAP didn’t manage a large number of SKUs in a company’s portfolio very well. (Materials Manager, Business A, ManA)

This inappropriate understanding of the work being automated (interpretive schemes) brought by the SAP consultants with experience in other implementations in the paren company contributed to IT infrastructure problems in the post-implementation period:

In terms of the grunt of the machine, it was undersized quite significantly, probably at least by 50% undersized, and we went through a process of upgrading the box, upgrading the memory and upgrading most of the infrastructure associated with SAP. (IT Manager, ManA)

There were performance problems and it took 9 months and a couple of hardware upgrades before these were resolved. This contributed to the long shakedown period at ManA and delayed the achievement of business benefits.

Managerial agency in the form of authoritative resources drawing on structures of domination influenced the degree of customization of the ERP system at ManB and ManA (4). At ManB, there was a strict policy of minimal customization. At ManB, there was a strict policy of minimal customization, and the differences in the way the plants operated was accommodated by using several different templates, with one plant even being allowed to keep its legacy system. However, at ManA, there was extensive customization in some areas, e.g. Production Planning. ManA went live with SAP knowing that there were still some unresolved issues:

. . . we made a decision to go-live knowing that there were some uncompleted design elements of SAP and they cascaded into some fairly serious problems when we did actually go-live. Our forecasting is probably the one that stands out the most. (IT Manager, ManA).

Problems with Production Planning meant that only 30–40% of the volume required was manufactured, and it took 12 months to overcome these problems. Insufficient stock was available for Christmas 2000 period (9 months after ‘go-live’). This meant that attention and resources were diverted from the achievement of business benefits in the post-implementation period. The decision to customize extensively at ManA also had ongoing consequences for the implementation of upgrades.

At ManA, SAP implementation team members drew on existing structures of signification in the Project phase. Accustomed to operating in functional silos, there was lack of communica tion and interaction between the team members in different functional areas (5):

. . . we didn’t actually have the interaction formalised in that we didn’t say at some stage, okay customer service interact with the warehouse so therefore I should be presenting to the warehouse or someone from sales should be presenting to the warehouse, this is how it is going to work. (Customer Service Manager, ManA)

This lack of cooperation caused problems when the ERP system went live:

. . . the problems occurred at the interface to the module. You had experts in each area but you didn’t have people who understood two areas that were interconnecting . . . with the implementation it was, oh well, it must be in customer service, and they would say no, it’s in your area, and you had this sort of grey area in between. (Site Manager, Business A, ManA)

This delayed the achievement of business benefits at ManA because resolving these problems contributed to the long Shakedown phase before the resumption of normal operations.

All of the organizations in this study had strategies for managing the change. However, only examples from the strategies used at ManB and ManD are described here because they drew on the existing structures of legitimation and domination to achieve their ends. Managers used authoritative resources and the existing organizational norms (formal and informal) to sanction the change to the new system (6). So, rather than simply distributing special newsletters and flyers to announce the change, at ManB, managers used knowledge of the existing communication mediums in the business. The change process

. . . focused on personal contact and knowing how the politics works . . . a lot of meetings with the people we knew were the shakers and movers – the ones that influence opinion. (General Manager, Information Technology & Business Solutions, ManB)

At ManD, change management started in the Chartering phase, and the importance of business representatives on the ERP project team was recognized from the beginning:

From the top down the managers called HODs, Heads of Departments, the HODs were told the system’s coming. They were involved in the planning. They were involved in allocating key people from their department in key areas. And then the company pushed that everyone supports it which they do anyway. But it was just reiterated that everyone must support this program and when someone’s got to go and do planning you’ve got to back up their job so it doesn’t fall over so that people can stay on these processes and get it right. (Vice President, Trading & Aftermarket Division, ManD)

As part of their change strategies, both ManB and ManD targeted those people in the organization who could influence the success of the ERP system. In the case of ManB, it involved both formal and informal lines of communication, while, at ManD, they targeted only the formal ones.

All of the above influences of managerial agency on the business benefits achieved in the four organizations occurred during the Chartering and Project phases. The influences of managerial agency from the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases on the business benefits achieved are presented now.

Senior managers at ManA drew on old, inappropriate social structures associated with previous experiences of IT projects when they expected business benefits within 3 months of going live with SAP (7):

As early as May last year one of the executive [senior management group of ManA] members, and this was three months into SAP, one of the executive members made the comment that they were disappointed that we hadn’t realized the benefits in SAP. (Logistics and Planning Manager, Business B, ManA)

This attitude on the part of management was not conducive to the provision of adequate resources for education, training and support during the post-implementation period. Users had difficulty understanding and communicating about the new ways of doing work that were required to acquire the most out of the use of SAP. This contributed to ManA’s long Shakedown phase and delayed the achievement of business benefits in all dimensions.

Although ManD did not have a formal benefits realization plan for the post-implementation period, senior management from head office encouraged the improvement of business processes in two ways. The first involved small (5%) reductions in budgets to encourage managers to reduce costs (8). Pressure for cost reduction meant that managers at ManD were forced to actively seek ways to use the ERP system to improve business process and hence reduce costs during post implementation. This increased operational business benefits. Ir addition, senior management from head office encouraged individual employees to improve business processes through an employee incentive scheme. As users understood and knew more about using SAP, they saw how business processes could be improved and were rewarded by management for their suggestions. This increased the operational business benefits achieved at ManD.

In all organizations, there was evidence that the use of SAP reinforced the traditional structural properties, i.e. the structures of domination in the form of management control by making users more accountable in the work place (9). The following quote gives one manager’s perspective:

. . . I think the one big benefit we get out of SAP is that because it’s a fully integrated system – that’s what ERPs really are about – is that it forces accountability . . . with SAP because it’s integrated you actually see the impact of your actions almost instantly . . . certainly it’s made people more accountable because there’s transparency in what happens . . . culturally it has made a big difference because you have somebody like a warehouse person who’d never worry about dollars before becomes a bit more aware of that. (Business Improvement Manager, Corporate Financial Shared Services, ManA)

This was facilitated because operational-level use of the SAP system is mandatory. This increased accountability of users to management is an example of the way that human action was constrained by the use of the SAP system. The nature and extent of the change is explained in the following quote:

. . . there is more visibility there and as a result of there being more visibility it means that that information can be accessed by any number of people and it’s accurate. Whereas in the past, well, because there wasn’t the visibility there, one person might have had control over those numbers. That gave them a fair amount of power in terms of, well, we can’t rely on this unless Joe Bloggs tells us because he’s the only one that really knows what this is all about, and that’s certainly changed it [sic]. (Group Business Solutions Manager – Finance, ManB)

There was evidence of changed structures of signification at ManB. Use of the ERP system facilitated understanding and communication in the strategic planning process (9). Senio management had:

. . . a common architecture, a common application and a common language . . . (Genera Manager, Information Technology & Business Solutions, ManB)

on which to base discussions. This contributed to the achievement of strategic business benefits at ManB in all categories of the strategic dimension of the Shang & Seddon (2000) ERP benefits framework

Although the use of an ERP system can be considered mandatory (e.g. Pozzebon, 2001), this is not true for all tasks and one example is management reporting. Although it was acknowledged at ManA that there was better information available for managerial decisionmaking, it was not generally felt that it was being used (9). One of the reasons may have been the difficulty of overcoming the effects of routine behaviour, which can obstruct the introduction of new ways of doing work (interpretive schemes). The introduction of financial shared services enabled by and implemented prior to the ERP system at ManA meant that, in many cases, managers, although reluctant, were forced to use the system to produce their own reports because the person who had previously performed this for them was no longer available. This forced the new norms to come into practice:

. . . people are used to just picking up the phone to someone and saying can you run this for me and you say well, actually you know it’s pretty easy for you to do it yourself so I will come up and show you how to run it. (Finance Business Analyst (Retail), ManA)

Some managers felt empowered through the changed work place norms:

I am able to get information I wasn’t able to get before and I can get it without having to go to a programmer, without having to put in a request and I can get it immediately and you can download the information to a file and sort it, do whatever you want without any real problem. (Site Manager, ManA)

However, this was not typical right across ManA. Whereas 95% of the users in the finance area had adjusted to the new norms, in other functional areas, it was as low as 50%.

In the other organizations, some managers continued to request reports from subordinates (routine behaviour) rather than obtain what they required themselves from the ERP system. A ManB

. . . it’s probably worse at a senior management level, where senior management will stil have that expectation that data will be provided to them by someone else, which is what, the system is not designed to do it that way. (Group Business Solutions Manager – Finance, ManB)

## and at ManD

. . . that cultural component really hasn’t changed very much. The people who, you might say that the people are still, if they still need a report, then SAP even five years after the effect, even though they would be able to go to the system and drill down reports, inquire the system and get things out of the system if they had the mindset to do that. [i.e. they stil request reports from the IT department] (Executive Vice President, Chief Financial Officer, ManD)

It was acknowledged at ManD that managers were expected to learn for themselves. Although the need for ongoing training during post implementation was recognized by senior management, it was not considered cost effective. Therefore, as illustrated in the quotes above, even in the organizations that achieved most business benefits (i.e. ManB and ManD), there was not widespread use by managers of the capability to obtain real-time online reports to improve management decision-making. Also, even in the organizations where the version of the ERP system used made it unnecessary, managers still persisted in downloading data into MS Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

This study examined the nature and role of managerial agency across all levels of business and IT management during ERP planning, implementation and use in the four organizations. It highlighted the complexity of the role of managerial agency in the achievement of business benefits from ERP systems. One might expect that, because in the long run, substantial organizational change is required, only managerial agency that supported a change to new structural properties would have had a positive influence on business benefits. However, this study showed that this was not necessarily the case. Managerial agency drew on old (before the ERP system was implemented) and new structural properties that either constrained or enabled the organizational change process of achieving business benefits from the ERP system. For example, a decision to choose the best business representatives for the imple mentation team had implications in terms of the configuration of the system. The best business people are more likely to have the ability to develop new ways of doing work (i.e. to change the structures of signification) in a way that can take advantage of the capabilities of the ERP system and improve business processes. When these improved processes are used in the configuration of the ERP system, then managers and users must learn and use new structural properties (changed structures of signification) during use of the ERP system, thereby increasing the business benefits achieved. However, in contrast, use of the ERP system served to strengthen the existing structural properties (structures of domination) by increasing the accountability of managers and users, which also had the effect of increasing the business benefits achieved.

Managerial decisions, i.e. managerial agency at all levels in an organization throughout the entire ERP life cycle, influenced the extent of business benefits achieved during post implementation. Managerial decisions made in the Chartering and Project phases influenced the extent of business benefits achieved during the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases. Previous works by both Orlikowski & Robey (1991) and Jones & Nandhakumar (1993) contend that the separation of the implementation and the use of IS is artificial. For ERP systems in particular, Markus & Tanis (2000) and Markus et al. (2000) found that problems and issues in the earlier phases of the ERP life cycle impacted on the later phases. This study supports these findings. Moreover, Holsapple & Sena (2005) found significant connections between the setting of decision-support objectives in the Chartering phase of the ERP life cycle and decision-support benefits eventually achieved. Because none of the organizations in this study set decision-support objectives in the Chartering phase (Holsapple & Sena, 2005), it is possible that this omission also influenced the limited managerial decision-making benefits achieved. However, the results from this study differ from those in the previous work by explaining in depth how and why influences from earlier phases occurred and what the eventual consequences were for achieving business benefits from the ERP system (see Table 3).

The context in which managerial decisions occurred was a critical influence on whether a particular decision contributed to achieving business benefits. Sensitivity to context was a natural by-product of the interpretive approach used for data collection in this study. For example, the same managerial decision on the choice of software in ManA and ManD resulted in very different consequences (see (1) in Table 3). The difference in consequences could be explained through an understanding of the different contexts in which the decisions were made. The complexity of human intentionality is shown by the identification of manageria actions that were soundly based at one point in time but had negative consequences at another point in time (unintentional consequences of intentional action). For example, the choice of second- and third-level business people for the ERP implementation team at ManA seems logical if the concern is to make sure that the business continues to run ‘as usual’. However, this decision had serious implications for the quality of the ERP configuration. An unintended conseguence of choosing second- and third-level people for the ERP implementation team can be:

If you automate a bad business process, you get a faster, more expensive, bad business process. (Markus, 2004, p. 12)

In addition, targeting the second- and third-level staff on the implementation team for redundancies meant that ERP expertise was lost during post implementation when this expertise was sorely needed to assist in maximizing the business benefits from the ERP system. These examples illustrate the importance of context in understanding and explaining the influence of managerial agency on the achievement of business benefits from ERP systems. Therefore, this study adds to a growing body of ERP literature that has considered the role of context in studying ERP planning, implementation and use (e.g. Sia et al., 2002; Dechow & Mouritsen, 2005; Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Wagner et al., 2005; Alvarez, 2008).

Few previous ERP studies have involved organizations with a post-implementation period greater than 18 months. The organizations in this study had gone live with their ERP system between 21 months and 4<sup>1</sup>⁄ years. Therefore, this study provides valuable insights into the role of managerial agency in achieving business benefits during a prolonged post-implementation period. Surprisingly, this increased elapsed time after go-live did not provide evidence that the ERP system was used extensively by managers for decision-making. These findings suppor those of Ross & Vitale (2000) and Boudreau (2003).

The lack of widespread use of the management reporting capabilities of ERP systems reported in these organizations has three possible but not mutually exclusive explanations. First, there was evidence that lack of training influenced the extent of use of the ERP system for management decision-making, supporting the findings of Boudreau (2003). Second, there was evidence in all the organizations that some managers persisted with routine behaviour by requesting reports from subordinates although it was possible to obtain the reports for themselves from the ERP system. Previous research has shown that these routine practices are difficult to change if allowed to continue (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994). Third, the structural properties of the social system that the ERP system operated in reinforced the autonomy of managers who are voluntary users rather than mandatory users of the ERP system. This structural contradiction between the actual (voluntary use) and desirable (mandatory use) actions of some managers served to limit the business benefits achieved. It may be that using the ERP system to produce reports is perceived by managers who are accustomed to requesting reports from subordinates as a loss of status. Therefore, although elapsed time after go-live has an influence on improved decision-making by, for example, ensuring that appropriate data is available, the actual extent of use of the ERP system by individua managers was also influential in achieving business benefits. This study showed that the business benefit of ‘improved managerial decision-making’ does not simply accrue based on the period of time since the ERP system went live but that it requires managerial agency to be fully achieved. The significance of improved managerial decision-making to organizations flags this as an important area for future research.

The use of structuration theory as a ‘sensitizing device’ resulted in a detailed, deeper understanding and explanation of the role of managerial agency than has been provided by previous ERP research. For example, ERP critical success factor research simply provides prescriptive phrases such as ‘top management support’ (e.g. Holland & Light, 1999; Somers & Nelson, 2001) without showing the detail of the relationship between the factors and the process of ERP planning, implementation and use. The theoretically based interpretation of the results in this study provided rich insights into these relationships and into the complex process of achieving business benefits from ERP systems. This demonstration of the usefulness of concepts from structuration theory contradicts the claims of Gregson (1989) that structuration theory is of little use in empirical research. It is hoped that this may encourage more IS researchers to use structuration theory in their work.

The findings from this study highlight some areas for improvement in IS management practice in terms of obtaining business benefits from ERP systems. First, in the Chartering and Project phases, increased cooperation and communication across functional areas can inform configuration decisions, thus, providing the opportunity to take full advantage of the integrated nature of the ERP system and its decision-making capabilities. Second, management can provide bonuses and/or incentives to retain employees with the necessary skills and abilities to help the organization to achieve business benefits from the ERP system. Third, during the Shakedown phase, it is not sufficient to provide training prior to go-live and then some one-on-one support for a month or so. Training needs to be ongoing throughout both the Shakedown and Onward and Upward phases. This is needed at both user and manageria level, and incentives may be necessary to bring managers to training sessions and to encourage them to use the ERP system to improve decision-making capabilities. Finally, although none of these organizations had a formal benefits realization plan, one of them had measures in place that encouraged business benefit realization. At ManD, the achievement of business benefits was driven by two features of the internal environment. The first was an employee incentive scheme instigated by head office. This scheme rewarded employees for improvements to business processes. The second was constant pressure from head office for managers to do the same work with less financial resources. With adequate attention to the specific context, these findings can be of some use to other organizations where ERP systems are either planned or already in use.
