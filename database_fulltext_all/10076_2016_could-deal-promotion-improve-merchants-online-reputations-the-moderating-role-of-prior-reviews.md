# Could Deal Promotion Improve Merchants’ Online Reputations? The Moderating Role of Prior Reviews

XITONG LI

XITONG LI (lix@hec.fr) is an assistant professor in the department of Information Systems and Operations Management, HEC Paris, France. He received his Ph.D. in management from the MIT Sloan School of Management, and his Ph.D. in engineering from Tsinghua University. His research interests include the economic and social impacts of using online data and information, and innovative technologies using online data and services. His research has appeared, or is forthcoming, in Journal of Management Information Systems, ACM Transactions on Internet Technology, IEEE Communications Magazine, IEEE Transactions on Automation Science and Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, and other leading journals and conference proceedings. He won the Best Paper award at the 46th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS).

ABSTRACT: It is by now almost accepted as a stylized fact that offering deal promotion (such as via Groupon or LivingSocial) deteriorates local merchants’ online reputations (e.g., the average of Yelp review ratings). However, in this paper we show that the stylized fact is not true in certain circumstances. We theorize that the valence and volume of prior reviews can play an important moderating role in the effect of deal promotion. Empirically, we show that restaurants with a relatively low prior average rating and a relatively small review volume have improved their online reputations by offering Groupon promotion. The proportion of such restaurants is substantial. The findings are robust to multiple identification strategies and econometric specifications. The results underscore the substantial heterogeneity in the effect of deal promotion on local merchants’ online reputations. Merchants need to understand the moderating role of prior reviews (e.g., the valence and volume of prior reviews) and design appropriate strategies to maximize the returns from offering deal promotion.

KEYWORDS AND PHRASES: deal promotion, difference-in-differences, moderating roles, online deals, online group sales, online reviews, propensity score matching.

The popularity of using daily deals as a new online marketing vehicle has dramatically increased in recent years [4, 16, 29]. Groupon.com, the leading daily-deal site, has successfully filed to go public within only three years since its launch in 2008 and gained a market value of \$4.9 billion by January 2015. As of April 2012, consumers in North America have spent approximately \$7 million a day (more than \$2.5 billion a year) on daily deals,<sup>1</sup> and it is projected to reach \$5.5 billion a year by $2 0 1 6 . ^ { 2 }$ Numerous restaurants have been offering deep-discount vouchers through daily-deal sites (e.g., Groupon, LivingSocial, and Amazon Local) and thus it is important to understand the impact of deal promotion on the long-term business performance of those restaurants.

Despite the tremendous popularity of using daily deals, anecdotal evidence suggests that the businesses of many local merchants have actually suffered because of offering deal promotions. For example, the owner of a local food business wrote an online blog about her “terrible” decision to offer a Groupon promotion, which caused a financial loss of \$8,000.<sup>3</sup> Similar anecdotal stories from practitioners have spread broadly online. Academic researchers have also started exploring the impact of offering deal promotions. Because data about local merchants’ financial performance are not easily available, researchers have examined the impact on online reputation as measured by the valence of user-generated online reviews (average review rating), which has been shown to be an important performance metric of many businesses, including restaurants [2, 34], books [8], video games [47], and mobile apps [24, 33]. Notably, Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [6] conducted the first empirical study on this topic and showed that on average there is a sharp decline in Yelp ratings of local merchants, which coincides with offering Groupon promotions. Given the anecdotal evidence and prior academic research, by now it has almost been accepted as a stylized fact that offering deal promotions will deteriorate local merchants’ online reputations. Consequently, many practitioners and researchers question whether deal promotion is a good online marketing tool and are concerned about the validity of the fundamental business model of daily-deal sites. However, the prior research by Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [6] neglects to establish a causal relationship between deal promotion and Yelp reviews, because potential confounding factors are not explicitly controlled in their empirical analysis. Therefore, in-depth empirical study is still lacking in the literature.

More important, despite the stylized fact about the negative effect of deal promotion, deal users may post favorable reviews about their postconsumption experiences. According to the consumer satisfaction model (CSM) developed in the marketing literature [1], a consumer’s postconsumption satisfaction is the sum of two functions: one is an increasing function of the experienced net utility and the other is an increasing function of the difference between the experienced net utility and the expected utility. The deep discount given by deal promotion would affect both consumers’ experienced net utility and expected utility. While the low price may increase consumers’ experienced net utility [31], it may also lower consumers’ expected utility because consumers often form a lower perception of product quality when the price is low or discounted [46]. As deal users have a higher experienced net utility and a lower expected utility, they are likely to have more positive postconsumption satisfaction and to post favorable online reviews [1, 37].

Therefore, whether deal promotion deteriorates or improves merchants’ online reputations (the average of online review ratings) is an empirical question. To uncover the condition under which the mechanism for the positive effect of deal promotion would dominate the opposite, we further explore how the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews positively moderate the effect of deal promotion. Considering the stylized fact about the negative effect of deal promotion, this study aims to answer two research questions:

1. Can the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews positively moderate the effect of deal promotion?

2. Can the effect of deal promotion be positive for merchants with a relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews?

To answer the research questions, we first collect time-series data about Yelp review ratings of a sample of restaurants before and after they have offered a Groupon promotion. In the preliminary study, we compare the monthly average Yelp ratings of the restaurants before and after Groupon promotion and use the standard fixed-effect model to estimate the effect of Groupon promotion. The results of the preliminary study suggest that while the aggregate average effect of Groupon promotion on restaurants’ monthly Yelp ratings is negative (consistent with the stylized fact), the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews positively moderate the effect of deal promotion. Furthermore, for restaurants with a relatively low prior average rating and a small number of reviews, their monthly average Yelp ratings in fact increase as a result of Groupon promotion.

While time-invariant restaurant heterogeneity (e.g., restaurant location and environment, food category) is reasonably controlled by using the standard fixed-effect model, the preliminary study has a critical limitation in establishing the causal effect of Groupon promotion. The key challenge lies in the fact that a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion is endogenous (also called the restaurant’s self-selection). For example, a restaurant may decide to offer a Groupon promotion because its online reputation has already suffered (i.e., the average Yelp review rating has been decreasing over time). In this case, we would observe a decrease in the average Yelp ratings after Groupon promotion even if it had no real effect on restaurants’ Yelp reviews. Without addressing the self-selection of a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion, the estimation of the effect of Groupon promotion would be biased.

Therefore, in the main study we collect a new set of data and use the difference-indifferences (DID) model combined with a preprocessing matching procedure to explicitly address the self-selection issue and estimate the causal effect of Groupon promotion. Specifically, we assemble a set of “control” restaurants that have offered Groupon promotion but only later than the “treated” restaurants (those are analyzed in the preliminary study). By using a preprocessing matching procedure, we ensure that the two groups of restaurants (treated versus control) are comparable in terms of a number of factors that may simultaneously influence a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion and its Yelp review ratings. Accordingly, the DID model allows us to identify the causal effect of Groupon promotion by estimating the difference in the change of monthly average Yelp ratings from the periods before to those after deal promotion between the matched treated and control restaurants [3]. The DID model combined with the preprocessing matching procedure is arguably a more appropriate econometric method of establishing the causal relationship, compared to the traditional instrumental variable approach, because no strong exclusion restriction assumption is required [19]. It has been widely used in the recent IS and marketing literature to identify the causal effects of an agent’s action [21, 23, 26, 41].

The results of the main study confirm that the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews positively moderate the effect of Groupon promotion. More important, the moderating role of prior reviews is sufficiently large to result in a positive effect of Groupon promotion. Restaurants with a relatively low prior average rating and a relatively small number of reviews have improved their Yelp review ratings by offering Groupon promotion. For example, the main study shows that for restaurants with a prior average rating not higher than 3.67 and the number of prior reviews not more than 30, the subsequent monthly average Yelp ratings increase by 0.22 stars after Groupon promotion. This specific type of restaurants accounts for about 24 percent of all restaurants in our sample that have offered a Groupon promotion. The results are qualitatively similar when we use different cutoffs for the prior average rating and review volume. The findings suggest that the stylized fact that offering a deal promotion would deteriorate local merchants online reputations is not true in certain circumstances. Whether deal promotion would deteriorate or improve a merchant’s online reputation depends on the merchant’s characteristics, such as the valence and volume of prior reviews.

In addition to addressing the potential self-selection bias introduced by the endogenous restaurant decision to offer a Groupon promotion, the matching procedure in the main study generates new substantial insights into the decision. Specifically, we find that a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion is likely influenced by restaurant age, distance from the city center, household income of the surrounding area, average review rating, and number of reviews. For example, restaurants that are relatively new and distant from the city center and have favorable Yelp reviews are more likely to offer a Groupon promotion.

In summary, this paper adds to the literature on information systems (IS), electronic commerce and marketing in several aspects. First, despite the large body of literature documenting the impacts of online product reviews on various outcomes [2, 8, 10, 11, 25, 28, 30, 31, 32, 47], relatively fewer studies have examined the determinants of user-generated online reviews [13, 31, 35, 45]. Our study complements those works by showing that deal promotion is a determinant of usergenerated online reviews. Specifically, we establish the causal relationship between restaurants’ offering a Groupon promotion and their Yelp review ratings. Second, we find that the valence and volume of prior reviews play an important moderating role in the effect of deal promotion. Despite the stylized fact about the negative effect of deal promotion, we show that the moderating role of the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews can be strong enough to result in a positive effect of deal promotion. Finally, we find a number of factors that are likely to influence a restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion [29]. We believe that the findings generate important implications and shed light on strategies not only for restaurants but also for local merchants in other industries (e.g., cleaning service, massage) who have online reviews and consider offering deal promotions.

## Related Literature

## Effect of Promotions

Promotions can induce consumers to switch brands and purchase earlier [40] and affect consumers’ consumption decisions [44]. While most prior studies on the effect of promotions focus on exploring the effect at the time during which they are offered, less attention has been devoted to studying the effect after the promotions have ended [14]. Researchers have examined the effect of promotions on brand evaluation and preferences in the long run, but the findings are largely mixed. Dodson, Tybout, and Sternthal [17] assert that the use of promotions undermines a brand’s evaluation, which is manifested in a lower aggregated probability of repurchasing the brand. Neslin and Shoemaker [39] show that the aggregate repurchase probability can decrease after a promotion, even if individual repurchase probability remains unchanged. In contrast, by directly measuring brand evaluation, Davis, Inman, and McAlister [12] find no evidence that the memory of promotions drives down brand evaluation. Based on a comprehensive meta-analysis, DelVecchio, Henard, and Freling [14] note that promotions can either enhance or undermine brand preference, depending on the characteristics of the promotion and the promoted product. Besides the effect on brand evaluation and preference, researchers show that in the long run promotions could increase consumers’ price sensitivity, especially for nonloyal consumers [36]. However, little extant literature has examined whether promotions affect consumers’ postconsumption satisfaction or their online feedback about the product after the promotions.

## Determinants of Online Reviews

While there is a large body of IS and marketing literature on the various impacts of online product reviews [2, 8, 9, 10, 11, 25, 28, 30, 31, 32, 47], relatively fewer researchers have examined the determinants of user-generated online reviews. Dellarocas, Gao, and Narayan [13] find that consumers prefer to post reviews for products that are less available and less successful or products that many others have already discussed online. Moe and Schweidel [37] investigate how previously posted review ratings affect an individual’s posting behaviors in terms of whether to post (incidence) and what to post (evaluation). Their results show that positive ratings increase posting incidence, whereas negative ratings discourage posting. Ma et al. [35] further show that the reviewer and review characteristics can moderate the relationships between prior reviews and subsequent reviews. Wang, Zhang, and Hann [45] study friends’ social influence in online review ratings and show that rating similarity between friends is significantly higher after the formation of friendships. Li and Hitt [31] explore how idiosyncratic preferences of consumers (especially early consumers) affect their behaviors in purchasing and posting online reviews. Despite the extant studies, little research examines whether and how firms’ online promotion practices would affect consumers decisions to post online reviews. Although the focus of the study by Li and Hitt [31] is not on the determinants of online reviews, their results imply that firms could design effective marketing strategies, such as pricing and promotions, to encourage consumers to self-select into the market early and generate positive online reviews. The current paper complements those studies.

## Hypothesis Development

Bridging the literature on online promotions and the determinants of online product reviews, we investigate the effect of deal promotion on subsequent online reviews and how prior reviews moderate the effect.

To the best of our knowledge, the only published work on the effect of deal promotion is that by Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [6]. Using a large-scale data set, they show that on average Yelp ratings of local merchants decrease after they have offered Groupon promotions. They examine a number of plausible explanations for the negative effect, including that (1) merchants who decide to offer Groupon promotion are of lower quality and thus receive poor reviews when they attract new customers through Groupon promotion; (2) the surge of too many customers with Groupon vouchers overwhelms merchants and thus the quality of their foods and services decreases; (3) merchants actively discriminate against customers with Groupon vouchers and selectively provide lower-quality services to them; (4) reviews from Groupon users are more likely to be real and accurate, whereas those from non-Groupon users may contain a higher proportion of inflated review ratings; (5) Groupon shoppers often engage in experimentation and thus may be more likely to try products or services that do not match their tastes; and (6) Groupon users are more critical and tend to give less favorable reviews than regular customers. Most of the plausible explanations are applicable in a broader sense of deal promotion and it has been almost accepted as a stylized fact that offering a deal promotion will deteriorate merchants’ online reputations (the average of online review ratings). Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1a: For restaurants offering a deal promotion, the average of their subsequent review ratings decreases after they offer a deal promotion.

However, there are theories predicting that deal users may post favorable reviews after promotion. We build the hypothesis based on the consumer satisfaction model (CSM) from the marketing literature [1]. According to the CSM, a consumer’s postconsumption satisfaction S is the sum of two functions: $S = f _ { 1 } ( U ^ { p } ) + f _ { 2 } ( U ^ { \mathfrak { p } } - \mu ^ { e } )$ , where $f _ { 1 } ( U ^ { p } )$ is an increasing function of the perceived quality $U ^ { p } ,$ , and $f _ { 2 } ( U ^ { p } - \mu ^ { e } )$ is an increasing function of the difference between the perceived quality $U ^ { p }$ and the expected quality $\mu ^ { e } .$

Perceived quality $U ^ { p }$ is the utility derived from consumption, which is represented as the actual consumption utility minus the product price [31]. We note that the deep discount given by deal promotion affect both $U ^ { p }$ and $\mu ^ { e } .$ On the one hand, the low price after the deep discount increases $U ^ { p } ,$ , because $U ^ { p }$ is the net consumption utility [31]. $\operatorname { A s } f _ { 1 } ( U ^ { p } )$ increases with $U ^ { p } ,$ , postconsumption satisfaction S would be greater given the low price. On the other hand, the low price may lower $\mu ^ { e } ,$ , because in many cases consumers form a lower perception of product quality when the price is low or deeply discounted [46]. As deal users have a higher $U ^ { p }$ and a lower $\mu ^ { e } , f _ { 2 } ( U ^ { p } - \mu ^ { e } )$ would increase. Accordingly, deal users have higher postconsumption satisfaction S and post favorable online reviews [37]. Therefore, the subsequent review ratings of local merchants are expected to increase after they offer a deal promotion with deep discounts. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1b: For restaurants offering a deal promotion, the average of their subsequent review ratings increases after they offer a deal promotion.

Because there are competing mechanisms, whether deal promotion decreases or increases the average of subsequent review ratings is an empirical question. To uncover the condition under which the mechanisms for the positive effect of deal promotion would be stronger than the opposite, we further explore how prior reviews moderate the effect of deal promotion. Specifically, we argue that relatively less favorable valence and small volume of prior reviews would enhance the mechanisms for the positive effect of deal promotion.

When merchants’ prior reviews have a less favorable valence (that is, a relatively low average of prior review ratings), consumers will form a lower expectation about their products (lower $\mu ^ { e } )$ , thus increasing $f _ { 2 } ( U ^ { p } - \mu ^ { e } )$ and postconsumption satisfaction S [46]. Therefore, consumers are more likely to post favorable reviews when a merchant has a relatively low prior average rating.

However, in a case where the less favorable valence of prior reviews is based on a large number of negative reviews, consumers may still not post favorable reviews even if their postconsumption satisfaction increases. This is because the large number of unfavorable reviews would create a negative rating environment in which consumers may be discouraged to post their online opinions [37] or their online opinions may be negatively biased [38]. Therefore, even when deal users postconsumption satisfaction is increased, they are still less likely to post favorable reviews if the merchants have a large number of negative reviews. In other words, the mechanisms for the positive effect of deal promotion are salient when the valence of prior reviews is relatively less favorable and the volume of prior reviews is relatively small.

In addition, in practice many online review sites restrict the ratings to within the bounds of between one and five stars. Because of this restriction, the average of the subsequent review ratings of a merchant whose prior average rating is already low (say 2.5 stars) may not have much room to decrease and is thus more likely to increase after deal promotion. Also, a relatively small volume of prior reviews means that the merchant’s review ratings have small “inertia” and thus the average of subsequent review ratings is more vulnerable to change.<sup>4</sup> Again, this suggests that the mechanisms for the positive effect of deal promotion are enhanced when the valence of prior reviews is relatively low and the volume of prior reviews is relatively small.

Therefore, when the valence of prior reviews is relatively low and the volume of prior reviews is relatively small, we expect that the average of the subsequent review ratings is more likely to increase after offering a deal promotion. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: A relatively low average and a relatively small number of prior reviews together positively moderate the effect of deal promotion on the average of subsequent review ratings.

Even though a less favorable valence and a small volume of prior reviews enhance the mechanisms for the positive effect of deal promotion, whether the moderate role is strong enough to make the positive mechanisms stronger than the opposite is an empirical question. If it is not sufficiently large, the stylized fact that offering deal promotion would deteriorate local merchants’ online reputations is still true. To test whether the moderating role of less favorable valence and small volume of prior reviews is large enough to result in a positive effect of deal promotion, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: For merchants with a relatively low average of prior review ratings and a relatively small number of prior reviews, the average of their subsequent review ratings increases after offering deal promotion.

## Preliminary Study

## Data and Results

We use the public data set provided by Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [5] (BMZ). The BMZ data set contains a nationwide sample of deals distributed from nineteen major cities across the United States.<sup>5</sup> The BMZ data set includes the characteristics (e.g., vendor, discounted voucher price) and accurate voucher sales for each deal. In the BMZ data set, Groupon deals are collected between January 3 and July 3, 2011. We focus on restaurant deals offered via Groupon. For each restaurant deal, we manually check whether the restaurant has a profile on Yelp.com.<sup>6</sup> We exclude restaurants for which we could not confidently find a Yelp profile,<sup>7</sup> and those with no reviews on their Yelp profiles. For the remaining restaurants, we use a computer program to automatically extract all individual Yelp reviews (including numeric ratings, textual contents, and dates).<sup>8</sup> Ultimately, there remain 2,294 Groupon restaurant deals, for each of which we compute the monthly average Yelp review ratings before and after the deal promotion. ## Discussion and Limitations

The preliminary study yields three interesting empirical findings. First, the aggregate average effect of deal promotion for the full sample is negative, consistent with the stylized fact and the findings of Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [6]. Second, we show that low average and small volume of prior reviews together positively moderate the effect of deal promotion. Finally and most important, we find that for restaurants with prior average rating lower than 3.5 and not more than 20 reviews, the monthly average Yelp review ratings in fact increase after deal promotion. The proportion of those restaurants is substantial, accounting for 17.4 percent (we will use a more relaxed set of cutoffs in the main study and show that the proportion of the improved restaurants is even larger). The findings suggest that the stylized fact that offering a deal promotion will deteriorate local merchants’ online reputations is not true in certain circumstances.

The key identification assumption of using the fixed-effect model in the preliminary study is that a restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion is exogenous after controlling time-invariant restaurant heterogeneity (e.g., restaurant location and environment, food category) and some time-varying variables (e.g., the numbers of different star ratings in the previous month). However, it is possible that a restaurant’s unobserved factors may jointly determine the restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion and the monthly average Yelp review ratings. For example, the socioeconomic environment or management intelligence of a restaurant (beyond the restaurant’s time-invariant heterogeneity) may simultaneously influence the restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion and consumers’ online feedback (monthly average Yelp review ratings). Therefore, a restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion could be endogenous and the estimation using a merely fixed-effect models may be biased. For instance, a restaurant may decide to offer a Groupon promotion when its average Yelp review rating has been decreasing, perhaps because of the changing socioeconomic environment. In such a case, we would observe a decrease in the average Yelp ratings after the Groupon promotion even if it has no real effect on restaurants’ Yelp reviews. In the main study, we will focus on addressing the possible endogeneity (self-selection) issue.

## Difference in Differences in Combination with Propensity Score Matching

## Identification

The main study will examine whether the findings of the preliminary study are robust when the endogeneity (self-selection) of a restaurant’s decision to offer a deal promotion is explicitly considered. To address the possible endogeneity issue, the main study uses difference-in-differences (DID) analysis in combination with propensity score matching (PSM). DID in combination with PSM is an econometric technique recommended by the recent literature [21, 23, 26, 41] to address the endogeneity of an agent’s action in identifying the causal impact of that action. For example, Rishika et al. [41] use this technique to identify the effect of customer participation in a firm’s social media efforts on their visit frequency. Goh, Heng, and Lin [21] use the technique to quantify the impact of customers’ becoming a fan in a brand community on their purchase expenditures.

The DID model allows us to identify the causal effect of deal promotion by estimating the difference in the change of monthly average Yelp ratings of restaurants from the periods before to the periods after deal promotion between two groups of restaurants [3]: treatment group versus control group. Restaurants in the treatment group are those for which we aim to estimate the effect of deal promotion, whereas restaurants in the control group are those that we use as the benchmark to compare with the treatment group. A common way of composing the control group is to use restaurants that did not offer a deal promotion [21, 41]. However, one may suspect that restaurants that did not offer a deal promotion are systematically different from those that have done so. To address this concern, we adopt a better research design recommended by Hosanagar et al. [23]. That is, we use the restaurants that have offered a deal promotion later than a certain time point as the control group, whereas we use the restaurants that have done so earlier than the time point as the treatment group. This research design affords a higher degree of similarity between the treatment and control groups, because both have offered a deal promotion.

Since restaurants in the control group have offered a deal promotion later than those in the treatment group, one may still be concerned that the control group is different from the treatment group. As suggested by the recent literature [21, 23, 26, 41], we conduct PSM and assemble a matched sample of “treated” and control restaurants. We ensure that the treated and control restaurants in each matched pair have almost the same probability of receiving the treatment (i.e., offering a deal promotion) so that the treated and control restaurants are reasonably similar and comparable [42]. Although hidden biases may still remain because matching cannot control for unobserved factors, we note that all the time-invariant and some time-varying confounding factors would be controlled by the DID model. In addition, we include some time-varying control variables in the analysis to control for the time-varying confounding factors.

In sum, we use PSM to assemble a matched sample of treated and control restaurants, among which the control restaurants have offered a deal promotion later than the treated restaurants. Subsequently, we use a DID model to measure the effect of deal promotion by estimating the difference in the change of monthly average Yelp ratings of restaurants from the periods before to those after deal promotion between the matched treated and control restaurants. By doing so, we are able to reasonably identify the causal impact of deal promotion [21, 23, 26, 41].

## Data and Propensity Score Matching

Restaurants in the treatment group come from the data set we used for the preliminary study. Specifically, the treatment group includes restaurants located in nineteen major U.S. cities that have offered a Groupon promotion on some day between January and June 2011. For the control group, we find a daily-deal aggregator that provides us with 556 restaurants located in the same nineteen major U.S. cities that have never offered any deal promotion by June 30, 2011, but have offered their first deal promotion through Groupon between July 2011 and July 2012. Therefore, we are able to use the research design recommended by Hosanagar et al. [23] in which both the treated and control restaurants have offered a Groupon promotion, but we ensure that the control restaurants have done so only after the treated restaurants.

PSM works well when a large number of pretreatment covariates that are likely to influence the decision to offer a Groupon promotion are considered [22, 43]. To satisfy this requirement, we find multiple data sources (including the BMZ data set, the deal aggregator’s data set, Yelp.com, and the U.S. Census Bureau) and collect a number of pretreatment covariates that are likely to simultaneously influence the decision to offer a Groupon promotion and the monthly average Yelp ratings. Table 3 presents the details of the key pretreatment covariates.<sup>10</sup> The covariates come from three categories: deal characteristics (e.g., discounted voucher price of the deal VPRICE), restaurant characteristics (e.g., restaurant AGE, number of total prior Yelp reviews NTREV, distance from the center of the city DIST), and the socioeconomic characteristics of the zip code area where the restaurant is located (e.g., total population POP, median household income INC). For example, one may expect that a younger restaurant is more likely to offer a Groupon promotion, or a restaurant located in an area where residents are more price-sensitive (with a lower household income) is more likely to offer a Groupon promotion.

Accordingly, we model a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion as a function of the three categories of covariates. We employ the PSM procedure and run a probit regression for whether offering Groupon promotion can be predicted by the covariates in Table 3 and their squared terms.<sup>11</sup> The results of the probit regression are reported in Table 4. We find eleven covariates that are significantly associated with a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion, while DIST^2 and POV are not significant. Based on the estimated model, we get the predicted propensity scores for the treated and control restaurants.

Before performing any matching, we first sort all the treated and control restaurants in a random order to make sure the ordering does not affect the subsequent matching. Then, we match the treated and control restaurants based on their predicted propensity scores. To reduce bias, we ensure that the treated and control restaurants share the common support of the predicted propensity scores [7]. Recommended by the existing literature [41], we use the nearest neighbor (NN) matching with replacement, wherein for each treated restaurant, the control restaurant that is most similar to the treated restaurant in terms of the predicted propensity score is chosen in the matched pair (the predicted propensity score of the chosen control restaurant is nearest to that of the treated restaurant). NN matching with replacement allows for minimizing the bias between the treated and control restaurants, because bias will occur when multiple (potentially less similar) control restaurants are chosen to match with the treated restaurant. Matching with replacement guarantees that the most similar control restaurant in the sample is chosen [7]. Another advantage of NN matching is that it enables us to deal separately with each matched pair of the treated and control restaurants and allows us to control for any residual variability in estimating the effect of Groupon promotion [26, 41]. NN matching faces the risk of bad matches if the nearest control restaurant is still far away. To avoid this risk, we impose a tolerance level of 0.001 on the maximal propensity score distance (caliper) in the NN matching. Imposing the caliper ensures that the nearest control restaurant chosen has to lie within the caliper of the treated restaurant (the maximal tolerance level of the propensity score distance) [7]. All these procedures that we employ in the PSM allow us to achieve the highest degree of similarity between the treated and the matched control restaurants. Ultimately, we successfully find the corresponding nearest control restaurants for 1,090 treated restaurants by using the PSM.

## Econometric Model

Inspired by the recent literature [26, 41], we use Equation (2) as the main econometric estimation model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} M A V E R _ {i j, t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} T R E A T _ {i j} + \alpha_ {2} P R O M _ {i j, t} + \beta T R E A T _ {i j} P R O M _ {i j, t} + Y Z _ {i j} \\ \qquad + \Theta X _ {i j, t - 1} + \eta_ {i} + v _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In Equation (2), t denotes the index of a thirty-day period and the time periods span from ninety days before January 1, 2011 to December 31, 2011 $( t = - 3 , - 2 , - 1 , 0 , 1$ $\cdots ^ { 1 1 ) }$ ; i indexes a matched pair of treated and control restaurants; j denotes a treated or control restaurant. The dependent variable $M A V E R _ { i j \ast t }$ is the monthly average Yelp review rating of (treated or control) restaurant $j$ in the matched pair i at time t. $T R E A T _ { i j }$ is the treatment dummy variable that equals 1 if the restaurant is in the treatment group and 0 if it is in the control group. $P R O M _ { i j , t }$ is a dummy variable that equals 1 for time periods after the Groupon promotion and 0 for time periods before the Groupon promotion. $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } _ { \mathsf { \Pi } }$ is the fixed-effect dummy variable for each matched pair of restaurants that controls for any time-invariant heterogeneity of the matched pair i. The vector $Z _ { \mathrm { i j } }$ includes the covariates of the deal and restaurant characteristics (e.g., VPRICE, AGE) and the socioeconomic characteristics of the zip code area where the restaurant is located (e.g., POP, INC). Moreover, Equation (2) includes two sets of additional controls $( \nu _ { t }$ and $X _ { i j , t - 1 } )$ to address the possible time-varying confounding factors: $\nu _ { t }$ is the fixed-effect dummy variable for each time period t, controlling for any common time shocks. The vector $X _ { i j > t - 1 }$ includes restaurant-specific time-varying control variables, that is, the numbers of different star ratings in the previous month $( S T A R I _ { i j , t - 1 } , \ S T A R 2 _ { i j , t - 1 } ,$ $S T A R 3 _ { i j , t - 1 }$ 2 $S T A R 4 _ { i j , t - 1 } ,$ , and $S T A R 5 _ { i j , t - 1 } )$ . Using the DID model as specified in Equation (2), the main parameter of interest is $\beta ,$ which estimates the average effect of Groupon promotion on the treated restaurants’ monthly average Yelp review ratings. We note that the econometric estimation model in Equation (2) includes a more comprehensive set of controls, because the econometric models suggested by the prior literature [26, 41] do not consider time-fixed effects $\nu _ { \mathrm { t } }$ or restaurant-specific time-varying controls $X _ { i j , t - 1 }$

## Coarsened Exact Matching

When using PSM, a function of covariates needs to be specified to estimate the propensity scores [7] and thus one may be concerned that our findings are dependent on the function form. To address this concern, we use coarsened exact matching (CEM) [27] as an alternative preprocessing matching method instead of PSM. CEM is a newly developed nonparametric matching method from the political science literature [27] and has a number of advantages over PSM. When using CEM, the covariates of the treated and control units are first coarsened and segmented into different strata. Only the strata that contain at least one treated and one control unit remain in the subsequent analysis. Accordingly, the treated and control units within the same strata are exactly matched based on the coarsened values of the covariates. The subsequent analysis is conducted based on the original (uncoarsened) values of the matched treated and control units. Iacus, King, and Porro [27] present a number of desirable advantages of CEM. For example, because CEM is an exact matching method, the balance between the matched treated and control units are reasonably guaranteed. Furthermore, because CEM is a nonparametric matching, there is no need to specify the function form for matching based on the covariates and thus the model dependence is largely reduced.

## Implications

The findings yield several interesting implications for theory and practice. First, while prior research on the determinants of user-generated online reviews [20, 37] has largely focused on the microlevel endogenous generation process of online reviews, few studies explore whether there are any actionable strategies that managers can use to stimulate the production of online reviews. The study shows that online promotion or discount pricing can significantly affect user-generated online reviews about local businesses. By offering online promotion, managers could design effective strategies to encourage consumers to post their online opinions and generate favorable online reviews [31].

Second, the results show substantial heterogeneity in the effect of deal promotion on merchants’ online reputations. In particular, the valence and volume of prior reviews can significantly moderate the effect of deal promotion. Offering deal promotion is just a new digital marketing tool and it does not necessarily hurt merchants’ online reputations in the long run. While many restaurants’ online reputations decline because of offering Groupon promotions, we find that the relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews can positively moderate the effect of a Groupon promotion. The moderating role of prior reviews in fact is strong enough so that restaurants with a relatively low prior average rating and a relatively small review volume have improved the average of their subsequent review ratings after offering a deal promotion. Therefore, for merchants who have not gained a favorable online reputation in the early stage, this study suggests that they have the option of offering a deal promotion to improve their online reputations, especially if the average of prior reviews is relatively low and the volume is relatively small.

Third, our analysis also shows that offering a deal promotion is likely to decrease some merchants’ online reputations, especially for those whose prior average review ratings are already high. Byers, Mitzenmacher, and Zervas [5, 6] discuss a number of possible reasons why merchants’ online reputations may suffer from offering deal promotion. For example, Groupon users often engage in experimentation and thus may be more likely to purchase products or services that do not match their tastes. Also, reviews from Groupon users may be more likely to be real and accurate, whereas reviews from non-Groupon users may contain a higher proportion of inflated review ratings. However, our findings do not imply that those merchants with a relatively high average rating should stop using deal promotions, because deal promotions have more implications beyond that in terms of online reputations. Dholakia [13] discusses many potential benefits of deal promotion for local merchants, including increased sales, increased brand awareness, increased customer base, and minimal upfront marketing cost. For those merchants whose prior average ratings are already high, they are able to gain potential benefits from deal promotion even if their online reputations may suffer. Managers of local businesses should be aware of not only the potential benefits but the potential risks of deal promotion. Herein, we suggest that the providers of deal promotion (such as Groupon, LivingSocial) take an active role in educating local merchants to be more informative about the potential benefits and risks of deal promotion. Local merchants and providers of deal promotion should work together to develop effective strategies to minimize the potential risks while maximizing the potential benefits. For example, while well-established merchants with high-ranking online reputations may not need to run general deal promotions, they use deal promotions to promote their new products or services. Also, merchants can use deal promotions to attract customers during idle periods when there is usually not much business so that their extra capacity can be used up.

Fourth, while restaurants’ process of deciding to offer a deal promotion is unobservable to researchers, the matching procedure we used in this study generates substantial insights into the decision. We find that a restaurant’s decision to offer a Groupon promotion is likely influenced by restaurant age, volume of online reviews, average review rating, distance from the city center, and population and household income of the surrounding area. For example, restaurants that are relatively new, distant from the city center, and having favorable Yelp reviews are more likely to offer a Groupon promotion. Local businesses in other service industries (such as massage, spa, and hair care) can take these factors into account when they consider offering online promotions.

Finally, as we discussed earlier, there are multiple underlying mechanisms for the positive and negative effects of deal promotion. Those underlying mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and the current study estimates the net effect of deal promotion. Future research can focus on separating the different underlying mechanisms for both the positive and negative effects of deal promotion. Further implications can be derived from separating the different mechanisms.

## Conclusion

This paper presents an empirical study and documents the causal relationship between deal promotion and merchants’ online reputations (average of online review ratings). Despite the stylized fact that offering a deal promotion will deteriorate merchants’ online reputations, the results show that a relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews can positively moderate the effect of deal promotion. In fact, we find that the moderating role of prior reviews is strong enough to result in a positive effect. That is, many restaurants with a relatively low average and small volume of prior reviews have actually improved their online reputations by offering a deal promotion. The findings are robust to multiple identification strategies and econometric specifications. This study underscores the substantial heterogeneity in the effect of deal promotion on local merchants’ online reputations. Merchants need to consider the moderating role of prior reviews (e.g., the valence and volume of prior reviews) and design effective strategies to maximize the benefits and minimize the potential risks of offering a deal promotion.
