# The Role of Online and Geographically Distant Social Networks in Political Decision-Making: Empirical Evidence from the 2016 United States Presidential Election

Ecem Basak,<sup>1</sup> Ali Tafti,<sup>2</sup> Min-Seok Pang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Baruch College, City University of New York, USA, ecem.basak@baruch.cuny.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Illinois Chicago, USA, atafti@uic.edu <sup>3</sup>Temple University, USA, minspang@temple.edu

## Abstract

Social ties play a prominent role in individuals’ political decision-making. They influence partisan defections, political participation, voting decisions, and political information acquisition. Much of the literature focuses on personal social networks or geographically close networks. Yet one’s social network might also include acquaintances or other connections in more distant places that are maintained via online networks. In this study, we exploit Facebook’s Social Connectedness Index, which reflects social connections across the United States, and we investigate the role of social connectedness in politica decision-making among individuals who are located across distant geographical regions. Our results suggest that social connectedness between counties has a homogenizing effect on voting for the same presidential candidate, either Democratic or Republican. On the other hand, social connectedness is likely to have a differentiating effect on voting for an independent or a third-party candidate. Moreover, this effect is moderated by the socioeconomic characteristics of the counties, such as education, race, population density, household income, industry, and gender composition.

Keywords: Social Connectedness, Online Networks, Geographically Distant Social Networks, Political Decision-Making, Electoral Decisions

## 1 Introduction

Social ties play a prominent role in individuals’ political decision-making, influencing partisan defections (Beck, 2002), political participation (McClurg, 2003, 2006; Pattie & Johnston, 2009), voting choice (Berelson et al., 1954; Huckfeldt & Sprague, 1991), and political information acquisition (Berelson et al., 1954; Huckfeldt, 2007; Huckfeldt et al., 2004). A large part of the literature focuses on personal social networks (e.g., family or friends) or geographically close networks. Yet one’s social network might also include acquaintances or other connections in more distant places that are maintained via online networks.

Relatively little is known about the interaction of political decision-making and social connectedness in geographically distant social networks. In particular, we know little about how online and geographically distant networks shape individuals’ political decisionmaking processes—in part because there is no comprehensive data on social connections in geographically distant networks, which is needed to understand the kinds of aggregate decision-making processes inherent in political choices. Until recently, the measurement of social connections across geographic regions (e.g., counties, states, or countries) on a large scale has been very difficult. Bailey et al. (2018b) addressed this issue by aggregating and anonymizing data from Facebook to construct a measure of pairwise Social Connectedness Index (SCI)

between geographic regions, including zip codes, counties, states, and countries. Prior research using this new measure has found a strong correlation between social connectedness and various outcomes, such as bank lending and knowledge flows (Bailey et al., 2018a; Diemer & Regan, 2022; Hung, 2021; Rehbein & Rother, 2020; Wilson, 2022).

In the present study, we exploit this unique dataset that reflects social connections across the United States (US) to investigate the role of social connectedness in political decision-making among individuals who are located across distant geographical regions. Our empirical analysis particularly focuses on voter preference measured by election outcomes, a reliable indicator of a community’s political decisions at the aggregate level. We make use of the SCI to understand the extent to which online and geographically distant social networks have influenced the 2016 US presidential election results.

To determine the influence of online and geographically distant networks on voting decisions, we linked county-to-county SCI with county-to-county vote share differentials. As social networks tend toward homophily, such that social groups of similar individuals are more likely to be connected to each other, using county-to-county vote share differentials helps us measure homogeneity in political decisionmaking between county pairs. We would expect that more socially connected counties would be more likely to have similar election outcomes. Indeed, our findings support the hypothesis that social connectedness homogenizes the election outcomes of county pairs. More specifically, the vote share differentials between county pairs for both Democratic and Republican presidential candidates are lower when counties are more socially connected. On the other hand, our results also show that when two counties are more socially connected, the vote share differentials of the thirdparty presidential candidate are larger.

The present study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it offers new insights into the role of social networks in political decision-making. Previous research has investigated the role of personal social networks on different political behaviors (Beck, 2002; Campos et al., 2017; Klofstad, 2010; Nickerson, 2008). While the question of how social networks impact political behavior has long been of scholarly interest, the present study contributes to the extant literature by examining the role of social connectedness in online and geographically distant networks on individuals’ political decision-making, with a particular focus on voter preferences. In this regard, we provide evidence on how online and geographically distant networks explain a substantial portion of election outcomes. Our study builds on previous literature that has found a positive impact of social ties on voting for major presidential candidates.

However, we make a novel contribution by showing that social connectedness has the opposite effect on voting for an independent or third-party candidate, highlighting the influence of social connectedness on the expression of alternative political choices. Second, we contribute to a growing literature on the role of social connectedness across distant regions in socioeconomic outcomes (Bailey et al., 2018a; 2018b; 2020; Carril-Caccia et al., 2023; Diemer & Regan, 2022; Rehbein & Rother, 2020). Furthermore, we contribute to the literature by investigating contextual effects that can provide a deeper understanding of the dynamics between social connectedness and political decision-making across different regions. This offers insights into the conditions under which the impact of social networks is intensified or attenuated. Finally, our study contributes to a broader literature that studies the impact of online social networks on human behavior (Aral & Walker, 2011; Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Huang et al., 2014; Onnela & Reed-Tsochas, 2010; Shriver et al., 2013; Sridhar & Srinivasan, 2012; Susarla et al., 2012; Zeng & Wei, 2013).

## 2 Research Background

This study is related to two streams of research. The first one relates to the growing literature on how social connectedness across distant regions impacts socioeconomic outcomes. Diemer and Regan (2022) examined how social ties connecting inventors across distant places promote knowledge flows between them. They showed that stronger social ties to other places could foster knowledge exchange. Bailey et al. (2018a) and Cao (2019) analyzed the effects of social interactions via distant networks on housing investment decisions. Rehbein and Rother (2020) studied how banks leverage geographically distant social connections in lending behavior. They found that strong social connections increase loan volumes and that sufficiently strong connections prevent cultural differences from creating lending barriers. Hung (2021) used SCI data to investigate how social interactions affect stock market participation decisions. The results suggest that peer effects increase focal stock market participation decisions. Carril‑Caccia et al. (2023) explored whether and to what extent social interactions explain the number and value of mergers and acquisitions within countries versus between countries. They found a positive association between social ties and the number and value of mergers and acquisitions. Using SCI data, Gavriilidis (2021) showed that social networks play a significant role in the propensity of people to travel. Wilson (2022) investigated the role of social networks on Earned Income Tax Credit claiming behaviors. Rind et al. (2022) used SCI data to examine how peers affect the employee welfare policies of organizations.

Another stream of literature is related to social influence over the political decision-making process. Political behavior in American politics is deeply rooted in social interactions (Sokhey & McClurg, 2012). The literature on political decision-making suggests that individuals embedded in social networks may be influenced by behaviors and opinion spillovers from others (Beck, 2002; Campos et al., 2017; Klofstad, 2010; Nickerson, 2008). People in one’s personal networks can act like agents who are capable of influencing changes in one’s political behaviors. These networks serve as a channel for information spillovers and can impact political outcomes. Nickerson (2008) conducted placebo-controlled experiments to explore the relationship between turnout within households and the attributes of other household members. Bond et al. (2012) identified the effects of Facebook messages on voter turnout in the 2010 US congressional elections and found that online messages influenced political mobilization. Jones et al. (2017) replicated the experiment in Bond et al. (2012) during the 2012 US presidential election and obtained similar results, suggesting that online social networks have significant effects on voter turnout behaviors. Klofstad (2010) showed that civics discussions among college dormitory roommates increased participation in civics activities. Campos et al. (2017) studied whether peer political identification influences individual political identification using a natural experiment among young adults attending a public university.

## 3 Proposed Contributions: Intersection of Online Social Networks and Voting Behavior

Our study lies at the intersection of online social networks and voters’ behaviors, with a specific interest in social relations across distant regions in relation to voter preferences. In general, social networks enforce party loyalty and amplify the perceptions of party preference. Beck (2002) found that personal social networks influenced the 1992 US presidential election. In particular, when personal discussants favored one candidate over another in the two-party system, voters tended to respond to this favoritism.

The premise of the current study is that one’s social network should, in theory, not only include closely connected personal contacts but also distant social connections that are maintained via online networks. In practice, however, we do not know whether and to what degree voter preferences are susceptible to online and geographically distant social networks. Our research goes beyond studies on political decision-making (Beck, 2002; Berelson et al., 1954; Huckfeldt, 2007; Huckfeldt et al., 2004; Huckfeldt & Sprague, 1991; McClurg, 2003, 2006; Pattie & Johnston, 2009), through analysis of comprehensive social-network data that covers geographically distant networks on an aggregate scale. Therefore, we ask the following question: Do online connections between geographically distant communities at an aggregate level operate like personal networks at an individual level, influencing loyalty towards political parties or their candidates?

Table 1 categorizes the types of constructs used in the related literature. Besides its novelty and positioning with respect to the existing literature, our research question is important for several additional reasons. First, we can gain insights into the role of social media in shaping political attitudes and behaviors by understanding whether online connections between geographically distant communities operate like personal networks. These results can be valuable for political campaigns and policymakers to better understand voter preferences as a community in the digital age.

Second, focusing on the impact of the online connections between geographically distant communities on election results at the aggregate level can provide insights that might be different from and complementary to those gained from studies at the individual level. It is plausible that social connections across geographically distant communities sharing similar political preferences might have an impact on election outcomes in a way that is not captured by studies at the individual level. This is important because election results are decided at the aggregate level. Therefore, understanding how online connections between geographically distant communities impact election results at this level can help in explaining patterns in voting behaviors that cannot be fully explained by individual-level factors. Overall, our study contributes to a broader understanding of the impact of social connections on societal outcomes as a community.

In this study, we posit that social networks not only influence voters’ preference toward the two major party candidates but also can influence third-party voting. In a party system dominated by two parties (e.g., Democrats and Republicans in US federal elections), third-party voting is defined as votes that are cast for parties other than the two dominant ones. In general, party loyalty inhibits partisan defection; however, when the two mainstream parties fail to run appealing candidates or address voters’ concerns, individuals votes could shift toward third-party candidates (Beck 2002). While the third parties rarely win presidential elections in the Electoral College, they often draw votes away from major candidates with similar political views and make these candidates more likely to lose (Nefussy, 2017). Even though third-party voting has a negative impact in the Electoral College on the major party that is more aligned with the candidate, voters are still likely to defect from their party loyalty due to either animosity toward or dissatisfaction with the two dominant parties.

In US politics, a third party refers to a political party that is neither Democratic nor Republican. In this case, a vote for a more liberal third party benefits Republican candidates whereas a vote for a conservative third party benefits Democratic candidates. In either case, a vote is taken away from the dominant party most well-aligned with the third party. <sup>1</sup> For instance, in the 2016 US presidential election, Libertarian and Green candidates did better than expected (i.e., over 3% and 1% of the popular vote, respectively), a result that many consider to be a deciding factor in the Democratic candidate’s (Hilary Clinton’s) surprising loss. Likewise, if Ralph Nader had not run in the 2000 US presidential election, the majority of Nader’s voters would have voted for Al Gore (Nefussy, 2017), likely leading to his victory in the election. It is possible that animosity or dissatisfaction with the two dominant political parties is exacerbated by social networks, given their role in influencing political decision-making. Hence, we consider the role of social networks in partisan defection decisions and switchovers to third-party candidates. Beck (2002) found that voters’ personal discussion network influences party defections and third-party voting, specifically for Ross Perot in the 1992 US presidential election. Hence, it is also important to consider the impact of online and geographically distant networks on third-party election outcomes, for which we do not have prior empirical evidence. Using third-party election outcomes also provides an empirical advantage. In political environments, increased homophily is likely to be one of the reasons for echo chambers and polarization (Boutyline & Willer, 2017; Cinelli et al., 2021). As a result, individuals in highly polarized political environments are likely to display more partisan behaviors and act similarly due to homophily. Including third-party election outcomes helps reduce the influence of cultural or historical ties to dominant political parties. Because third-party voters are less likely to be swayed by partisan factors, their choices are often driven by different considerations, such as policy preferences or individual candidate qualities. We mitigate the influence of unobservable historical or culturally rooted partisanship by including third-party vote shares in our analysis.

## 4 Data and Variables

We collected data from multiple sources, including Facebook, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), and the US Census Bureau, to examine the impact of the aggregate social connectedness between a county-pair on aggregate county-to-county election outcomes. The unit of analysis in our study, therefore, is an edge-level measure that corresponds to county-tocounty relations.

Our independent variable is Facebook’s Social Connectedness Index (SCI), which was used in Bailey et al. (2018b).<sup>2</sup> Bailey et al. (2018b) first mapped Facebook users to their county locations and obtained the total number of friendship links between their locations. They assigned the locations to Facebook users based on the information provided on Facebook. Then, they constructed the SCI between all pairs of 3,136 counties using aggregated information from the universe friendship links among all Facebook users who have interacted with Facebook over the 30 days prior to the April 2016 snapshot. Even though it is a one-time snapshot, the SCI reflects patterns of immigration, culture, and socioeconomic characteristics apart from geographic closeness. Specifically, the SCI measures the relative frequency of Facebook friendship links between every county pair. It is constructed to have a maximum value of 1,000,000, which is the score assigned to the aggregate connections within Los Angeles County in California, where Facebook users had the most friends with other people in their county (Bailey et al., 2018b). However, in our study, we disregarded within-county connections, as we were only interested in the role of social connectedness in geographically distant social networks. The SCI values linking different counties ranged from 0 to 86,508, with the highest in this range belonging to the Los Angeles County-Orange County pair in California. There are a total of 4,915,680 aggregated county-pair connections; of those, 186,016 (just under 4%) county-pair connections had an SCI value of zero, meaning that there was no social connectedness between those pairs.

There is a close correspondence between the Facebook network and real-world social networks in the US. In the US, Facebook essentially serves as a platform for realworld friends, acquaintances, or geographically distant connections to interact online (Hampton et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2013). Forming a friendship link on Facebook requires both individuals’ consent. Hence, the friendship link is bidirectional. There is an upper limit on the number of friends that can be added. This makes Facebook different from other online social networks such as Twitter, where the relationships are unidirectional, and LinkedIn, which is a professional network rather than a personal network (Bailey et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2020).

Moreover, Facebook provides high coverage and representativeness of the US population. Greenwood et al. (2016) showed that US Facebook users are relatively representative of the US population. They reported that as of April 2016, more than 79% of the US population and 68% of US adults used Facebook. They also indicate that Facebook usage rates are relatively consistent across racial, education, income, and urban, suburban, and rural groups, and its usage rates decline with user age in a manner similar to other social network platforms such as Instagram and LinkedIn. Hence, we believe that the SCI by Bailey et al. (2018b) provides a reasonable proxy for social connectedness across counties, as it is based on hundreds of billions of Facebook friendship links from Facebook users (Bailey et al., 2020).

One might argue that at least in the US, geographic closeness is a proxy for social connectedness. Bailey et al. (2018b) found that a 10% increase in geographic distance separating a pair of counties (measured via county centroids) correlates with a 14.8% decline in the number of friendship links between those counties. However, geographic proximity is not the only determinant of social connectedness. Social connections across counties have historical roots (i.e., linking extended families across specific distant pairs of counties over generations), and other cross-county connections are strengthened by historical patterns of migration. Political boundaries such as state lines, exposure to large movements, and other contemporaneous factors also shape social connectedness. For example, the SCI reflects long-lasting historical connections between the residents of Chicago and the counties along the southern Mississippi River; such connections were forged by the migration of African Americans a century ago (Bailey et al., 2018a, 2018b). Kern County, California, has significant ties to certain counties in Oklahoma that were forged by the migration caused by the Dust Bowl in the 1930s (Badger & Quoctrung, 2018). Therefore, as one of the most popular and largest social networking sites, Facebook is a fitting context for our research purpose.

Our dependent variable represents the vote-share differential, specifically the absolute value of the countypair vote-share differential of the same party presidential candidate (Diff). This serves as a proxy for the difference between any two counties in voter preference for the same party. We consider the vote share differentials for three groups: The Democratic Party, the Republican Party, and the independent or third-party candidate.<sup>3</sup>

We also included several control variables in the empirical model. Our first control variable is the geographical distance between a county pair. Data on county-to-county distances (Dist) comes from the NBER. These distances are based on the distance between county centroids, as defined by the US Census Bureau. Next, we included the socioeconomic characteristics of counties. A demographic breakdown of party supporters includes several factors such as location, income, age, education, and race. For instance, Democrats dominate high-density urban areas, suburbs tend to lean Republican, and rural areas are predominantly Republican. Therefore, we included the differentials of the following county-level characteristics in terms of the absolute value: the percentage of the White population (Diff\_Whi), the percentage of manufacturing establishments (Diff\_Manuf), the percentage of the population without a high school diploma (Diff\_Highschool), the percentage of the male population (Diff\_Male), median household income (Diff\_Inc), and population density (Diff\_Dens). The data on county-level characteristics came from Bailey et al. (2018b). Lastly, we included two dummy variables that indicated whether a county-pair shared a common border (Neighbor) and whether they were in the same state (State). Overall, we obtained information on 4,729,670 county pairs. The descriptive statistics and correlations are given in Tables 2 and 3, respectively.

## 5 Empirical Specifications

To examine the relationship between social connectedness and political decision-making at the county-pair level, we propose the following econometric model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} D i f f _ {i j} = & \beta_ {1} l n (S C I _ {i j}) + \beta_ {2} l n (D i s t _ {i j}) \\ & + \beta_ {3} D i f f \_ W h i _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {4} D i f f \_ M a n u f _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {5} D i f f \_ H i g h S c h o o l _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {6} D i f f \_ M a l e _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {7} l n (D i f f \_ I n c _ {i j}) \\ & + \beta_ {8} l n (D i f f \_ D e n s _ {i j}) \\ & + \beta_ {9} N e i g h b o r _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {1 0} S t a t e _ {i j} + \epsilon_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In our regression model, we consider the effect of social connectedness between counties on their similarity in political decision-making, while controlling for specific county-pair characteristics. We used three alternative dependent variables represented by $D i f f _ { i j } .$ The absolute value of the differential in election results between counties i and j for (1) Democratic $( D i f f \_ D e m _ { i j } ) .$ , (2) Republican $( D i f f \_ G o p _ { i j } )$ and (3) independent or third-party $( D i f f \_ O t h _ { i j } )$ presidential candidates. The main independent variable of interest for each pair of counties is $l n ( S C I _ { i j } ) .$ representing the natural log of the SCI between counties i and j. This independent variable represents the strength of social ties between each pair of counties in the US.

The county-pair characteristics are represented by several variables. $l n ( D i s t _ { i j } )$ is the natural logarithm of the geographical distance in miles between counties i and j; $D i f f _ { - } W h i _ { i j }$ is the difference in the percentage of the White population in absolute terms between counties i and j; Diff\_Manuf<sub>ij</sub> is the difference in the percentage of manufacturing establishments in absolute terms between counties i and j; $D i f f \_ H i g h S c h o o l _ { i j }$ is the difference of the percentage of the population without a high school diploma in absolute terms between counties i and $j ; D i f f \_ M a l e _ { i j }$ is the difference in the percentage of the male population in absolute terms between counties i and j; $l n ( D i f f \_ I n c _ { i j } )$ is the natural logarithm of the difference in median household income in absolute terms between counties i and j; and $l n ( D i f f \_ D e n s _ { i j } )$ is the natural logarithm of the difference in the population density in absolute terms between counties i and j. We also included two dummy variables: Neighbor<sub>ij</sub> is a binary variable that is equal to 1 if both counties share a common border, and $S t a t e _ { i j }$ is a binary variable that is equal to 1 if the counties are part of the same state.

## 6 Results

## 6.1 Baseline Results

The baseline results are given in Table 4. The estimated coefficients of ln(SCI) show that social connectedness between counties has a homogenizing effect on voting for the same presidential candidate, either a Democratic or Republican presidential candidate (see Columns 1 and 2). These results suggest that voters in more socially connected counties are less likely to defect from their party loyalty to either of the other mainstream parties.

On the other hand, we found that when two counties are more socially connected, the vote share differentials of the third-party presidential candidate are likely to be higher (see Column 3), suggesting that social connectedness has a differentiating effect on voting for a third-party candidate. One possible explanation for this counterintuitive finding could be that voters may have more diverse opinions and preferences, which can lead to a wider range of voting outcomes across socially connected counties. When two counties are connected more extensively, it is more likely that two people with divergent political inclinations will be connected with each other (i.e., a liberal person in County A connected to a conservative person in County B). They might want to self-disclose and enhance their identity as independent thinkers, or they might prefer to deviate from the choices of their strong social connections to show their independent thinking. Moreover, if two socially connected counties have a strong historical voting pattern for a particular party, voters may feel inclined to deviate from the majority if they have a different ideology. This can lead them to support third-party candidates to express themselves better (Neville-Shepard, 2019; Rockler, 2013). According to self-presentation theory (Goffman, 1954), third-party voters might aim to selfdisclose accurate information to convey both idealized and realistic aspects of themselves. They might choose not to vote for the dominant party specifically because their social connections vote for the dominant party (i.e., they intentionally flout what is popular among their connections). Exploring this aspect of self-presentation could shed light on the differentiating effects of social connectedness.

Our findings should be interpreted with some caution, especially because different third-party candidates are in the mix. The presence of multiple third-party candidates with different ideologies and platforms may affect individuals’ voting choices in socially connected counties differently from those in less socially connected counties. Although these results show a positive association between the SCI and the differential in third-party voting—suggesting that online and geographically distant connections influence the vote—additional factors could explain this positive association. Such factors could include local issues and concerns, dissatisfaction with the major parties, or exposure to diverse perspectives. Further research could be conducted to better understand the underlying mechanisms driving the relationship between social connections and third-party outcomes.

## 6.2 Instrumental Variable Approach

Our baseline results are not without endogeneity concerns. It is plausible that social connections across counties are associated with unobservable factors that impact election outcomes. To address this concern, we employed an instrumental variable approach. We used two instruments that have been used in the study by Rehbein and Rother (2020): (1) Highway network and (2) Highway age. Rehbein and Rother (2020) used these instruments to examine the impact of online connections between distant communities on bank lending. Their study provides evidence that the highway network and its age influence social connections across geographically distant locations. We obtained this data from Baum-Snow (2007). To the best of our knowledge, there are no other studies that have used highway network data as an instrument, although Duranton and Turner (2012) used the 1947 plan of the interstate highway system to examine the relationship between transportation infrastructure and urban growth.

Following Rehbein and Rother’s (2020) strategy, our first instrumental variable is an indicator variable that equals 1 if two counties are connected by the same highway. The construction of the highways was motivated by the logistics in the war efforts during World War II and they were built after the war. It is very plausible that social connections might have developed along these highways and that these social ties still exist today (Rehbein & Rother, 2020). Moreover, it would be implausible that the highway network directly influences voting patterns through mechanisms that are independent of the social connections across regions; hence, the highway network represents a valid instrumental variable for the purpose of our study.

We observed a weaker correlation (0.14) between ln(SCI) and the same highway dummy. Column 1 of Table 5 reports the results of 2SLS, showing the coefficient of social connectedness to be negative and statistically significant. Hence, this result supports our findings on social connectedness. As seen in Column 1, we also report the diagnostic results of our instrumental variable approach. We first looked at the diagnostic test of weak instruments. For instruments to be valid, they must have a low correlation with the endogenous explanatory variable (Stock & Yogo, 2002). The Stock and Yogo (2002) test is statistically significant (with a large magnitude and small p-value), rejecting the null hypothesis that the instrument is weak; thus, we can proceed with the knowledge that this is a strong instrument. Next, we looked at the results of the Wu-Hausman diagnostic test for endogeneity. This test determines whether endogenous variables in the model are truly endogenous (Greene, 2003). If they are not, OLS would produce efficient and consistent results, and there would be no need for 2SLS. On the other hand, if they are truly endogenous, we would need 2SLS to get unbiased and consistent coefficients. The results of the Wu-Hausman diagnostic test show a large test statistic and a small p-value,<sup>4</sup> providing evidence for the necessity of the instrumental variable approach.

Our second instrumental variable measures the number of years that have passed since the construction of a highway connecting two counties. Social ties across counties did not emerge overnight but slowly over time (Rehbein & Rother, 2020). Therefore, we believe that the number of years that counties are connected via the same highway can be used as an instrument for social connectedness. Baum-Snow (2007) provides highway construction dates. We observed a weaker correlation between ln(SCI) and the number of years that counties were connected by a highway (0.28). Column 2 of Table 5 reports the results of 2SLS. Consistent with the results using our first instrument, we found social connectedness to have a negative and statistically significant effect on the vote share differentials of the Democratic presidential candidate. Our diagnostic test results provide similar outcomes.

We repeated the analysis for the vote share differentials of the Republican presidential candidate and observed similar results, as shown in Table 6. Econometrically, we found social connectedness to have a negative and statistically significant effect on the vote share differentials of the Republican presidential candidate. As social connectedness increases, vote share differentials are likely to decrease. The results are consistent with our main results, suggesting that more socially connected counties are less likely to defect from the two-party mainstream.

Lastly, we repeated the analysis for the vote share differentials of the third-party presidential candidate. The results are given in Table 7. Our first instrument yielded an insignificant result, whereas the second instrument demonstrated a significant impact. In this case, highway age might be a better-suited instrument than the same highway for addressing endogeneity. We can offer some plausible reasons for this. The same highway as a binary instrument might not have enough variation to adequately address endogeneity in the context of third-party election outcomes. It might not be able to capture the nuanced variations in social connectedness that affect third-party outcomes. This might be better captured by the highway age as a continuous measure of time. Therefore, the continuous “highway age” variable might be a stronger instrument than the binary “same highway” variable in the context of third-party outcomes, leading to statistically significant results.

## 6.3 Heterogeneous Effects

Individuals’ voting preferences may not only be impacted by their social connections or personal attributes but also by the socioeconomic context in which they are embedded. Individuals might adapt their voting preferences to align with the norms and expectations of their social environment. This also aligns with self-presentation theory (Goffman, 1954), as socioeconomic characteristics can shape individuals’ perceptions and behaviors. Therefore, we next explore heterogeneity in the impact of social connectedness on voter preferences for both Democratic and Republican presidential candidates.

In examining the heterogeneous impacts of socioeconomic characteristics (see Tables 8-9 for results), we first observed that geography negatively moderated the relationship between social connectedness and the vote share differentials for both Democratic and Republican presidential candidates (see Columns 1 and 8 in Tables 8 and 9, coefficient of ln(SCI) × ln(Dist)). This result shows that as the distance increases between a country pair, the impact of social connectedness on vote share differentials for each candidate decreases. It suggests that the role of social connectedness is stronger when two counties are geographically close. Then, we observed that as racial disparity increases (i.e., the difference in White population percentage increases), the effect of social connectedness on vote share differentials for each candidate increases (see Columns 2 and 8 in Tables 8 and 9, coefficient of ln(SCI) × Diff\_Whi). This finding suggests that when racial disparity is higher across counties, the homogenizing impact of social connectedness on the vote share differentials for each candidate is stronger. We observed similar impacts with other socioeconomic characteristics, including industry (Diff\_Manuf), gender (Diff\_Male), and population density (ln(Diff\_Dens)); see the interaction terms in Columns 3,5,6, and 8 in Tables 8 and 9. When we look at the moderating impact of income disparity, our findings indicate that as income disparity increases, the impact of social connectedness on vote share differentials for each candidate decreases (see Columns 7-8 in Tables 8 and 9, coefficient of (SCI) × ln(Diff\_Inc)).

## 7 Discussion and Conclusion

This study examines the role of online and geographically distant social networks in political decision-making. In particular, we examine whether and to what degree online social network connections between geographically distant counties are influential in voters’ election decisions. In order to answer our research question, we leveraged comprehensive aggregate data on county-tocounty Facebook relationships, which provides measures of the social distance between US counties (Bailey et al, 2018b). While most of the prior literature has focused on personal social networks or geographically close networks, our study considers connections that are maintained online across socially distant regions as a new channel for political decision-making.

First, we found that social connectedness between counties has a homogenizing effect on voting for the same presidential candidate, whether a Democratic or Republican presidential candidate. Specifically, an increase in social connectedness across US counties was associated with a decrease in the vote share margin of both the Democratic and Republican presidential candidates in the 2016 elections between county pairs. This finding suggests that online and geographically distant social ties have an impact on voters’ decisionmaking and that they can influence them to vote for one of the two mainstream parties. On the other hand, social connectedness has a differentiating effect on voting for third-party candidates. When two counties are more socially connected, their vote share differentials for third-party presidential candidates are higher. This counterintuitive finding can be explained by voters having more diverse opinions and preferences, dissatisfaction with the major parties, or local issues and concerns. We also examine the moderating effects of socioeconomic characteristics on the relationship between social connectedness and vote share differentials.

This study is subject to a few limitations. First, even though the strength of a relationship between every county pair corresponds to the relative frequency of Facebook friendship links, each link is treated identically. Although the strength of the individual ties was not incorporated in the construction of the SCI, our aggregation of the number of social ties between counties does provide an accurately differentiated representation of county-pair ties. The second issue relates to the possible endogeneity of the SCI measure. Social network ties might not be purely exogenous. They could be influenced by unobservable factors that might also affect voting preferences. However, the historical rootedness of social connections across geographical distances—on an aggregate scale— mitigates the possibility that the social network graph is endogenous (at the aggregate level of our study) since any individual is unlikely to alter the extended network of social connections at the county-level scale of the population. We have also attempted to further mitigate this concern by using an instrumental variable approach. Nevertheless, we would still advise caution on the causal implications of these findings. We also acknowledge possible limitations of our choice of instrumental variables because they could be related to election outcomes through paths other than social connectedness. For instance, it is possible that building highways is related to other government policies or factors that could affect voting outcomes, such as economic development or population growth.

This paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, our study highlights the importance of online and geographically distant social networks in political decision-making, focusing on voter preferences. In this regard, we provide evidence on how online and geographically distant networks explain a substantial portion of election outcomes. Our study builds on the previous literature that has found a homogenizing influence of social ties on voting for either Democratic or Republican presidential candidates. However, we make a novel contribution by showing that social connectedness has a differentiating effect on voting for an independent or third-party candidate, which suggests that when two counties are more socially connected, the vote share differentials of the third-party presidential candidate are likely to be higher. Second, we contribute to a growing literature on the role of social connectedness across distant regions on socioeconomic outcomes (Bailey et al., 2018b; 2020; Carril-Caccia et al., 2023; Diemer & Regan, 2022; Rehbein & Rother, 2020). Lastly, we contribute to the literature by investigating the moderating effects that can provide a deeper understanding of the relationship between social connectedness and political decision-making across different regions.

The study highlights the importance of social connectedness in shaping political decision-making across distant geographical regions. Our findings on the homogenizing effect of social connectedness on voting for the same presidential candidate in the context of a two-party system can have important political implications. Policymakers and political strategists can leverage this understanding to design more targeted campaigns and policies that account for the influence of social networks on election outcomes. First, political campaigns can focus on creating connections between counties in order to reduce vote share differentials for their preferred candidate. They might consider online platforms to bring voters from different counties together.

Second, political campaigns could consider crafting messages that resonate with voters’ shared values or interests in socially connected counties. By doing so, the campaigns may be able to increase support for their candidates in those counties and reduce vote share differentials. For example, if two socially connected counties have a large percentage of rural voters, a campaign might focus on issues related to farming or agriculture. Alternatively, if two socially connected counties have a large percentage of young voters, a campaign might focus on education or student debt issues.

Lastly, recognizing the impact of social connectedness on election outcomes, voter engagement and mobilization efforts can be tailored to leverage social networks. Encouraging individuals in more socially connected counties to actively engage their networks in political discussions and activities can amplify these networks’ influence on their members’ voting decisions. Political campaigns may also want to focus their voter outreach efforts on counties with low levels of social connectedness. By doing so, campaigns may be able to reach voters who are more likely to have divergent political beliefs and values and potentially sway them toward supporting their candidate.

Our study also has a number of theoretical implications. First, our findings provide empirical evidence supporting the hypothesis that social connectedness tends to homogenize election outcomes between county pairs. This contributes to the existing literature on the role of social networks in shaping political behaviors. Second, our study reveals a counterintuitive relationship between social connectedness and third-party presidential candidates. When two counties are more socially connected, the vote share differentials of thirdparty candidates are likely to be higher. This finding offers a nuanced understanding of how social networks can impact political behavior. Moreover, our study focuses on communities across distant geographical regions, highlighting the role of social connectedness in bridging gaps and influencing political decision-making beyond one’s immediate network. Examining the dynamics of social connectedness across spatial boundaries enhances our understanding of the influence of online social networks on political behaviors.
