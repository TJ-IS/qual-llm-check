## A B S T R A C T

This paper reports on a case study of adoption of a contained nomadic information environment, exemplified by the use of Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) to keep track of hospital patients. The technology, organization, and environment (TOE) framework is applied to gain insights concerning contextual influences on the adoption of patient tracking RFID, including some RFID-specific issues. The results of this study provide insights to health care organizations embarking on RFID. The resultant model is a step towards developing a theory of adoption of contained nomadic information environments, of which RFID is one instance.

## 1. Introduction

In this paper, we investigate a particular type of computing which we refer to as a contained nomadic information environment. In general, a nomadic information environment ‘‘is a heterogeneous assemblage of interconnected technological, and social, and organizational elements that enable the physical and social mobility of computing and communication services between organizational actors both within and across organizational borders’’ [28]. We add the adjective ‘‘contained’’ to describe an environment that has physical boundaries and to highlight that inside these boundaries a manager or owner can hold some elements to more specialized purposes or to higher standards than are normally found in the world at large. Examples of places where contained nomadic information environments could be implemented include hospitals, museums, and residential homes. In such environments users, as well as other people and things, are mobile.

The focus of our study is the adoption of Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) patient tracking in hospitals. Hospitals are special environments in which both patients and providers may frequently change locations but remain within the confines of a building or campus, including areas where the RFID tracking technology can be installed. This makes patient tracking RFID in hospitals an important element in hospitals’ role as contained nomadic information environments. Keeping track of the location of patients is a critical hospital function and a survey conducted on more than 300 health care organizations indicated that the key potential benefit of RFID is its contribution to patient safety [11]. Indeed, health care is a significant growing sector for RFID applications with a global market of US \$1.7 billion by 2018 [17]. On the other hand, the healthcare industry includes special barriers to technology adoption. Some of these barriers include concern by administrators about the costs of new technology, fear of physicians that the technology will be disruptive and can add risks to patient safety, concern for security and privacy of patient data, general lack of knowledge about IT applications by health care professionals, concerns by employees about being monitored and the possible impact on their job performance and even potential lawsuits, and the need for buy-in from various stakeholders including patients, physicians, nurses, and administrators who may not all have identical agendas [23]. An additional barrier to RFID adoption is the somewhat hostile condition in which the technology must operate, such as the need for devices to be frequently cleaned and disinfected. Widespread adoption and implementation of RFID technology has not yet occurred in health care [26] and current use of RFID in the healthcare supply chain is not only limited due to the high cost but also the unknowns associated with full implementation of a system based on this technology [25]. Nonetheless, research based on multiple case studies has found that RFID technology has the potential to transform the healthcare sector and help healthcare organizations to achieve business values [1].

One important emerging research question in contained nomadic information environments is to study how nomadic services are adopted and used by organizations and how the contained nomadic information environments influence organizational operations, structure, performance, and effectiveness. Furthermore, little academic research in the health care-specific application of RFID exists, let alone research that systematically investigates the factors influencing health care organizations’ adoption of RFID applications. The health care industry is an appropriate choice for study because billions of dollars are currently being spent on IT adoption of relatively new applications and because of the relatively unexplored status of factors giving challenges to IT adoption inherent in the health care industry.

The research reported herein fills these voids by studying adoption and implications of an RFID patient tracking application in a hospital environment with the aim of contributing toward a theory in this emerging research area. The central research question is: ‘‘How do health care organizations adopt and implement RFID applications?’’ To answer this question, we need to understand the important constructs identified by nomadic RFID stakeholders related to RFID adoption as part of a contained nomadic information environment. To guide our case analysis, we begin with the technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework, and consider the influence of its elements on the adoption process. The outcome of this study is a model that identifies the main constructs of adopting nomadic RFID applications in hospitals. The results of this study not only contribute to our understanding of the emerging phenomenon of RFID applications in the health care field, but also provide guidelines and suggestions to practitioners who are considering the adoption of contained nomadic information environments.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. IT adoption in the health care industry

The health care industry is facing increased pressure to function more efficiently and one solution to meet the new demands has been the introduction of information technology [34]. Using both primary and secondary data, Menachemi et al. [29] examined the relationship between information technology (IT) utilization and hospital financial performance in Florida hospitals. Their findings suggest a significant and positive relationship between increased levels of IT use and various measures of financial performance. Nevertheless, the late and sparse adoption of new technology in the health care sector is both well known and documented [19,36,41,55]. The majority of academic research in IT adoption in health care has focused on the individual level. For instance, Chau and Hu [8] examined physicians’ acceptance of telemedicine technology. They suggested that in a typical professional setting, the essential characteristics of user, technology, and context may differ considerably from those in ordinary business settings. Bandyopadhyay and Schkade [3] asserted that there was no known published empirical research addressing the understanding of IT adoption within the health care industry. More recently, Khoumbati et al. [22] proposed a conceptual framework for enterprise application integration (EAI) adoption in health care. Using a fuzzy cognitive mapping approach, they tried to evaluate the factors that influence EAI adoption in the health care sector. Because the data were collected from a public health care organization in the United Kingdom, according to the authors (p. 105) it is difficult to generalize the results to other parts of the world because the organizational structures for the delivery of health care services vary from country to country. In summary, little research in the health care-specific IT adoption at the firm level exists, let alone research that systematically investigates the factors influencing health care organizations’ adoption of RFID applications [26].

## 2.2. RFID applications

Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) refers to the use of radio frequency wireless communications for labeling and identifying objects [52]. Although RFID technology has existed for 50 years, recent advancements in RFID technology, significant declines in RFID costs, and improvements in Electronic Product Code (EPC) standards have made RFID practical for wide adoption [7]. RFID has become so popular nowadays that we are ‘‘witnessing the forward progress of an unstoppable technology adoption’’ [35]. RFID can be an important component of a contained nomadic information environment, in which an RFID tag is attached to a roaming object or. RFID has potential to add value in situations where an infrastructure to support RFID can be established and the objects or people to which RFID tags are attached are not permanently sitebound.

RFID can provide a very high level of information accuracy and real-time visibility across the supply chain; RFID increases the reliability by eliminating the error caused by paper-based systems [30]. RFID, once established, can institute significant cost savings. Still, RFID adoption is not without its challenges. Sometimes the data generation from RFID is not perfect and also results in what is called ‘‘dirty data’’ and noise [21]. These problems can be addressed by the application of middleware to process the data to filter out redundant and unneeded data elements. RFID tag reliability can also be problematic. RFID tags are not immune to attacks from hackers or competitors, which result in loss and misuse of important and confidential data [53], but the security of a hospital or other important place can be improved by controlling the facility access using an RFID system. There is the concern that RFID may increase infringement on consumers’ privacy due to its perpetual tracking ability [42]. Finally, it is possible for any third party equipped with an RFID scanner to determine what medicines patients are taking by scanning their tagged pill bottles [54].

Although companies benefit from RFID applications by cost savings gained through information accuracy, the initial investment of setting up the RFID system is very high. Therefore, the support of the organization’s financial committee is essential in RFID adoption. According to Premkumar and Ramamurhy [33], top management support has been consistently found to be important in IS adoption cases and RFID literature also supports their notion. Organizational variables unique to the health care industry include specialization, functional differentiation, physician and administrator relationships, and telemedicine [22].

According to Fadlalla and Wickramasinghe [15], the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) deals with ensuring the privacy of health care information. The purpose of HIPAA is to maintain strong protections for the privacy of individually identifiable health information, to address the unintended negative effects of the privacy requirements on health care quality or access to health care, and to relieve unintended administrative burdens created by the privacy requirements. HIPAA compliance is another critical environmental factor that tends to affect RFID adoption.

## 2.3. TOE framework

Adoption of innovations is a research topic of enduring interest to IS researchers. Various models have been developed to identify adoption drivers and predict adoption of innovations. Tornatzky and Fleischer [50] developed a generic technology diffusion framework (TOE) which includes three broad categories: technological context, organizational context, and environmental context (Fig. 1). Other research soon found support for the importance of these contexts [20,33].

The technology context examines both the existing technologies in use and new technologies relevant to the organization. The organizational context encompasses descriptive measures about the organization. The environmental context refers to the organization’s industry, competitors, and regulatory factors. The TOE framework has guided various studies on technology adoption, most noticeably on the adoption of electronic data interchange (EDI) and electronic business (e-business). Table 1 summarizes previous adoption research based on the TOE framework.

## 2.3.1. Technological context

Expected benefits, process compatibility, standards uncertainty and technology readiness were influential technology factors in the adoption of interorganizational process standards [51]. Technology readiness was important in e-business [60]. Based on the innovation diffusion theory, Thong [49] found relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility as major factors influencing information systems adoption in small business. Relative advantage of an innovation occurs when it is superior on one or more dimensions to the product it replaces [38,47]. The complexity dimension was supported by Chau and Tam [10] and Cooper and Zmud [12]; complex innovations are difficult for consumers to understand and use. The compatibility dimension was also supported Cooper and Zmud [12]. Compatible innovations fit with the attitudes, beliefs and behaviors of consumers.

## 2.3.2. Organizational context

Organizational factors, which refer to the structure and process of an organization, can constrain or facilitate the adoption or implementation of innovations. Organizational innovativeness, for example, affected the adoption of interorganizational business process standards adoption [51]. The diffusion literature asserts that organizational culture, organizational structure, and scope of the project are important organizational factors for technology adoption [37]. IS literature also echoed this assertion [24,31,58]. Zhu and Kraemer [57] stated that financial commitment is also a key organizational factor in IS adoption.

## 2.3.3. Environment context

Relational trust is important in adoption of interorganizational business process standards [51]. Competitive pressure has been widely recognized and empirically supported in the IS adoption literature as one of the major factors in the environment context of the TOE framework. Competitive pressure refers to the degree of pressure that the company feels from competitors within the industry [32]. Adoption of a new innovation might help a firm to gain and sustain competitive advantages over its competitors.

In summary, the literature regarding TOE is has largely neglected the healthcare industry with the limited exception of Khoumbati et al. [22]. Prior TOE research summarized in Table 1 shows that the domain of each was fairly specific (e.g., a specific category of software in a specific industry or context). As the TOE is very general, the specificity of prior studies indicates there is need for future research to instantiate the TOE framework in other domains of technology adoption. Moreover, the adoption of contained nomadic information environments has largely been neglected in prior TOE research. In this research project, we will expand and validate the framework presented in Fig. 1 using a case study approach. By interviewing various stakeholders in a hospital organization (e.g., administrators, physicians, and patients), we will gain a better understanding of the health care industry and identify factors unique to health care organizations that influence organizations’ adoption of a contained nomadic information environment.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Case study

The case study approach is used in this study. As the aim of this research is to identify the important constructs in organizations decisions to adopt nomadic RFID applications in the health care industry where few studies exist, the case study approach is wellsuited for this study. A case study is an ‘‘empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, especially when the boundaries between the phenomenon and context are not clearly evident’’ ([56], p. 13). Benbasat et al. [6] also stressed that the case study is a powerful methodology that allows researchers to study information systems in natural settings, learn about the state of the art, and generate theories from practice. Case study allows the researchers to understand the nature and complexity of the process that is taking place and gain an in-depth understanding of the phenomenon under study. In addition, case studies are used for studying new phenomena where quantitative research methodologies are not possible or appropriate. Contained nomadic information environments, instantiated in RFID applications, are a relatively new type of phenomena with great potential in the health care industry which have not been widely adopted and used yet. Therefore, case study is an appropriate methodology for this research.

## 3.2. Company background

The organization that we studied is a major hospital system in a large city in the Midwestern United States. The organization has eleven hospitals and several physician practices in the metropolitan area and surrounding region in a large Midwestern city. The organization is a not-for-profit health care system that is committed to providing high quality health care and health related services in the communities it serves. The organization values patient safety and was ranked in the top 10 percent of the hospitals in terms of patient safety.

The organization also values technology innovation and believes in the benefits and opportunities technology can provide to organizations. After attending a session on innovative RFID applications offered by a leading technological consulting company, the CIO started to realize the potentials of RFID applications for patient tracking and supply chain management in hospital settings, and started to explore possible RFID applications within the organization. In June 2006, the IT department partnered with their wireless service providers on the pilot project of an RFID application in patient tracking at one of their hospitals. The project team also includes six people from the organization including the CIO, the corporate director on technology and security, the site manager, the project manager, and two system analysts. A consulting firm also participated in the pilot project to assist with the installation, integration, validation, and testing of the applications.

The pilot project was a proof-of-concept of RFID applications for patient tracking and lasted for two weeks. The RFID application was installed in two departments in one of the hospital facilities of the organization: the Emergency Department and the Radiology Department. The basic RFID technology consists of readers (beacons), tags (transponders) and end servers to process the information collected from the tags [4]. As shown in Fig. 2, InnerWireless’ system (a type of RFID systems) consists of beacon (reader), tag, master radio, and server and it is implemented through the use of numerous beacons that are installed one per room/zone. In other words, location is defined by associating a zone identifier with each location beacon. The tags are the major system components: each tag collects the signal strength of all beacons within its operational range and the tag location is assigned to the strongest beacon. Then the tag transmits this signal strength information back to the master radio, which then relays it back to the InnerWireless SPOT server for analysis. Subsequently, the middleware connects the InnerWireless server with the data server (data transformation) and the client displays patient location information.

During the pilot project, 128 battery-powered fixed location beacons (wireless receivers) were installed to cover the Emergency Department and the Radiology Department. Approximately 50 battery-powered mobile RFID tags were issued during the pilot project. The beacons can operate up to 7 years on a lithium battery, while the tags used in this project were predicted to last approximately 2 months between battery replacements. The set up of the RFID application is depicted in Fig. 2.

When a patient arrived at the front entrance of the Emergency Department, admitting personnel linked an RFID tag with the patient by opening the patient’s account in the traditional patienttracking system and assigning a patient number to the RFID tag. The tag was then placed into a durable plastic sleeve attached to a lanyard and placed around the patient’s neck. The tag would be worn by the patient during the duration of his/her visit to the hospital. Upon discharge, the RFID tag was collected from the patient and unlinked in the patient-tracking system by the hospital staff.

## 4. Data collection and analysis

## 4.1. Data collection

We contacted the CIO of Munificent<sup>1</sup> Healthcare Systems and she connected us with the IT Liaison at Munficent, Jack Adams (MD/MBA), who then invited the participants of the case study. The participants we interviewed were all part of the teams involved in the RFID Patient Location System Implementation Projects—one in the Emergency Department and the other in the Radiology Department at one of hospitals in the Munificent Systems. These participants, whether they are technical or non-technical people, all went through extensive RFID training before and during the implementation. As such there is little bias involved in the selection process.

We interviewed fourteen employees of the organization who were involved in the pilot project of the RFID application for patient tracking. Among the fourteen people we interviewed, seven were IT managers (i.e., one CIO, two system analysts, one project manager, one information security manager, one network manager, and one site IT manager), two were corporate managers (that is, the director of financial services and the director of material management), one was a physician, and four were nurses (i.e., the admitting manager, the radiology manager, the manager of the emergency department, and the chief nursing operating officer).

## 4.2. Data analysis

The analysis performed was guided by Strauss and Corbin [45]. In the initial stage of data analysis, two researchers reviewed the interview transcripts and looked for discrete incidents, ideas, events, and acts and then gave names (called ‘‘conceptual labels’’) for those concepts. Throughout the data analysis, the researchers performed constant comparative analysis. That is, when the objects, events, acts, or happenings shared some common characteristics, they were grouped together to form a category which captures their shared characteristics. Categories are concepts, derived from data that stand for phenomena. Thus, the researchers were able to reduce the vast amount of raw interview data into smaller, more manageable pieces of data.

Axial coding is the process of relating categories to subcategories along the line of their properties and dimensions. By relating categories with each other, one can answer questions about the phenomenon such as when, where, why, who, how, and with what consequences, thus giving the concept greater ‘‘explanatory power’’. We integrated and organized categories along the ‘‘paradigm’’ of conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences. Causal conditions usually represent sets of events or happenings that influence phenomena. Contextual conditions are the specific set of conditions that intersect dimensionally at this time and place to create the set of circumstances or problems to which persons respond through actions/interactions. This paradigm facilitated making connections between a central phenomenon and its subcategories.

## 5. Research results

## 5.1. Categories and concept

Following the principles of open coding, two researchers went through all of the interview transcripts, notes taken by the researchers during the interviews, and relevant documentation, and identified a list of concepts from the raw data. The researchers compared the concepts that emerged across different participants and multiple data resources for validation. For example, concepts that emerged from the interview transcript of participant A were corroborated with the interview transcript of participant B, or checked against the documentations. Triangulation across data resources helps to strengthen the emerging concepts. Prior literature was used for ‘‘supplemental validation’’ ([13], p. 209). That is, references from prior literature gave validation for the accuracy of the findings and helped in naming the concepts.

Through discussions, the two researchers reached a consensus on the concepts identified from the data, as well as the naming and phrasing of the concepts. The two researchers then reviewed all concepts and categorized the concepts into categories based on similar characteristics shared among the concepts.

The results of the open coding include a list of categories and concepts, shown in Table 3, along with exemplary quotes from the data. The categories identified from the data include some related to RFID in the technological context, organizational context, and environmental context for RFID applications. Other categories include perceived benefits of RFID applications, cost of RFID applications, organizational IT readiness in adopting RFID applications, as well as the reactions to RFID applications from the different stakeholders.

## 5.2. Framework for adoption of contained nomadic information environments

After identifying concepts and categories from the raw data, the next step was to relate the categories with each other, that is, axial coding. A framework that depicts the TOE contextual components, causal conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences of RFID application in the health care organization was developed and depicted in Fig. 3. The figure shows the categories and concepts that emerged from the data analysis, as well as how they interact with each other.

The central phenomenon under study in this research is adoption of a contained nomadic information environment in the form of an RFID application in a health care organization. As indicated in the framework, the health care organization’s decision to adopt an RFID application is influenced by the TOE components, which are the characteristic of the emerging technology, the organizational context, and environmental context. After evaluating the benefits, costs and organizational IT readiness, the organization chooses to invest in RFID applications, which involves initiating the project, selecting vendors, deciding the scope of the project, and then implementing the project. Various stakeholders react differently to the adoption of a contained nomadic information environment in the organization as they have different perspectives, expectations, and experiences with the application. The causal conditions (benefits, costs, and organizational IT readiness) and actions involved in adoption, as well as the consequences of adoption, are influenced by the characteristics of the technology, organizational context, and environmental context.

## 5.2.1. Technology characteristics

Advancements in RFID applications have generated considerable interest at the organization we studied. Compared to the bar-code system, the RFID application is able to send out wireless signals automatically without human intervention, which could potentially reduce human error and increase efficiency.

RFID applications, however, are usually not stand-alone systems and compatibility with existing systems is a very important issue. For example, the organization we studied has over 47 different applications and over 600 servers in its data center. Substantial effort had been made during the pilot project to make sure the RFID application could be integrated with the existing systems (e.g., non-RFID-based patient tracking system).

Participants in this study also highlighted a few challenges encountered during the RFID pilot project. One of them is related to the accuracy of the information transmitted from the RFID application. The problem was due to the phenomenon of ‘‘jittering,’’ which was explained by one of the participants.

‘‘We have so many beacons reporting the signals. Sometimes if somebody was walking down a hallway they may have been closer to a beacon in an adjacent room than they actually were to the beacon in the hall way. So the information we get could bounce back and forth, like you are in the hallway, you are in the room, you are in the hallway, and you are in the room. . . That is what we call jittering.’

The ‘‘jittering’’ issue has affected the accuracy of information in RFID applications. Currently, the information accuracy rate is 85% but this level is low enough to create a nuisance. More generally, such type of jittering is always a possibility that needs to be taken into account in a contained nomadic information environment whose components depend on wireless signals.

During the pilot project, patients were asked to wear RFID tags so that their locations could be automatically tracked and updated in the patient-tracking system. There were some concerns about having patients (especially kids and older people) wear large RFID tags. According to the participants in this study, ‘‘the smaller the tag is,the better.’’

All the RFID tags and beacons are battery-powered. One valid question, as highlighted by our participants, is how to manage the maintenance of the system if all batteries go out at the same time (which is quite possible as all the tags and beacons are from the same batch). Among a few alternative products the vendors provided, the organization chose to adopt the RFID tags that have the longest battery life.

Security and privacy were also taken into account in the pilot project. When a patient checked into the hospital, a random number was assigned to the patient, which was then encoded in the RFID tag. Therefore, the signals that were sent out from the RFID tags were numbers temporarily assigned to the patients, rather than numbers permanently assigned to the patients’ personal information or health record, which ensured privacy and security of patients’ information.

## 5.2.2. Organizational context

The organization we studied has an ‘‘aggressive’’ culture for technology adoption. Technology innovation is one of the core values of the organization. According to one of the participants in this study,

‘‘We are constantly looking for opportunities to use technology in various ways. There is a desire within the organization to look at this type of technology to see if it gives something that is going to benefit the organization.’

Top management in the organization is very supportive of technology innovations. In fact, the RFID pilot project was initiated by the CIO and supported by directors at the corporate level. Top management support has been found to contribute to the success of IT projects in prior literature. We believe that management support is also one of the reasons for the success of the RFID application in this organization we studied.

The organizational structure is fairly decentralized in the organization under study. The organization is a health care system with multiple hospitals in the metropolitan area of a large city in the Midwest. Each hospital is an independent cost center which decides on its own budgeting. The IT department at the corporate level initiates and coordinates various IT projects in the organization. As each hospital has different IT needs and preferences, decentralization could be a potential problem for widespread IT adoption. For example, when asking the IT manager at the hospital that implemented the RFID pilot project about their plan for future RFID applications, we found out that RFID applications are not on the list of their top IT projects for the next year.

One of the major inhibitors for RFID applications in the health care organization is cost. Therefore, an organization’s financial commitment is the key to the success of the RFID project. In the pilot project, the cost was incurred at the corporate level, which contributed to the successful implementation of the pilot project in the test site.

## 5.2.3. Environmental context

The health care industry is probably one of the most heavily regulated industries. Laws and legislation (e.g. HIPAA) are in place to guide the use of patients’ personal information in the health care organizations. Therefore, RFID applications must comply with these regulations and ensure the security and privacy of patients information.

However, patients’ privacy expectations are relatively low in the health care industry. As one participant explained,

‘‘People usually won’t like to be watched or monitored in their daily lives, but things are different in the hospitals. Patients have different expectations on their privacy.

The hurdle related to patient privacy concerns has already been taken care of in the health care industry – from the patients perspective, the RFID application is no different from the bar code systems.’’

External pressure has been found to significantly influence organizations’ intention to adopt information systems (e.g., [48]). Nonetheless, external pressure was viewed as low on this organization’s adoption of RFID. The organization’s adoption of RFID applications was initiated by the organization itself rather than being pushed by competitors or suppliers.

## 5.2.4. Causal conditions—benefits and costs

Rational business decisions are often made based on an evaluation of perceived benefits or costs [18]. This is true when it comes to decisions to adopt RFID applications in the health care organization. An RFID application can automatically track patient locations without human intervention, which could provide physicians or nurses real-time information about their patients. This, in turn, could help improve efficiency of the processes in the hospitals. As highlighted by one of the participants in this study:

‘‘I can always know where my patients are, which really improves efficiency in that I don’t have to waste my time to go to a patient’s room when the patient is not there.’

The cost of RFID applications cannot be overlooked either. The cost includes not only the direct cost, such as the cost of RFID tags and the cost of wireless infrastructure, but also includes the maintenance cost such as the cost of replacing batteries in the beacons and tags. Consistent with the prior literature, cost is one of the major concerns in adoption of RFID applications in the health care organization. One participant mentioned that,

‘‘As technology becomes further developed, hopefully that expense will go down too. The tags become less expensive. Then we will definitely consider more RFID applications.’

## 5.2.5. Actions/interactions in adopting RFID application

In the health care organization under study, the RFID project was initiated by the CIO. According to the current CIO,

‘‘My predecessor [CIO] actually was really the one who was pushing, exploring RFID applications. He had a real desire to do that and he brought the different vendors together.’

The vendor selection was based on a several criteria, according to the results of our interviews, including functionality the vendors could offer, efficiency of the vendors, and their ability to adhere to various standards in the organization (e.g., standards for privacy protection, data transmission, and facility structure).

For the pilot project, the scope was limited because ‘‘cost was part of the reason’’ (according to one participant from the interview). The RFID application was installed in two departments in one of the hospital facilities of the organization: the Emergency Department and the Radiology Department. The choice of the location for the pilot project was based on the following reasons: (1) the hospital chosen has established wireless infrastructure and is using a non-RFID-enabled version of a patient tracking system; (2) there is a significant amount of traffic flowing between the Emergency Department and the Radiology Department, which makes them ideal locations for testing the effects of RFID applications for patient tracking.

## 5.2.6. Reactions to RFID applications

There are various stakeholders in the health care organization such as IT managers, administrators, physicians/nurses, and patients. These stakeholders reacted differently to the RFID application as they have different perspectives, expectations, and experiences with the application.

IT managers were very positive about RFID applications and believed that ‘‘RFID has the potential to do it all,’’ including patient tracking, asset tracking, and supply chain management. Administrators were satisfied with the results of the RFID pilot project and were convinced of the promising future of RFID applications.

As for the physicians and nurses, they were not as enthusiastic about the RFID application as the IT managers or administrators. In this pilot project, nurses were the end-users, that is, they interacted with the RFID applications in their everyday work during the trial of the application. However, they did not see much benefit from the application because of the limited scope of the pilot project. As one participant (nurse) said,

‘‘The RFID application was only installed in the Emergency Department and the Radiology Department. It is too limited to see the real benefits of RFID application. If the patient is not in the Emergency Department, the only thing the RFID application can tell me is whether he/she is in the Radiology Department.’

Patients were very accepting of the RFID application during the pilot project. No patient resistance was observed during the project.

## 5.3. Reliability of the research results

To increase the rigor of the study, we followed principles of data collection proposed by Yin [56].

(1) Using multiple sources of evidence. Multiple data sources can contribute to a high degree of construct validity [40]. In our case study, we interviewed IT managers, administrators, and end-users regarding their opinions and experience about RFID applications. In addition, we used documentation such as the technical report provided by the consulting company involved in the pilot project, and the organizations’ website to provide us a better understanding of the organization, and to substantiate and complement the results of the study.

(2) Creating a case study database. A case study database helps to increase the reliability of the case study. A case study database should have the following four components: case study notes, case study documents, tabular material, and a case study narrative. In our study, we maintained case study notes by taking notes during the interviews. We also audio recorded the interviews for documentation purposes.

(3) Maintaining a chain of evidence. This principle allows an external observer to trace the chain of events occurring in this case study. In our research, we provide anecdotal evidence from the case study database to explain and illustrate the adoption and impact of RFID applications in the organization under study. We also followed the steps for articulating the interviews and organizing the results. This provides the chain of evidence leading up to the results of this study.

(4) Reaching the point of saturation. As recommended by qualitative researchers [45], the sample size was decided by the results of the interviews. As Glaser and Strauss [16] indicated, the researcher ‘‘cannot state at the outset of his research how many [subjects] he will sample during the entire study; he can only count up the [subjects] at the end’’ (p. 61). When subsequent interviews did not yield any new objectives, we concluded that the ‘‘point of saturation’’ was reached. That is, additional interviews would not provide any added insights to the study. This is the standard ‘‘stopping rule’’ for case study research. Similar arguments were also put forth by Tan and Hunter [46] for the Repertory Grid technique, which is a qualitative research methodology to develop constructs through interview data. In our study, the comprehensive and intensive nature of our interviews allows us to reach the point of saturation after the sixth interview. The focused nature of our research question also helps to limit the number of subjects required to reach the saturation point. We continued to interview more subjects (i.e., fourteen subjects in total, which is double the required sample size) to ensure that we had indeed reached the ‘‘point of saturation’’.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Mapping the constructs to prior literature

We now map our findings to previous innovation diffusion and RFID literature. Table 4 summarizes prior studies that support the constructs derived from this research and also highlights constructs that are new to this study.

We begin with the TOE components. For technological context, some of its dimensions (i.e., comparative advantages and compatibility) are supported by classic innovation diffusion literature [12,47,49], while others (i.e., security and privacy protection and information accuracy) are echoed by various RFID applications literature [1,26,27,43,53,54]. We also discover three new salient factors in the technological context, namely, the effect of jittering on information accuracy, battery life, and size of RFID tags. These are important factors that are intrinsic to the contained nomadic information environments.

Our findings demonstrate that organizational context also plays a major role in RFID adoption in the health care industry. Four dimensions (organizational culture, organizational structure, management support, and financial commitment) are derived from our study and all of them are supported by prior literature [31,33,57].

Finally, our research finds three dimensions of environmental context influencing RFID adoption in the health care sector. They are compliance with legislations and standards, patient privacy expectations, and external pressure. Among these three dimensions, external pressure finds overwhelming support from prior literature [22,33,57–60]. It confirms the notion that health care is a very competitive industry [34]. While patient privacy expectation is supported by IS diffusion research in health care [22], compliance with legislation and standards is reiterated by RFIDspecific studies [42,54]. Four dimensions of reactions to RFID applications are found in our research to influence the RFID adoption decision-making process: the reactions of administrators, IT managers, physicians and nurses, and patients. The first three dimensions are supported by prior literature [22,31]. The patients’ dimension is a relevant factor uncovered by our research. It means that patients play an important role in RFID adoption in a health care environment.

Our findings suggest that the perceived benefits construct is an important contributing factor in facilitating RFID adoption in the health care sector. Three dimensions of the construct — improved efficiency, reduced human intervention, and real-time information about patient location — are supported by prior studies [27]. We also uncover a salient characteristic in perceived benefits of RFID in healthcare—streamlined process. This notion is consistent with the goal of process improvement expressed in many other contexts. We note that our results consider cost as a critical issue in RFID adoption, which is echoed by several previous studies [5,44]. All three dimensions of the cost construct are support by the literature. Our findings show that organizational readiness is of paramount importance in RFID adoption and implementation. The existing infrastructure dimension is widely supported by the previous research. While the IT support dimension is backed by Khoumbati et al. [22], IT knowledge of users is fully supported by Orlikowski [31] and Zhu et al. [58].

6.2. Expanding the TOE in contained nomadic information environments

Once the important factors in TOE contexts have been identified that influenced the RFID adoption and implementation, we are able to summarize them in the model shown in Fig. 4. Relative advantage and compatibility are viable generic dimensions for the technological context aspect of the TOE framework proposed for contained nomadic information environments. Based on literature and our findings, we consider that information security and privacy are major dimensions in the technology context. Our findings identified technical factors that are important in the nomadic environments, namely the degrading of information accuracy caused by ‘‘jittering’’ and the importance for the mobile components of both battery life and physical size. Integrating the empirical evidence and the literature review, we consider four major dimensions of organizational context in RFID adoption: organizational culture, organizational structure, management support, and financial commitment. Based on our research results and prior literature, we find three dimensions that we consider to be pertinent in the health care environment, including compliance with legislation and standards, patient privacy expectation, and external pressure. Fig. 4 depicts a meta-model populating the established TOE framework with the components we identified for a contained nomadic information environment.

The TOE contextual factors all influence the decision to adopt a contained nomadic information environment. Our research shows that this decision is based in large part on considerations of benefits, costs, and IT readiness.

## 7. Future research and limitations

Our study addressed RFID as one example of a contained nomadic information environment. While we believe the categories in our model are fairly general, these RFID-derived factors likely do not constitute a complete list for the more general model. When conceptualizing about contained nomadic information environments, it is important to remember that RFID tags are not the only types of mobile devices. Other devices could do more than keep track of their location; they could passively measure vital signs (for example) or could even allow for active input from the wearer or user. This possibility raises the stakes in the importance of design of both the environment and the devices that are contained in it and may introduce other TOE contextual factors that our research has not revealed. Furthermore, the factors in our model are not immune to advances in technology. For example, new technology might solve the problem of jittering.

There are some limitations of this study that warrant further discussion and need to be kept in mind when interpreting the results. This study is a single case study on a private non-profit health care organization in the United States. Caution needs to be taken when generalizing the results of this research to for-profit health care organizations or applying the model generated from this study to health care organizations in other countries, whose models for health care delivery may differ substantially from the US. As RFID applications are still new to the health care industry, few organizations have actually adopted and implemented RFID applications yet. Considering the state-of-the-art of the technology and the purpose of this study, which is theory building rather than theory testing, a single-case study is appropriate [6], but more research could be done. Future research can expand this research by conducting a multiple-case study to account for organizational, cultural, and national differences. Multiple-case study will also allow for cross-case analysis and yield more general research results [6], which would help to validate and extend the results found by this study.

## 8. Conclusions and contributions

Contained nomadic information environments offer great promise and potential in many industries including the health care industry. In this research we conducted a case study of one particular instance, the implementation and implications of an RFID application for a health care organization. We interviewed different stakeholders in the organization regarding their opinions and experiences about the RFID application. Then the data were analyzed qualitatively. The result of this study is a list of categories and concepts derived from the data, as well as a model that depicts the context conditions causes, actions/ interactions, and consequences of RFID application in the health care organization. More generally, these categories and concepts are appropriate for the higher level classification of environments we refer to as contained nomadic information environments.

This study also has significant practical implications. RFID has received considerable attention from health care organizations. This study reported the RFID pilot project for patient tracking in a large health care organization. Challenges the organization encountered during the RFID pilot project were discussed and suggestions were made. Therefore, the results of this study can provide guidelines or suggestions to health care organizations that are embarking on RFID applications.

In this research we have identified TOE contextual factors in the literature as well as new factors that emerged from our case study. We also identified factors related to causes and consequences of the decision to adopt RFID in hospital setting, specifically, and to adopt a contained nomadic information environment, more generally. These factors can inform organizational decision makers who wish to improve their own insights related to this type of decision in their own organization. As information technology improves and becomes less costly, and as success stories begin to proliferate, organizational leaders will be enticed to adopt or enhance a contained nomadic information environment, and this research can give them one perspective for the decisions they will need to make.
