# Maintaining alignment over the long-term: lessons from the evolution of an electronic point of sale system

David Boddy, Robert Paton

School of Business and Management, Gilbert Scott Building, University of Glasgow, Glasgow, UK

Correspondence: D Boddy, School of Business and Management, Gilbert Scott Building, University of Glasgow, Glasgow G12 8QQ, UK.

E-mail: d.boddy@mgt.gla.ac.uk

## Abstract

Research in both the organizational and information systems areas suggests that aligning information systems and dimensions of the organization improves performance. However, the dynamic nature of both information systems and organizations makes a close alignment more an aspiration than a state. This paper examines alignment from the perspective of the management processes through which companies may achieve and sustain alignment in dynamic environments. In so doing they may also develop core capabilities in managing IS innovation, which enable sustained competitive advantage. The paper traces the evolution of alignment in a company that used information systems to develop the business in new strategic directions, and shows how semi-structures contributed to this.

## Introduction

mplementing a major information system (IS) differs from other forms of organizational change in that it frequently involves recently developed technologies, whose effects are hard to predict. Using them to improve organizational performance is also a lengthy process, in part because the benefits depend on making complementary changes in organizational assets and capabilities such as structure, culture or business processes (see, for example, Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000). Performance will be further enhanced if these assets align both with each other and with the (changing) external context (Sabherwal et al., 2001). An interpretive perspective (Walsham, 1993) stresses that this context is not an objective reality, but is selectively and subjectively interpreted by stakeholders with different interests (Knights and Murray, 1994). These interpretations, and their perceived interests, shape what people do in relation to an IS project.

This situation, in which those shaping an IS project subjectively interpret their dynamic context, has two related implications. The first is that it will be practically impossible for managers to specify, plan and implement a ‘theoretically optimal’ (Sabherwal et al., 2001) alignment pattern: the most that appears feasible is movement towards that vision. Second, success will depend on the management processes used to implement IS and complementary organizational changes (Markus, 2004). Methods based on imposing control may be unable to cope with the inherent uncertainties and ambiguities (Drummond and Hodgson, 2003), so this paper will examine an alternative approach, based on Brown and Eisenhardt’s (1997) concept of semi-structures. Firms that develop the ability to manage IS projects in this way may thereby develop a core capability for maintaining alignment that is of longer-term strategic significance (Melville et al., 2004).

The empirical basis is a long-term study of Kwik-Fit, a chain of roadside vehicle repair depots. The company introduced an electronic point of sales system (EPOS) in 1982 (an innovative application for the time), and IS developments have continued to play a major role in company strategy. This long-term micro-level study shows how managers implemented successive changes to maintain, over the long-term, a high alignment between IS and organizational structure.

The next section develops our theoretical perspectives on the evolution of alignment profiles, interpreting contextual contingencies, and managing through semi-structures. We then describe the company and our research methods: the case is presented and analysed with the questions prompted by the theoretical model.

## Theoretical perspectives

## Evolving alignment profiles

Studies of organizational performance (e.g. Drazin and Van de Ven, 1985; Miles and Snow, 1994; Whittington et al., 1999) suggest that aligning two or more core (Siggelkow, 2002) elements of an organization enhances performance. Miles and Snow (1994) showed the benefits of a ‘fit’ or ‘alignment’ between strategies, structures and processes. Whittington et al. (1999) found that higher performing European firms achieved ‘whole system’ change: ‘the benefits ofynew organizational practices depend on the context of other changes in which they are set, and are especially strong when combined within comprehensive organizational change’ (p. 598).

Studies in the information systems (IS) field reach similar conclusions. Focusing on the alignment between IS and the external context, Chan et al. (1997), Levy et al. (2001) and Croteau and Bergeron (2001) show that companies perform better if they align their realized IS strategy with their realized business strategy. Such a link ensures that managers allocate IS resources to strategically important applications (Levy et al., 2001) and implies that they use IS to support current strategy or develop a new one.

Others (such as Sauer and Yetton, 1997; Boonstra et al., 2004) focus on internal alignment, and show that the outcomes of an IS investment depend on the extent to which managers support IS strategy with appropriate organizational strategies in areas such as business processes, people and culture. Econometric work by Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2000) shows that managers who combine technical innovation with appropriate organizational innovation secure higher returns from IS investments than those who attend only to technical factors. Sabherwal et al. (2001) provides qualitative, case-study evidence that high alignment of both strategic and organizational aspects of IS leads to superior performance.

While high alignment appears to benefit performance, few companies achieve and sustain it (Burn, 1996; Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001; Grant, 2003). This may reflect the fact that the changes, which lead (or not) towards alignment, take place in a dynamic context that, as Pettigrew et al. (1992) point out, has external, internal and historical dimensions, the latter in particular implying ‘the need to locate change in past, present and future’ (p. 269). Managers implement IS against the background of an established pattern of alignment, the result of earlier changes. Securing and maintaining alignment therefore implies balancing three streams of activity (Burn, 1996) – developing IS that match external demands and opportunities, ensuring that core internal elements align with these, and managing the relationship between historical, current and future alignments.

This implies that IS alignment will be constantly evolving, similar to Mintzberg’s (1994) views on organizational strategy as a process best described as emergent or adaptive. Many observers of IS-based changes find that they are not the result of a scientific, comprehensive or rational analysis, but of an iterative, incremental process, characterised by restricted analysis and bargaining between the players (Waema and Walsham, 1990; Knights and Murray, 1994; McLoughlin and Clark, 1999; Boddy, 2000). IS projects take place in volatile, uncertain environments, as people with different interests and priorities try to shape both the ends and the means. Successful managers ‘proceed flexibly and experimentally from broad concepts to specific commitments, making the latter concrete as late as possible’ (Walsham, 1993: 146) – creating an evolving pattern of alignment, suited to changing contingencies.

## Interpreting contextual contingencies

The contingencies in the (changing) context are not objective realities awaiting rational analysis. People act according to subjective interpretations of their world – ‘our knowledge of realityyis a social construction by human actorsythere is no objective reality which can be discovered by researchers and replicated by others’ (Walsham, 1993: 5). Stakeholders interpret the existing context and act to change it to promote personal, local or organizational objectives. A manager may see a change in the company’s external environment, and respond by, say, advocating the purchase of a computer system (technology) or a change in reporting relationships (structure). Others interpret this proposal in the light of their perspective of the context and try to influence decisions in a way that suits their interests. The outcomes of these interactions change the context – which now provides the historical background to future interpretations and actions (Orlikowski, 1992). The essential idea is that the relation between people and their context works both ways. People shape the context, and peoples’ interpretation of the context shapes human action.

Siggelkow (2002) pointed out that an implication of this interpretive process is that there is no agreement on universally relevant core elements of an alignment profile. Different interpretations of the context imply different contingencies upon which to focus attention. Whether a performance-enhancing profile emerges in an organization depends on how stakeholders act in relation to each other as the project evolves. They could use a method based on rational models of change, emphasizing extensive planning and control, or an approach that reflects the emergent, interpretive nature of IS projects and their context – recognizing that ‘the real word inevitably involves some thinking ahead of time as well as some adaptation en route’ (Mintzberg, 1994: 24). Semi-structures constitute one such approach.

## Managing through semi-structures

A general problem in strategic change is to reconcile the contradictory demands of stability and innovation. Some (see, for example, Benner and Tushman, 2003; Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004) advocate the development of ‘ambidextrous’ organizations, able to pursue two disparate things at the same time – such as maintaining an existing business by aligning current activities while exploring a potentially disruptive new venture. This can be done by structural means – such as setting up the new venture as a separate operation in a new business unit. Siggelkow and Levinthal (2003) advocate a temporarily decentralized form, which allows people to act with a high degree of autonomy, exploring possible solutions free from the constraints of the established context. Eventually, they are reintegrated with the firm to ensure coordination with other activities. Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) introduce the idea of ‘contextual ambidexterity’. Rather than adopting an ambidextrous structure, managers develop a context in which staff develop the ability to switch between the demands of stability and innovation, rather than depend on a higher level structural separation.

Some IS projects are intended to reinforce stability, others to foster innovation. In those that are meant to support established, predictable routines managers are likely to be well aware of the core technical and organizational changes required and can plan them with some confidence. The elements of an alignment profile may be clear, in which case it will be appropriate to use a structured approach to planning the change. In projects involving novel IS applications and new business developments the way forward will be much less certain: achieving alignment will depend on people discovering solutions during, and indeed after, implementation. It will not be possible to predict and plan the core elements of an alignment profile, nor how stakeholders will interact with successive proposals and changes. In such conditions, Markus (2004) suggests that IS and organizational activities need to be integrated with each other, and that an iterative, incremental approach will often be appropriate.

A model, which suggests a way of doing that, is Brown and Eisenhardt’s (1997) idea of ‘semi-structures’. In a study of product development, they observed that companies which developed this form – rigid enough to provide a structure to enable action, but not so rigid as to inhibit creativity – were relatively successful. Although they studied product development the idea of semi-structures may be relevant to those implementing major IS change in changing conditions. ‘Semi-structures’ lie between the rigidities of mechanistic organizations, and the fluidity of organic. ‘Change readily occurs because semi-structures are sufficiently rigid that change can be organized to happen, but not so rigid that it cannot occur. Too little structure makes it difficult to co-ordinate change. Too much structure makes it hard to move’ (p. 29). Their research identified the characteristics of organizations, which were able to develop and implement new products continuously and profitably by, in effect, combining elements of mechanistic and organic structures (Burns and Stalker, 1961). The features included five practices, which seem potentially useful in IS-based process innovation:

 clear responsibilities

 clearly understood priorities and focus

 extensive communication within and between projects, using formal and informal means

 experimentation – a willingness to try something quickly and cheaply

 strategic partnering – with suppliers or customers.

Taken together, these features of the semi-structured form (similar to the ‘dynamic capabilities’ identified by Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000) constitute routines by which managers allocate their resource base. They enable them, for example, to integrate resources around successive projects, reconfigure resources between alternative uses, and gain resources through learning or creating alliances. We expect them to enable organizations to achieve a higher-than-usual degree of IS alignment, as they introduce a sequence of projects with different core contingencies.

## Research setting and method

## Company background

At the time of the study, Kwik-Fit (created by Tom Farmer in 1971) was Europe’s largest independent tyre and exhaust retailer. Ford acquired the business in 1997 but retained the brand and did not interfere in operational or IS matters. Ford sold the business to a venture capital company in 2003. The core business remains the drive-in, ‘while you wait’, fitting of replacement parts such as tyres, exhausts and batteries. Services are delivered to customers through road side depots, each with a manager and several fitters. The company slogan is ‘100% delighted customers’. A critical and costly aspect of the business is to have enough spare parts to cope with the range of cars requiring immediate service. Transactions mean administration - and in the early years the company kept records of transactions, receipts, and stocks on paper.

## Research method

The study within the company has continued, at intervals, since 1986 – soon after the company installed the first EPOS system – and so uses a longitudinal research design (Eisenhardt, 1989). The data relate to four phases in the evolution of the system and the organization. Phase 0 relates the early evolution of some key management practices; Phase 1 is from 1982 to 1989, when EPOS was introduced with a focus on internal efficiency. Phase 2 is from 1990 to 1994, when the company introduced a divisional structure (Boddy and Gunson, 1996). Phase 3 covers the development of a new business, Kwik-Fit Insurance Services (KFIS), from 1995.

The data on successive alignment patterns need to be anchored to a consistent, validated framework. Sabherwal et al. (2001) provide this with their Strategic Information System Management Profile (SISMP). This is a researchbased profile that enables a researcher to assess a company’s alignment on four dimensions - business strategy, business structure, IS strategy and IS structure. Together with two ‘cross-alignments’ this gives the six dimensions in the left-hand column of Table 3.

Sabherwal et al. (2001) defined alignment as ‘the extent to which (two or more) organizational dimensions meet theoretical norms of mutual coherence’ (p. 179) and identified three ‘ideal-types’ for each aspect of alignment. Table 1 gives an example for business alignment, showing three ideal-type strategies (defender, analyzer and prospector), and the structure associated with each.

Alignment between two dimensions is regarded as high if they are from the same row (defender and centralized), medium if they are from adjacent rows (defender and semistructured), and low if they are two rows apart (defender and decentralized). We used this model to trace the evolving pattern of alignment.

The second piece of the model is the influence of subjective interpretations of the context, implying that it is impossible to predict universally relevant core elements of an alignment profile. Different interpretations of the context will imply different core contingencies, and different elements upon which to focus attention. It is however possible to identify core elements of a project empirically in a particular project (Siggelkow, 2002). We adopted this approach, in the sense of showing how key players changed their interpretation of key contingencies at different stages of the story.

The third component is the semi-structure. We identified five of the eight semi-structure features identified by Brown and Eisenhardt (1997), which seem most relevant to process (as distinct from product) development. These are shown in Table 4–7, which also provide evidence of the extent to which these features were used in successive phases.

To gather data on the three components of the theoretical model, we worked inductively using interviews, observation and document review. Interviewees included Directors, IS specialists, Area Managers, Depot Managers and staff. We also interviewed some staff responsible for the company’s operations on mainland Europe. Table 2 shows the number and seniority of the staff interviewed. The interviews usually lasted 45–60 min, and were recorded and transcribed. The research team reviewed the transcripts independently for dominant themes, and a preliminary account of the case was circulated to senior managers to check for factual accuracy, and inviting comments on our interpretation of the data. We also observed how staff interacted with the EPOS system and customers (and later did the same in KFIS). Documentary evidence included internal unpublished reports, Annual Reports, other published studies, and media coverage. We maintained contact until 1996 (Phases 1 and 2 in Table 3) and in 2002 the opportunity arose to discuss how the system now supported a new business venture.

## Case study

The story is presented in four phases - pre-project, efficiency; divisions and KFIS; Table 3 summarises our perception of the evolving alignment pattern.

## Phase 0 – pre-project

From the earliest years of the company, management had worked to perfect a smooth ‘back-office’ system to reduce administrative costs. An effective manual system was exemplified in the ‘Management Summary Card’, which gave clear guidance to managers on how to run their depot. Although they sent regular information on performance to Head Office, managers had considerable autonomy. There was a company price list, but Head Office did not know the prices which depots charged until weeks later. Depot managers ordered replacement stocks. Area managers tried to monitor their depots, but one commented:

Before computerisation it was uncontrollable. There was a price list, but we never knew if the depot was selling at that price, or at half of it.

There was a small management team in Edinburgh. Farmer believed firmly in keeping the number of administrative staff low and throughout the study period, as the number of depots grew towards 1900, it employed only about 90 staff at Head Office. Severe administrative problems appeared in 1980, when the company took over two similar businesses. It now had three separate and incompatible systems, and the growing costs of running the enlarged business threatened the ethos of tight administration. This became the focus for the EPOS project – to keep administrative costs down, and to provide more effective control over the depots.

Yet despite the concern over autonomy, a parallel aspect of the prevailing ethos was that managers throughout the company were expected to support the depots, not to dominate them. An Area Manager:

We’re not a big organization – we’re a collection of small businessesyin our company culture, head office is seen as a support operation.

A final aspect of the context before the project was that the company valued fast action. People were expected to do things today rather than tomorrow. The first IT Director commented:

It is part of the character of Farmer that things should be done fast – in his inimitable phrase ‘I want it done immediate’. And that whole attitude stretches right through the organization.

The Kwik-Fit structure matched this philosophy, described by an Area Manager as:

a very flat structure, inasmuch as if we wanted to change something tomorrow we could do it – we do not have to go through a hierarchy.

## Summary

In the early years of the business, the company followed a Prospector strategy – growing rapidly by opening depots and acquiring rivals. The business structure was decentralized, IS did not hold a strategic position in the company, and the founder of the business regarded administrative costs as too high. Although successful, alignment was medium, in terms of the SISMP model. The company had already developed some distinctive features of the semistructured organization, as Table 4 shows.

## Phase 1 – efficiency

In 1980, the company had bought a rival that had developed a computer system – indeed this was one of the reasons for acquiring the company. Farmer was enthusiastic about the possibilities: as the IT Director at the time observed:

we recognised that we needed a good computer system – we couldn’t continue the way we were. Management had to have access to instant information, to know the effect of making changes on our profitability.

A team to develop a new system for the whole company worked closely with the IS supplier (Farmer had been impressed by a system they had developed for a fast-food chain), who spent many weeks in the business, identifying how it worked, and the information which staff needed. In addition:

There was a big think tank or brainstorming session. Maybe 20 key people – depot managers, area managers, auditors – all levels of management were involved in it, and asked for their opinions about what they wanted from it. Especially depot managers, we hand-picked them.

Managers planned the project thoroughly and implemented it rapidly in 1982 – reflecting the company style. Tom Farmer recalled:

We decided not to have a long testing period – we’d put it into two centres, and if it worked there, we’d put it into the others. We went live very quickly.

The EPOS system consisted of terminals in each depot, linked to a mainframe at Head Office. The terminals (known as Management Action Terminals (MATs)) were robustly designed to be used by managers and fitters, holding prices and descriptions of the inventory. During the day, MATs performed administrative tasks in the centre, such as issuing quotations, invoices and updating inventory records. Each night, they sent data about the day’s transactions to the mainframe. That processed the data into daily management reports for internal use, and into instructions to suppliers and banks.

The keyboard was clear and easy to use, each key being marked with a system function – such as ‘cash sale’ or ‘stock delivery’. Pressing a key led the user through the required steps. These fundamental processes did not change – EPOS automated the existing, and widely understood, manual system. A Divisional Director:

There was a clear vision, a simple set of procedures and a simple process. Our business is not complicated. I think vision is very important: one of the beauties of the system is that it is entirely robust and is clearly capable of operating in the relatively harsh environment of the depots.

A major effect was that all administrative tasks were now performed automatically, including stock replenishment. More significantly, accurate information about the performance of each depot was available the following day to managers at Head Office. A Divisional Manager:

EPOS focuses the managers’ attention on doing things exactly the way Kwik-Fit requires them to be done. That really was the breakthrough. It still allows discretion on the part of management, but any discretion has to be accompanied by a reason.

Staff readily accepted the system. They saw the fit with established procedures:

That’s what made implementation so successful – it was very easy to put the system into the depots. As soon as you have pressed a function key on the keyboard you are then locked in and everything you do for that transaction is prompted. A novice can pick it up and use it very quickly. The combination of ease of use and being totally allied to the procedures has been the success.

## Summary

Management interpreted key contingencies in the context as requiring them to plan and implement a system which would implement the founder’s strategic vision for an EPOS system. This closely matched the manual procedures, and staff interpreted the change as reinforcing the existing (effective) alignment between them and their work. It enabled centralization and allowed depot managers to concentrate on customers. The business was following a Defender strategy consistent with a focus on costs, a centralized structure and a centralized IS system. IS was now strategically important, and alignment was high (Table 5).

## Phase 2 – divisions

As managers in the company came to understand the potential of EPOS, they identified ways of using it to grow the depot network more rapidly, and to improve depot performance by using better information. Having computerized the manual system to reduce administrative costs, managers quickly realized the value of the information which EPOS provided. The IT Director who had implemented the system:

We under-estimated the potential for management information; we got it wrong - and that applied to the company as a whole. We were suddenly producing tables and statistics that surprised a lot of people.

However, this technical capability was being delivered within the traditionally centralized structure. The geographical divisions were concerned with sales, not profitability, and this affected the way staff interpreted information. Although Head Office managers now had information about depot performance, they had difficulty persuading divisional managers to act on it. A newly appointed Divisional Director, previously in Head Office:

You couldn’t get down the line, and get hold of it, because there were too many people to deal with. We couldn’t get them to understand how important it was to hold the right levels of stock. The computer system allowed us to see the problem, but not to get to grips with it.

The Board interpreted events as raising a new contingency, requiring a structure that would enable managers to use the information the system now provided, and so in 1992 they gave those managing the five geographical divisions greater autonomy. Divisional Directors were not completely independent, but had full profit responsibility. Senior management eliminated several central departments and passed the work to the divisions – for example, all physical aspects of the depots, such as maintenance or equipment, became a divisional responsibility. The IS was re-designed to align with the organizational change – divisions could view their data, while senior management could see that for the whole business.

## Summary

The company continued with a Prospector strategy, emphasizing the performance of depots and their managers. However, senior managers perceived a mis-alignment between the IS and the structure, which the Board resolved by introducing Divisions with profit responsibilities. This change was not part of the EPOS plan, but had emerged from managers’ new understanding of their IS capabilities. The Prospector strategy is consistent with a decentralized structure and a shared IS to support business growth. After a brief mis-alignment, the company returned to a high alignment (Table 6).

## Phase 3 - Kwik-Fit Insurance Services

EPOS provided the base for several new business ventures (such as Fleet Sales) which managers believed would not otherwise have been possible. The most visible of these is Kwik-Fit Insurance Services (KFIS), whose unique feature is the use of the customer database generated through the depot terminals (Waite, 2001). A group of insurance professionals developed the idea of selling motor insurance through a well-known brand and identified Kwik-Fit as a suitable vehicle. The Kwik-Fit Board agreed to the proposal, launching KFIS in May 1995. It is located in a separate office building in Central Scotland, with most staff working as call centre agents.

The transaction data are passed electronically from Kwik-Fit Head Office to KFIS the following day. Staff in the KFIS call centre call up each customer to thank them and to ask about the quality of service. They convert the replies into a scale and the system automatically returns these data daily to Head Office. Finally, staff offer the customer a free insurance quotation and, if accepted, enter the renewal date into an electronic diary, which in due course prompts a call to give a quotation.

Managers interpreted the context as offering opportunities for rapid sales growth, which they believed required an aggressive incentive scheme: sales staff could earn a bonus that doubled their basic salary. They were given intensive sales training, and led by a charismatic sales manager. To increase staff rapidly, the company outsourced recruitment and selection. While the approach increased the sales of policies (to almost 400,000 in the year to February 1998), quality fell. Customers perceived the company as selling too aggressively, and insurers perceived the company to be selling too many policies to high-risk customers. Business was lost, and profits fell sharply in the year to February 1999.

Management quickly concluded that in building the business quickly, they had paid little attention to the human aspects of the business, especially the people they employed. A director said:

We did not invest in people enough in the early days: it was two years before we employed an HR professional. We were not good at recruiting, training or devising payment systems – but we then (expected them) to have a dialogue with our customers. We cut corners in terms of our people. For a while we were very profitable – but we were doing potential damage to the quality image.

The Board of the parent company reacted quickly to this new interpretation of key contingencies, changing the KFIS management. They appointed a Director of Human Resources to create an HR strategy that aligned more closely with the company strategy, including a new pay structure, and a comprehensive communication system. They also appointed a Quality Manager. Together these ensured that several aspects of the Kwik-Fit culture were transferred to KFIS:

Our current obsession with customer satisfaction is not an accident – that’s happened because of Kwik-Fit. They make sure that we look after customers in the way that Kwik-Fit would like them to be looked after. We had some senior managers from Kwik-Fit to help us get the satisfaction up, by working on all our systems, processes and people to see where the weak links were. The quality manager was instrumental in changing our culture. We have a much more controlled mechanism now – we know what’s going on. We record all our calls, we monitor our calls, we make sure our interface with the customer is now policed. If we get a complaint we can listen to the call and find out what went wrong. We have also introduced mystery shoppers, which is a regular Kwik-Fit method for monitoring how we deal with customers.

This change appeared by late 2001 to be working well, as indicated by performance indicators such as customer satisfaction, staff performance and profitability (see also Waite, 2001). In early 2002, the company reported a 30% rise in customers compared to the previous year. After an initial stage in which the approach was inconsistent with that of the main company, changes had been made to reestablish alignment within the subsidiary.

## Summary

The company allowed the new business to operate in a decentralized way. At first, the core contingencies emphasized sales rather than quality, producing a mis-alignment between the HR and business strategy of the subsidiary, and between the subsidiary and the parent. The Board was monitoring the situation closely and acted rapidly to bring the internal structure of the subsidiary into a closer alignment with the parent company. The Prospector strategy is consistent with a decentralised structure and a shared IS system supporting growth. By changing the subsidiary’s methods, management established high alignment (Table 7).

## Discussion

This section relates the narrative to the questions prompted by the theoretical model.

## Did the alignment profile evolve over the long term?

The study has shown that alignment is not static, but can evolve with changing circumstances. The founder of the company interpreted the pre-project alignment as inefficient and costly, so not meeting his personal objectives. He acted to change this profile when he introduced the EPOS system, deliberately seeking to align its features with the existing, well-defined systems and with the centralized structure. There was a deliberate, high alignment between the new system and established operating processes, management structures and peoples’ work, which aided acceptance. As managers became familiar with the information which the system provided they learned that they needed to disrupt that alignment by making a further change – introducing a divisional structure.

In extending the information system to create KFIS they again disrupted alignment by establishing it as a relatively autonomous unit. Its managers, with an objective of rapid sales growth, introduced an incentive-based payment system, which encouraged staff to focus on sales, not quality. As senior managers realised the problems this caused for the main business they acted quickly to limit the autonomy of KFIS and to implement practices to align the original and the new business. Did this reflect changing interpretations of the core contingencies and elements to be aligned?

Phase 1 of EPOS was intended to apply an established IS to a known process, and as such it was consistent with, and reinforced, the inherited alignment pattern. Staff could therefore plan and implement the project using relatively structured processes. This change had an unexpected effect in demonstrating the value of management information about depot performance – but managers perceived that to use this new resource effectively they needed to move from a centralized to a decentralized structure. The change to a divisional structure was a much more uncertain project, as it challenged the long-established tradition of centralisation. The project involved reconfiguring both managerial and IS resources, requiring a willingness to experiment with an unfamiliar style. It was successful, as the divisional structure restored high alignment.

KFIS was a project that aimed to gain resources by a strategic partnership with external entrepreneurs to exploit a new opportunity. Senior management in the original and the new business initially misinterpreted the core contingencies, focusing on sales growth by an independently managed business unit. This led to a low alignment of HR and quality management practices between it and the core business, with damaging effects on customer service and profits. The Board responded quickly to this unexpected outcome by making another ‘postrevolutionary adjustment’ to ensure consistent standards of customer care.

## Did using semi-structures help to achieve and maintain IS alignment?

Successive projects needed to manage different contingencies and to align different core elements. Some structured projects (such as Phase 1) could be planned and implemented in a way that deliberately reinforced an established alignment profile. Mechanistic forms (clear responsibilities, priorities and focus) had long been part of the company style, with tight, clearly defined management structures and responsibilities both for staff and managers. This approach worked well in managing Phase 1, and continued to be a central feature of the way the company worked – though the nature of those responsibilities evolved. However that style in itself did not guarantee alignment, as when the initial EPOS system generated information, which was at variance with the inherited pattern of responsibilities. Similarly, the responsibilities in the early days of KFIS were clearly set out – but neglected important aspects of customer service. In other words, Phases 2 and 3 were less predictable. They involved responding to new contingencies by creating alignment patterns that were unfamiliar, and ultimate success depended on, amongst other things, being willing to experiment, manage strategic partnerships and communicate extensively with those affected. That the company was able to maintain a relatively high degree of alignment over many years appears to have been supported by this ability to combine mechanistic and organic structures as the need arose. A notable feature of the style is that missteps were quickly corrected by other features of Brown and Eisenhardt’s (1997) semi-structures.

Extensive communication was an early feature of the company – the management was expected to know what was happening in depots and to support them. This ethos pervaded the EPOS development process, ensuring the system fitted depot needs as well as those of senior managers. There was extensive involvement of operating staff in the design of the system, and a willingness to take ideas from all levels in the business. The changes were introduced in a way that matched the action-centred style of the company, and a deep willingness to experiment. They implemented the initial EPOS quickly, and took a similar approach to KFIS. Here the initial plan was misguided but close involvement with the business ensured that senior managers soon saw the damage this was doing, and acted quickly to remedy the position – re-establishing a new alignment between the subsidiary and the parent. Finally, a willingness to engage in strategic partnering was evident – in working closely with a carefully selected IS supplier, and with external insurance experts to build KFIS.

## Conclusion

This paper has examined the evolution of alignment over 20 years in a single company. We conclude by discussing five points, which show the wider implications of our analysis for those implementing major IS. These concern supporting evidence, project failures, a succession of projects, core capabilities and a question about project contingencies.

We believe that the evidence in this case supports the view that managers developed and used a series of practices termed semi-structures to manage their implementation of a succession of IS projects. These practices contributed significantly to the success of the projects and to the competitive position of the company. Other research, in IS and in other forms of organizational change, supports the value of some form of structural support for major change. Pettigrew (1987) observed that there are limits to what individuals can achieve, and that these can be overcome by supporting individual action with appropriate structures and mechanisms. This has received quantitative and qualitative support in studies of organizational change in general (Boddy et al., 1998) and IS-based change in particular (Sauer and Yetton, 1997; McDonagh, 2003; Markus, 2004). People use appropriate structures to raise concerns and ensure that issues are debated. They allow a range of views to be brought together and help ensure continuity. McDonagh (2003) noted that projects are frequently managed with a technical rather an organisational focus, reflecting the relative isolation of the executive, IS and organizational development communities ‘Executive management view the introduction of IT as an economic imperative while IT specialists view it as a technical imperative. The coalescent nature of these two imperatives is such that human and organisational considerations are regularly marginalised and ignored (during IS projects)’ (pp. 14–15). A senior management wishing to take into account the contribution of all three perspectives can only do so if they create institutions on which each is adequately represented. Whatever is agreed outlasts the individuals involved and can be more easily transmitted to many places and people. The evidence from this study showing the utility of semi-structures is consistent with this wider literature.

The general relevance of semi-structures is strengthened by research into IS project failure (Sauer, 1993). Knights and Murray’s (1994) study of Pensco (an insurance company that implemented a new computer system from which it obtained few benefits) found that responsibilities for the project were unclear and that departments stressed different priorities. Communication was poor, with evidence of ‘competing narratives’ between senior managers and operating staff. A weak project structure was unable to resolve these differences, resulting in a project which failed to strengthen the market position of the company.

Drummond’s (1996) study of the Taurus project at the London Stock Exchange draws similar lessons. The powerful financial institutions affected by the project to automate share trading had different objectives: these differences were never resolved, and the project was beset by conflicting priorities. While many committees were created their responsibilities were unclear, with little communication between them. Structural and cultural barriers impeded communication between senior managers and operational staff. These factors undoubtedly caused delays and eventually to the spectacular failure of the project. In summary, there is evidence suggesting that the absence of the semi-structures that contributed to success at Kwik-Fit has contributed to failure elsewhere.

Another aspect of general interest is that the case deals not with a single project, but with a succession of linked developments. Major IS projects do not happen in isolation, but are typically one of many projects taking place to support a larger initiative, sometimes referred to as a programme (Boddy et al., 2005). These projects will depend on each other, and have effects throughout the organization. While senior managers need to manage individual projects well, they also need to ensure that they work together towards the aims of the overall programme. The success at Kwik-Fit appears to have lain in that managers were able to initiate a succession of IS and organizational change projects to deal with emerging contingencies, by aligning core elements of each project with each other and with the inherited alignment pattern. The dynamic nature of both information systems and organizations ensures that the close alignment, which supports high performance, is fragile and transient – an aspiration rather than a state. The use of semi-structures appears to have enabled managers to maintain a degree of coherence between successive projects with different requirements – some requiring a mechanistic approach, others an organic. The case, showing how the use of semi-structures enabled the company to attain and maintain a relatively high degree of alignment over the long term, is typical of the linked changes that many companies undertake.

A final implication of this study is that it shows how the company developed a strategically valuable core capability (see Wade and Hulland (2004) for a discussion of the Resource-Based View in relation to IS). Resources and capabilities are core (Siggelkow, 2002) when they interact with, reinforce, and so add value to, other resources. Firms develop core capabilities by discovering successful and repeatable routines for managing their resources: the harder they are to imitate, the greater their strategic value. Developing a capability to manage IS-based change in a way that adds value to the human and technological resources they consume would indeed fit this definition of a core capability. The company in this case has, by developing its use of semi-structures to manage the alignment of a succession of IS-based changes, created a strategically distinctive capability of wider application.

Further research is needed to ascertain the conditions most suited to semi-structures. In this case, IS projects were supported by semi-structures, which in some respects were embedded features of the organization, preceding the EPOS system. Research into IS and business alignment in organizations with different characteristics and in different contexts may show how widely useful the semi-structured form of organization is likely to be. In this case the nature of the business meant that major change originated from the centre. Research into the evolving alignment profile in more loosely coupled organizations would illustrate different project elements and would test the suitability of semi-structures in those conditions. Conversely, IS innovation in a tightly coupled business may find that semi-structures are less appropriate: the authors are currently conducting research in a major bank to examine this possibility.
