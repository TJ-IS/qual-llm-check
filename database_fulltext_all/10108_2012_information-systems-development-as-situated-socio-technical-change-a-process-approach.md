RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Information systems development as situated socio-technical change: a process approach

Laurie McLeod<sup>1</sup> and Bill Doolin<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Computing & Mathematical Sciences, Auckland University of Technology, New Zealand; <sup>2</sup>Business School, Auckland University of Technology, New Zealand

Correspondence: Bill Doolin, Auckland University of Technology, Private Bag 92006, Auckland 1142, New Zealand. Tel: þ 64 9 921 9999; Fax: þ 64 9 921 9876; E-mail: bill.doolin@aut.ac.nz

## Abstract

We outline a process approach for conducting qualitative research on how contemporary information systems (IS) development is enacted in practice. The approach involves a longitudinal case study to obtain rich data from which a detailed process narrative is produced. The narrative is informed by a conceptual model of IS development as situated socio-technical change, generating a theoretical explanation that highlights the temporal, emergent and contingent nature of IS development. This approach extends prior process studies of IS development by focusing on micro-level project activities, examining change as a continuous process and theorising IS change as the outcome of a dynamic trajectory of situated and socio-technical interactions. The methodological approach developed here can be used by other researchers to inform process studies of this complex organisational phenomenon.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) development continues to be a phenomenon of considerable research interest and practical importance given the significant role IS play in contemporary global, distributed and networked organisations. The IS development environment is characterised by high levels of packaged software acquisition and customisation, greater outsourcing of IS development, and an increasing emphasis on enterprise-wide and inter-organisational IS (Sawyer, 2001; Kautz et al, 2007). Combined with the fact that at the same time IS have become increasingly sophisticated and integrated, the potential for unpredictable or unintended consequences has grown. Increasingly, the traditionally assumed rational and planned nature of IS development is being challenged by perspectives that posit a more contingent and situated dimension to the IS development process (Gasson, 2006; Suchman, 2007).

In this paper, we build on such studies by presenting an approach to researching IS development that facilitates understanding and explanation of this complex and multi-dimensional phenomenon using a process perspective. Process analyses involve longitudinal, qualitative research and are concerned with developing knowledge of how and why particular outcomes in organisational change develop over time. Outcomes are analysed as the consequence of a sequence of interrelated and interdependent events or instances of social action in organisational processes. Process studies also accommodate the possibility of unintended or unanticipated events influencing outcomes (Markus & Robey, 1988;

Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). In a recent commentary, Lyytinen & Newman (2008) suggest that prior process studies of IS development are limited in their explanation of complex, socio-technical change across multiple levels. We would agree with this observation and with the need to develop a more detailed and sophisticated understanding of how contemporary IS development is enacted in conditions of increasing scale, complexity and uncertainty.

In this paper, we present a process analysis of a contemporary IS development project in a large manufacturing organisation. We draw on theories of situated action and socio-technical change to produce a narrative explanation of the IS development that occurred in the project as an emergent and less than predictable process. The paper is structured as follows. First, we discuss the use of narrative in process analysis. We then present the theoretical underpinnings of our study and the conceptual model used to frame and analyse the IS development process. In the subsequent section, we describe the case study and research methods used, before presenting our process analysis of the project studied. Finally, we discuss the process approach presented in the paper and evaluate its usefulness for qualitative research on contemporary IS development.

## A narrative approach to process analysis

Our approach to process analysis is grounded in a body of research that views processes as a fundamental characteristic of organisations (Tsoukas & Chia, 2002). Organisations are studied as verbs – that is, social processes, rather than as nouns – that is, social entities (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). Entities, events and states are ‘unpacked’ to reveal the complex processes from which they are constituted (Hernes et al, 2009). The focus is on understanding the processes by which particular effects or outcomes develop over time (Markus & Robey, 1988; Langley, 1999). Following Pettigrew (1987; 1990; 1997; Pettigrew et al, 2001), the aim is to develop an analysis that investigates multiple and interconnected levels of context, reveals the temporal interconnectedness of phenomena, examines the reciprocal relationship between context and action, acknowledges the pluralistic nature of organisational participants’ interpretations, and recognises that explanations of change are likely to be holistic and multifaceted, rather than linear and singular.

Narratives are a useful strategy in process analysis because they incorporate time as an organising device (Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999). Indeed, most process research uses narrative to some extent to make sense of process data, ranging from a relatively simple chronological description of an event sequence to the more substantial role advocated by Pentland (1999). For Pentland (1999, p. 711), narratives are ‘stories [that] help explain the relationships between events in a process’, and thus generate a meaningful explanation of what is causing a particular outcome. He cautions that overly focusing on event sequences can exclude important details of, or abstract away from, the focal actors, their social relations and narrative voices, and the evaluative context that gives events meaning for them and guides their actions – all of which are needed ‘to tell a whole story’ (Pentland, 1999, p. 721).

An emphasis on telling ‘the whole story’ lends itself to the ‘thick description’ of ethnographic research (Langley, 1999), ‘saturated with contextual and cultural overtones’ (Putnam, 1983, p. 44). The rich qualitative data produced from a researcher’s immersion in the IS development setting over an extended period can be used to explore the complex micro-level interactions of the IS change process (Langley, 1999). In the study presented in this paper, a longitudinal research methodology was used to collect such fine-grained data as the IS development project unfolded, producing a detailed local and contextual narrative.

However, in producing process narratives, it is important to avoid treating description as an end in itself (Knights, 1995; Langley, 1999). Meaningful explanations of process outcomes require more than the description of event sequences – a case study, rather than a case history (Pettigrew, 1997). Identifying the ‘generative structures’ that shape a process (Pentland, 1999, p. 722) requires a theoretical interpretation that enables knowledge and understanding of that process. That is, some theoretical apparatus is needed with which to articulate how and why a particular process outcome emerges. As Heiskanen et al (2000, p. 10) note, theory ‘supplies language from which to construct particular descriptions and themes from which to develop particular interpretations’. In the process analysis presented in this paper, we theorise IS development as situated socio-technical change.

IS development as situated socio-technical change We adopt an ‘emergent process’ perspective (Markus & Robey, 1988) to conceptualise IS development as a dynamic, multi-dimensional process, in which a development outcome emerges unpredictably from complex and reciprocal interactions between people and technology within an organisational context. As Orlikowski (1992, p. 421) notes:

How people design, interpret, and use technology y is a function of the material components comprising the artefact, the institutional context in which a technology is developed and used, and the power, knowledge, and interests of human actors.

Inherent to our conceptualisation is the assumption that IS development is both situated and socio-technical.

A situated action perspective on IS development acknowledges that the form and nature of IS development activity is interrelated with, and inseparable from, its contextual setting (Gasson, 1999). As Suchman (2007, p. 70) suggests, ‘every course of action depends in essential ways on its material and social circumstances’. She emphasises the contingent and emergent properties of action – the way that, despite the intended rationality and planned character of people’s behaviour, actions emerge out of and in response to the ‘specific, unfolding circumstances’ (Suchman, 2007, p. 27) of a particular situation. The participants in an IS development project are knowledgeable actors who routinely and reflexively monitor their actions, those of others, and social and material aspects of the local settings and practices in which (inter)action occurs (Giddens, 1984). Their actions are thus both reflective and practical (Engestrom, 2004), with effects or consequences that help constitute the social and material circumstances in which ongoing action occurs (Suchman, 2007), and thus shape future actions in an emergent process involving continuous local adaptations and change (Luna-Reyes et al, 2005). Accordingly,

Rather than attempt to abstract action away from its circumstances and represent it as a rational plan, the approach is to study how people use their circumstances to achieve intelligent action. (Suchman, 2007, p. 70)

A socio-technical perspective attempts to overcome the limitations inherent in viewing IS development either as primarily a technical, rational and controllable process to be engineered or managed (Doherty & King, 2005), or as a social process involving actors in various political, cultural or interactional roles (Robey et al, 2001). Instead, IS development is viewed as simultaneously a technological and social phenomenon (Sawyer et al, 2003), the analysis of which must address both of these aspects, as well as the reciprocal relationship between them. From a socio-technical perspective, the apparent boundaries between the technological and social aspects of an information technology (IT) artefact or IS become less clear-cut, and such entities are better conceptualised as complex socio-technical ‘ensembles’ (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001).

Figure 1 presents a conceptual model of IS development that can be used to inform a process analysis. The model synthesises our understanding of IS development as an emergent process of situated action and sociotechnical change (as introduced above and discussed in more detail below). As noted earlier, process narratives need to go beyond description to produce a meaningful explanation of process outcomes. We suggest that the conceptual model shown in Figure 1 does this by capturing the generative mechanisms that shape the IS development process. The form of the model is adapted from a sociological model of actor-system dynamics first proposed by Burns & Flam (1987) and subsequently used in research on socio-technical systems (Geels, 2004) and IS (Kim & Kaplan, 2006), although our intention is not to replicate the theory of actor-system dynamics underpinning Burns & Flam’s model.

The focus of the model is on the situated action that constitutes IS development. We suggest that IS development processes provide both opportunities and sites for situated action and interaction among the internal and external actors involved in IS development. Processes and activities typically associated with IS development range from requirements determination to the change management associated with the implementation of a new IS. They are placed at the heart of the model to reflect their central role in the enactment of IS development practice (cf. Rockart & Scott Morton, 1984).

Four interrelated and complementary dimensions of situated action are proposed, based on Leavitt’s (1964) four components of a socio-technical system. Leavitt views an organisation as a dynamic system of four interacting variables: actors (or people), task, structure and technology. Change in one component leads to organisational change through effects on one or more of the other components. Leavitt’s model is relatively simple, yet generic and inclusive, and has been widely applied (Lyytinen et al, 1996, 1998). As Lyytinen et al (1998) note, the four components can be translated into dimensions of IS development. Thus, actors are the project participants and stakeholders, including their characteristics and attributes; task refers to what and how the required development work is accomplished, including the project’s goals, resources and deliverables; structure represents the institutionalised rules and arrangements that shape people’s behaviour; and technology includes the development tools, hardware and software used to develop the IS.

While in the model the four dimensions are separated out for analytical convenience, in practice they constitute a mutually interactive, complex socio-technical ensemble. The arrows used in the figure to connect them with the centre of the model are not intended to represent mechanistic or deterministic causality, but rather to indicate their inclusion within the central field of situated socio-technical action.

The basic definitions of the four components taken from Leavitt’s (1964) model can be extended with theoretical concepts from a situated and socio-technical perspective on IS development, providing a richer vocabulary for process analysis (cf. Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). First, contemporary IS development often takes place in cross-disciplinary and temporary project-based settings, and involves the participation of a wider range of people than the traditional user, developer or manager groups. These may include software and hardware vendors, outsourced contractors, external project managers and consultants. As noted above, such participants in an IS development project are knowledgeable actors, who engage in processes of sense-making, communication and negotiation in order to construct meaning in relation to the project and develop an emergent IS solution (Galliers & Swan, 2000; Constantinides & Barrett, 2006). They act purposefully within defined roles and relationships and with differential access to material and non-material resources. Their knowledge and skills, expectations and interests, values and beliefs also shape their sense-making, decisions and actions. Second, actors’ decisions and actions are oriented towards the espoused project task in IS development, and informed by their interpretations of and action in relation to aspects of the task at hand, such as project scope and goals, size and complexity, and available resources. In addition, task is defined through the project deliverables (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008), such as specifications for the proposed IS solution.

Third, actors act and interact within the opportunities and constraints offered by structures and properties of the context in which action occurs – both the immediate organisational context and the wider socio-economic environment within which the organisation is located. These function as conditions of possibility that are drawn on and mobilised by actors to understand, rationalise and legitimate particular courses of action (Knights & Murray, 1994). In this way, the course of IS development may be shaped by formal and informal organisational structures of relations and authority, institutionalised norms and rules that constitute organisational culture, established organisational policies and practices, or industry, market and regulatory conditions (Orlikowski, 1992; Knights & Murray, 1994). The structuring conditions of situated action do not operate in a deterministic way. Rather, they form a virtual order of rules and resources that shape what is considered feasible or appropriate action in specific contexts (Giddens, 1984; Jones, 1999).

Fourth, the decisions and actions taken in IS development are mediated by ‘the actual material capabilities and practical demands of the technology in question’ (Asaro, 2000, p. 286), whether the hardware and software from which the IS solution is constructed, or the artefacts and tools produced and used in IS development. The influence of technology on IS development may be understood by using the concept of ‘affordances’ – how the material properties of a given technology shape the range of possible ways in which an actor perceives it can be used (Hutchby, 2001; Leonardi, 2011). In addition, various project artefacts and representations produced in the course of an IS development project facilitate knowledge sharing and communication between different groups of actors by offering a common reference point in the negotiation of meaning and action – in effect, functioning as ‘boundary objects’ (Gasson, 2006; Star, 2010).

More detail of what constitutes the respective dimensions of the conceptual model is provided in Table 1 (see also McLeod & MacDonell, 2011). These lists are intended to help understand the sort of phenomena that might be focused on in an analysis. What to include within the scope of an analysis or within a particular dimension is largely a matter of subjective judgement by the researcher.

A further aspect of the conceptual model is the effects that situated action has in material and non-material domains, which may be both intended and unintended (Giddens, 1984; Burns & Flam, 1987). These effects have the potential to shape future situated actions through their structuring (either by reproduction or transformation) of aspects of the four dimensions of situated action (structure, actors, task and technology). For example, the recursive nature of structuration (Giddens, 1984) allows for institutional structuring, in which the continued reproduction of practices surrounding IS development reinforces existing institutionalised structures, or the potential for change is introduced if actors choose to do otherwise (Orlikowski, 1992; Davidson & Chiasson, 2005). Some effects of situated action may reinforce aspects of the actors involved in an IS development project, including their positions and relationships, and access to resources. This actor structuring (Geels, 2004) also includes the possibility of change as actors actively evaluate the effects of decisions and actions, and learn from them. Effects, either intended or unintended, may also influence the task or technological content of a project. Task structuring can result in the definition, confirmation or change in characteristics and attributes of the project, including its scope, goals, deliverables and availability of resources. Technology structuring may confirm the selection of tools and technologies used in IS development or introduce new technology and material artefacts into the project. Actor, task and technology structuring are envisaged as operating within the timescale of an IS development project, although some structuring effects may have longer-term implications.

## Case study

AlphaCo (a pseudonym) is a large New Zealand company operating in a competitive global manufacturing and distribution environment. The IS development project studied involved the development of a database solution to replace existing financial spreadsheet models used to manage the company’s new IT outsourcing contract. The project owners were the unit responsible for managing this contract, the IS outsourcing management (ISOM)

team. Company policy and practice advocated the acquisition of packaged software rather than internal development of a solution. Organisational policies such as these constitute a set of ‘guiding principles’ intended to steer particular aspects of IS practice in the company. They are supplemented by a digital repository of documented IS processes. IS development projects are governed by an in-house IS project life cycle, administered by the IS Project Office. After an extended vendor identification and evaluation process, SoftCo (a pseudonym) was hired to supply and develop the desired database solution.

The project was intended to be a straightforward migration of existing spreadsheet models to a database solution. From the outset, it was perceived to be well defined, with no major threats to project delivery. Adequate resources for the project were available, management supported the project, the users of the existing and proposed systems were committed, and no implementation issues were expected to arise due to the project’s size or complexity. In practice, however, the project was subject to various delays and problems so that it stretched over a protracted period of time, eventually losing much of its currency as events and changes overtook it and reduced its relevance within the organisational context.

The project was followed ‘in action’ (Latour, 1987) from mid-2005 to mid-2007 by the first author. Field work involved an initial intensive 8-month period of participant observation coinciding with the main project activity, followed by a number of follow-up site visits as work on the project became more sporadic and some form of closure could be made in terms of use of the new solution. In total, 558 h were spent on-site, observing project activities and meetings or conducting 34 semistructured interviews with internal and external staff. Combining interviews with observation enabled questions to be tailored to the individual experiences of key informants (Whittle, 2005), in an iterative process of observation and verification (Pettigrew, 1990). All project documentation was made available to the researcher through the co-operation of project managers from both AlphaCo and SoftCo. A range of AlphaCo internal documents and electronic sources, as well as publicly available articles on AlphaCo and its IS function, were accessed and reviewed to provide contextual information.

A comprehensive thematic analysis was initially conducted on the data collected from field notes, interview and meeting transcripts, emails, and various project and organisational documents. Data were read and re-read multiple times, categorised and compared across common themes that emerged in the enactment of the project. This process was informed by the relevant literature on IS development. In this way, the data analysis was an emergent process involving interplay between data interpretation and theory (Putnam, 1983; Walsham, 2006). Retention of the chronological order and temporal relativity of the data throughout the analysis facilitated the subsequent identification and description of events and activities within the project.

In reviewing the findings of this initial data analysis, it became apparent that the project trajectory and outcome could not readily be reduced to a single set of contributory factors, but were the result of a number of complex, interrelated influences and (often unintended) effects. To reveal the emergent and multi-dimensional process through which this occurred, the approach outlined earlier in the paper was used to perform a process analysis of the case study data. This involved developing a detailed process narrative that not only provided a chronological description of the events and activities involved in IS development, but also an explanation of how and why the particular sequence of events and activities identified resulted in the IS development outcome observed.

The conceptual model of IS development as situated socio-technical change presented in Figure 1, and its theoretical underpinnings, were central to the develop ment of this explanation. At a meta-level, the conceptual model offered a holistic way of thinking about the IS development that occurred in the project studied as a dynamic, emergent and multi-dimensional process. At a more functional level, it focused analytical attention on particular dimensions of IS development practice, how they changed over time and the relationships between them. In doing so, it provided an explanatory mechanism for analysing the ‘moment-by-moment contingencies that constitute the conditions of situated interaction (Suchman, 2007, p. 183).

Although it is notionally possible to interpret any situated action occurring in IS development, in practice a ‘moment-by-moment’ analysis of situated action is not feasible. Instead, the researcher focuses on events and actions considered to be important in developing a process explanation. Conceptualising and identifying which events and actions to analyse is largely a matter of subjective judgement on the part of the researcher based on the case study data collected (Heiskanen et al, 2000; Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). In the analysis of the AlphaCo IS development project, data derived from the initial thematic analysis were examined and used to compile a list and graphical representation of critical events and actions, together with changes in aspects of the four socio-technical dimensions of situated action (actors, task, structure and technology), across elapsed project time. Some 21 project activities were identified that reflected significant researcher-perceived changes in the continuity and nature of the situated actions in the IS development. The decomposition of the data in this way enabled the examination of how the effects of situated action in one activity led to changes in the four sociotechnical dimensions that shaped situated action in other activities (Langley, 1999). While these activities loosely formed a temporal sequence, aspects of the analysis reflected the often non-linear and iterative nature of IS development in the project.

In this paper, we present a somewhat abridged process narrative in order to demonstrate our approach to process analysis and the theorisation of IS development as situated socio-technical change (for the full original process narrative, see McLeod, 2008). The 21 activities in the original process narrative are reduced to 10 key episodes in the project trajectory, each based around a particular set of project activities sharing a logical or natural association of tasks or actions readily distinguishable from those in adjacent episodes (Langley, 1999). In the following case narrative, we concentrate on the main socio-technical influences on situated action and the consequential effects that shaped the course of IS development in the case study.

## Episode 1: Establishing the need for change (2004)

During the first year of the new IT outsourcing contract, the organisational need for information, and hence the focus of the ISOM team, was on the ongoing evaluation and management of contract performance. A set of financial spreadsheet models had been built for contract appraisal and negotiation, but these had been developed in an ad hoc manner and had become large, complex and cumbersome, making them difficult and time consuming to update or modify. In addition, the material nature of the spreadsheet models did not afford the capacity for complex analysis, modelling and reporting. ISOM team members became increasingly frustrated with the limitations of the original spreadsheet models, and began discussing the more favourable functionality that a database technology would afford: ‘We want to make a living, breathing animal y dynamic, not static’ (ISOM analyst). In this, they were influenced by their interpretation of the changing contractual context, including the possibility of further outsourcing contracts in the future, and their understanding of an organisational guiding principle that emphasised scalability and adaptability to change: ‘Scalability y is a crucial factor right now y If we do go down that path then we have to have the tool available to do it’ (ISOM analyst). A perceived risk in relying on the tacit knowledge of the original creator of the spreadsheet models also encouraged them to consider a solution that reduced the company’s reliance on a single person.

The ISOM team problematised the need for change in such a way that migration of the original spreadsheet models to a centralised database solution with user-friendly analysis and reporting functionality was the obvious solution. In the process, team members reproduced, and thus reinforced, the guiding principle around scalability, established the nature of a desired solution, and became committed to a course of action that resulted in the initiation of a new IS development project.

Episode 2: Initiating the project (February–March 2005) The development of a new database solution was incorporated into the AlphaCo IS planning cycle and budget for the 2005 financial year, acquiring formal project status and becoming subject to AlphaCo’s IS project life cycle. ISOM team members’ perceptions of the task involved influenced how they approached initiating the new IS development project. In particular, as the project was perceived as relatively small and well defined, and was owned by an AlphaCo IS team, the usual formal process to define and scope the business problem was not strictly followed:

It’s obviously easier just to shortcut the process [laughs] y It’s like the mechanic’s car, if you know what I mean. When it’s your process y then perhaps you’re not quite as formalised as you should be. (IS architect)

A ‘very, very abbreviated’ (IS project officer) project concept document was prepared, with an indicative budget and a 6-month time frame for project completion by December 2005, in time for organisational reporting needed on the outsourcing contract anniversary in February 2006. On this basis, organisational approval was given to proceed with the project in March 2005.

Episode 3: Sourcing a project manager (April–May 2005) As it became evident that the ISOM analyst who had created the original spreadsheet models was too busy to be released to manage the project, it was decided to source an external project manager. In fact, this was AlphaCo standard practice, influenced by unsatisfactory prior experiences of training internal project managers and the company’s commitment to outsourcing non-core business functions (consistent with industry best practice). The need for an operational solution by December 2005 added further impetus to the decision. Given the nature of the desired solution and complexity of the financial models involved, the ISOM team preferred to appoint someone with financial analysis skills and experience in delivering model-based database solutions. The characterisation of the project as well defined and straightforward meant that project management expertise was considered less critical.

After a month’s delay, a suitable candidate was appointed in May 2005. His appointment introduced a new actor, with different knowledge, skills and interests, who shaped the future trajectory of the project and emergent IS solution in significant ways. In the process, organisational practices relating to outsourcing were reinforced. and the ISOM analyst referred to above re-evaluated her future role in the project as limited to ‘strategic oversight’.

Episode 4: Defining the project requirements (June 2005) The task of defining the new project requirements fell to the new external project manager. In order to develop an understanding of how the original models worked and the requirements for the new solution, he decided to review and document the existing spreadsheet models. He also met with members of the ISOM team to formally establish the purpose, scope and deliverables for the project. A packaged solution would be sought, consistent with AlphaCo’s guiding principles for software acquisition and development, which encourage ‘buy not build’ and ‘vanilla’ implementation:

We want a packaged solution that is highly configurable y That complies with our principles for a solution like this, which is something standard, not customised, off-the-shelf. (IS architect)

The perceived small size and well-defined nature of the project task meant that a Request for Information (RFI) process was considered adequate (rather than a more detailed Request for Proposal process). An RFI document was produced for distribution to potential solution vendors.

Episode 5: Creating design artefacts (June–July 2005) As the existing spreadsheet models were regarded by members of the ISOM team as too complicated for any solution developers to follow, the project manager decided to develop a simpler and rationalised prototype model (using spreadsheet technology): ‘In the format that it was in, it would’ve made absolutely no sense to them. It would have driven them mad trying to track it down’ (ISOM analyst). The project manager also extracted existing contract data into a file for uploading into an eventual database solution. It later became apparent that there had been multiple versions of the original models in circulation, and that (lacking the necessary local knowledge of which version was most current) he had extracted an out-of-date version of the data.

To ensure that the prototype was an accurate representation of the original models, he distributed it to the ISOM team to check. He received only cursory feedback and it was presumed that the prototype was correct and could substitute for the original models as the basis of future project work. However, the prototype model was incomplete in two aspects. First, a particular set of organisational reporting requirements was continually evolving, and, second, some desired functionality made possible by using database technology could not be incorporated in the prototype. Instead, the project manager produced a set of business rules for this additional functionality. The creation of the prototype model, data file and business rules reinforced the project manager’s understanding of the original models and encouraged the ISOM team to view him as a competent model user: ‘He knew it inside out’ (ISOM analyst). The introduction of these new design artefacts meant that any simplifications, assumptions, limitations or errors in them had the potential to shape development of a database solution.

## Episode 6: Finding a vendor (July–October 2005)

An organisational policy encouraging the strategic development and use of preferred suppliers meant that the RFI document and project design artefacts were distributed to six vendors of enterprise-level packaged software. Only one vendor submitted a comprehensive RFI response and undertook a product demonstration. Although relatively expensive, the product had the necessary functionality to meet project requirements. A formal evaluation exercise established it as the preferred solution and approval was requested for the project to proceed to the next phase of the IS project life cycle. However, the inadvertent discovery by the project manager of another potential vendor, SoftCo, with a product already used by an AlphaCo subsidiary, put these plans on hold.

SoftCo quickly submitted an RFI response and demonstrated their product, a multi-dimensional database and OLAP engine, called MDS (a pseudonym). The ISOM team were impressed with both the presentation and the product (including functionality, ease of use, cost, local support and task/technology fit). The SoftCo negotiator perceived AlphaCo as a major player in its industry and as an important potential client for other applications. Accordingly, he was keen to secure the project in order to establish an ongoing relationship with the company: ‘Look, this is AlphaCo. We want to get this deal’ (SoftCo representative). His enthusiasm led him to underestimate the complexity of the current project requirements and over-promise in terms of solution delivery time and cost: ‘Two weeks to develop the application itself, and you can hold us to that y We’re pretty keen’ (SoftCo representative). This gave the ISOM team the impression that building an MDS solution would be straightforward and rapid. A second formal evaluation exercise was performed, and this time the MDS product emerged as the preferred solution, mainly on the grounds of cost, since software licenses and a hardware server could be shared with the AlphaCo subsidiary. This was also consistent with an organisational guiding principle on leveraging existing technology. Organisational approval was subsequently given to proceed with this option. However, this unanticipated late emergence of a new vendor delayed the start of solution development until November 2005.

Episode 7: Negotiating development (November 2005) An initial planning meeting with SoftCo established that development was to be done by SoftCo developers on-site at AlphaCo (on a stand-alone server supplied by SoftCo), but would involve considerable input from the AlphaCo project manager and the member of the AlphaCo project team who would be a MDS solution and tool ‘super user’, in order to transfer MDS development knowledge from SoftCo to AlphaCo. Development was to be completed by mid-December 2005. Although a detailed project plan document was yet to be prepared (it was eventually approved in mid-November), approval was sought and obtained from the AlphaCo IS Project Office to commence development immediately.

Concerned about the tight project costs and time frame her company had committed to, the SoftCo project manager (who had not been involved in the earlier vendor negotiations) decided to utilise SoftCo’s most experienced developers, who were pulled off other projects. She also proposed reducing the extent of involvement of the intended AlphaCo ‘super user’ in development work, given the intensive training required to acquire expertise in building MDS applications. In this negotiation she was assisted by that person’s reluctance to take on a more technical role, a position he successfully asserted by drawing on AlphaCo’s guiding principle of ‘buy not build’:

I don’t know if I’d ever really want to be able to program y to have the skill y Is it going to be our intention to build models? $\mathrm { O r } ,$ is it to get people to build them for us? (ISOM analyst)

A compromise was negotiated, in which the AlphaCo team member would be ‘involved y as much as possible’ (SoftCo project manager), although largely in a testing role (along with the AlphaCo project manager), but would not undertake detailed development work using the MDS tool.

## Episode 8: Building the solution (November–December 2005)

Given AlphaCo’s perceptions of a well-defined and straightforward project and SoftCo’s tight project time frame and budget constraints, the SoftCo developers started building the MDS solution immediately, rather than spending time modelling and designing a solution as they typically did in other projects. The relatively late arrival of the lead developers meant that they lacked familiarity with the project specifications and, consequently, their understanding of what was required took time and occurred in an incremental and piecemeal manner. In addition, the financial models used by AlphaCo were unfamiliar to them: ‘We kind of lost the week on that y trying to get our heads around the terminology and what they actually wanted’ (SoftCo developer).

To develop their understanding and guide development, the developers relied heavily on the project design artefacts provided by AlphaCo. While these artefacts had been intended as ‘boundary objects’ – a basis for discussion and knowledge sharing between the two project teams, the tight project time frame constrained this. Instead, the developers developed their own understanding of the artefacts by interacting with them as development proceeded. Built by the external AlphaCo project manager with little input or validation from the ISOM analysts, the design artefacts were not a complete or necessarily accurate translation of the original spread sheet models, leading to partial or incorrect interpretations of what was required by the developers: ‘The problem from our end was that we’d come to an understanding, but it wasn’t quite right y when we got further down the line’ (SoftCo developer). In hindsight, the SoftCo developers believed that development would have been smoother had they had direct access to both the original spreadsheet models and their creator: ‘[The AlphaCo project manager] thought he was making it easier for us, however I believe it caused more problems’ (SoftCo developer).

As a consequence, solution development proceeded in an iterative and sometimes ad hoc manner, involving cycles of building elements of the solution, their testing by AlphaCo project team members, and subsequent amendment and rework by the SoftCo developers. The flexibility of the MDS tool afforded this approach to building the solution: ‘If we build it wrong, we can keep breaking it up and putting it back together’ (SoftCo project manager). However, development quickly fell behind schedule, and project milestones had to be revised. By the end of 2005, the expected project completion time and contractual departure date for the AlphaCo external project manager, the MDS solution lacked documentation and required further testing and data reconciliation. The iterative development approach was also reflected in the final form of the MDS solution, which grew progressively and incrementally over time in order to accommodate the emerging understanding of the solution requirements.

## Episode 9: Closing the project (January–August 2006)

Following the external project manager’s departure (together with his tacit knowledge of the design artefacts, the emerging solution and prior development decisions taken), responsibility for the project was given to the ISOM analyst intended to become the MDS solution ‘super user’. This involved uploading and reconciling historical data files in order to test the MDS solution and get it operational. This process was delayed by the need to re-create a base data file and the discovery of problems in the MDS solution that needed fixing by the SoftCo developers, now busy on other jobs. In addition, extensive checking finally revealed that errors in the original spreadsheet models (known to the original model’s creator but not the ISOM analyst in charge of the project) were preventing full reconciliation with the MDS solution. Despite these ongoing problems, organisational pressure was mounting to complete the project and demonstrate its benefits:

This is like a long sunset, isn’t it? y We’ve got to demonstrate that we have finished, we’re using it and the benefits promised are being realised. (ISOM manager)

In early April, the ISOM analyst was requested by the IS Project Office to formally close the project so that it could be capitalised within the 2005 financial year and avoid registering as a problem at the organisational level. It was not until mid-May that the ISOM analyst was satisfied that the MDS solution was working as intended, and a final ‘close-off’ project meeting with SoftCo was held in early June. His protracted role in this phase of the project further established him as the MDS solution ‘super user’.

Technological issues also delayed transfer of the completed solution to the company’s networked environment. Back in April, the ISOM analyst had explored installing the MDS solution on the shared server belonging to AlphaCo’s subsidiary. However, subsidiary staff were concerned about the impact of this and insisted on a formal change request to assess the impact on the server’s performance, something the ISOM analyst appeared not to have realised:

I thought y we could just put it on there y It seems obvious now that you should have to do something like that, to not impact the running of the server. (ISOM analyst)

A slow response from AlphaCo’s outsourced IT infrastructure support team, as well as instability and capacity problems with the server itself, meant that this change request process took 4 months to complete (instead of the usual 2 weeks). Eventually, in mid-August, a new server was purchased and the MDS solution was finally available for use within AlphaCo’s networked environment.

## Episode 10: (Not) using the MDS solution (August 2006 to mid-2007)

During the first half of 2006, a major restructuring of the AlphaCo IS functions occurred, introducing a high degree of disruption and change. The relatively small size of the ISOM database project meant that the MDS solution ‘fell off the radar when the restructuring occurred’ (ISOM manager). The restructuring and accompanying changes in senior IS management personnel (including the CIO) led to changed reporting requirements for the ISOM team, which was down-sized and given new and broader responsibilities. The restructuring preoccupied IS staff and made it difficult to identify appropriate people to attend a training session for the MDS solution, held in June 2006. The only attendees were the ISOM analyst responsible for the project and a junior analyst (who subsequently left the company).

In effect, the restructuring of the ISOM team removed an important source of organisational legitimacy for their prior reporting on the IT outsourcing contract: ‘You can’t produce a report for a team that doesn’t exist’ (ISOM analyst). Throughout the remainder of 2006 up until at least July 2007 (the end of the research period), the MDS solution was essentially unused. No analysis and reporting from it were undertaken, and it was only occasionally used by a few ISOM analysts to retrieve data for ad hoc tasks. As the ISOM analyst responsible for the project noted ironically: ‘It’s a good repository, but we could’ve done that in an Access database!' (ISOM analyst).

Nevertheless, the ISOM analyst continued to believe that the MDS solution could be of value to the company, and that the main reason it was underutilised was that it lacked visibility within AlphaCo’s senior IS management: ‘No one knows it exists, really’ (ISOM analyst). However, a June 2007 meeting that he organised to demonstrate the MDS solution and discuss its future was not attended by senior IS management. The ISOM analyst perceived this as a lack of support for the MDS solution: ‘It shows the low priority of the model’ (ISOM analyst). The changed focus and priorities of senior IS management and consequent lack of demand for the reports that the MDS solution was intended to provide meant that its use did not become institutionalised within AlphaCo. Thus, despite the eventual delivery of a technically successful MDS database, a wider ‘solution’ success (Markus & Mao, 2004) was not achieved.

## Summary

Figure 2 summarises the process narrative presented above. The visual mapping of the project trajectory (Langley, 1999) and the various socio-technical influences upon it illustrates the complexity and interrelationships between influences, events and effects that can occur in IS development practice. In particular, it highlights the multiple and multi-dimensional influences on situated action or decisions. The four socio-technical dimensions were a useful categorisation in our analysis, but others are of course possible. Further, the definition and delineation of influences and effects to particular dimensions were analytical decisions made by the researchers. For example, in Episode 3, the nature of the proposed financial database solution is shown as a task influence (on the need for project management based on specific skills and experience), rather than, say, a technological dimension of the project. In practice, however, the four dimensions are not discrete or unrelated constructs, but form an interrelated socio-technical ensemble.

In addition, the socio-technical influences we identified are dynamic rather than static. They changed and evolved over time, often in response to the effects of outcomes of earlier situated action. A useful example was the ongoing negotiation and interpretation of the ‘super user’ role for one of the ISOM analysts between Episodes 7 and 10. Alternatively, the outcomes of situated action may reinforce particular socio-technical influences. For example, the decision to ‘buy not build’ (Episode 4) both reflected and reinforced AlphaCo’s guiding principles for software acquisition and development. Similarly, the lack of use of the eventual MDS solution (Episode 10) was both a consequence and cause of its low profile in the wider organisation. The multi-dimensional nature of this eventual project outcome is shown across the four socio technical dimensions in Figure 2.

The effects of the outcomes of situated action shown in Figure 2 were both intended and unintended. For example, the decision to share software and hardware with an AlphaCo subsidiary (Episode 6) necessitated reliance on an unstable and inadequate server, producing unintended delays to the transfer of the MDS solution to the AlphaCo networked environment and the extra cost of a new server (Episode 9). This example also illustrates the way in which effects of the outcome of a situated action may not necessarily be immediate, but influence situated actions at a subsequent point in the project trajectory. The multi-dimensional and non-linear representation of IS development in Figure 2 is consistent with Pettigrew’s (1990, p. 270) argument that ‘changes have multiple causes and are to be explained more by loops than lines’.

## Discussion

The process analysis of the longitudinal case study data generated a detailed qualitative explanation of the complex and multi-dimensional process through which IS development in the project studied emerged (somewhat unpredictably) over time. It involved the production of a process narrative in which IS development is theorised as situated socio-technical change and demonstrates the utility of a narrative-based process approach for conducting qualitative research on IS development. By exploring the interconnected context, content and process of IS change over time (Pettigrew et al, 2001), the process narrative complements and extends prior process analyses of IS development in the following six ways.

First, longitudinal fieldwork facilitated the analysis of IS development as a trajectory of temporally connected situated actions (Pettigrew, 1990). The importance of time in our process analysis is reflected in the changing characterisation of aspects of the project task and content, the variable time frames over which the outcomes of particular situated actions affected future actions and decisions, how unanticipated events (such as the discovery of a new potential vendor) emerged with time and altered the expected project trajectory, and how the ‘formative context’ (Ciborra & Lanzara, 1994) within which the IS development project was initiated changed over time and thus undermined the relevance of the developed MDS solution. With regard to the latter, Pettigrew (1997, p. 339) observes that ‘shifting contexts may mean that social processes are inherently discontinuous and open-ended’.

Second, combining longitudinal research with a conceptual model of IS development as situated sociotechnical change (Figure 1) provided a holistic and multi-dimensional explanation of change (Pettigrew, 1990). The conceptual model framed our process analysis in terms of four dimensions of situated action: the characteristics and attributes of the actors involved in IS development (actors); the goals, resources and deliverables of an IS development project (task); the institutionalised rules and structures that shape and guide actors’ actions (structure); and the material and technological content of the project itself (technology). Aspects of each dimension played a role in shaping the trajectory of development at various points during the proiect studied.

Third, longitudinal data collected as the project unfolded revealed the political and negotiated nature of project tasks and activities and how actors’ interpretations shifted over time. The inclusion of multiple project participants and stakeholders in our analysis acknowledged the pluralistic nature of actors’ interests and perceptions and the need to analyse these multiple and often conflicting representations of reality (Pettigrew, 1990; Knights, 1995). For example, initial characterisations of the seemingly straightforward and well-defined project by AlphaCo staff shaped decisions that affected the subsequent project trajectory, such as forgoing a formal problem definition process. The resistance of an individual ISOM analyst to the more technical identity initially proposed for him enabled the SoftCo project manager to renegotiate the nature of development in the project so as to minimise the costs incurred by her company.

Fourth, the situated action perspective, acknowledging the inseparability of action and context in analysing process (Pettigrew, 1997), demonstrated how aspects of structure, such as institutionalised guiding principles, historical organisational practice and wider industry best practices, influenced the project-related choices and decisions around, for example, the acquisition of packaged software, the use of an external project manager and outsourced development, and the sharing of existing hardware with an AlphaCo subsidiary. The notion of structuration incorporated in our conceptual model helps us to understand how actors’ mobilisation of these structural properties and conditions during the project reproduced and reinforced the structures they represented. Such an approach represents a ‘constitutive process perspective’ (Garnsey, 1993, p. 241), in which the meaningful interactions of actors continuously maintain (and potentially change) the social systems to which they belong.

Fifth, the socio-technical perspective introduced an explicit treatment of technology to our process analysis (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), recognising its centrality in the processes and practices of IS development. The emerging solution was developed within the constraints and affordances of the material and technological content of the project. For example, the multi-dimensional OLAP capabilities of MDS afforded technological possibilities not available or poorly performed in the original Excel spreadsheet models. As a development tool, MDS also offered a high degree of flexibility in constructing and modifying the various components of a solution, accommodating the SoftCo developers’ incremental approach to development. However, a number of idiosyncratic features necessitated ‘work-arounds’ to achieve certain desired functionality. Our analysis also revealed the importance of various design artefacts created by the AlphaCo project manager as boundary objects in mediating knowledge transfer between the two project teams and forming the basis of the SoftCo developers’ understanding of the solution requirements.

However, a number of material errors and omissions in these artefacts meant that amendments to the emerging MDS solution were continually required as problems were discovered, contributing to the incremental and prolonged nature of solution development.

Finally, the process analysis also took into account the interrelatedness of the four dimensions of situated action – ‘the convergent interactions and interconnected loops among [them] y over time’ (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 270). Our conceptual model incorporates feedback loops that extended the analysis to the consequences and effects (both intended and unintended) on future situated actions (examples of which are given in the discussion of Figure 2). Thus, we explain IS development as an emergent and iterative process involving continuous local adaptations and change (Luna-Reyes et al, 2005), as actors recognise and respond to changes in the local setting and the consequences of prior decisions and actions (Galliers & Swan, 2000).

## Evaluating our approach

The methodology outlined in the paper offers a robust and insightful approach for researching IS development. Here, we evaluate our methodology in relation to other approaches for studying IS development, including other process studies, before reflecting on aspects of its use and limitations.

Lyytinen & Newman (2008) present a helpful typology that we can use to categorise the range of approaches for studying IS development. They propose a continuum of explanations of IS change from totally closed to totally open, where the terms ‘closed’ and ‘open’ refer to ‘the degree of explicit description and explanation of change that the chosen model of the IS change admits’ (p. 607). Located at the closed end of the continuum are factor models. These cross-sectional studies attempt to predict outcomes of IS change from a set of one or more contingent conditions (variables) that act upon the outcome in a causal and deterministic way (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). The precursor variables are hypothesised as necessary and sufficient conditions for a particular outcome (Markus & Robey, 1988). Much of the research on IS development to date has utilised factor studies. Part of their popularity stems from the fact that they are relatively easy to replicate and to develop prescriptions from (Sauer, 1999). While factor studies are useful for examining the correlates of change and describing such tendencies in populations, they say little about the actual process by which IS change occurs (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008).

At the completely open end of their continuum, Lyytinen & Newman (2008) place idiographic models. Based on the ‘thick description’ of ethnographic research, these involve the production of detailed contextual and historical narratives of IS change with little a priori theorising. Instead, the interpretation of IS change in organisations is informed by relevant social theory (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). Examples include the application of structuration theory (Chae & Poole, 2005), actornetwork theory (McGrath, 2002), or the work of Foucault (Doolin, 2004), to organisational IS development and implementation.

Somewhere between the closed and open ends of their continuum, Lyytinen & Newman (2008) locate the ‘middle-range’ process models that have been used by a number of IS researchers to understand how and why particular IS change outcomes develop over time. A particularly influential stream of this work is that of Newman, Robey and their colleagues (e.g. Newman & Noble, 1990; Newman & Robey, 1992; Newman & Sabherwal, 1996; Robey & Newman, 1996; Heiskanen et al, 2000). These studies analyse IS development as a sequence of episodes of relative stability in the relationship between key stakeholders (e.g. users and developers), punctuated by critical events or encounters between them that disrupt or change the nature of this equilibrium, within a context described by antecedent conditions. The authors develop process models that depict the trajectory of IS development as changes in the state of the stakeholder relationship over time. Typically involving a single dimension of IS development and one level of analysis, their level of abstraction means that they are quick to construct and can be used to graphically show their claims about IS change over time (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008).

A number of attempts have been made to develop more sophisticated process models that address the complex socio-technical interactions that occur in IS development; emphasise the role of context in shaping the development trajectory; and incorporate simultaneous events, interrelationships and feedback processes to reflect the emergent, iterative and recursive nature of IS development (Luna-Reyes et al, 2005; Kim & Pan, 2006; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). In particular, Lyytinen & Newman (2008) develop a Punctuated Socio-technical IS Change (PSIC) model that retains a basis in punctuated equilibrium and maps event sequences, but incorporates multiple levels of analysis and the influence of the organisational and wider environmental contexts. It also introduces a socio-technical perspective by using Leavitt’s (1964) model to track misalignments or ‘gaps’ between task, structure, actor and technology, which produce instability and lead to changes in state. The PSIC model extends prior IS process studies by identifying multiple sources of IS change and providing a more comprehensive theoretical vocabulary with which to explain that change. While the PSIC model increases the complexity of process studies of IS change, Lyytinen & Newman (2008) note that it is still able to offer a middle-range theory of IS change, in that it retains ‘a means to determine dynamically the boundaries of closed-boxing not available in ethnographic accounts’ (p. 609).

The process approach presented in this paper, in which we apply a process perspective to an ethnographic study of IS change, lies closer to the ‘open explanation’ end of Lyytinen & Newman’s (2008) continuum. Rather than focusing on discrete events and discontinuous change over a long time-frame, the analysis explores the incremental and evolutionary nature of IS development as a continuous process, recognising ‘the potential for change in every instant of action y [and] the way in which gradual change may be happening all the time’ (Jones & Karsten, 2008, p. 145). The process narrative is the primary means of interpretation and explanation, drawing on the rich data available from the researcher’s immersion in the IS development setting to explicate the complex socio-technical interaction constituting IS change, which otherwise ‘remains opaque’ (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008, p. 609).

Theorising IS development as situated socio-technical change informs the process narrative and enables the generation of a meaningful explanation of the process outcome. Our approach can be located with other research on IS change that complements a process analysis with social theory, such as Gasson’s (2006) genealogical analysis of IS design or the event-based actor-network analysis of Cho et al (2008). As the latter note, combining the temporal and episodic aspects of process analysis with social theory helps researchers structure and emphasise their analyses. The combination provides a higher degree of ‘closed-boxing’ not normally available in ethnographic accounts (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008), producing ‘a structured though rich and multi-dimensional understanding of the change process’ (Cho et al, 2008, p. 627).

Dividing the process narrative into a number of episodes supplied a temporal dimension that helped structure our process narrative (Pentland, 1999). It also enabled us to organise our data and analysis into ‘manageable subsets of concern’ (Cho et al, 2008, p. 627). This allowed us to focus on the actions and interactions in one episode, while also considering them in the context of the larger temporal whole or interconnectedness (Pettigrew, 1990). It also meant that we could explore the main socio-technical influences on situated action across comparative units of analysis (Langley, 1999).

There is always a tension between the level of detail presented and the need to structure a coherent and readable narrative. Identifying and defining appropriate and useful episodes became a matter of subjective judgement, consideration of alternative interpretations and explanations, and reflection on the plausibility and validity of the account we were constructing. Our emphasis on the continuity of the situated actions in the IS development meant that, rather than include or exclude particular activities from the analysis, we increased or reduced the granularity of their discussion according to their perceived effects on situated action in subsequent activities and the course of IS development in the case study. Nevertheless, creating an explanatory account necessarily foregrounds particular actions and interactions at the expense of others. Within each episode, we used our conceptual model of IS development as situated socio-technical change (see Figure 1 and

Table 1) to focus analytical attention on the four sociotechnical dimensions, how they changed over time and the relationships between them. This provided a means for filtering the large volume of rich data collected during fieldwork and for structuring the subsequent analysis and explanatory narrative produced.

While we conducted our process analysis after an initial thematic analysis (reflecting our increasing recognition of the relevance of a process perspective), the process approach described here can be used from the outset to conduct longitudinal case studies of IS development, implementation and use. In particular, the situated sociotechnical model can be employed as a sensitising device by the researcher immersed in longitudinal organisational fieldwork, providing criteria to guide data collection, analysis and presentation.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we emphasise the utility of process analysis as a qualitative approach for addressing the complexity of IS development processes as enacted in local settings. The process approach presented here highlights the nonlinear and iterative nature of IS development and offers a sophisticated, multi-dimensional and holistic analysis that avoids a narrow focus on individual dimensions of the phenomenon. IS development, at least in its contemporary form, is not necessarily a straightforward process, but emerges less than predictably over time through complex socio-technical interactions and subject to unanticipated events and unintended consequences.

The use of a longitudinal case study to produce a detailed process narrative opens up the ‘black box’ of IS development, focusing on the actual process by which development and change occurs. Informed by the conceptual model of IS development as situated sociotechnical change, our process approach offers a coherent theoretical explanation of IS development in practice. In particular, it incorporates the consideration of time necessary to reveal the dynamic and emergent nature of the IS development process. It also highlights the situatedness of IS development activities, in which the reciprocal relationship between context and action introduces a more contingent dimension to IS development than its traditional understanding as a planned and rational process. Finally, it emphasises the importance of not neglecting technology in analyses of IS development, both in mediating development practices and constituting technological conditions of possibility within which the emerging IS solution is developed.

The methodological approach and conceptual model presented in this paper can be used to examine IS development in small and seemingly mundane projects, revealing how even these are the outcome of complex, situated and contingent socio-technical processes. IS researchers can use the process approach developed here to analyse the processes occurring in other longitu dinal case studies of IS development, providing the opportunity to develop holistic explanations between comparative cases (Pettigrew, 1997) and build a body of knowledge about IS development in practice (Madsen et al, 2006). The process approach can also potentially be applied to understanding other situated actions related to IS use in organisations.
