# Negotiating ‘‘best practices’’ in package software implementation

Adrian Yeow <sup>\*</sup>, Siew Kien Sia

R.H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland – College Park, College Park, MD 20742, USA Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore

Received 23 February 2007; received in revised form 17 February 2007; accepted 17 July 2007

## Abstract

Package software is often marketed with the promise of ofering cutting-edge ‘‘best practices’’. However, questions remain as to how diverse groups in an organization arrive at a consensus abou what constitutes as ‘‘best practices’’ in package software and how these ‘‘best practices’’ are appropriated to the specific local contexts. In this case study, we examine the incongruence in the technological frames of the diverse groups with respect to these ‘‘best practices’’ and trace how these groups implement specific political and discursive strategies to overcome and resolve these incongruent frames. We find that it is an intricate process that demands not only that management make a concerted efort to create and actively work to coax and sustain allies, but also champion, and advocate for the rhetorical justification behind these ‘‘best practices.’’ The negotiated frames of parties are eventually inscribed into the software itself. - 2007 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Technological frame; Power and politic; Discursive strategy; Best practices; IT implementation

## 1. Introduction

Package software is often marketed with the promise of ofering cutting-edge ‘‘best practices,’’ that is, a set of proven business methods or an exemplary business scenario pre-configured within the software. Software vendors claim that the most efective way to implement such systems is through ‘‘plain vanilla’’ adoption, so that organizations can tactically ‘‘leverage the industry best practices embedded in the software’’ (Davenport, 1998; Wagner, Scott, & Galliers, 2006). However, the notion that ‘‘best practices’’ are context-free and can be readily acquired, stored, and transferable across organizations is questionable. Soh, Sia, and Tay-Yap (2000), for example, highlighted the challenges of implementing ‘‘best practice’’ ERP systems in the contexts of non-US/European, nonmanufacturing, and non-private organizations. Lucas, Walton, and Ginzberg (1988) has revealed that discrepancies between the organizations’ needs and the package software’s features will afect overall project success. Others have noted accounts of delayed implementation, overrun budgets, poor user satisfaction, and even project abandonment for large package software systems (e.g., Asbrand, 1998; Bulkeley, 1996; Liang, Xue, Boulton, & Byrd, 2004).

While these studies are insightful, they fail to take into account the non-monolithic nature of organizations. An organization often comprises numerous sub-groups, each has its own agenda in determining whether a particular package software meet its needs. Hence, the ‘‘best practices’’ embedded within package software have to be contested among the diverse groups in the organization. How do the diverse users within an organization arrive at a consensus of these ‘‘best practices’’? How are the embedded ‘‘best practices’’ in package software appropriated to the specific local context? The literature reveals few accounts of the process by which these technologically based ‘‘best practices’’ are contested and changed. In-depth understanding of how to make these packages work and the related social dynamics remains obscure (e.g., Wagner & Newell, 2005; Wagner et al., 2006). Such a deeper understanding is crucial, not only to minimize the negative consequences of implementation failures, but also to ensure that the system, once put in place, will be used. This is particularly pertinent when embracing ‘‘best practice’’ packages because such an approach requires organizations to change radically in terms of their work processes and practices.

Using a social constructivist approach to Information Systems (IS) research (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Prasad, 1993; Sahay & Robey, 1996), this paper seeks to better illuminate the process of constructing ‘‘best practices’’ in package software implementations. We argue that the notion of ‘‘best practice’’ is an artifact of social construction articulated from the perspectives of diferent technological frames and can evolve subject to negotiation among the diverse social groups. We show that this intricate process demands not only that management actively work to interest, and sustain allies, but also make a concerted efort to create, champion, and advocate for the rhetorical justification behind these ‘‘best practices’’ to ensure the package software’s success and ultimate acceptance. The negotiated needs and interests of parties are eventually ‘‘inscribed’’ or codified into the software itself.

The paper is organized as follows: First, we review and synthesize the key concepts in the theory of the social construction of technology and the related IS implementation literature. This provides the conceptual framework that served as our analytical lens for this study. Using data from an in-depth interpretive case study of a Singapore-based public agency, we studied the process through which these ‘‘best practices’’ (as manifested in the design and features of the package software) were socially shaped and constructed by the diverse user groups. Next, we describe research methodology of our in-depth case study before presenting our findings and analyses. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications and this study and conclude by ofering suggestions for future research.

## 2. Conceptual framework

To take into account the important social and organizational change aspect of package software implementation, we adopt a social constructivist perspective as our analytical lens. This perspective is grounded in Berger and Luckmann’s (1967) basic premise that all events, objects, and knowledge are socially constructed through interactions among various individual’s understanding and perceptions. Scholars from the tradition of science and technology studies have applied the social constructivist perspective to understand the process of technological innovation or SCOT (Bijker, Hughes, & Pinch, 1987). In turn, the field of IS has itself adapted key notions from the SCOT perspective to understand the process of implementing and using information technology (IT) systems within and across organizations (Orlikowski, 1992; Walsham & Sahay, 1999). Viewed from this lens, information systems and technology are endowed with social meanings, shaped by the social interactions as well as the cognitive perceptions of relevant social groups within organizations.

Adapted from SCOT as well as organizational cognitive research, Orlikowski and Gash (1994) have noted that members of organizations make sense of these IT systems and their ‘‘best practices’’ through their ‘‘technological frames.’’ They defined the concept of technological frame as the set of assumptions, expectations, and knowledge of the technology’s nature and role within the organization (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, p. 178). They also argued that because each social group within the organization has its own shared technological frame, one that is highly contextualized in its own local setting, diferent social groups within the organization inevitably will come to have diferent technological frames. These researchers have concluded that these diferent social groups, with their diferent roles and relationships, may find it dificult to share information and find a bridge across their diferent technological frames (McLoughlin, Badham, & Couchman, 2000). As such, Orlikowski and Gash (1994) posited more study is needed to better understand incongru ence in technological frames.

The technological frame approach is useful in understanding the diferent articulations of ‘‘best practices’’ for diferent social groups within organizations, particularly when they exhibit incongruence. Despite sensitizing us to the complex social interpretations, such research provides only partial insights because it ofers no guidance to the resolution processes that proceed from incongruent frames and how these resolutions become inscribed in the design and use of the IS. This process that follows from the initial frame analysis is especially salient given the radical organizational change aspect of adopting package software. Davidson (2006) review of technological frame research suggested that studies of power in organizational context (e.g., Grifith & Sobol, 2000; Markus, 1983) can serve as a complementary perspective to examine how such incongruence among technological frames can be resolved.

McLoughlin et al. (2000), Davidson (2002) and McGovern and Hicks (2004) provide examples of power and politics where organizational actors exercised power endowed by formal structure and/or by virtue of access to critical resources (e.g., funds and social ties). Specifically, these studies showed that a ‘‘dominant’’ frame emerged when members of that social group applied their organizational power in their social interactions to dominate the interpretive process.

Others such as Hardy and Phillips (1998) and Lin and Silva (2005) noted the use of discursive strategies in influencing and swaying opposing technological frames. As Bourdieu (1991) pointed out, such power is exercised through both symbolic and language means. This notion of symbolic power is well researched within the institutional change literature. Numerous articles show that discourse and rhetoric are part of the toolkit that institutional entrepreneurs use to motivate, legitimize, and enact changes in existing organizational and industry institutions (e.g., Greenwood, Hinings, & Suddaby, 2002; Heracleous & Barrett, 2001; Phillips, Lawrence, & Hardy, 2004; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). Tolbert and Zucker (1994) provided the basic concepts for the discursive approach in their ‘‘theorization’’ framework. According to this framework, change agents deploy discursive strategies to frame their proposed changes for relevant social groups, couching them in strong rhetoric. Tolbert and Zucker (1994) also posit that change agents can achieve a great level of success in enrolling these relevant social groups into their frame. They manage this by invoking the target social groups’ existing values and then aligning the organizational or work-process changes with these values. Other relevant studies show how change agents used discursive strategies to adapt and reinterpret these existing values in such a way to enhance their own positions and claims (e.g., Creed, Scully, & Austin, 2002; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005).

Together these studies suggests that organizational power is enacted along two broad approaches: one, through the exercise of formal power using traditional means of hierarchical authority and control of critical resources; two, a more subtle discursive route, one that uses existing cultural resources (e.g., appealing to the values embedded in existing frames) (Lounsbury & Glynn, 2001; Maguire, Hardy, & Lawrence, 2004). Our review suggests that we can get a stronger theoretical foundation to clarify and explicate the mechanisms and process in resolving incongruence among technological frames if we blend together the technological frame approach with formal power politics and discursive per spectives (see Fig. 1).

This theoretical foundation serves as the framework to investigate and examine the process through which ‘‘best practices’’ embedded in package software are localized and eventually inscribed into package software. The integrated view suggest that the contestation of ‘‘best practices’’ is a consequence of incongruent technological frames which are resolved through political negotiation or bargaining, and also through discourse as a means to influence each others’ frames. This perspective guides our inductive research process as it sensitizes data collection and data analysis. An important element of our work included a continuous process of iterating between the data and the framework while data was being collected. This inductive process is important because existing theories do not fully explain the phenomenon at hand (Sahay & Robey, 1996). The next section describes the methods for our in-depth interpretive case study.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research context

The research study was conducted as part of a series of case studies made for a major public organization, Singapore Public Organization (SGPO). In this particular case study, the context was an e-procurement solution (EPS) for SGPO with a base of 6000 users and an annual transaction volume of at least \$60 million. Developed on Ariba Solution, the \$15 million EPS comprised an intranet-based procurement system for decentralized purchases by SGPO units, and an Internet-based business-to-business gateway to facilitate end-to-end connectivity with suppliers and trading partners. The Ariba solution included Ariba Buyer, Sourcing, and Marketplace modules (see Appendix A for the solution over view). The EPS project was expected to slash purchasing costs by 20%, or an estimated \$3 million a year. The project was placed under the purview of the operations department (OPS), whose main role was to coordinate and leverage operational capabilities across all of the three SGPO divisions – Units A, B, and C.

With representatives from finance, procurement, and IT, a project working committee (PWC) was formed to implement the EPS. In addition to the overall project management and system development functions, the PWC also managed issues such as process reviews, change management, funding requirements, and potential contents that would reside on the EPS. As such, the PWC was organized into four functional tracks: System Capability build-up, Catalog and Content build-up, Process and Policy build-up and Buyer and Supplier Community build-up. In addition to the government staf, a dedicated team from the vendors (IBM and Ariba) were themselves part of the EPS project group. This team was in charge of providing expertise in implementation of the system and access to worldwide IBM and Ariba resources.

The EPS project group reported to the Enterprise Working Committee (EWC) and to the Enterprise Steering Committee (ESC). In turn, the ESC itself is part of a larger and more senior forum that includes the Administrative Systems Steering Committee (ASSC) and the Financial Review Committee (FRC). The ASSC was in charge of all administrative systems and policies in SGPO, while the FRC spearheaded the organization’s changes, with regards to financial policies (see Appendix B for the project-management structure). The project was formally kicked of in July 2002, with a strong mandate from PWC management to adopt the Ariba solution wholesale by following a strict no-modification pol icy. The project was completed in April 2003 and launched in October 2003.

As the project neared completion, a research team was commissioned by the ESC, as part of a study group formed to evaluate and distill key learning points from the EPS project; the authors were the main researchers of this study group. The goal of the research was to provide a comprehensive case study for SGPO, which would be incorporated as part of its internal knowledge database. Our field study took more than 11 months to complete, beginning in June 2003 and continuing into April 2004. We submitted the completed case for SGPO review. Participants from all the key groups (OPS, Users, FPD, IT) all contributed to SGPO’s review and their feedback were collated for our reference. After a few iterations, the final version was approved and included in its knowledge database in

January 2005. By that time, the EPS system had gone live and was fully implemented within SGPO.

## 3.2. Research design and data collection

We conducted an in-depth interpretive case study (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1995). The approach allowed us to gather data not only through interviews with participants intimately involved with the process, but also through written documentation that contained the stories, metaphors, and visual images used by these participants as part of the project and organizational discourse related to this particular system implementation. This approach also allowed us to engage directly with the artifact, that is, the system and to observe the system in situ, or from within the organization. We are especially interested in teasing out the cognitive frames as well as the various political and discursive moves that are both subjective and contextual. The basic premise of interpretive study is that all data are subjective, and one can attempt to study and analyze the data from the subject’s perspective (Davidson, 2002; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

Our data were collected from two main sources: First, we examined online and ofline archival data concerning the project. As a government entity, SGPO maintained a comprehensive and voluminous archive. Data included more than 300 documents, including project-proposal reports, tender specifications, contracts, internal and external meeting minutes, project reports, policy minutes, system documentation, requests for change, and promotional documents. The period covered was from 2001 to 2003, and this wide range of documents were especially useful in enabling us to understand and come to appreciate the key participants who were involved in the implementation process, as well as the sequence of actions taken by the social groups and the key events that occurred as a result.

Second, both authors were involved in interviewing the key participants of the EPS project. Altogether, we conducted 24 formal interviews with key participants across different hierarchical levels and project sub-teams. These key participants were identified from internal meeting minutes as well as from the formal project structures. Although an initial interview protocol of 31 open-ended questions was developed to focus on the key issues that arose during the course of the implementation and the attempts made to resolve them, later interviews could have other specific focus, following Glaser and Strauss’s saturation principle (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). These interviews were conducted over a three-month period, in tandem with our analysis of archival data. Table 1 lists the details of these interviews, which typically lasted from one to one-and-a-half hours.

## 3.3. Data analysis

As the data was collected, we developed an in-depth narrative of the entire implementation process (Eisenhardt & Bourgeois, 1988; Maguire et al., 2004). This narrative (a dynamic document) served as the foundation document used for arranging the vast amount of information available in the primary dataset of archival and interview data, and it allowed us to focus on the social groups, artifacts, and actions associated with them. Our analysis of the key challenges in the EPS project surfaced three ‘‘best practices’’ that had been intensively contested during implementation of EPS. Each of the three contests required features that were beyond the existing configuration options available in the package and would (or potentially) involve extensive modification to the EPS. These three contests therefore represent a theoretical sample that witnessed a situation of frame incongruence among social groups within the organization and would potentially provide insights into the process through which the frame incongru ence was resolved. We then focused on the key social groups involved and applied the technological frame analysis to explicate their respective histories, goals for technology, relevant problems that they were trying to solve, and their solution criteria (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), thus establishing an initial benchmark of the frame incongruence among the diferent social groups.

Throughout the time in which the functionalities and processes of the system were being determined, we found that the main aspects of the frames were motivations for introducing the IS technology; the goal(s) of this technology; and the success criteria used for evaluating the IS technology. These aspects map closely to Orlikowski and Gash’s (1994) ‘‘technology strategy domain which ‘‘refers to people’s views of why their organization acquired and implemented the technology . . . their understanding of the motivation or vision behind the adoption decision and its likely value to the organization’’ (p. 183), as well as Davidson’s (2002) ‘‘business value of IT’’ (p. 337). The three aspects of the frame provided a coherent dominant theme for each social group that also highlighted each group’s diferences (see Table 2).

## 4.1.1. OPS technological frame – ‘‘Eficiency’’

OPS was a new department formed in 2000. Its main purpose was to coordinate the logistics of all three units (A, B and C) and to leverage their operational capabilities to ensure more efective and eficient operations. Most of its executives came from these three units. It controlled the funding of IT systems and held the mandate over cross-unit logistics issues. Based on our analysis of the interviews and memoranda, we found the theme of eficiency was a consistent current running through this department’s motivations, goals, and criteria for success for EPS. This theme resonated with the history of the department as well, and quotations taken from the Operations and Project managers from OPS support this. In essence, they saw the implementation of EPS as an opportunity to consolidate disparate purchasing practices, inconsistent policies, and incompatible data structures across the three units. The process-streamlining, standardization, and consolidation would enable the reaping of further bulk discounts from a controlled pool of suppliers via demand aggregation. As such, OPS’ technological frame can be summed up as the ‘‘Eficiency’’ frame, and highlighted with the following quotation: ‘‘EPS must adopt the ‘best practice’ of a standardized process based on unified policies, practices and financial settlements’’ (Project Manager, OPS).

## 4.1.2. FPD technological frame – ‘‘Risk-reduction’’

As its name suggests, the Financial Policy Department (FPD) manages the financial policies for SGPO. FPD’s policies are enforced through financial directives issued to all SGPO units. Prior to the EPS project, a series of financial improprieties (e.g., instances of audit issues and maverick buys) – some of which were highly publicized in the media – had occurred and FPD was under pressure to improve the compliance of financial process among the units. As part of its strategy to enforce its financial policies to reduce the risk of future improprieties, FPD had proposed a more limited version of the EPS solution earlier. This limited proposal became the precursor of the EPS and, in a sense, paved the way for the current EPS project.

‘‘FPD knew exactly what they wanted from the start, that is, to have the checks and controls and [we] designed the EPS to have these functions. These functions were to reduce audit issues in one clean cut’’ (Manager, FPD).

As reflected in the sentiment of the above quote, the particular goal to reduce financial risk was the key motivation and goal for FPD with respect to the EPS. We therefore termed FPD’s frame as the ‘‘risk-reduction.’’

## 4.1.3. Users’ technological frame – ‘‘Management control’’

The EPS users refer to the senior and middle management of SGPO units, i.e., Unit A, Unit B, and Unit C.<sup>1</sup> These three units were not homogenous in that each one had diferent structures and requirements. Unit A had the largest number of sub-units and the largest number of staf. As a result, Unit A’s management faced a greater challenge in managing the decentralized purchases. It also sufered from a higher staf-turnover rate, and a poorer staf quality, and as a result of these two factors, it had experienced a higher number of audit and control issues historically. Units B and C, on the other hand, were relatively smaller compared with Unit A. Structurally, both units’ procurement practices were more decentralized, concentrating around the respective regional ofices. As both units’ work was more technical, they had a better-trained and professional staf.

Although they faced diferent challenges, all the units shared a common set of motivations and goals for the EPS, that is, they were motivated by the need to have stronger management control over their sub-units’ procurement budgets. Such a system was currently lacking as purchases were performed manually. In essence, EPS was to provide the management of each unit more fine-grained and more flexible management control function alities, as the quote from the manager from Unit A suggests: ‘‘We don’t want to worry about not having [a] suficient budget for even things like grass-cutting. We want EPS to set aside the money for that specific purpose. We can have peace of mind!’’ (Manager, Unit A).

Following Orlikowski and Gash’s (1994, p. 203) proposition that incongruent technological frames lead to the relative degree of dificulties and conflicts around implementing technologies we examined next the incongruence among the three technological frames identified.

## 4.2. Congruence of technological frames

In our examination of frame congruence, we used a two-step analysis. In the first step, the congruence of frames was determined by examining how the diferent aspects of the technological frames related to each other by content and structure. Orlikowski and Gash (1994) gave the example of similar expectations around the role of technology in business process as congruence of frames, while incongruence refers to important diferences in these expectations and understanding of the technology.

Using these as our basic rules of (in)congruence, we compared the three frames in pair wise fashion, that is, OPS vs. FPD, FPD vs. Users, OPS vs. Users. From this examination, we found that the FPD and the Users’ frames were congruent in that both frames focused on the audit and control issues, while, simultaneously, these two frames were significantly diferent from OPS’ frame of eficiency. Despite the congruence between the FPD and the Users’ frames, their emphases were diferent, as reflected their respective interests and roles. In FPD’s case, control is the means to the end of risk reduction, while control is the end, or desired result, from the Users’ perspective. The subtle diference subsequently emerged to explain the diferent responses between FPD and Users in contesting ‘‘best practices.’’

While this analysis of incongruence hints at the potential conflicts that can plague the implementation and design process for EPS, it is comparing each social group’s frame in abstract. Following Orlikowski and Gash’s (1994, p. 201) suggestion that the notion of incongruence and frames should reflect specific contexts, the second step in our analysis was to examine the frame congruence in relation to concrete aspects of the technological features and organizational practices. This more specific congruence analysis took into account the diferent frames and the three contested ‘‘best practices’’ (see Table 3).

Diferent issues generated incongruence in the technological frames of diferent social groups, invoking diferent intensity of ‘‘best practice’’ contestation. For example, in the case of monitoring by sub-vote, a significant incongruence between OPS’ Eficiency frame and Users’ Control frame became evident. The proposal to do away with the sub-vote structure was in line with OPS’ Eficiency frame, as it would assist in simplifying vote monitoring and integrating financial budgets across the units. Furthermore, such practices were common in commercial implementations, which fit into OPS’ eficiency criteria for success and it was noted: ‘‘We want the market to drive the changes’’ (Operations Manager, OPS). However, such a desire did not satisfy Users’ Control frame. Users’ wanted to have a finer-calibrated control mechanism that would require the addition of the sub-vote management functionality: ‘‘If we are restricted in the creation of sub-votes, we will find ourselves managing funds in a ‘black box’’’ (Unit A Users). Unfortunately, this requirement would require customization, adding to the complexity and the cost of EPS. The incongruence between OPS and Users’ technological frames becomes clear when we juxtapose their frames with respect to this specific feature and practices.

Similarly, in the case of streamlined procurement approval, OPS’ Eficiency frame called for the adoption of the more ‘‘streamlined’’ commercial e-procurement ‘‘best practice’’ to enable users to conduct end-to-end transactions. The existing approval workflow was ‘‘one step too-many’’ (citing the OPS manager) for small-value purchases, which constituted approximately 64% of total transactions. The ‘‘Express Checkout’’ concept would empower a Unit Resource Oficer (URO) to approve both purchase requirements and fund commitments, thus reducing the number of approvals required and improving eficiency and cycle time. Again, this clashed with Users’ Control frame, as they argued that this would lead to a reduction in the management’s ability to exert control on inappropriate behavior. In both cases (monitoring by sub-vote and streamlined procurement approval), FPD did not conflict with OPS as the proposed practices did not undermine its risk-reduction frame. Under its resource management policy, the risks to FPD were the same (whether or not these controls were exerted through system designs or management accountability was irrelevant to them).

However, incongruence between OPS and FPD emerged when we considered another specific set of practices and technical features, that is, the adoption of direct payment via automated three-way match. In this case, OPS believed that the elimination of manual invoice verification would improve the eficiency of the procurement system, as proven in many commercial implementations. From FPD’s perspective, however, this implementation could result in a loss of control in fund disbursement. Without the scrutiny by payment oficers, there might be higher risks of inappropriate payments. For example, some proper but less crucial purchases might flow through, thereby increasing the risks of insuficient funds later on for more important purchases. Incorporating this new practice in EPS would thus go against FPD’s risk-reduction frame.

From this more situated analysis of frame congruence with respect to each of the three contested ‘‘best practices’’, we next describe how the key groups used both political and discursive strategies to either legitimatize or resist the inscriptions of these practices in EPS.

## 4.3. Contestation among frames

## 4.3.1. Political strategies

Our analysis revealed the interesting use of political strategies in frame contestations. This was especially evident in the contesting sub-vote monitoring (see Table 4, first col umn). In this case, when Unit A saw that its coalition of opponents was being threatened by OPS’ arguments and OPS’ move to co-opt Unit B, Unit A decided to escalate the subvote issue beyond the PWC to the ESC.<sup>2</sup> Unit A had hoped to leverage the political power of ESC to negate OPS’ position in the PWC. OPS, however, had efectively co-opted Unit B prior to Unit A’s move by adopting Unit B’s vote structure as its proposed new vote template. Unit C, being smaller and also in tune with OPS’ empowerment rhetoric, had agreed not to undermine OPS’ proposal. Without the support of Unit B and Unit C, Unit A faced the uphill task of fighting a lonely battle. OPS then bargained with Unit A by ofering a soft-control version of the sub-vote – that is, the sub-vote features were not hardwired into the EPS but some visibility of expenses at the sub-vote level (through the commodity codes available in EPS) was to be provided. OPS later moved the issue of sub-vote as well as its ofer of soft-control version to the ASSC. ASSC’s endorsemen of the proposed program of action firmly established OPS’ position, giving it the necessary formal justification to push forward with its proposal.

This type of multi-level escalation and politicking was also apparent in the two other cases. In proposing the ‘‘express checkout’’ concept to streamline procurement approval, OPS had worked closely with an external party to evaluate and endorse its proposal before the FRC, which paved the way to getting an endorsement from the ASSC. OPS argued that the product/service catalogues in EPS were pre-negotiated and the need for ‘‘approval of purchase’’ (e.g., to ensure value-for-money) had been covered under the period-contracts with suppliers. OPS also suggested the role of a ‘‘watcher,’’ that is, a manager who would be notified of any approval of purchase by his executives via email. The additional ‘‘watcher’’ role gave the senior forum the impression that Users’ concerns of ‘‘losing control’’ were somewhat addressed. Similarly, in resisting the proposed payment via automated three-way matching, FPD aligned its agenda with Users and together they raised the matter to EWC to undermine OPS.

Another key aspect of being a skilled political player is the sense of timing and strategic opportunism. According to the OPS manager, he believed that one factor that enabled OPS to be successful was ‘‘choosing the right battles to fight and knowing when to fight those battles.’’

Accordingly, OPS did not champion every ‘‘best practice’’ with the same tenacity; it prioritized issues into dominant (the ‘‘must-haves’’) and subordinate (the ‘‘nice-to-haves’’) categories. For example, OPS strongly pushed for eliminating the sub-vote structure, as it was a key artifact in the legacy of the vote-management system and its removal would signify a significant shift in the overarching logic of the system. As pointed out by the FPD manager: ‘‘The sub-vote structure has the widest implications – resolving it is also symbolic to tell others that SGPO is changing the way it operates. It would be the first manifestation of the new financial paradigm.’’ Compared to the sub-vote contest, OPS considered the issue of streamlined procurement approval as relatively less crucial. Although the inscription outcome was weak (being included as just one option in the procurement workflow), it was viewed positively by OPS as an important first step.

The use of political strategies also stemmed from the formal power of specific social groups and their representatives. For example, OPS’ project manager was a key figure within SGPO. Not only was he the project manager for EPS, he was also an influential member of ESC and had good relationships with the senior management from prior engagements. As pointed out by institutional literature, the OPS project manager occupied a central position in the organizational network that conferred both formal legitimacy and the means to impose OPS’ collective voice on the other groups (Phillips et al., 2004). FPD, in turn, occupied a similar advantageous organizational position in the Direct Payment issue by virtue of its being recognized as the formal authoritative figure with respect to all payment and financial issues. Being recognized as the legitimate agent provided FPD with the formal power to influence the contestation in this last case.

## 4.3.2. Discursive strategies

While the above analysis provided us with important insights into the contestation of ‘‘best practices’’ in terms of political strategies, our data also informed us of another set of strategies that involved a series of discursive maneuvers to solidify their case. First, we observed that there was a marked diference in the pattern of arguments used, that is, proponents of change (i.e., package software ‘‘best practices’’) had a larger array of arguments targeted at more groups, while opponents of change had a relatively focused set of arguments targeted mainly at the proponent (see Table 5). OPS’ use of an array of arguments towards multiple stakeholders takes into account that there was significant heterogeneity among the target audience (Greenwood et al., 2002; Maguire et al., 2004). For example in the contest over sub-vote monitoring, OPS appealed to industry ‘‘best practices’’, risk issues and pragmatic concerns that spoke to the user group as well as FPD and members of the senior forum. This shows that even though the various social groups were from the same organization, each had a distinct set of organizational and professional cultures, as manifested in the respective technological frames. While the schisms among them are not as wide or as intense as one might find among diferent communities in a society (in institutional change), the diferences were significant in the process of contestation.

This pattern, in turn, has some tentative relationship with the outcome inscription. By assembling an array of arguments that framed problems and justified the proposed new ‘‘best practice’’ to appeal to a variety of diferent stakeholders, OPS was able to create a broad base of support for its goals and was more successful; it ‘‘won’’ the first two cases of monitoring by sub-vote structure and streamlined procurement approval. In the third case, for which the unit failed to build up a large enough base of support, it was not as successful.

In addition, we also observed the selective and strategic manner in which OPS framed and justified its arguments. It was selective in that OPS had deftly reinterpreted and combined multiple organizational issues and values, which not only enhance its positions and claims, but also de-legitimize competing claims (Creed et al., 2002; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). It was also strategic, in that OPS had ofered multiple reasons to satisfy a variety of groups, and this tactic resonated with the diferent technological frames of the diferent groups (Maguire et al., 2004; Wagner & Newell, 2004).

A key example of selective arguments is seen in the case of sub-vote monitoring. OPS couched its proposal to remove sub-vote monitoring as part of the new financial paradigm about flexibility and autonomy. This new financial paradigm was part of a new strategic vision instituted by its corporate headquarters. Instead of defensively acknowledging the limited budgeting functionality of the package software, the removal of sub-votes was pre sented as a ‘‘planned’’ organizational move to empower its members. By interpreting its ‘‘best practice’’ as part of this overall organizational vision, OPS managed to cast its opponents’ proposal in a negative light; Users were now seen as resisting empowerment and not suficiently ‘‘mature’’ in embracing the flexibility and autonomy inherent in the new finan cial paradigm.

Strategically, OPS was also conscious of its opponents’ prevailing values and concerns and made specific counter-arguments that leveraged its opponents’ technological frame. For example, OPS had studied the existing sub-vote structure in the various units and found that the assignment of sub-vote for purchases were often miscategorized in practice and, hence, open to error and misuse. Further, it argued that pushing for more comprehensive sub-votes (i.e., totaling more than 2000) introduced substantial complexity that would work against the control- and risk-concerns of Users and FPD. By using arguments that exposed the apparent contradiction between the opponents’ proposal and their own technological frame, OPS efectively de-legitimized its opponents’ claims. OPS also tai lored its arguments to the target social group’s prevailing values, specifically its technolog ical frame, making OPS arguments compelling and legitimate for adoption (Greenwood et al., 2002; Phillips et al., 2004). For example, in the case of the streamlined procurement approval, given the senior management’s intention to move towards commercial operations within the SGPO, OPS appealed to the logic of eficiency and also, the fact that most of these practices had been adopted (or not adopted) by exemplary agencies or organizations.

While we witnessed an attempt at selective and strategic use of logic and the tailoring of arguments for OPS’ target groups, the opponents tended to adhere to strictly historical logic (e.g., existing regulations, current practices) which appealed to their current technological frames, e.g., control and risk reduction. They also drew from their respective contexts and settings and used rhetoric that claimed that their organizational form occupies a unique category (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). For example, Unit A claimed that its specific context of high turnover and large size created a compelling reason for adopting the sub-vote structure. The uniqueness rhetoric, however, was challenged by OPS as they attempted to dilute the argument by demonstrating that Units B and C could live with the new proposed vote template.

From this set of analysis, we observe that the process through which the contestation of ‘‘best practices’’ resolves itself is inherently complex. Depending on the social groups involved and the context of the contest, a variety of political and discursive stratagems are employed to shape the ‘‘best practices.’’ Successful social groups must be politically savvy, in terms of understanding where and how power and authority can be gained and leveraged to one’s advantage (Maguire et al., 2004; Markus, 1983) as well as ‘‘skilled cultural operators,’’ adept at manipulating rhetoric and arguments (Lounsbury & Glynn,

2001). By engaging in the tactics of bargaining, negotiating, and an escalating of the issues with important and powerful allies, competing social groups create alliances that support their interpretation of ‘‘best practices’’ in the package software (Bloomfield, Coombs, Cooper, & Rea, 1992). These moves are also dynamic and recursive as social groups take into account each others’ previous moves. Consequently, we observed that the outcomes for each of the three contested ‘‘best practices’’ are diferent (see Table 3, last column). We discuss these outcomes in the next section.

## 4.4. Outcome of the contestation of frames

## 4.4.1. Inscriptions in technology artifacts

To understand the outcomes of the contests, we examined the inscriptions in the EPS using system documentation, observations in the field, and project notes. We found two interesting observations. First, it appears that ‘‘winning’’ the ‘‘best practice’’ contest was essential in enabling the inscription of the ‘‘winner’s’’ frame on the artifact. For example, OPS’ managed to do away with sub-vote monitoring in EPS, while FPD made sure that the existing manual review for payment was retained in completing the three-way matching. The final inscriptions in EPS embodied the ‘‘winning’’ technological frame that emerged after the contest (Bijker, 1995; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Second, and more interestingly, ‘‘wins’’ in the ‘‘best practice’’ contest may not necessary lead to a ‘‘strong’’ inscription of the frame. We found that in the second case, the ‘‘express checkout’’ for procurement approval was not made mandatory in the EPS, despite being inscribed into the technology. We refer to this type of inscription as a form of ‘‘weak’’ inscription (Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997).

In this specific case of weak inscription, the respective units of Users were free to adopt any of the standardized workflow procedures they deemed suitable – one-step, two-step, or even three-step checks (that is, diferent combinations used in segregating approval of requirements, approvals of purchase, and approvals of fund commitment). This type of inscription, therefore, allowed the technological artifact (EPS) to retain a semi-open structure that can provide for a constrained set of interpretive flexibility (Bijker, 1995). The motivation behind this type of inscription in this case was related to the need to quickly win over the users on board the EPS adoption by tailoring it to their comfort levels. EPS, as the technological artifact, was also suficiently flexible to cater to the diferent configuration settings in this regard. The observation seems to suggest a diferent negotiation dynamic when the technological artifact ofers flexibility that widens organizational options. More research is needed to explore this form of inscription in ‘‘best practice’’ contestation.

## 4.4.2. Precariousness of inscriptions

Most previous research appears to have assumed that once frames are inscribed into a technological artifact, they automatically acquire a degree of inertia and resistance to change (Bowker & Star, 1994). In our case, we found that even though the roll-out was launched with strong top-management endorsement, the project committee had to engage in actions that sustained their technological frames as embodied in these inscriptions (Jansen, 2004). One example of such actions included OPS deploying IT personnel to help units chase after suppliers for invoices in an attempt to expedite the requisition-to-payment cycle time. This was to shore up evidence that the EPS does provide the eficiency as promised in OPS’ framing of the technology. OPS also made eforts to implement a quick roll-out to all units and to continually push EPS’ adoption. These adoption rates were important to sustain the overall eficacy of the EPS and thereby justify the technological artifact as a whole. Indeed, these postimplementation actions reveal that inscriptions and frames in technological artifacts do not become ‘‘taken-for-granted’’ once the contests were over (Berg, 1997). Rather, the process before they become ‘‘taken-for-granted’’ requires ongoing eforts to sustain these inscriptions (Garud, Jain, & Kumaraswamy, 2002) by continually creating evidence in meeting the success criteria of the dominant technological frame (e.g., the expectation of a 50% reduc tion in requirement-to-payment cycle time).

## 5. Discussion

The analysis from this paper sheds light on how the process of contestation plays a key role in shaping the outcome of the package software’s ‘‘best practices’’ evolution. It shows that one key source of contestation is the incongruence of the relevant social groups’ tech nological frames. Specifically, the findings show that frame incongruence sets the stage for a series of negotiations and discourse that finally result in the constitution of localized ‘‘best practices’’ in the EPS. It highlights that implementing a ‘‘best practices’’ package involves a political process of persuasion to build a coalition of major stakeholders (Latour, 1999; McLoughlin et al., 2000). It also requires building a coherent set of rhetoric (e.g., arguments and counter-arguments) to articulate rational evaluation (Maguire et al., 2004). In the following paragraphs, we discuss the key insights derived, as well as consider its theoretical and practical implications.

## 5.1. Emergent and non-deterministic nature of contestation

While our analysis provides some clarity as to the mechanisms involved, it also shows that the process of ‘‘best practice’’ contestation does not lend itself to simple deterministic conclusions. Rather, we conclude that the eventual outcomes of each ‘‘best practice’’ contest are conditioned by the incongruence among technological frames and the political and discursive strategies employed during the process.

The dynamics are such that there is often not merely one voice, but multiple perspectives that must be heard, contested, and negotiated. To some extent, this is manifested in the vary ing strength of inscriptions of ‘‘best practices’’ in the EPS. Some embedded ‘‘best practices’’ were rejected and the package had to be modified to fit the organization’s mold of local practices (e.g., disabling direct vendor payment via automated three-way matching). Still others were weakly inscribed (e.g., streamlined approval through the ‘‘Express Checkout’’ as one of a few workflow options). The resulting EPS system was thus a mixture of embedded ‘‘best practices’’ and local design features, some of which also appear inconsistent or contradictory (e.g., tighter control of vendor payment through supervised three-way matching vs. looser control through the elimination of the sub-vote structure).

While we observe the multiplicity of voices, the process is not necessarily driven by the will of the majority. Instead, formal power politics comes into play, as exemplified in this case by the strong influence of OPS, which tilted the balance in negotiating ‘‘best practices’’.

We found that OPS was in a strong position to enact change, given its formal power (appointment as project manager) and access to critical resources by virtue of its gate keeper role (social ties, peer influence, and informational resources), and in particular, as evidenced by its ‘‘divide and conquer’’ (citing the project manager) strategy in dealing with users separately (Hardy & Phillips, 1998). The OPS project manager was also a key driver behind the political negotiations, as noted by the Finance Manager (FPD).

‘‘There were many Indian chiefs<sup>3</sup> around. People like to make things to suit themselves. But he [the OPS project manager] really drove the system. He put his foot down and got them focused. He was very forceful and would bulldoze through the initiatives if necessary’’

The final EPS system thus accommodated the interests of some organizational groups (e.g., OPS) more than those of others (e.g., Unit A). Through interactions conducted over the course of negotiation, a dominant technological frame – that is, an understanding of the key problems and the directions in which solutions should be sought through an EPS – gradually emerged. EPS was intended to be a ‘‘procurement system and NOT a financial system’’ (Project Manager, IT). The marketing slogan was ‘‘Buying Made Simple,’’ targeting ‘‘NOT the professional buyers but the big community of users.’’ Over the course of implementing the package software, the flexibility in interpreting the technological frames narrowed and reinforced itself into a dominant technological frame (largely that of OPS) which, in turn, guided the ensuing acts of inscription. Every request that came up had to be assessed against OPS’s dominant frame. Alternative frames, such as stronger financial management, were displaced.

## 5.2. Pursuit of discursive and political strategies, and opportunism

Apart from the emergent and non-deterministic nature of the process, we also observe that successful social groups did not rely solely on one type of strategy to influence the negotiations of the ‘‘best practices’’. Rather, it would seem that groups who actively pursue both sets of strategies can gain an advantage and overcome other competing social groups. In other words, we find that political and discursive strategies have mutually reinforcing efects and that potential synergies can be realized when pursuing both actions. The case of OPS and its ability to push forward the removal of sub-votes is the main example of how social groups can efectively bring both political and discursive strategies to bear.

Moreover, the decision to use either or both strategies requires the groups to have a certain sense of timing and gumption to seize key opportunities as discussed in the findings above. This finding extends the notion of ‘‘institutional bricolage’’ (Garud & Karnøe, 2003; Maguire et al., 2004), in that institutional entrepreneurs, such as OPS, not only need to take small steps to build and structure bridges to diverse groups, but also must know when and which bridges to build.

The case of ‘‘weak inscription’’ adds a unique technical insight to the idea of bricolage and timing. In this situation, the ambiguity or embedded contradiction that comes with weak inscriptions plants the seed for future transitions (Seo & Creed, 1997). In the case of streamlined procurement approval, the non-mandatory ‘‘Express Checkout’’ workflow allowed units to be exposed to more ‘‘eficient’’ process while allowing them to retain their existing workflow. According to OPS, this allowed them to lay the groundwork necessary toward removing or allaying Users’ doubts and await the opportunity to win over opposing group.

## 5.3. Implications for theory

First, our analysis adds several insights to the technological frames perspective and we hope it advances this framework in significant ways. While many of the technological frame studies have used frames to bring to the surface potential conflicts or issues that might impact IS implementation and use, we have attempted to stay closer to the social construction roots of the framework by examining such an implementation one step prior to actual IS use, and then considering how specific ‘‘best practices’’ are designed and inscribed into the IS. Consequently, unlike most frame research that concerns itself with the impact of frame incongruence on IS usage and evaluation, our emphasis has been on gaining an understanding as to how frame incongruence with respect to ‘‘best practices’’ is resolved and eventually inscribed into the IT artifact.

As a result of this subtle shift of our analytical focus, we find that the initial benchmarking of the incongruence between the frames of OPS, the Users, and FPD could paint only a broad-brush view of the potential conflict with respect to the EPS. Accordingly, we concur with Orlikowski and Gash’s (1994) suggestion that the analysis of incongruence must move closer to both the artifact and the organizational practices. By taking into account specific aspects of the IT artifact (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) (e.g., the embedded ‘‘best practice’ and the technical configurability or customizability) and juxtaposing that with the analysis of the key social groups’ frames, we were able to tease out specific instances of incongruence between the frames and the specific aspects of the EPS that were subject to contestations. We also show that in certain cases, frame incongruence, e.g., between FPD and OPS, was not problematic – as that incongruence was sometimes irrelevant as in the streamlined procure ment approval contest. In the other case of the sub-vote monitoring, we found that the apparently incongruent frames may be in fact ‘‘complementary.’’ Specifically, the removal of the sub-vote not only meant that it was more eficient (in line with OPS’s frame) but also a less complex and hence low risk process (in line with FPD’s frame). Furthermore ‘‘complementarity’’ may have been leveraged by OPS in its discursive moves.<sup>4</sup> This approach, therefore, provides a more contextualized and finer-grained approach to the analysis of frame congru ence and we believe is one potential research direction for developing the notion of incongruence (Davidson, 2006; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

Second, we have attempted to link technological frame research not only to power and politics studies in IS, but also to the discursive perspective in the institutional change lit erature. Future research could take a more integrative approach to understand how initiatives are developed, interpreted, and exploited, to corroborate or contradict the embedded organizational values that lends legitimacy to change in the context of IT implementation (Heracleous & Barrett, 2001). The interplay between political and discursive strategies could be explored further. Another research angle might be to adapt existing analytical concepts from research in the process of framing and apply that to the discourse generated during the implementation process (Benford & Snow, 2000).

Lastly, most research on frames has tended to show that significant diferences exist between the frames experienced by IT and by users. At the same time, many go on to assume, or have found, little or no incongruence among user frames. Our research shows that this might not be the case, especially in large corporate environments like multinational corporations that span not only geographical but also cultural boundaries. In such cases, it would be useful to take a more granular approach to tease out user frames in a way that reflect such organizational realities.

## 5.4. Implications for package software implementations

For our study, while we analyzed the ‘‘best practice’’ contests individually, in reality, the multiple threads of ‘‘best practice’’ issues are interwoven. Contests are not isolated ‘‘silos’’ that groups can choose to deal with independently. Instead, focal groups must pay attention when coalitions break apart, because they may converge or diverge on different issues. In other words, two groups may align on one issue and conflict with one another on others. Unit B supported the OPS proposal to eliminate the sub-vote structure, yet formed an alliance with Unit A against fully endorsing the ‘‘Express Checkout’’ concept to streamline procurement approval. Similarly, while FPD was relatively neutral regarding eliminating the sub-vote structure, it was openly antagonistic toward OPS over the ‘‘direct payment via three-way matching’’ dispute. Issues such as these reflect the fact that coalitions among the various groups were somewhat tentative and fleeting in nature. These findings illustrate that while political strategies are vital to the social construction process, these politically motivated alignments are fragile and inherently unstable, and dependent on the ongoing series of issues that surface over the course of implementation (Callon & Latour, 1981). Managing the delicate relationships requires some degree of reciprocity to sustain commitment levels. Wagner and Newell (2005) note the importance of establishing reciprocity in various contentious episodes to move projects forward. The aim is to create solutions that are perceived to be ‘‘good enough’’ for the diferent parties involved. Taking a big picture perspective, such give-and-take reciprocity not only facilitates acceptance but also develops potential goodwill that may smoothen future alliances. As observed in our case study, these political maneuvers and discursive negotiations adapt technological ‘‘best practices’’ to ‘‘good-enough-practices’’ that meet the needs at hand.

Further, organizations that hope to enforce a ‘‘no-modification’’ policy with respect to package software adoption need to realize the amount of political will and power which will be required to enroll the interests of other groups. Part of this process involves creating and championing a set of rhetorical justifications that eventually become the legitimate reasons for change. We echo the advice of Wagner and Newell (2004) that organizations jumping onto the package software bandwagon must be cautioned to not underestimate the need for political enrollment. Strategies as to which parties ought to be tasked with particular projects, whom to involve, ‘‘what battles to fight [and] when to fight these battles’’ (in the words of study participants), should be carefully considered. While the process of negotiating and enrolling interests is dynamic and emergent, greater sensitivity to the sociopolitical environment will greatly improve the chances of success in implementing ‘‘best practice’’ IT systems. This is particularly relevant to large package software system deployments across enterprise users from diverse backgrounds.

## 5.5. Limitations

This study is part of ongoing research that attempts to understand the process by which ‘‘best practices’’ in package software are negotiated among diverse groups. As an early foray into this area, and with results based on a single case study, we caution against generalizing results from this study to other contexts, such as beyond a single organization. The study also focuses on best practices with respect to package software, and hence, it is limited to this specific context. However, as noted in other similar package software studies, such as ERP systems (Wagner & Newell, 2004), the dynamics of negotiations are highly comparable. As such, we believe that there is strong validity in the majority of the observations and findings.

## 6. Conclusion

This research adds to the stream of work concerning ‘‘best practice’’ package software implementation (Soh et al., 2000; Swan, Newell, & Robertson, 1999; Wagner et al., 2006; Wagner & Newell, 2004). Using a theoretical framework built on a blend of technological frames, power and politics, and discursive perspectives, this study provides an analysis that is grounded in data derived from an interpretive case study. Our findings show that ‘‘best practices’’ are socially enacted knowledge, and they involve a dynamic process of politics and discourse to acquire new legitimacy. The study highlights the challenges inherent in infusing embedded ‘‘best practices’’ in package software into organizations, especially in ‘‘[plain] vanilla’’ package software implementations. Organizations often underestimate how great will be the need to apply pressure in changing institutional norms, and also in the building-up of political resources as a means to shaping the appropriation of the IT artifact. Thus, the success of package software implementations requires organizations to recognize and take into consideration such complex issues in the earliest stages of the process.
