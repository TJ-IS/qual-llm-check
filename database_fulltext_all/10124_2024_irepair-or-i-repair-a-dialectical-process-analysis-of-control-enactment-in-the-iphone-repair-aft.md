# IREPAIR OR I REPAIR? A DIALECTICAL PROCESS ANALYSIS OF CONTROL ENACTMENT IN THE IPHONE REPAIR AFTERMARKET <sup>1</sup>

Jan Recker Faculty of Business Administration, University of Hamburg, Hamburg, GERMANY {jan.christof.recker@uni-hamburg.de}

Roman Zeiss and Mario Mueller Cologne Institute for Information Systems, University of Cologne, Cologne, GERMANY {roman\_zeiss@hotmail.de} {mariomueller@mailbox.org}

We study how Apple and independent repair service providers used different physical, regulatory, and digital instruments to influence each other’s abilities to control the repair aftermarket of the Apple iPhone between 2007 and 2020. We show how the emergence of digital instruments for enacting control, made possible through emerging functionality for tethering, encryption, and temporary binding implemented in the iPhone itself, was shaped by and shaped the actions of Apple and the independent repair service providers, and led to a dominant tension between encrypted authorization and inscription of control through Apple and collective legal action by independent repair service providers. Our analysis provides a more nuanced understanding of control enactment by highlighting the implications of using different mediums for exercising control, and we provide a new way to understand the dialectics involved in enacting control in digital product aftermarkets. Our study provides insights that can inform the regulation of digital product aftermarkets—in particular, the ongoing debate about rights-to-repair legislation.

Keywords: Smartphone aftermarkets, control, dynamics, dialectics, repair, case study

## Introduction

Because more than three quarters of the world’s population own a smartphone (Laricchia, 2022), the aftermarket downstream from the initial smartphone purchase has grown to enormous size and importance. Aftermarket products (e.g., custom or spare parts) and services (e.g., repair, modification, and customization) now generate up to 30% of revenue and 45% of total profits for smartphone original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) such as Apple, Samsung, and Xiaomi (Dennis & Kamnil, 2003). In 2020, the smartphone repair aftermarket was valued at US\$ 185 billion, 50% of the value of the smartphone sales market, and further growth is expected (UnivDatos, 2021).

Because of the value and size of the smartphone aftermarket, it is heavily contested. On the one hand, smartphone OEMs are trying to “own” the aftermarket by, for example, conditioning the sale of their products on the purchase of proprietary aftersales products or services (Durugbo, 2020), or by retaining ownership of required spare parts and tools that they exclusively deal to licensed partners (Wagner et al., 2018). On the other hand, independent repair service providers (IRSPs) are attempting to “liberate” the aftermarket by providing alternative aftermarket offerings (e.g., repairs no longer covered by OEM warranty), producing nongenuine spare parts and tools (e.g., replacement screens or batteries), and competing with OEMs on price (Wagner et al., 2018). The smartphone aftermarket is also increasingly being impacted by regulation. For example, legislative acts such as the Extended Producer Responsibility

Scheme (OECD, 2016) and Europe’s Radio Equipment Directive (European Union, 2018) have increased the regulatory pressure on OEMs to assume more responsibility for their products beyond the point of sales. Other acts, such as the Right to Repair movement in the U.S. and Europe (Jin et al., 2023; Šajn, 2022) have attempted to enable more competition in repair service provision for smartphones.

How OEMs, on the one hand, and IRSPs, on the other hand, are attempting to take control over the provision of repair services in smartphone aftermarkets is thus an important question. Smartphones are unlike software products such as enterprise software (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012), operating systems (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013), and internet browsers (Song et al., 2018) because they also contain physical components, such as casings and mechanical and electrical parts. For example, Apple’s iPhone contains not only the App Store, which attracts complementary software applications provided by third parties (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013), but also physical components such as screens, batteries, and other parts, which attract third-party offerings for repair (e.g., screen replacements), customization (e.g., product accessories), and modification (e.g., storage expansions) that may not be authorized by the OEM (Cohen et al., 2006; Wagner et al., 2018). Moreover, as devices with both physical and digital components, smartphones provide more variety than traditional products in how their use or repair can be managed. Historically, OEMs have had difficulties maintaining access to physical products once they are sold to consumers, which is why OEMs have traditionally either tried to contractually tie the sale of products with aftermarket services such as repair and customization through warranty clauses or purchase terms and conditions or tried to retain exclusive ownership over required components needed for repair, such as spare parts, tools, and manuals (Durugbo, 2020). However, smartphones are at least partially accessible and modifiable through digital interfaces (Yoo et al., 2010), which means they can be monitored, accessed, and modified digitally long after the original point of sale.

We carried out a longitudinal case study of repair service provision in the aftermarket of the Apple iPhone. We used dialectical process analysis (e.g., Putnam et al., 2016; Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) and organizational control theory<sup>2</sup> (e.g., Cardinal et al., 2017; Ouchi, 1979) to understand how the two main competing actors operating in the aftermarket—Apple and IRSPs—conduct repair services, how these actions in turn impact the other actor’s ability to conduct repair services, and how changes made to the Apple smartphone over the course of different device releases influenced how Apple and IRSPs were able to conduct repairs.

We developed a dialectical process model of control enactment in the repair aftermarket of the Apple iPhone that conceptualizes the actions by Apple and IRSPs as a dialectical process of action and response to confront tensions between self-resourcing (by IRSPs) and authorization (by Apple), reverse engineering (by IRSPs) and inscription (by Apple), and collective public action (by IRSPs) and sanctioning (by Apple). Over time and because of each other’s actions, these dialectical processes shifted because Apple and IRSPs changed the mediums (from physical and regulatory to primarily digital instruments) through which they exercised control over repair service provision.

To theorize about these dynamics, we introduce the concept of control instrument, which qualifies how control is exercised. We show that emerging functionality made available in new releases of the Apple smartphone led to the increasing use of digital control instruments, which shifted control over the smartphone repair aftermarket in favor of the OEM and led to the emergence of a new, overarching tension between digitalized encrypted authorization and inscription by Apple and collective legal action by IRSPs. These findings have important implications for regulatory initiatives, specifically the ongoing Right to Repair legislation, which we detail in the paper.

## Background

## Control on Smartphone Repair Aftermarkets

Both OEMs and IRSPs have incentives to control the smartphone repair aftermarket (Cohen et al., 2006). Aside from their economic value, aftermarkets also offer costefficient ways to establish and retain close relationships with customers (Guajardo & Cohen, 2018). Moreover, aftermarkets have become increasingly relevant in light of increasing demand for more sustainable production and consumption (Yu et al., 2006).

The difference between OEMs and IRSPs is that OEMs typically attempt to defend “their” aftermarkets while IRSPs typically attempt to gain access and leverage over how repair services can be carried out independently. Both parties implement different tactics to achieve their goals. For example, because OEMs oversee the manufacturing and sales of smartphone devices, they typically attempt to bind aftermarket demand to their own product and service portfolios: an OEM can contractually condition the sale of a device on the purchase of its own secondary products or services (called contractual tying, Chen & Ross, 1993) or make deliberate physical product design choices (called technical tying, Durugbo, 2020) that bind a product to an OEM’s aftermarket service portfolio.

IRSPs on the other hand typically try to resist these ties—for example, by engaging in “jailbreaking” or “rooting” (Cavusoglu et al., 2020) to circumvent protection measures implemented in smartphone devices, by attempting to obtain spare parts either from OEMs or from nongenuine part manufacturers (Breitschwerdt et al., 2017), or by engaging in collective action initiatives to exert public, social, or legal pressure onto OEMs to change the way they act in the aftermarket to even out the competition. One example of such an initiative is the Right to Repair movement founded by the Repair Association (https://repair.org).

Control theory (e.g., Cardinal et al., 2017; Ouchi, 1979) provides a useful lens to study how OEMs and IRSPs attempt to influence each other’s actions in order to achieve their goals—be it to defend the aftermarket or to get access to it. Control theory originated in organizational research to understand “what” controls are available to manage an organization, “how” these controls are enacted, and “why” (Cardinal et al., 2017; Wiener et al., 2016; Wiener et al., 2019). It has since been used in many other contexts, including IS projects (e.g,. Kirsch, 1997; Wiener et al., 2016), outsourcing (e.g., Goo et al., 2009; Gregory et al., 2013), and management (Möhlmann et al., 2021; Wiener et al., 2019), but also to understand how different parties influence each other in markets for digital products such as handheld devices (Boudreau, 2012), operating systems (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018), web browsers (Song et al., 2018; Tiwana, 2015), and enterprise systems (Wareham et al., 2014).

While no study has yet examined control in the aftermarket for smartphone devices, the available literature still provides several insights relevant to the choices available to OEMs or IRSPs to exercise control. For example, existing studies on platform control have found that platform owners use codified instruments such as terms and conditions (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Wareham et al., 2014), license agreements (Croitor & Benlian, 2019; Karhu et al., 2018), execution rules (Eaton et al., 2015; Song et al., 2018), and promotion regulations (Rietveld et al., 2019) to influence how third-party providers (e.g., software development companies or complementors) act according to their interests. Moreover, digital instruments (e.g., Cram et al., 2022; Stark & Pais, 2020), such as online selection and approval processes (Thies et al., 2018; Tiwana, 2015), APIs and SDKs (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018), technically encoded rules (Stark & Pais, 2020), and online ratings and reviews (Kornberger et al., 2017), are also ways in which control can be carried out.

Yet these insights alone do not sufficiently explain what control choices OEMs and IRSPs have available in smartphone aftermarkets, let alone how they enact these choices. First, for the most part, the available studies have ignored any dynamics in enacting control (Wiener et al., 2016), which are important to understand because not all choices in enacting control are always equally effective (Gregory et al., 2013). Second, the relationship between OEMs and IRSPs is one of conflict whereas the relationship between the product owner and third-party providers in platform markets for other digital products, such as enterprise systems, operating software, and browsers, is typically one of complementarity, where all parties benefit from some level of access and openness (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018). Third, smartphone devices also contain physical components, which are typically developed, owned, and protected by an OEM and are generally not as openly available for modification, customization, and repair as software products.

In turn, we study control in smartphone repair aftermarkets with a focus on the dynamics in enacting control to evaluate not only the different choices of control but also how these choices are implemented in practice. Following Wiener et al. (2019), we start by building a vocabulary that allows us to study “how” different choices of controls (control modes signaling “what” a control is) are enacted by OEMs or IRSPs, given their different intentions (“why”) to defend their position in the aftermarket (OEMs) or to gain access to it (IRSPs).

Choices of control can be distinguished in informal and formal modes (Jaworski, 1988; Ouchi, 1979; Wiener et al., 2016). Whereas informal modes such as clan control or selfcontrol involve unwritten rules such as traditions or cultural values as choices to influence implicit determinants of another party’s behavior (e.g., through ceremonies and rituals, or through empowerment and autonomy, see Jaworski, 1988; Wiener et al., 2016), formal control modes—that is, input, process, and output control (Jaworski,

1988; Ouchi, 1979; Wiener et al., $2 0 1 6 ) ^ { 3 } –$ —are explicitly codified, traditionally as official written procedures and rules (Cardinal et al., 2004) or encoded into algorithms (Cram et al., 2022), and manifest as explicit prescriptive acts enacted by one party to influence another party’s behavior according to their goals (Ouchi, 1979).

## Control Enactment and the Choice of Different Instruments

Formal control modes give us a basic vocabulary for identifying what choices OEMs and IRSPs have available to influence each other’s actions in the smartphone repair aftermarket. To examine how these different choices can be implemented and exercised by OEMs or IRSPs, we introduce the concept of a control instrument<sup>4</sup> to qualify the salient medium through which control over smartphone repair is exercised by one party to influence another. Distinguishing different instruments for enacting control is thus a decisive step forward to extend the literature on IS control, which has so far theorized about “how” control is enacted by focusing on control style (authoritative vs. enabling manner of implementation, see Adler & Borys, 1996) and control congruence (communicational vs. evaluational, see Wiener et al., 2016).

We make this move because the medium of a class action lawsuit about supply chain contracts is qualitatively different in instrumentation and effectiveness from producing nongenuine spare parts for smartphone repair. Both mediums allow one party to exercise control over the other, but their difference in instrumentation or effectiveness cannot be captured in terms of style or congruence. A class action lawsuit is a discretionary act that provides a legally binding outcome through a slow process, whereas a nongenuine spare part can provide a temporary and immediate outcome—with potential repercussions (e.g., a lawsuit because of copyright infringement).

To qualify different instrument choices for enacting control, we define physical instruments as physical mediums, such as spare parts, tools, and other material elements, that can be used to exercise control on the basis of their spatiotemporal properties, such as the location and availability of spare parts or the physical connection made between components through gluing, screwing, or welding (Wagner et al., 2018).

We define regulatory instruments as codified mediums that can be used to exercise control based on institutional rights, such as legal clauses (e.g., intellectual property rights, warranty clauses), policies and formal conventions (e.g., terms and conditions, no-tampering guidelines, developer rules), and legitimized collective actions (e.g., petitions, written requests) (Durugbo, 2020).

Finally, we define digital instruments as algorithmically organized mediums embodied in information and communication technology that can be used to exercise control based on rules enforced through software and data, such as an algorithm contained in a smartphone or a dedicated software application (Cram et al., 2022; Yu, 2015).

We make the distinctions between different control instruments because physical, codified, and digital mediums are qualitatively different. Physical mediums are bounded by spatiotemporal properties such as location, volume, and mass. Codified mediums require interpretation and enforcement by an institutional body. Digital mediums are accessible, modular, and reprogrammable. In turn, smartphone devices (which are both a traditional physical product and a digital device and are also subject to codified laws, policies, and regulations) allow different ways to exercise control. In other words, not only can traditional physical and regulatory control instruments, such as technical and contractual ties, carry out control, but digital instruments can do so as well through embedded connected technological components (e.g., firmware, applications, sensors, processing units).

This is important because the different control instruments available for enacting control differ in aspects such as availability (e.g., physical instruments require spatiotemporal access, whereas digital instruments are accessible through an internet connection), binding (e.g., physical instruments such as component soldering are permanent, whereas digital instruments such as software sanctions may be temporary because they are loosely coupled), and temporality (e.g., physical and regulatory instruments are effected discretionarily but digital instruments can be effected dynamically because they are malleable). In turn, the range and configuration of the portfolio of controls (Wiener et al., 2016) available for enactment in the smartphone repair aftermarket is considerably more varied and expansive than in other settings.

## A Dialectical Process Perspective on Control Enactment

At present, evidence regarding the dynamics and consequences of the enactment of control is available only from settings such as project escalation (Mähring & Keil, 2008), outsourcing (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Heiskanen et al., 2008), offshoring (Gregory et al., 2013), and systems development and implementation (Kirsch, 2004). None of the available studies have examined smartphone devices or repair aftermarkets or studied choices in instruments for control enactment for OEMs vs. IRSPs.

We decided to proceed inductively to build a new conceptual understanding of the dynamics of control enactment in smartphone repair aftermarkets from the ground up. A dialectical process perspective provides two main advantages to that end (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). First, it focuses on how control enactment emerges, develops, changes, and terminates over time (Langley et al., 2013) and which events trigger changes in the enactment of control over time (Wiener et al., 2016). This allows us to pay attention to the changes in instruments through which control is put into practice in smartphone repair aftermarkets, a perspective that has so far not been examined.

Second, this perspective focuses on explaining change that happens through tensions that exist between opposing entities that represent contradictory yet interrelated elements (Farjoun, 2010; Karjaleinen et al., 2019; Lindgren et al., 2021; Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2021) and how these tensions produce progress in the form of temporary resolutions as constructed responses (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). This perspective recognizes that tensions exist between OEMs and IRSPs that, instead of being resolved by reaching an eventual final synthesis, may in fact not be resolved; instead, progress might be achieved through continued, dynamic, and interdependent responses to each other’s actions, leading at best to temporary states of equilibrium (Mumby, 2005; Smith & Lewis, 2011).

Finally, a dialectical process perspective also recognizes that IRSPs have at least partial autonomy (Benson, 1977): they can influence the behavior of an OEM. For example, wellknown terms such as “jailbreaking” or “rooting” (Cavusoglu et al., 2020) describe incidents where IRSPs have resisted and circumvented protection measures implemented in smartphones or other handheld devices through technical workarounds, which in turn has prompted OEMs such as Apple to rollback and update some of their products to make them compatible with third-party spare parts. Likewise, the successful collective action movements and class action lawsuits of the independent repair communities also demonstrate that IRSPs can, in fact, at least partially influence OEMs (Gonzales et al., 2023).

## Method

## Design and Setting

We engaged in a form of grounded theorizing (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Urquhart, 2013) based on a longitudinal single-case study of repair service provision for Apple’s iPhone. We collected data as representative facts (Sarker et al., 2018) about the control-relevant operations of the different repair actors operating in the iPhone repair aftermarket over a period of 13 years since the introduction of the first iPhone in 2007. Our data analysis strategy was inductive and employed coding strategies such as temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) and open, selective, and theoretical coding (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Organizational control theory (Cardinal et al., 2004; Ouchi, 1979) was our primary sensitizing lens to support the iterative interpretation process of concepts and categories generated through our analytical process (Urquhart et al., 2010).

We chose the iPhone repair aftermarket setting for three main reasons: First, smartphones are the most widely used digital devices in the world (von Abrams, 2020). The iPhone is one of the leading smartphones, next to devices produced by Huawei and Samsung (Laricchia, 2022), and the repair aftermarket for iPhones is considered the largest and most vibrant (Slinn, 2018). Second, regular device releases and operating system updates for the iPhone between 2007 and 2020 create sufficient variance in both the physical and digital components of the smartphone device. iPhones have also been the focus of several lawsuits and collective action movements, thereby also yielding variance in regulatory instruments (such as laws, terms and conditions, and contract clauses, but also petitions and lawsuits) that could be examined. Third, we wanted our theoretical contributions to extend the existing knowledge on control and therefore deliberately chose a case setting that has been previously analyzed (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013).

In the iPhone repair aftermarket, two main actors can be differentiated: The first actor is Apple, the OEM, who provides repair services through either Apple’s technical support staff working in Apple Stores or mail-in repair service centers or through licensed partners—that is, Apple-certified repair service providers that have exclusive access to Apple’s training, repair manuals, software, and original spare parts and tools. These partners must meet certain standards defined by Apple that cover business (e.g., financial stability), operational (e.g., certified personnel), and shop requirements (e.g., interior design) and they are monitored by Apple against a set of performance metrics including, for instance, repair turnaround time and parts per repair. For our analysis, we regard these licensed partners as acting on behalf of Apple, with little salient degree in individual autonomy.

The second actor is IRSPs, which are OEM-unauthorized third-party repair service providers, ranging from small suburban mom-and-pop shops to multimillion-dollar franchise enterprises. IRSPs rely on aftermarket repair parts manufactured independently, which include extracted or refurbished components from used iPhones as well as copied nonoriginal components. IRSPs self-organize in online communities, both curated (e.g., iFixit) and noncurated (e.g., YouTube and Facebook), which provide information such as repair guidelines and ways to source repair parts and tools.

## Data Collection

We collected data in three waves from both primary and secondary sources in Germany to establish a rich picture of the iPhone repair aftermarket (Table 1). Our first wave of data collection started in late 2018, with the aim of immersing ourselves in the case setting. We observed and interviewed IRSPs (both independent repairers and resellers of repair parts and tools). Through semi-structured interviews, we explored their business models, their selfimage, and their understanding of independent aftermarket services. We observed and documented their daily operations, including the sourcing and distribution of repair parts and tools, and attended several repairs of broken iPhone devices. The empirical evidence gained in this wave helped us contextualize and better understand the specificities of the iPhone repair aftermarket but was primarily anecdotal and largely represented the informants experiences over the last two to three years.

In the second wave in mid-2019, we followed a more structured, longitudinal data collection approach, with the aim of tracing the historical outline of the repair aftermarket since the release of the first iPhone in 2007. We established this longitudinal perspective primarily through online data gathered from tech bloggers and vloggers. We identified over 200 relevant entries from the blog aggregator Techmeme by querying their curated database with the search string “(‘Apple’ OR ‘iPhone’) AND ‘repair.’” By snowballing references and blog authors, we collected and transcribed more than 20 hours of YouTube vlog entries from the repair community commenting on events that transpired in the iPhone repair aftermarket.<sup>5</sup> Where possible, we drilled down to the origin of the entries to verify their credibility and evaluated their temporal occurrence. The sources range from press releases and public policies announced by the aftermarket actors to legal documents to leaked internal files and whistleblower information from current or past Apple employees.

At the end of the second data collection wave, we felt better informed about the events that represented critical incidents in the historical development of the aftermarket. Yet while we felt like we now understood the evolution of the iPhone repair aftermarket through the eyes of the tech community, press, regulators, and IRSPs, we did not sufficiently understand whether IRSPs had acted independently or instead reacted as a response to actions taken by Apple. Also, we realized that up to this point, our data sources were biased toward IRSPs and lacked the perspective of the OEM.

Thus, in our third wave of data collection, from the end of 2019 until October 2020, we engaged in more field visits and interviews with IRSPs to discuss the main salient events occurring in the iPhone repair aftermarket that we had identified at that point. We also collected more than five additional hours of blog and vlog material. Additionally, we interviewed Apple service managers operating in certified partner stores to (dis)confirm our understanding of the data based on IRSP interviews, observations, and the longitudinal data, and we had discussions with senior management staff at Apple Europe responsible for the management and operations of repair services. If our understanding based on IRSP data was challenged by the information received from the OEM, we returned to the IRSPs and our collected secondary data to probe their perspective further. This back-and-forth between data collection, different sources, and analysis over time helped us generate a holistic and contextualized understanding of the iPhone repair aftermarket.

## Data Analysis

To analyze our data, we followed established guidelines for grounded process theorizing (e.g., Berends & Deken, 2021; Langley, 1999) and qualitative, inductive research (Charmaz, 2006; Gioia et al., 2013; Urquhart et al., 2010). Our aim was to understand the dynamics of how control enactment unfolded in the iPhone repair aftermarket over time, over the course of manifold changes to the device, its software, and the surrounding contracts and laws, and considering the actions carried out by OEM and IRSPs. We started with temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) to identify the chronology of the main salient events that occurred in the iPhone repair aftermarket. We constructed a timeline of the most salient critical incidents (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) that occurred in the iPhone repair aftermarket between 2007 and 2020. Figure 1 visualizes the release dates of new iPhone devices and displays the main incidents that occurred in the aftermarket over that period based on our data, distinguishing between incidents related to actions taken by the OEM (shaded white), by IRSPs (shaded black), and by regulatory entities (shaded grey), particularly legal institutions and law enforcement agencies.<sup>6</sup> This chronology of incidents served as our construction of the empirical mystery (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007) we sought to explain: To what extent can the different events be considered responses by one party to actions carried by the other party? To what extent did new device releases influence the progression of these events? How were regulatory actions initiated and what effect did they have?

Our data analysis, which progressed in tandem with data collection, was organized around two main critical incidents, namely repair events and control events. These were where “the rubber met the road” about how repairs were carried out and how this was influenced by actions taken by the two main parties. We defined repair events as time-stamped actions performed by an actor that aimed to reinstitute deficient functionality in the iPhone, such as the restoration of a deficient touch functionality through the replacement of a broken display panel. Control events were any time-stamped actions performed by one party that intentionally or unintentionally constrained or expanded the options for repair actions available to the other party. Examples of control events include the development of new calibration software for repair (by the OEM) and public petition initiatives demanding changes to copyright law (by IRSPs).

This distinction allowed us to relate events to control events so that we could understand how the action of one actor constrained or expanded the range of actions available for the other actor, who then responded, either by accommodating the constraint or by resisting it through workarounds, both of which would manifest in repair events occurring after the original events. For example, the OEM’s decision to solder the connectors of the first iPhone battery to the motherboard constrained the set of options available for battery replacement. IRSPs could either accommodate this constraint by foregoing battery replacements or resist it through laborious soldering.

## Findings

We start by describing three main dialectical processes that we uncovered in the actions between the OEM and the IRSPs. For each process, we present case evidence to illustrate how Apple and IRSPs engaged in a dialectical process of action and responses in which they varied their use of physical, regulatory, and digital control instruments. Evidence from blog and vlog data provided are referenced as {web reference ID}.

## The Tension Between Self-Resourcing and Authorization

One salient dialectical process that we observed in the actions of Apple and IRSPs can be construed as a tension between self-resourcing (by IRSPs) and authorization (by Apple). Figure 3 visualizes the dialectical process sequence between Apple (top row) and IRSPs (bottom row) over time. It also indicates which instruments were primarily used to exercise control: actions that feature regulatory instruments, are underlined, physically instrumented actions are in italics, and digitally instrumented actions are in bold.

Initially, Apple authorized access to relevant repair resources through regulatory instruments, typically in the form of legal agreements with component suppliers about its intellectual property and thus the corresponding repair parts, tools, and guidelines {B1}. As a result, Apple’s original parts, tools, and guidelines de facto were not available to independent parties until 2022.

Since the release of the first iPhone and in response to the regulatory access authorization by Apple, IRSPs engaged in self-resourcing actions to gain access to alternative repair parts, tools, and guidelines {B2}. A pioneering act in 2007 was the development of iFixit, an online community providing access to aftermarket repair parts and tools as well as a wikibased knowledge platform:

We attempted to fix some other [devices] but had trouble finding parts. So we bought a broken [device] on eBay and stole parts from it. Then we decided to start selling the parts ourselves, and iFixit was born. ... We wrote some [more] instructions the first chance we got. And we posted them online, for free. (excerpt from iFixit’s “About Us” statement, 2009)

After each release of a new iPhone generation, iFixit published repair guidelines and started distributing relevant repair parts and tools shortly after. The guidelines were created from so-called “teardowns,” where each newly released iPhone was dissected piece by piece {B3, B4, B5}. Over time, iFixit evolved into a wiki-based platform with guidelines resulting from teardowns and communitycurated, collective knowledge creation through trial and error. Apple responded in 2015 by removing the software from its app store.

With increasing sales of iPhone devices over the course of ensuing product generations, the independent repair aftermarket also grew. Third-party distributors of repair parts, tools, and guidelines emerged {B6}. While, initially, repair parts were predominantly refurbished components from broken iPhone devices, manufacturers started to produce and sell copy components, such as batteries or displays. This brought more options to the independent repair aftermarket but also created new challenges from a legal, quality, and reliability perspective {B7, V1, V2, V3}.

Apple responded by developing and deploying new control instruments. Between 2007 and 2017, Apple primarily perused regulatory instruments. Specifically, they enforced legal actions through law enforcement agencies such as the U.S. Customs and Border Protection {B8, V4, V5, V6}. Around 2013, Apple also began using digital instruments to gain more immediate and selective control over its repair parts, tools, and guidelines. Central to this digital instrumentation of control was the development and implementation of Apple’s Global Service Exchange (GSX), a web-based repair and order management software tool that provides Apple partner stores with access to repair diagnostics, and calibration software, and original repair parts {B9, V7, V8}. GSX can only be accessed with a valid Apple Connect ID that only certified repair technicians possess:

[The Apple Connect ID] works the same way as an Apple ID, but repairers can use it to log into other programs, such as GSX. GSX gives repairers direct access to Apple's systems and databases. They can retrieve various data via the iPhone’s serial number and also order spare parts via it. GSX also ensures that removed components are returned. ... So, they have more possibilities than we do. (IRSP 7, 2020)

In response to this digital way of authorizing access, some IRSPs initially tried to illegally acquire an Apple Connect ID. But as Apple noticed these illegal actions, they refined and tightened their digital instrument by adding encryption measures such that the Apple Connect ID remained necessary but insufficient to gain access to original repair parts, tools, and guidelines:

[Now,] you need a computer [with installed certificates], the computer needs internet access via a whitelisted IP, you need software, you need a special cable, and you also need to open a [repair request] in the service console [(i.e., GSX)] at Apple [with your Apple Connect ID]. ... Apple wants to make sure that only the people who are allowed to do it do so. (Apple Partner 4, 2020)

In response, most IRSPs diverted to freeware software tools that provided a partially effective yet incomplete alternative to inaccessible original repair tools {V9}. It mimicked some of the relevant repair functionality, such as the algorithmic pairing of aftermarket repair parts with the repaired original device, that otherwise would only be available via GSX.

A lot of things we can work around because there is freeware … but you can’t do everything right away. And that is the big problem. With the [iPhone] 11, for example, we still don’t have a solution, and it’s been on the market for almost half a year. ... [For a display repair], you definitely have to go through the Apple Connect ID. (IRSP 7, 2020)

## The Tension between Reverse Engineering and Inscription

A second dialectical process that we observed in the actions of Apple and IRSPs can be construed as a tension between reverse engineering (by IRSPs) and inscription (by Apple). Figure 4 visualizes this dialectical process sequence, in the same manner as before.

Traditionally, choices in the physical design of the smartphone architecture inscribed permissible ways in which the device could be repaired. For the first iPhone, for instance, Apple decided to solder the battery wire to the motherboard {B10}. This design rendered battery replacement challenging for anyone without appropriate soldering skills and tools. Over time, further device design choices allowed Apple to exert physically instrumented control over repair in similar ways, such as when Apple glued antenna connectors to the motherboard {B11}, displays to the case {B12}, or when they soldered two motherboard layers together {B13}. For example, the glued display improved the device’s water and dust protection, yet also complicated its body access (Apple Partner 4, 2020). Of particular note is a design choice made in Generation 4 of the iPhone that fixed the rear panel to the body by a special, nonstandard pentalobular screw {B14, B15}. This design choice rendered repair procedures for IRSPs cumbersome because the required screwdriver was unavailable to them:

This screw head is new to us. In fact, there isn’t a single reputable supplier that sells exactly the same screwdrivers Apple’s technicians use—which is Apple’s point. They picked an obscure head that no one would have. ({B16}, 2011)

In response to these ways of physically instrumenting control through inscription, IRSPs originally engaged in reverse engineering the device through teardowns to better understand the repair procedures inscribed into the physical device design and to identify the tools necessary to perform repair. Once understood, it did not take long until third-party repair tools appeared in the iPhone repair aftermarket, such as glue-dissolving heat beds and special screwdrivers for accessing the iPhone device {B16}.

Since limitations in the physical instrumentation of control became increasingly palpable as IRSPs found ways to respond to them, Apple moved to develop and use digital instruments for inscribing repair procedures into the components of the device. The first key manifestation of this form of instrumentation surfaced with the iOS 8 firmware update in September 2014. This update disabled iPhone devices whose home buttons, which physically carried a fingerprint sensor, had been replaced through independent repair {B23, V10}. Apple’s design choice to algorithmically pair the fingerprint sensor via encrypted serialization to the device’s motherboard meant that any replacement of the sensor-containing home button required an additional software recalibration step.

We protect fingerprint data using a secure enclave [i.e., encrypted motherboard storage], which is uniquely paired to the touch ID sensor. When an iPhone is serviced by [Apple] for changes that affect the touch ID sensor, the pairing is re-validated. This check ensures the device and the iOS features related to touch ID remain secure. ... When an iPhone is serviced by an [IRSP], faulty screens or other invalid components that affect the touch ID sensor could cause the check to fail if the pairing cannot be validated. (Apple, 2016 {B26})

Figure 4. Dialectic Sequence of Actions Relating to Reverse Engineering and Inscription

Thereafter, digitally serialized algorithmic pairing and calibration of physical product components became more prevalent. For instance, iOS 11 {B30} and iOS 11.3 {B31, B32, V11, V12} both caused touch input failures on uncalibrated repair aftermarket displays. The update to iOS 11.1 {B33} disabled two display functionalities on devices with uncalibrated displays {B34}. While the touch input failures were resolved by Apple in subsequent patches {B35}, only one of the two disabled display functionalities was reenabled through an iOS update {B36} when IRSPs started using regulatory instruments, in particular lawsuits against copyright law (in 2014) and petition bids for the right to smartphone repair (in 2018).

Unlike tamper-resistant encrypted serialization of securityrelevant device components, IRSPs responded to unencrypted serialization instruments inscribed into the iPhone device through digital instruments of their own. They developed freeware software tools for conducting repairs that included algorithms for the recalibration of microchips that held a nonencrypted serialization number. For example, when an original component, such as a display, needed to be replaced, the procedure required IRSPs to use a freeware software calibration tool to extract the unencrypted serial number from the original component and rewrite it to the replacement component {B37, B38, V13}.

In response to these actions taken by IRSPs, since 2017, Apple began increasingly implementing encrypted serialization algorithms, which were tamper-resistant against the IRSP’s freeware. Specifically, Apple began pairing parts of the front sensor assembly (iPhone X) {B33}, battery (iPhone XS) {B39, B40, V15, V16}, display (iPhone 11) {B41, B42, B43}, and parts of the rear camera assembly (iPhone 12) {B44} to the device’s motherboard {V17, V18}. The IRSPs in turn responded by increasing their collection action movements (i.e., a regulatory instrument)—for example, in the form of the Right to Repair movement that formally launched in the U.S. in 2016 and in Europe in 2020 with the aim to petition for new bills granting an independent right to repairs.

As Apple started to digitally control repair procedures by inscribing algorithmic recalibration as a necessary repair step into digital components (firmware and operating system) of the iPhone device, recalibration software thus became a relevant repair tool whose access could be regulated digitally (through the GSX software). Through this interdependent, digitally instrumented enactment of control over both authorization and inscription, Apple established an effective way to monitor and enable authorized forms of repair only.

## The Tension Between Sanctioning and Collective Public Action

The third dialectical process we observed in the actions of Apple and IRSPs can be construed as a tension between sanctioning (by Apple) and collective legal action (by IRSPs). Figure 5 visualizes this dialectical process sequence, in the same manner as before.

![](/api/attachments/HNGXS9WK/fulltext/images/cdc64a37ddd9a0f7083d1d772b2f60a9582610f8ef6f775123bbe508ce73c55e.jpg)  
Figure 5. Dialectic Sequence of Actions Relating to Sanctioning and Collective Public Action

Initially, Apple used regulatory instruments to sanction repairs performed by IRSPs. Specifically, they contractually denied repair under warranty for devices previously serviced by IRSPs {B45}. Over time, the scope of this regulatory instrumentation of control diminished step-by-step as IRSPs began to resist this form of instrumentation by initiating collective action movements to build public pressure. Of note here was the formal launch of the Repair Association in 2013, an organization that started to collect and register public dissent over copyright and trademark law. Their actions eventually led to the signing of new laws that yielded more freedom and competition in smartphone repair. In response to the law amendments (for example in February 2017), Apple excluded screen replacements from the list of third-party repairs that would void the device’s warranty {B46}; followed by an exclusion of third-party battery replacements in March 2019 {B47}.

As their own regulatory instruments to sanction repairs increasingly proved ineffective, Apple began implementing digital instruments to monitor repairs of iPhone devices. In 2014, as part of an iOS update, the firmware started checking the integrity of all iPhone device components and was programmed to algorithmically sanction a restored and functional device in a positive or negative way.

This “digital sanctioning” manifested first with the iOS 8 update that caused “error 53” {B23}. In this case, the check carried out as part of the iOS update installation disabled those iPhone devices that contained uncalibrated home buttons. As a response, IRSPs publicly protested this authoritative enactment of control through negative media coverage {B24, B25} and a class action lawsuit {B27}. In response, Apple released a software patch that reenabled disabled iPhone devices {B28, B29}. Moving forward, Apple then modified the way it digitally instrumented sanctions, such that only selected functionalities would be disabled as an outcome of the update software check, such as touch input or automatic display adjustments while the device itself remained at least partially usable.

Currently, we have the problem with the iPhone 11 that you cannot perform a display replacement. If you do [without recalibration], you’ll get the message “your display has been replaced by an unauthorized repair shop.” (IRSP 7, 2020)

The digital instead of regulatory instrumentation of sanctioning allowed Apple to reconsider, when needed, whether it would constrain or expand the options for IRSPs to conduct repairs. Expansion was typically the outcome when IRSPs successfully used regulatory instruments of their own, as in the collective public action they launched in the “error 53” case. Similarly, in the case of the touch input issues caused by iOS 11 and iOS 11.3, Apple initially constrained IRSPs repair options as it disabled nonoriginal repair aftermarket displays, but then re-expanded options for independent repairs by fixing both issues in subsequent iOS updates after registered petitions and bad press accumulated online {B35, B36}. Using a digitally instrumented form of sanctioning thus provided Apple with a way to temporally bind any sanctions they wished to carry out.

The increasing use of digital instruments for sanctioning also meant that Apple could algorithmically audit the compliance of device components and repair procedures via integrity checks contained in iOS firmware update rollouts. As long as the required hardware to store and execute the algorithms was included in the iPhone devices, these checks could be remotely switched on and off. For instance, the encrypted chip holding the battery’s serial number was already present in the device architectures of previous iPhone generations {V14}; however, the corresponding algorithmic sanctioning— through push notifications informing end users about nonoriginal battery replacements—was only enacted with the release of the iPhone XS {B41}.

Apple always has control and an overview of what, when, where, by whom, and why was installed in which devices. ... After everything is reassembled, timeconsuming final diagnoses have to be performed: Whether the touch still works, whether the buttons all still work and things like that. (Apple Partner 2, 2020)

This way of compliance auditing repair procedures and device components also informed the physical design of new iPhone devices. They started to include new microchips that could digitally pair additional components to the motherboard. Similarly, they informed the adaptation and design of the set of repair parts, tools, and guidelines to improve the effectiveness of access authorization.

## A Dialectical Process Model of Control Enactment on the iPhone Repair Aftermarket

We summarize and formalize the insights we gained through our dialectical process analysis of how control has been carried out in the iPhone repair aftermarket in the form of the theoretical model shown in Figure 6. Our model conceptualizes the enactment of control in the iPhone repair aftermarket as an evolutionary dialectical process triggered and continuously guided by conflicting objectives (to gain access to the repair aftermarket vs. to defend the repair aftermarket) and manifesting in three main tensions between Apple and IRSPs over how repairs are conducted (between authorization and self-resourcing, inscription and reverse engineering, and sanctioning and collective public action). Over time, because of the tensions that manifested, the actions taken by the different parties (involving regulatory or physical instruments primarily), and the device changes that were implemented in new smartphone releases, this dialectical process evolved: device changes that led to the emergence of tethering, encryption, and temporal binding functionality brought about changes in the instruments that could be used to exercise control (from primarily physical and regulatory to primarily digital). In response to this interdependent process between actions surrounding the three main tensions and the device changes that new smartphone releases brought about, the original dialectic tensions subsided because the control instruments focal to the original tensions were replaced with digital instruments. Instead, a new dominant tension emerged that now revolves around the use of encrypted authorization and inscription instruments used by Apple and collectively organized legal action pursued by IRSPs.

The model in Figure 6 also visualizes a qualitative difference in the emergent dominant tension because unlike the original dialectic tensions, the now dominant use of digital instruments for the enactment of control invokes reinforcing mechanisms (compliance auditing and resource-architecture binding) that create interdependencies between the digital authorization and inscription of control that, in turn, strengthen the entire portfolio of control (Ouchi, 1979; Wiener et al., 2016) that is available to Apple but not IRSPs. We detail the main conjectures of the model in the following.

## Digital Control Instruments Shift How Control is Enacted

The first conjecture of our model is that the emerging availability and use of digital instruments for exercising control is both a consequence of and a reason for the dialectical actions and responses undertaken by Apple and IRSPs with regard to the instruments originally available to influence how repair services in the smartphone aftermarket could be carried out (e.g., physical instruments such as resource authorization or component inscription, and regulatory instruments such as lawsuits, media, and petitions).

As tethering, encryption, and temporary binding functionality over time was implemented directly in the iPhone device, Apple gained more effective instruments to carry out the authorization, inscription, and sanctioning of iPhone repairs, which in turn rendered the traditional ways in which IRSPs exercised control (through self-resourcing, reverse engineering, and collective public action) less effective because the device’s functionality allowed Apple to increasingly and exclusively monitor and intervene remotely third-party device repairs. Apple gained more capacity to impose and enforce control over the direction of events.

This shift in the dialectics is visible throughout all the main tensions: IRSPs originally performed self-resourcing actions of the tools and parts they needed, to which Apple eventually responded through the use of digitally orchestrated access authorization for focal resources instead of continued reliance on contractual agreements with suppliers and the legal enforcement of intellectual property rights. Digitally orchestrated identification, authorization, and calibration proved to be more tamper resistant and allowed for deferred and dynamic ratifications of compliance audits and sanctions, which in turn substantially changed how IRSPs could respond. For example, refurbishing or copying physical repair parts and tools, which had previously exploited the remotely unobservable properties of the iPhone device in the repair procedure (e.g., using a nonoriginal, remanufactured pentalobular screwdriver), became remotely observable once each device and repair component received a digital serial number analyzable through Apple’s GSX software suite.

Similarly, the algorithmically encrypted inscription of repair into iPhone device components rendered the repair actions traditionally available to IRSPs (i.e., reverse engineering) less effective; in contrast, the digital instrumentation of repair by

Apple became more tamper resistant than their previous physical instrumentation efforts. While a special screw that impeded access to the device (physical inscription) could eventually be self-resourced by IRSPs (e.g., by making copies), digital instrumentation through algorithmically encrypted inscription became more challenging to resist, forcing IRSPs to also develop digital instruments (freeware calibration software) rather than remanufacture spare parts.

Finally, the ratification of aftermarket repairs through enforcement of contractual clauses (e.g., excluding devices from warranty that had previously been repaired by IRSPs) also changed as IRSPs began to raise public awareness about anticompetitive sanctions through collective public action. While these regulatory instruments remain available to IRSPs to this day, the increasing use of digital instruments for sanctioning allows Apple to constantly audit device compliance and temporarily bind these audits to their desired level of ratification (e.g., by algorithmically enabling or disabling functionality). These possibilities for controlling repair outcomes are more dynamic, effective, and immediate and can be more easily modified or reversed, compared to the slow, complex, and uncertain use of regulatory instruments such as class action lawsuits. The remote monitoring capabilities of the iPhone device enable Apple to observe and ratify repair outcomes in near-real time, at lower cost, in spatiotemporally decoupled ways, and in a more adaptable format. Its remote intervention capabilities allow Apple to situationally adjust the enactment of control based on actions or responses by IRSPs. For instance, Apple can quickly remove their disablement of product functionalities in the event of public backlash.

Together, these shifts in the dialectical process between Apple and IRSPs that can be traced back to the use of digital instruments for effectively enacting control replaced the three original tensions with one dominant tension—the resistance to encrypted authorization and inscription by Apple through collective legal actions instigated by IRSPs. The most prominent manifestation of these efforts is the current international attempts to implement Right to Repair legislation (Breuninger & Feiner, 2021; Gonzales et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2023).

## Digital Devices Become a Focal Object for the Enactment of Control

A second key conjecture of our model is that in the enactment of control over the repair aftermarket, the device itself becomes the focal object of instrumentation, rather than surrounding material (e.g., spare parts) or legal documents (e.g., terms and conditions). While control enactment over products has always involved a portfolio of modes (Ouchi, 1979; Wiener et al., 2016), the key point in our setting is that the digital instrumentation of control modes can be exercised by OEMs directly through their products, an option that is not available in equivalent ways for IRSPs.

For example, a smartphone device’s addressability and connectivity provide OEMs with the ability to remotely sense and connect to their product. This is important because OEMs can leverage remote monitoring functionality to exercise control while IRSPs cannot. Likewise, because digital product components are reprogrammable, OEMs can leverage remote intervention functionality for exercising control. OEMs can audit and interfere in repair services without needing to be spatiotemporally present.

## Using Digital Instruments for Control Enactment Reinforces the Portfolio of Control for OEMs

The third conjecture of our model is therefore that remote monitoring and remote intervention bolster the effectiveness of OEM actions to control the repair service aftermarket. Control has traditionally been exercised through physical and regulatory instruments for authorizing access to key resources (e.g., repair parts, tools, and guidelines). Through digital instruments, key repair resources can be digitally tethered. The addressability of actors (e.g., algorithmic identification of authorized repairers via Apple Connect ID) and resources (e.g., digitized serialization of repair parts and tools) via webbased services (e.g., Apple’s GSX software) allows Apple to engage in selective resource access authorization in near real time. Moreover, while tethering renders access authorization more responsive to independent repairs, it also allows Apple to improve the quality of authorized repairs. In Apple’s iPhone repair aftermarket, for instance, any authorized repair runs through an automated final software check that captures and forwards relevant repair information to Apple, allowing them to learn from past repairs.

Likewise, through digital instruments, the inscription of repair rules into a product architecture can be encrypted. Traditionally, OEMs have inscribed rules and procedures physically into device components (e.g., by soldering battery connectors to the motherboard). But when components are digitally addressable (e.g., through algorithmic serialization), they provide OEMs with more effective possibilities to remotely intervene in repairs by inscribing nonidentifiable and temper-resistant rules and procedures (e.g., by enforcing an algorithmic recalibration step at the end of repair) into the device, which allows OEMs to counter the typical way in which IRSPs have carried out repairs (through reverse engineering).

Finally, contractually ratifying device repairs through warranty clauses or law enforcement has historically relied on manual enforcement, such as the through visual inspection of any device before a repair to check for unauthorized tampering. But the addressability and connectivity of repair parts and tools (e.g., display serialization) and the reprogrammability of key software components of the device (e.g., the firmware) now allow OEMs to monitor any device change (e.g., unauthorized aftermarket display replacement) digitally at any time and to intervene even remotely (e.g., by flashing on-screen notifications about nonoriginal aftermarket displays). The increasing use of digital instruments thus provides OEMs with the ability to temporarily bind—or unbind—sanctions to ratifications of repair, for example through firmware updates that selectively enable or disable functionality.

Because of the increased use of digital instruments for control based on tethering, encryption, and temporary binding functionalities, the entire portfolio of control available to OEMs (but not IRSPs) can be reinforced. Specifically, resource-architecture binding couples repair procedures algorithmically to the provision of focal repair resources and vice versa because inscribing algorithmic recalibration rules into the digitized parts of a device restricts which repair parts or tools can be used to modify the device. This informs decisions about which repair resources are relevant to control in the first place. Further, both repair resources and procedures can be digitally linked with sanctions because digital instruments allow compliance auditing: The (non) conformance with prescribed repair procedures and authorized access to resources can now be evaluated and ratified at any time (both dynamically and deferred) after a repair is finalized.

## Discussion

## Contributions to Research

Our research makes two main contributions to the literature on control. Our first contribution is the development of the concept of a control instrument to qualify the concrete mediums through which control is enacted. This conceptual move is important because it allows for a more nuanced analysis of the different ways in which control can be enacted. The literature has thus far focused mainly on selected individual modes of control in an overall portfolio configuration of possible choices. For example, several studies have investigated how platform owners use contractual or digital gatekeeping mechanisms to regulate which complements are allowed (e.g., Croitor & Benlian, 2019; Thies et al., 2018; Tiwana, 2015). Others have studied controls such as APIs and SDKs (Song et al., 2018), promotion schemes (e.g., Rietveld et al., 2019), and the use of online ratings for product evaluations (e.g., Kornberger et al., 2017). When examining these choices through the lens of the instrument that is in use, we notice that promotion schemes are regulatory instruments while APIs or online ratings are digital instruments. These choices are not equal. Promotion schemes are legally binding contracts that require stipulation and discrete enforcement to take effect. APIs or even online ratings are algorithmically organized digital mediums that can themselves be dynamically altered or enforced in near real time.

The focus on control instruments also highlights that by varying the form of control enactment from physical and regulatory to primarily digital instruments, the role of different controls changes in the entire portfolio. Our model shows that both Apple and IRSPs have continued to exercise different controls—from authorization to inscription to sanctioning, for example—but in decisively distinct ways and with varying effectiveness, depending on the instruments used to enact them. Moreover, our analysis suggests that the increasing use of digital instruments for enacting controls such as authorization and inscription bolsters their effectiveness and, in turn, reinforces the entire portfolio of controls—but only for those that can benefit from the digital instruments, that is, the OEM and its licensed partners.

A second main contribution is that we provide a detailed chronological account of how different instruments for control are enacted over time between two opposing entities and how, as a consequence of dialectic actions, one party (Apple) was able to “tighten” its control of the repair aftermarket. The increasing use of digital instruments to enact authorization and inscription has further allowed Apple to couple resources and repairs with sanctions they wish to execute. Through resourcearchitecture binding and compliance auditing, Apple can remotely monitor and ratify actions taken in the smartphone aftermarket in dynamic and temporally binding ways.

These new empirical and theoretical insights substantially expand the scarce available evidence about the dynamics of control enactment (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Gregory et al., 2013; Heiskanen et al., 2008; Wiener et al., 2016). Our setting of the repair aftermarket for smartphone devices is important in this regard because the tensions that exist here are different in this “terrain” (Kellogg et al., 2020) from other settings. The two main parties pursue conflicting rather than complementary goals, which is different from, for example, platform ecosystems, where owners and complementors both try to balance flexibility and stability (Tiwana, 2015). This means that the dialectical process often plays out in a constructive manner (Benson, 1977) toward an optimal balance between innovative creativity and control (Karhu et al., 2018). In smartphone repair aftermarkets, however, the relationship between the actors is characterized by conflict over access to focal resources such as devices, spare parts, processes, and tools. Here, the digital instruments chosen for enacting control, while somewhat similar in type to those used elsewhere (e.g., Cram et al., 2022; Hoofnagle et al., 2019; Stark & Pais, 2020), lead to a different and contested terrain (Kellogg et al., 2020): They are used to enforce and wield power over resources rather than to grant access to them or increase their flexibility. The move here is away from openness, not toward it.

These findings identify some important constraints that must be imposed on other conclusions that have been drawn about control. For example, in their study of Apple’s iOS service system, Eaton et al. (2015) suggested that “the power dynamics among actors with different power is balanced by the unique material characteristics [e.g., reprogrammability] of digital technology” (p. 239). They argued that the reprogrammability of the iPhone device should result in a balanced power landscape between Apple and IRSPs. We cannot confirm this prediction. In recent years, Apple has increasingly relied on encrypted microchips mounted on device components (e.g., the battery) that hold important serialization data necessary for successful component replacement. While the chip is in fact reprogrammable, the ensuing functionality only benefits those that are algorithmically authorized, which excludes IRSPs.

## Informing the Debate about Digital Product Aftermarket Regulation

The main practical implication of our research concerns the ability to inform ongoing debates about the regulation of digital product repair—in particular, the discussions surrounding Right to Repair legislation in Europe and the U.S. (e.g., Gonzales et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2023). Our findings suggest that the increasing use of digital instruments for enacting control, which are available primarily to OEMs, can in fact spawn self-reinforcing and discriminatory control regimes that, if OEMs pursue exclusionary intentions, may harm the competition. Our analysis shows that digitally orchestrated remote monitoring and intervention functionalities in smartphone devices qualitatively change how OEMs can potentially enact restraining activities, which become not only more tamper-resistant and targeted but can also be carried out with less effort and cost. Such functionalities are not restricted to smartphones—appliances, vehicles, wearables, and other digital products can feature them as well.

These developments have important implications for antitrust investigations. First, restraining activities can no longer only be enacted during initial digital product design but also during use (and repair). In traditional antitrust investigations, regulators primarily search evidence for so-called contractual or technical tying arrangements in which the OEM would condition the sale of a product on the purchase of a complementary product or service (Holzweber, 2018; Yu, 2015). Our findings show that OEMs can conduct restraining activities via remote intervention long after initial product design. Second, restraining activities can be enacted dynamically. In contrast to regulators’ traditional, static tying arrangements, technical ties in digital products can vary over time and are less deterministic. Our findings imply that through digital functionality for remote monitoring and intervention, OEMs can essentially manage important product interfaces either in an exclusionary or integrative manner. This choice equips OEMs with unparalleled bargaining power over third-party providers and end users (Hoofnagle et al. 2019). The implication is thus that regulation must find ways to govern not necessarily control modes but rather the instruments through which these can be enacted.

This insight is relevant and timely in light of the current plans to establish legal “rights to repair” in Europe and the U.S. (Breuninger & Feiner, 2021; Šajn, 2022). Both legal directives focus effectively on altering digital product repair resource authorization: They stipulate making spare parts and tools (such as displays, batteries, and cameras for smartphones) more widely available and legal to use for repair. One example is the new European law enforcing the use of standard product components such as USB-C interfaces (Hern, 2022).

But while these legislations will make resources required for digital product repair more available to IRSPs, OEMs like Apple may still retain their digital instruments to monitor and sanction digital product repair. For example, to complete an iPhone repair independently or through self-service, Apple’s recalibration software is still required, affording Apple possibilities for tethering and, in consequence, remote monitoring and intervention. Thus, while some instruments (specifically, physical resources and to some extent contractual options) relevant to repair will become more widely available in the aftermarket, the increasingly important digital instrumentation of control will likely remain firmly vested with OEMs, which will continue to ensure that they have tight control over digital product repair. To illustrate: none of the current legislation drafts even mention authorization or calibration software—meaning their use for smartphone repair will remain exclusive to Apple and its partners.

Finally, our analysis carries implications for how OEMs can use digitally instrumented control capabilities for competitive advantage. For instance, encrypted inscriptions in digitalized products through tamper-resistant software can both be beneficial to competitive markets and detrimental to monopolized markets. The difference lies in deciding which product components to encrypt and whether to use the tamperresistant encryption as a control instrument. For example, on the operating system or application level, the encryption of security-related software functionality can protect against harmful intellectual property attacks. However, the encryption of firmware interfaces may also result in anticompetitive moves, such as the restriction of interoperable repair parts and the inhibition of possible repair or modification options. Likewise, the tamper-resistant encryption of software rules and sanctions in response to noncompliance are interrelated yet distinct practices. For instance, encryption in combination with digital remote monitoring and intervention capabilities could help tackle fraudulent behavior that might occur in deregulated aftermarkets. Again, the onus will be on regulators to identify nuanced and targeted ways to balance security with the right to repair.

## Limitations

Two boundary conditions stem from our choice of setting. The first is our choice of the repair aftermarket for smartphone devices. As we explain throughout the paper, smartphone devices are different from other digital products where control enactment has been previously studied. Since choices and instruments for control, as well as their use and effectiveness, will be different in other settings, our empirical insights cannot readily be generalized to other smartphone devices or other digitalized products, such as appliances, wearables, and cars. Different digital products are associated with different design choices and have different functionality and meaning (Hylving & Schultze, 2020; Wang et al., 2022). The extent to which changes in the architecture, design, or meaning of these kinds of digital products influence control enactment in the repair aftermarkets they sustain remains an empirical question. However, our new concept of a control instrument can be used to generalize analytically across types of markets (e.g., platform ecosystems) and products (e.g., software instead of devices).

A second boundary condition flows from technological design choices germane to the smartphone we examined. The device architectures of smartphones produced by Google, Samsung, and Huawei, in particular their firmware and interfaces, are different from Apple’s. Their devices often contain more open interfaces and some parts of their firmware are open source. Still, the trajectory we analyzed may be similar in these settings. For example, Samsung also recently started to use serialized displays (Shahid, 2021).

Our qualitative research design also has limitations. We attempted to report our procedures, data, and abstraction in a transparent way, but other interpretations and analyses remain possible. For example, we were selective in the controls we focused on (we examined formal but not informal controls). To enable readers to trial different interpretations and analyses, we provide key iterative outcomes of our analysis openly.

Our main source of data was interviews, some of which were retrospective. This strategy is prone to interviewee bias, recency bias, and selection bias, which could impact data accuracy. By using a variety of data sources (archival documents and vlogs) and by focusing on key events that are publicly traceable, we tried to mitigate these biases. We also exposed our data and analyses repeatedly to different viewpoints held by IRSPs and Apple. Finally, our analysis was focused on how control was enacted in the iPhone repair aftermarket. It should not be conflated with a causal explanation for why control was exercised in the reported way.

## Conclusion

More and more everyday products are digitalized, providing additional value for end users but also changing how such products can be controlled after sales. Smartphone devices, as one of the first and most widely used digital products, offer a way to gain insights into how the future of product and market control might play out. The insights we glean from how competition has played out in the iPhone repair aftermarket offer a new conceptual way of thinking about the instrumentation of control over similar digitalized products, be they medical devices, printers, cars, or home systems. We hope that these insights will be useful for understanding how such products and markets can be governed or regulated in the future.

## Analytical Procedures

In the first stage of data analysis, we openly coded repair and control events by distinguishing actions (e.g., disassembly, replacement, reassembly), actors (e.g., OEM, IRSPs, aftermarket suppliers), and focal object (e.g., smartphone devices, repair parts, repair tools). For example, we noted that IRSPs frequently faced repair challenges, such as unclear defect causes or inaccessible components, it prompted them to invent workarounds, such as testing single traces on the printed circuit board with a multimeter, to identify the defect’s cause and proceed with the repair. IRSPs frequently framed these occasions as barriers induced by external actions of actors, such as the OEM declining to provide open documentation, which we coded as a relevant control event. In that spirit, we browsed the entire timeline of data to identify all salient control events that palpably influenced how repairs could be carried out. For example, we coded documented petition initiatives instigated by IRSPs (such as those demanding changes to trademark or copyright law) and documented class action lawsuits (e.g., against IRSPs for trademark violation) and the enforcement of laws (e.g., by U.S. customs). These events were important because they triggered changes to how future repair events would be carried out.

In the second stage of data analysis, we identified the dialectical process sequences in which these events featured. We constructed these sequences to describe how the two main actors—OEM and IRSPs—engaged in interdependent actions in response to each other’s actions and to understand how these actions either expanded or constrained the actions possible for the other.

To that end, we temporally and logically related all identified repair and control actions that intentionally or unintentionally influenced how the other party conducted repairs in terms of the effects they produced on the other party, and linked these to any response actions taken by the controlled actor that in turn influenced the controller (Ouchi, 1979). We grouped events into a sequence if the actions and responses revolved around one common theme, which we denoted as the central dialectic tension. For example, we traced how, in the shift from iPhone 7 to Generation 8, Apple decided to feature soldered battery connectors in the device (a form of physical inscription), which prompted IRSPs to acquire soldering stations (a form of self-resourcing). In that vein, we identified three main dialectic tensions that we used to group logically and temporally related events into dialectical process sequences.

We then used theoretical coding to analyze in more detail how control was enacted in these sequences. As a theoretical lens, we used our new concept control instrument to differentiate physical, regulatory, and algorithmic mediums used in the exercise of control. The different control instruments earned their way into data collection and analysis (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) because the notion of a control instrument emerged as central to understanding the unfolding of the dialectic processes. Finally, for each dialectical process sequence, we constructed a visual timeline from 2007 to 2020 (Figures 3-5).

In the third stage of data analysis, we then engaged in selective coding (Urquhart et al., 2010) to identify a main narrative that would serve as our explanation for the dialectical processes that became evident in our data. We settled on understanding how the evolution of the iPhone device itself influenced the enactment of control and the tensions that manifested over time. This focus was important to us because as a technical, manufactured device, sovereignty over the device functionality (and accompanying tools) rests primarily with the OEM who thus possesses more agency to influence the existing tensions and dialectical processes involving IRSPs.

We proceeded in two steps. First, we theoretically sensitized our analysis of the data by studying the literature on key characteristics of digital product components (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo, 2010; Yoo et al., 2010), such as addressability, homogeneity, reprogrammability, and self-reference. Of these, during our data analysis, three characteristics emerged as relevant to the enactment of control in the iPhone repair aftermarket. Addressability, the unique identification of digitalized artifacts in a computing infrastructure (Yoo, 2010) manifested, for instance, when Apple digitally marked physical device components through a unique digitally encoded serial number stored on a flash chip located on the physical component itself. Connectivity, a digital artifact’s ability to link multiple, sometimes spatiotemporally dispersed, social or technical entities via information transmission infrastructure (Leonardi & Treem, 2020), manifested, for instance, every time when Apple released a new iOS update downloadable to all devices via internet connection. Finally, reprogrammability, a digital artifact’s ability to be “accessible and modifiable by a [software program] other than the one governing [its] own behavior” (Kallinikos et al., 2013, p. 359), manifested, for instance, in iOS releases that updated firmware over the air. These three digital device characteristics yielded functionality to the OEM that became increasingly relevant to the way the dialectical process unfolded between Apple and IRSPs: On the one hand, addressability along with connectivity provided Apple with the ability to audit iPhone use without the necessity of being spatiotemporally present (remote monitoring). On the other hand, connectivity along with reprogrammability provided Apple with the ability to interfere with iPhone use without the necessity of being spatiotemporally present (remote intervention).

Second, we started to “theorize the arrows” (Langley, 1999), that is, we searched our data and emerging concepts for connections between device changes that yielded functionality relevant to control, and the ways different control instruments were used by the main parties. We first zoomed into the different dialectical process sequences, to understand how the occurrence of device changes related to control instruments manifesting in temporally neighboring and logically connected events. Zooming in allowed us to carve out three ways in which device changes yielded digital functionality relevant to the enactment of control, namely tethering (the digital pairing and access authorization of access to focal device or repair resources), encryption (the tamper-resistant digital encoding of calibration procedures into product components), and temporal binding (the dynamic, on-demand digital ratification of repair outcomes). For example, Apple traditionally authorized relevant repair resources such as tools and repair parts through intellectual property rights codified in contracts. After IRSPs began resisting this enactment of control by self-resourcing nongenuine tools and repair parts (a physical instrumentation), Apple began digitally serializing each device component and developing a dedicated inspection, calibration, and diagnosis software tool that allowed them to monitor device composition and access to tools and repair parts.

Second, we zoomed out of the different dialectical process sequences to observe the broader implications of how the emerging and increasing use of digital instruments for enacting control altered the entire portfolio of control for Apple. This was important because control is always enacted holistically through a portfolio (Cardinal et al., 2017; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Wiener et al., 2016). We coded all salient interdependencies we noticed in the use of digital control instruments across different episodes to understand how they together formed a comprehensive portfolio that was enacted in different configurations over time. To illustrate, consider how the control over repair procedures was originally instrumented by physically designing component interfaces in the iPhone device in such a way that they would prescribe which tools could be used for diagnostics, disassembly, and modification. Over time, the increasing use of digital instruments for authorization and inscription of components not only rendered the responding reverse-engineering actions of IRSPs less effective but also tightened the access to focal repair resources necessary for handling the digitally tethered and encrypted serialized components. We coined this digitally enabled coupling of authorization with inscription as the mechanism of resource-architecture binding, that is, the linking of tethered focal repair resources necessary for following algorithmically encrypted prescriptions digitally encoded in the repair procedure.

Tethering, encryption, and temporary binding also enabled new possibilities for sanctioning repair outcomes digitally in a dynamic and temporary way instead of the traditional discretionary and permanent way through ratification of warranty clauses, yielding a second mechanism that we labelled compliance auditing, that is, the evaluation of device conformance after repair with prescribed repair procedures and authorized access to focal repair resources.

To ensure quality of our theorizing, we followed the typical iterative process of analyzing data, engaging with literature, and collecting new data. In our data analysis, we followed guidelines for open, selective, and theoretical coding (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). We constantly compared interim coding outputs (e.g., the chronologies of repair and control events, their codification by control instruments, and the identification of tensions that characterized the dialectical process sequences) to the existing literature and any newly gathered data, ensuring tight linkages between the data and our conceptualizations (Charmaz, 2006; Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Urquhart et al., 2010). We used theoretical sampling to identify follow-up cases and interviewees to query specific outcomes of our iterative analysis outcomes (e.g., the identification of salient repair or control events determined whether we contacted IRSPs or Apple representatives next) and to challenge our observed patterns (e.g., the logical and temporal sequence of control and repair events as experienced by Apple as compared to IRSPs). This allowed us to test the internal validity and replication logic of our emerging conceptualization. Finally, we developed our emerging theoretical model by drawing on guidelines for scaling up from data to concepts to categories (Gioia et al., 2013; Urquhart et al., 2010).
