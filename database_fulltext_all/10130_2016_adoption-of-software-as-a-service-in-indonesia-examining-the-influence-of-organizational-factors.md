Highlights

We investigate the influence of organizational factors on the adoption og software as a service (SaaS) in Indonesian companies.

Our findings on organizational size and organizational readiness contradict the extant literature.

Theory regarding information technology (IT) innovation adoption should be revisited in light of innovations such as SaaS.

## Abstract

In this study, we investigated the organizational factors that influenced Indonesian companies in their decision to adopt software as a service (SaaS). Based on a multiplecase study of 18 Indonesian companies, we identified three patterns: Top management support is an enabler for SaaS adoption; small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are more likely to adopt SaaS than large companies; and organizational readiness is not an enabler for SaaS adoption. The last two patterns contradict existing information technology (IT) innovation adoption research. We explain this by examining the nature of SaaS as an IT innovation and the characteristics of Indonesia as a developing country.

Keywords: software as a service; adoption; organizational size; top management support; organizational readiness; Indonesia

## Introduction

The emergence of software as a service (SaaS) as a trend in the information technology (IT) industry has attracted considerable interest from both researchers and practitioners. SaaS enables users to access an application over the Internet that resides and runs on an SaaS provider’s servers and use it either for free or for a fee based on usage (Marston et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2011; Sultan, 2010; Chong & Carraro, 2006). This eliminates the need to install and run an application on the user’s computer. Some examples of well-known SaaS solutions for personal use are Yahoo Mail, Google Docs, Facebook, and Twitter; for business users, examples include enterprise resource planning (ERP) software such as NetSuite and customer relationship management (CRM) software such as Salesforce.com, Oracle Siebel on Demand, and Microsoft CRM. SaaS is perceived as an innovative technology (Lin & Chen, 2012) that can provide its adopters with several strategic and operational advantages. Often-mentioned advantages include cost savings, increased flexibility, and the reduced need for up-front investments or skilled IT workers (Benlian & Hess, 2011; Venters & Whitley, 2012).

Several reports have predicted that these advantages may be especially interesting for developing countries (Cowhey & Kleeman, 2012). However, although the global SaaS adoption rate is steadily growing, as reported by large analysts such as Forrester (Hamerman et al., 2015), in developing countries such as Indonesia (Erisman, 2013), Malaysia (Saedi & Iahad, 2013) and Thailand (Hsu 2013; Yen et al. 2013), the adoption rates appear to be rather low. In our earlier research, we found evidence for this low adoption rate (Mangula et al., 2014), which is also confirmed by market analysts such as the Asia Cloud Computing Association (2015), which reports that only 14% of Indonesian small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have adopted cloud computing. Furthermore, the 2015 SMB Cloud Insights research for Indonesia (Odin, 2015) estimates that only 24% of the total Indonesian cloud market is accounted for by SaaS, or as they call it “business apps.” Although reports on cloud and SaaS adoption are difficult to compare due to the use of varying metrics, we do see a large difference with global and Western cloud adoption rates. For example, BCSG (2015) reports an adoption rate of 64% of SMEs in Western Europe and IDG (2014) reports a global cloud adoption rate of 69%. Reasons that are used to explain the low rate of SaaS adoption in Indonesia are, among others, the relatively low reliability of Internet infrastructure and the low level of government support (Mangula et al., 2014).

To investigate the low rate of SaaS adoption in Indonesia, we turn to innovation adoption theory, which has been widely used to investigate technology innovation adoption in organizational studies (e.g., Alshamaila et al., 2012; Pudjianto et al., 2011; Alam et al., 2011). An innovation can be defined as an idea, practice, or object that is perceived as new by an individual or any other unit of adoption (Rogers 1995). IT innovations are regarded as enablers for improving organizational competitiveness (Wu et al. 2011) and productivity (Oliviera & Martins, 2010). An often-used framework in information systems (IS) is the technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework developed by Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990). This framework explains that the intention to adopt an IT innovation is based on factors in the organizational context and its environment as well as the characteristics of the innovation technology itself (Lai et al., 2010; Kuan & Chau, 2001).

The adoption of technology innovations may change the adopting organization internally and/or externally (Damanpour, 1991); hence, the decision to adopt such innovations should be taken carefully. As such, much research has been conducted to understand factors related to organizational aspects that influence the decision making behind technology adoption (Damanpour, 1991), such as top management support, information

#

intensity, innovativeness, organizational readiness, prior IT experience, technologysensing capability, and organizational size (Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Alshamaila et al. 2012; Tan and Lin, 2012; Tehrani, 2013). These organizational factors are considered important determinants of IT innovation adoption (e.g., Yen et al., 2013; Low et al., 2011; Jang, 2010; Pearson & Grandon, 2008; Damanpour, 1991). With respect to the influence of these organizational factors, some recent studies on SaaS adoption in Indonesia (e.g., Mangula et al., 2014; Erisman, 2013) contradict existing theory. We believe this may be due to the nature of SaaS, being a service model highly suitable for organizations with few resources, as well as the characteristics of Indonesia as a developing country. Therefore, we contend that the extant theory regarding the influence of organizational variables on adopting IT innovations should be revisited in light of recent IT innovations such as SaaS. Encouraged by the findings and the theories presented earlier, we propose the following research question: “How do organizational factors influence SaaS adoption in Indonesian organizations?” In particular, we shed light on 1) the influence of organizational factors on the adoption of SaaS and 2) the adoption of SaaS in Indonesian companies.

## 1 SaaS adoption

IT innovation adoption has always been an attractive topic for IS scholars, as IT innovations have a profound impact on organizations (Lee & Xia, 2006). IT innovations influence almost every aspect of organizational life (Fichman, 2004) and generally intend to contribute to performance and growth by increasing productivity, competitiveness, efficiency, and effectiveness (Hameed & Counsell, 2012; Lee & Xia, 2006).

SaaS is a form of cloud computing. This concept was coined around 2006, when Amazon introduced a commercial web service known as the Elastic Compute Cloud (EC2). This enabled small companies and individuals with a limited budget for investing in IT infrastructure to put their own applications on the provider’s site (Marston et al., 2011). Cloud computing is performed by customers on hardware or software that they do not necessarily own or maintain (Wu et al., 2011), or, in other words, the hardware and software resources are delivered as a service through the Internet. There are essentially three service models of cloud computing: SaaS, infrastructure as a service (IaaS), and platform as a service (PaaS). With the SaaS service model, an application is owned by the vendor and hosted on their site; instead of installing and maintaining the application themselves, customers simply access it over the Internet as a service provided by th vendor (Marston et al., 2011; Sultan, 2010). Hence, SaaS provides customers with advantages such as cost efficiency, as they only pay for a service based on their usage (Wu et al., 2011), and increased flexibility and adaptability to react to market changes (Low, 2011).

## 1.1 Determinants of SaaS adoption

Rogers (1995, p. 21) defined adoption as “a decision to make full use of an innovation as the best course of action available.” Various theories in the IS field have been used to find factors that significantly or insignificantly influence SaaS adoption in organizations. Examples include PEST (political, economic, social and technological) analysis (Lee et al., 2013), the perceived e-readiness model (Madisha & Belle, 2011), the technology acceptance model (Wu, 2011), the theory of reasoned action (Benlian & Hess, 2011), and the TOE framework (Yeboah-Boateng & Essandoh, 2014; Yen et al., 2014; Alshamaila 2012).

Several factors that can be found within an organization – but also in the organization’s environment and in the technological characteristics of SaaS – should be considered before taking the decision to adopt SaaS (Low et al., 2011). The TOE framework developed by Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990) covers all of these aspects; it has been extensively used by earlier scholars to investigate cloud services adoption (for an overview, see Table 1). The TOE framework identifies technological, organizational, and environmental aspects that affect the intention to adopt IT innovation in organizations (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990) The technology aspect describes both the existing technologies in use and the emerging technologies that are relevant for an organization. Examples of technological factors include the innovation characteristics as suggested by Rogers (1995), namely relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability (e.g., Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Mangula et al., 2014), as well as perceived cost and security (e.g., Saedi & Iahad, 2013). The organization aspect refers to the internal elements of organizations that may influence the adoption of IT innovations, such as top management support, organizational readiness, organizational size, innovativeness, prior IT experience, and information intensity (e.g., Oliveira, 2014; Tehrani, 2013). The environment aspect relates to elements of the surroundings in which an organization conducts its business, such as external pressure, competition intensity, and government support (e.g., Borgman et al., 2013; Hsu, 2013; Saedi & Iahad, 2013).

In Table 1, we present an overview of SaaS adoption studies that use technological organizational, and environmental factors to explain SaaS adoption. We created this table based on a literature review, where we searched Google Scholar using the search strings “SaaS” “adoption” “TOE” and “cloud” “adoption” “TOE.” We included studies that were of an empirical nature, and included TOE variables that were studied in relation to cloud or SaaS adoption. From each study, we extracted the tested TOE variables and indicated whether their significance. Table 1 shows the results of this review.

In addition to Table 1, we present an overview of the identified variables according to the number of times that they have been found to be significant (see Table 2). This overview only includes variables that were found to be significant in at least one of the studies.

Extant research shows that organizational factors are the most widely studied variables for (IT) innovation adoption (Damanpour, 1991; Jeyaraj, Rottman & Lacity, 2006). This may be because organizational factors are often seen as the most important determinants of IT innovation adoption in organizations (Damanpour, 1991; Kimberly & Evanisko, 1981; Jeyaraj, Rottman & Lacity, 2006). Subsequently, several scholars have focused their research on the organizational factors that drive or hinder the adoption of IT innovation in organizations (Yen et al., 2013; Low et al., 2011). Various factors of the organizational context have been examined, such as firm size, technology readiness, top management support, prior IT experience, information intensity, and innovativeness (Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Oliveira et al., 2014; Yeboah-Boateng & Essandoh, 2014; Tehrani, 2013). As we can see in Table 2, three organizational factors stand out: top management support, organizational size, and organizational readiness. To clarify the organizational factors involved in this study, the following paragraph provides a description of each of the three factors. For each variable, we discuss the existing literature, both from a general innovation adoption perspective and specifically focused on SaaS and cloud studies, and we provide the definition that we used in our research.

Top management support. Several studies have found that the support of top management is one of the most important factors influencing the intention to adopt IT innovations (e.g., Sabherwal et al., 2006; Scupola, 2009; Oliveira, et al. 2014; Yen et al., 2013; Alshamaila et al., 2012). When top management support is high, executives are more likely to attend project meetings and participate in important decisions concerning IT innovation (Sabherwal et al., 2006).

When adopting SaaS, several changes in business processes and activities may be necessary as well as integration with the existing systems; hence, top management support

#

and commitment are essential to convince an organization about the importance of the innovation and influence internal users to participate in the adoption processes (Low et al., 2011). This is also underlined by Oliveira et al. (2014, p. 502), who state that top management is important for cloud computing adoption as it guides “the allocation of resources, the integration of services, and the re-engineering of processes.”

Following Sabherwal et al. (2006) and Scuploa (2009), we define top management support as a favorable attitude toward SaaS in general.

Organizational readiness. Several scholars have used the concept of organizational readiness to explore or predict the adoption of innovations (e.g., Iacovou, Benbasat & Dexter, 1995; Low et al., 2011). Organizational readiness is defined as the availability of organizational resources to adopt new technologies (Iacovou, Benbasat & Dexter, 1995; Wang & Ahmed, 2009; Ramdani, Kawalek & Lorenzo 2004). This concept is often further specified under three headings, including financial, human resources, and infrastructure aspects. Financial aspects refer to the allocated financial resources to invest in new technologies (Wang and Ahmed, 2009). Most researchers consider these financial resources in a general sense, namely for any technology innovation adoption, rather than as being specified for a particular technology (e.g., Wang & Ahmed, 2009; Ramdani, Kawalek & Lorenzo 2004; Khemthong & Roberts, 2006). One exception is the work of Iacovou, Benbasat, and Dexter (1995), who apply their concept of financial readiness explicitly to financial reserves for electronic data interchange (EDI), the technology they investigate in their paper. Second, the human resources aspects refer to the existence of employees with the knowledge and skills to adopt and implement new IT innovations (Zhu, Kraemer & Xu 2006; Wang, Wang & Yang, 2010; Low, Chen & Wu, 2011). Finally, infrastructure aspects refer to the installed IT that provides a platform on which new IT innovations can be built (Zhu, Kraemer & Xu, 2006; Wang, Wang & Yang, 2010).

The exact influence of organizational readiness on the adoption of SaaS is unclear. Some authors have found that companies tend to delay adoption until they have all of the required resources (Yen et al., 2013). Along similar lines, several studies have identified that organizational readiness is an important driver of SaaS adoption (e.g., Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Yen et al., 2013). However, Chen and Wu (2011) found in their empirical research on cloud computing adoption in Taiwan that organizational readiness was not a significant discriminator of cloud adoption. Furthermore, some studies have identified high organizational readiness as a barrier to SaaS adoption rather than an enabler (Mangula et al., 2014; Erisman, 2013).

Following Wang and Ahmed (2009) and Low, Chen, and Wu (2011), we define organizational readiness as the availability of a) sufficient financial resources for adopting IT innovations; b) human resources with the sufficient skills, experience and knowledge for implementing and integrating SaaS applications; and c) sufficient installed and in-use enterprise systems and network technologies on which SaaS applications can be built.

Organizational size. Empirical studies have shown that organizational size positivel affects an organization’s willingness to adopt IT innovations (Hung et al., 2010; Khemthong & Roberts, 2006; Joe & Kim, 2004). This result has also been reported for the adoption of SaaS and cloud (e.g., Low et al., 2011; Oliveira et al., 2014). However, other results have also been obtained. For example, Alshamaila et al. (2012, p. 262) noted in their empirical research that in particular “start-ups and small businesses were found to be inclined to adopt cloud services,” and Misra and Mondal (2011) reported that cloud was highly suitable for SMEs.

To classify the size of each company participating in our study, we used a quantitative measurement based on the number of employees in an organization. This is in line with the World Bank definition of organizational size. The general size categories used are micro enterprises, with one to nine employees; small enterprises, with 10–49 employees; medium enterprises, with 50–249 employees; and large enterprises, with ≥250 employees (Kushnir, Mirmulstein & Ramalho, 2010). To simplify this process, organizations are categorized into two groups, small and medium sized (including micro enterprises), with up to 249 employees; and large, with ≥250 employees.

## 1.2 SaaS adoption in Indonesia

In 2006, the first Indonesian IT provider launched cloud services (Mangula et al., 2012). Since then, the number of providers has increased. However, SaaS adoption in developing countries remains in its infancy, as seen, for example, in Indonesia (e.g., Mangula et al., 2014; Erisman, 2013), Malaysia (Saedi & Iahad, 2013), and Thailand (Hsu, 2013; Yen et al., 2013). This is also confirmed by several professional reports, such as the abovementioned Asia Cloud Computing Association (2015), which found that only 14% of Indonesian SMEs have adopted cloud computing, and the Asia Cloud Readiness Index (Asia Cloud Computing Association, 2014), which ranked Indonesia 12th out of 14 Asian countries, slightly above India and Vietnam. This last report lists broadband quality, international connectivity, and data center risk as three indicators on which Indonesia scores especially low. The Global Cloud Computing Score Card (BSA, 2013) also ranks the cloud readiness of Indonesia as “problematic” compared to other countries. According to the report, this is mainly caused by government regulations that create barriers for cloud service providers.

## 2 Method

In this study, we followed a multiple-case study approach to investigate how organizational factors influence the adoption of SaaS in Indonesian organizations. The case study method enabled us to investigate a contemporary phenomenon, namely the adoption of SaaS, in its real-world context (Yin, 2014). We approached our study from an

#

organizational perspective; that is, we focused our research on three organizational variables: organizational size, organizational readiness, and top management support. These variables were defined a priori, based on the existing literature, to shape the design of our research (Eisenhardt, 1989). In our case studies, we systematically compared and contrasted our findings with the existing theory. We then used this analysis to further our understanding of the adoption process and explain why Indonesian companies adopt SaaS or not.

With our research method, we intended to determine why the adoption rate of SaaS in Indonesia is relatively low and also whether and how the three organizational variables influence the adoption decision. Therefore, we used an explanatory case study approach to explain how or why a certain condition (in this study, the adoption or non-adoption of SaaS) came to be (Yin, 2014). More specifically, we followed a multiple-case design, which allowed direct replication, thereby enabling more powerful analytical conclusions, as well as the ability to use cases that offered contrasting situations (Yin, 2014).

In the remainder of this section, we present how we selected the case companies in our study. We then describe how we collected, processed, and analyzed the data.

## 2.1 Case selection

All of the cases in our research were located in Indonesia. The second author of this paper is Indonesian and therefore has relatively easy access to Indonesian organizations and was able to conduct interviews in the interviewees’ native language. The country is also particularly interesting for this research when we consider its high prevalence of SMEs (99.9% according to Asia Cloud Computing Association, 2015), considering that several reports and publications have identified that cloud and SaaS solutions may be especially attractive for SMEs (McKinsey, 2009; Misra & Mondal, 2011). The results of our earlier research, in addition to the most recent market analysis reports, contradict this, thus sparking our interest. Finally, because Indonesia has the typical characteristics of a developing country (Lertwongsatien & Wongpinunwatana, 2003; Seyal et al., 2007), and particularly a developing country in the Asia Pacific region, the findings of this study may be relevant for similar developing countries (Kurnia et al., 2014).

We used our own network to identify the case companies. We selected our cases through a combination of theoretical and literal replication (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 2014). Theoretical replication is used to generate “contrasting results…for predictable reasons” (Yin, 2014); for instance, we were interested in how large organizations and SMEs or organizations with sufficient readiness and those with insufficient readiness differ in their adoption processes. Literal replication is used to predict similar results within groups of cases with similar characteristics, thus strengthening the robustness and reliability of our study (Yin, 2014). Previous studies have used theoretical and literal replication to select their cases; for example, Sherif et al. (2006) used these methods to select case sites for their research on software reuse as a disruptive IT innovation, Mehrtens et al. (2001) used them to select firms for their study of Internet adoption by SMEs, and Rahrovani at al. (2014) used them to select SMEs in their exploration of contingent factors that affect IS planning approaches.

We used the three variables organizational size, organizational readiness, and top management support as well as the outcome variable (adopt or not adopt) as conditions to determine the types covered by our theoretical replication. The types based on the size condition (SMEs and large organizations) could be defined beforehand, whereas the other types (organizations with sufficient vs. insufficient organizational readiness, organizations with sufficient vs. insufficient top management support, and organizations that adopted SaaS vs. those that did not) were described after the interviews and first analyses. Yin (2014) argues that we need at least two individual cases within each subgroup in order to complement the theoretical replications across the groups with literal replication within each group. If, after the first analyses, it appeared that this was not the case, we added extra cases to our study.

We sent a letter of permission to a total of 40 companies, of which 18 eventually agreed to participate in our study. Three informants were interviewed twice, namely C1, C11, and C17. Of these 18 companies, five had adopted SaaS and 13 had not and did not intend to adopt within the next 3 years. All case companies were located in Jakarta, with the exception of C11, C15, and C18, which were located in Bandung.

Table 3 displays information about the companies that participated in our study. The cases are divided over different sectors. Six of them are health-care organizations, varying from large hospitals distributed across Indonesia to small outpatient clinics located in Jakarta. The three cases in the education sector are all higher education facilities, located in Jakarta. Three of our cases provide IT services to local customers in Jakarta and Bandung. The two cases from the hospital sector are both hotels; one is a large multinational with several hotels in Indonesia, and the other is an independent hotel located in Bandung. The two organizations in the banking sector are both Indonesian banks that also have branches overseas. The organization in the media sector is a well-known Indonesian media corporation that mainly operates online. Finally, our last case is a small retailer in Bandung that sells fashion products.

## 2.2 Data collection

We used semi-structured interviews (Yin, 2014) as our primary data collection method. This research approach ensured that we obtained all of the required information from our interviewees (Paré, 2004); it also gave us more room to ask for clarification, add questions, or follow up on interviewees’ comments, allowing us to gain additional insights into and understanding of the adoption or rejection decision made by our case companies. We used an interview guide in each of our interviews that we refined over the course of the interview series. We complemented our interview data with field notes and (online) documentation, such as corporate websites and annual reports of the companies and their IS.

Data collection was performed by the second author of this paper, which took place in two periods. The first period covered October and November 2014, while the second was in January 2016. In total, 21 interviews were conducted; this includes three informants (C1, C11, and C17) who were interviewed twice, in November 2014 and January 2016. Most interviews (n = 18) were carried out in the informants’ offices and lasted between 30 and 90 min. The three remaining interviews (C9, C14, and the first interview of C17) were conducted via e-mail. Our informants came from different management functions and included IT supervisors, IT managers, IT directors, and business owners, as can be seen in Table 3.

Before starting the interview sessions, the interviewer introduced herself and explained the aims of the study and how the interview would be carried out. The interview topics included the company’s background, the informant’s awareness of SaaS, and the impact of the three organizational factors on SaaS adoption. In order to clarify an informant’s basic awareness of SaaS, the interviewer first asked whether the informant had ever heard about SaaS and, if so, if they could explain what SaaS was. The interviewer then explained our own definition of SaaS and provided several examples of SaaS solutions that are implemented in corporate or personal settings. Once the informant and the interviewer had the same understanding of SaaS, the interviewer continued with the other questions.

The interviewer assured informants that all information would be treated confidentially. In line with the informants’ requests, company names cannot be mentioned in our research paper; the companies are therefore represented by the identifiers C1–C18. The face-to-face interviews were audio-recorded with the permission of the informants. As soon as the interviewer finished an interview session, she summarized the interviews and asked for feedback and approval from the informants.

## 2.3 Data processing and analysis

To aid our analysis, we used six codes to organize our data: SaaS awareness level, top management support, organizational readiness, organizational size, SaaS adoption and use, and developing country. We used NVivo 10.0 to code our interviews and documentation with these four themes. Table 4 shows our coding scheme with a detailed description of each code and examples of real text translated from Indonesian.

We analyzed our data in several iterations. We started with a within-case analysis of each of the cases to structure, define, reduce, and make sense of the information. We evaluated each case separately in terms of the three organizational variables and outcome variable by using transcripts, field notes, and (online) documentation on the companies and their IS. Furthermore, we analyzed the data for evidence related to SaaS awareness level, SaaS adoption and use, and issues that are typical for developing countries.

The results of the within-case analyses were processed in an informal qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), a method originating in management research that helps to “discover combinations of conditions that sufficiently explain a certain outcome” (Schultze-Bentrop, 2011, p. V). This method allows for cross-case comparisons but also does justice to within-case complexity (Rihoux & Ragin, 2009). QCA assumes that in order to enable the systematic comparison of complex cases, these cases need to be transformed into configurations (Rihoux & Ragin, 2009). These configurations are a specific set of factors (in this study, organizational variables) that produce a given outcome of interest (in this study, the adoption of SaaS). In the IS field, QCA is particularly interesting for finding configurations of factors that explain IT innovation outcomes (Fichman, 2004) and has, for example, been used to explain how IT implementers responses influence user resistance (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012).

Finally, we carried out an across-case analysis. We used the QCA results as a basis for identifying the different configurations that led to either the adoption or non-adoption of SaaS. The goal of the across-case analysis was to find similar patterns across our cases, enabling us to draw conclusions about the influence of our three organizational variables (Yin, 2014).

## 2.4 Validity

Four types of validity are important for assessing the quality of empirical research: construct validity, internal validity, external validity, and reliability. Construct validity ensures that the correct measures are used in a study (Yin, 2014). The steps we took to ensure this type of validity were 1) using multiple sources of evidence (interviews, field notes, and documentation), 2) establishing a chain of evidence during data collection, and 3) summarizing the interview results of each case company for feedback and approval. Internal validity concerns the evaluation of the causal relationships found in a study (Yin, 2014). We followed an explanation-building procedure to strengthen our internal validity. External validity relates to the generalizability of the research. We used a multiple-case design in which we used replication logic to strengthen the robustness and the generalizability of our research (Lee & Baskerville; 2003; Yin, 2014). Finally, we ensured the reliability of our research by using a case study protocol and maintaining a case study database. This guaranteed that the data collection and analysis could be repeated (Yin, 2014).

## 3 Results

We present our results in three parts. First, we elaborate on the findings of our within-case analysis. We then present our QCA in order to show how the different cases scored on the three organizational variables in relation to the outcome variable, SaaS adoption. Finally, we present our across-case analysis, in which we elaborate on the patterns we found and illustrate these with interview quotes to reveal more about the interviewees’ perceptions regarding these variables.

## 3.1 Within-case analysis

Our within-case analysis required a careful analysis of each separate case based on the three organizational variables and the outcome variable (adoption or non-adoption of SaaS). We also analyzed other case details, such as awareness of SaaS and any other characteristics that surfaced.

The analysis of the three organizational variables consisted of two parts: first, we searched for ways to assign a value to each of the variables. For organizational size, this was easy: This variable is a measurable, objective value that we acquired via either the interviewee or additional documentation (e.g., company websites and annual reports). Top management support and organizational readiness, however, were more difficult to assess. For organizational readiness, we looked at the three sub-concepts financial resources, human resources, and installed and in-use enterprise systems and network technologies. By examining the information acquired from our interviews and additional documentation we evaluated whether these conditions were sufficiently present or not. As an example of a case with insufficient organizational readiness, we present our evaluation of C1. The informant indicated that “[the company had] budget restrictions for purchasing data storage and hiring an IT professional,” and “currently we have only one part-time IT employee.” From this, we concluded that there were insufficient financial resources; no human resources with sufficient skills, experience, and knowledge; and insufficient infrastructure to implement and integrate SaaS applications. By contrast, we provide an

#

example of an organization with sufficient organizational readiness, C5. The informant in this case indicated that his company had “all necessary resources to develop our own information system,” and that they “use a Hospital Information System that had been developed by ourselves.” Complementing this with the information on human resources and technology investment in the organization’s annual report, we concluded that C5 had sufficient financial resources; human resources with sufficient skills, experience, and knowledge; and sufficient infrastructure to implement and integrate SaaS applications.

Top management support was assessed based on how each informant perceived this variable in his or her organization. For example, the informant in C17 explained that the rector of his educational facility “suggested us to adopt Office 365,” based on which we concluded that top management support was sufficient in this case. Finally, the evaluation of the outcome variable (adoption or non-adoption of SaaS) was also based on the information provided by informants.

## 3.2 Qualitative comparative analysis

In this section, we discuss how the cases can be classified according to the variables organizational size, organizational readiness, top management support, and the outcome variable, SaaS adoption. We used an informal QCA to present the results (following the approach of Rihoux and Ragin, 2009).

First, we dichotomized each variable with either a 1 or a 0, with 1 indicating that a given condition or outcome was present and 0 indicating its absence. Following good practice in QCA (Rihoux & Ragin, 2009), the choice of 1 or 0 was based on the existing theory. According to several studies (e.g., Khemthong & Roberts, 2006; and Joe & Kim, 2004) large organizations are more likely to adopt an innovation. Therefore, we coded large organizations with 1 and SMEs with 0. The same holds for the other two variables: sufficient top management support and organizational readiness were coded with 1, whereas insufficient top management support and organizational readiness were coded with 0. We used the results of our within-case analysis to assign the values to the variables, as can be seen in Table 5.

Table 5 shows the data set table that summarizes our findings. Based on this table, we developed a truth table that shows all possible configurations of the three different conditions (three organizational variables) that were considered to affect an organization’s decision to adopt SaaS. As can be seen in Table 6, eight configurations were possible. Six of these configurations were found in our data set, namely configurations A, B, C, E, F, and G. It is worth noting that the configurations leading to the adoption of SaaS are configuration B, containing SMEs with insufficient organizational readiness but sufficient top management support, and configuration F, containing one large organization with insufficient organizational readiness but sufficient top management support. Configurations A, C, E, and G did not lead to SaaS adoption. Two configurations were not found in our data set, namely configuration D (SMEs with sufficient organizational readiness and top management support that have adopted SaaS) and configuration H (large organizations with sufficient organizational readiness and top management support that have adopted SaaS).

In addition to the truth table, we present a set-theoretical representation of our data set in Fig. 1. In this figure, the cases that adopted SaaS are marked in gray. Cases that were not large and had no sufficient organizational readiness and top management support are placed outside the three circles. A few points are worth noting in this representation. First, sufficient top management support seems to be the strongest predictor for the adoption of SaaS in our study. Second, cases with sufficient organizational readiness for IT innovation are not among the adopters of SaaS. Finally, most large organizations, with the exception of C12, did not adopt SaaS.

## 3.3 Across-case analysis

In this section, we elaborate on the results of our interviews and try to find general patterns that help us understand and explain the influence of the organizational variables on the adoption of SaaS. To set the stage, we present a general discussion of the SaaS awareness level of our interviewees. Second, we elaborate on the findings concerning the variables top management support, organizational size, and organizational readiness. Finally, we discuss findings related to Indonesia as a developing country.

## 4.3.1 SaaS awareness level

To analyze the SaaS awareness level of our informants, we classified them into four groups: 1) very basic level – the informant has heard about either cloud computing or SaaS but is not able to give a correct description of the terms; (2) basic level – the informant has heard about both cloud computing and SaaS but is not able to give a correct description of either of these terms; (3) medium level – the informant has heard about cloud computing and SaaS and is able to give an accurate description of one of these terms; and (4) high level – the informant is able to give a correct description of both cloud computing and SaaS. We discovered that five of the 18 informants had only a very basic level of SaaS awareness, whereas nine others were at the basic level, as we can see in Table 3. Only two informants could be classified at the medium level and two could demonstrate a high level of awareness of SaaS. In other words, most of our interviewees had heard of cloud computing or SaaS but were not able to describe the concepts accurately. Most informants described SaaS as a web application, which is not entirely in line with the definition of SaaS used in our study. SaaS applications may indeed be accessed via the Internet but more importantly, data storage is on the provider's server instead of on the user’s server or hard disk. Below is a selection of the interviewees’ responses:

“Cloud computing...Yes, I’ve heard about it…SaaS is a web application.” (C11). “Yes, I did hear about cloud...I think SaaS is a web application.” (C10).

## 4.3.2 Top management support

In our study, top management refers to a person or group of people that makes the decision to adopt or not adopt SaaS and to allocate the necessary organizational resources to support the adoption process. In our cases, the decision to adopt or reject SaaS was made, for example, by the business owner, the IT director, or the IT manager, as reflected in the following quotes:

“I am the owner and have sufficient knowledge about IT; the decision was taken by myself and the IT director.” (C1)

"As the head of the IT department, I make the decision.” (C12)

Top management may also refer to a person who has a key role in influencing all decisions taken by a decision maker. In one of our case companies, even though the IT manager had no power to make any decisions regarding the adoption of new IT innovations, he did have some power to influence the decision makers in his company to adopt certain IT innovations:

“We have just developed our new Hospital Information System; therefore, I do not think that we will adopt SaaS within the next few years. The decision lies at the board of commissioners, I am just giving them some suggestions on IT implementation.” (C4)

Five of our cases showed sufficient top management support for SaaS, and each of these cases had actually adopted SaaS. All of these cases showed that the top management was convinced of the benefits of SaaS, as, for example, can be seen in the following quote:

"When email would go down, top management would be very disappointed…They feel the importance of email, so they are very supportive of using Google Corporate email…I did not have to bother to convince top management to use Google for our corporate email, because they already felt that there would be a severe impact in case email would go down." (C12)

C17 is of particular interest. The informant in this educational organization was interviewed twice, with 1 year in between interviews. In the first interview, the informant indicated that SaaS was not being discussed by top managers and that his organization would probably not be using SaaS in the near future. However, 1 year later, the situation had changed and the organization had started using a free version of Microsoft 365 for its e-mail services. When our interviewee, an IT supervisor, was asked whether he was the one who had suggested this SaaS solution to the organization, he denied it and explained that the idea actually came from the top management:

“No...In fact, [the rector] suggested us to adopt Office 365.” (C17)

In several cases, we found that top management was strongly influenced by external parties, such as government policies and the professional community. In our study, two companies ran their businesses in the banking sector, where there is the obligation to conduct business in accordance with the regulations of the central bank of Indonesia. The nature of SaaS, especially concerning data storage outside the customers’ chosen organization, raises all kinds of compliance problems. This means that adoption decisions have to be taken at the highest level of management or even by governmental bodies, as is illustrated in the following quotes:

"As a banking corporation, each new IT innovation that is to be adopted must comply with the regulations made by the government, such as by the central bank of Indonesia, which has the authority in managing all activities and banking regulations in Indonesia.” (C8) "The decision rests with the board of directors, commissioners, and the IT committee of our holding company…I am somewhat pessimistic, because due to our banking institutions, many factors must be taken into consideration…It will depend on the permission from the Central Bank…and attitude of the decision-makers (whether they are conservative or daring towards testing new technologies)." (C9)

In other cases, informants explained that their top management sometimes took suggestions from their professional community into consideration before deciding to adopt a new innovation, including SaaS:

“The decisions are taken by the hospital management with considering the IT department recommendations. However, they also sometimes consider the suggestions from their colleagues from other hospitals.” (C3)

Based on our findings, we concluded that top management support had a major influence on the adoption of SaaS. Although in several cases IT employees had some influence on the decision, we identified that SaaS applications were only actually adopted in cases where top management was enthusiastic about SaaS. Therefore, we concluded that top management support had a positive influence on the adoption of SaaS.

## 4.3.3 Organizational size

We determined organizational size by measuring the number of employees in each case company. We classified our case companies into two groups: SMEs and large companies. As shown in Table 2, seven large companies and 11 SMEs participated in our study. One out of seven large companies and four out of 11 SMEs adopted SaaS. To explain these findings, in this section, we elaborate on the rationale behind adopting or not adopting SaaS and attempt to link this to characteristics related to company size.

For the non-adopting large companies, instead of adopting SaaS, some chose to develop their own IS or purchase them from an application vendor, with slight modifications if necessary. One reason for this was the uniqueness of their business processes, as can be seen in the following quote:

“Since 2009, we have moved to a new web-based Hospital Information System. We purchased it from an application vendor and made about 20% of modifications to the flow of the system and the features. The uniqueness of our information system is related to our business processes, which are different from other hospital business processes.” (C2)

Another company mentioned the obligation to use IS that were already developed and standardized by the parent company as one of the reasons for not adopting SaaS:

"For our company, it seems quite difficult to adopt SaaS since we have to follow a standard system set by the principal of our hotel group. We should have a discussion first with the stakeholders if we want to adopt any new IT innovations." (C10)

We can conclude from these quotes that the size of the company, and the resources that come with this, enables companies to make the choice to use customized IS and to keep them in house for more control. Another factor that affects the decision to not adopt is compliance with the rest of the organization: Choices are made by headquarters and not by subsidiary companies.

If we look at the single large company that did adopt SaaS, the intention to adopt was related to the efficiency of its resource utilization. Using cloud appeared to be more efficient and enabled the company to focus more on its core business activities:

"For our long-term planning, after we did some calculations and made a comparison between maintaining our own email server and renting an email application from Google, we concluded it would be more efficient on budget spending if we chose the second option We have 12 IT employees and three of them were allocated for maintaining our own email server, which was not efficient. After we started using Gmail, we could focus on our core

#

business activities.” (C12)

Of the 11 SMEs, we found that four adopted SaaS, but the other seven did not. The adopting SMEs decided to adopt SaaS for different reasons, but most mentioned their expectations of cost reductions and the new functionality that the SaaS applications could provide, as illustrated by the following quotes:

“...this new application is already web-based, user friendly, and supported with experienced IT personnel of the provider. Therefore, this will reduce our spending on IT infrastructure and IT personnel. Currently we have only one part-time IT employee” (C1) “The adopted SaaS application we currently use has enabled us to collaborate with the teams internally in our company as well as to share knowledge regarding the existing problems and how to solve them.” (C14)

The non-compliance of SaaS applications with the flow of the organizations’ business processes was mentioned by two non-adopting SMEs:

"We were thinking to buy a new application; however, since the application was not

compliant with our business process, we decided to develop our own application.” (C4).

“...we will consider to adopt SaaS if its features and functions are similar to or are even

better than our current business processes, so we don’t have to do lots of customizations

that will affect the costs.” (C7)

Based on the findings presented here, we can conclude that organizational size is generally not positively related to SaaS adoption. It appears from our data that SMEs are more likely to adopt SaaS than large companies.

## 4.3.4 Organizational readiness

The readiness of a company to adopt new IT innovations can be considered in terms of three types of organizational resources, including the availability of financial resources for adopting IT innovations, human resources with the necessary IT skills and knowledge, and

installed and in-use enterprise systems and network technologies. In the existing literature

the general idea is that the absence of one or more of these resources is likely to hinder a

company’s intention to adopt an innovation. However, our QCA showed that none of the

organizations with sufficient organizational readiness adopted SaaS.

Examining the responses of our interviewees, we found some evidence to support the

claim that the absence of organizational resources prevents an organization from adopting

an innovation. The IT manager of a medical organization explained, for example,

“Considering the IT infrastructure that we have now, I think we’re not ready yet to

connect with the cloud...we have IT personnel with their own expertise...but we don’t have

a budget to frequently replace our applications.” (C2)

However, in contrast to this response, we also found that the lack of budget allocation for

IT infrastructure and expertise led to SaaS adoption:

“At first, we preferred to place the application server at our location. However, due to

budget restrictions on purchasing data storage and hiring IT professionals to maintain our

servers, we later decided to put the system in the cloud.” (C1)

Furthermore, we noted that for some of our cases, sufficient funding, good IT

infrastructure, and sufficient IT expertise did not automatically trigger the intention to

adopt SaaS, as confirmed by the following quote:

"In 2000, we started to use a Hospital Information System that has been developed by

ourselves. We do not host it at another place...we have all necessary resources to develop

and host our own information systems.” (C5)

C8 and C9 were the two organizations with the sufficient organizational readiness in terms

of budget, human resources, and infrastructure. C8, for example, allocated more than

US\$50 million in 2014 for IT investments, maintained an educated IT workforce (through professional certification trajectories, among others), and had well-developed IT infrastructure (for example, with a dual data center facility). C9 had allocated US\$26 million to IT infrastructure, had won a prestigious IT excellence award, had employees with a sufficient knowledge, and had well-developed and recently updated IT infrastructure. However, neither organization mentioned the terms “cloud computing” or “SaaS” in their plans for future IT developments; their budgets, skills, and existing infrastructure enabled them to develop and host their own applications and retain a maximum level of control:

"We currently use 100% on-premise applications. All servers are placed at our organization…Thus, it is always under our control. For an application with specific requirements, we prefer to develop it by ourselves.” (C8)

Based on these arguments, the presence of sufficient financial resources, skilled IT employees, and good IT infrastructure, that is, sufficient organizational readiness, does not seem to have a positive influence on the decision to adopt SaaS. Moreover, the informants responses were an indication of the opposite: Organizational readiness seems to be inversely associated with SaaS adoption.

## 4.3.5 Adopting SaaS in a developing country

In addition to findings related to the a priori defined variables, during our analysis, we found extra determinants of SaaS adoption that related to Indonesia as a developing country.

Several of our informants noted that the Internet connections of some vendors were still not reliable, as illustrated in the following quotes:

“I think we are not ready yet...On the one hand, [SaaS] is a good application, but we also have to consider the Internet providers. If they have a good infrastructure, it’s ok for us. But we’re still concerned about the bandwidth they offer.” (C3)

“We are not considering adopting SaaS since we’re concerned about the Internet reliability offered by the providers.” (C15)

Furthermore, some of our informants pointed out that they did not want to use SaaS due to their lack of trust in the local SaaS vendors in terms of data security, as argued by one of the cases:

“I simply rejected SaaS adoption since I still have doubts concerning its security. [The local providers] have not convinced me that SaaS is secure.” (C11)

Another issue that arose during the interviews is related to the low awareness level of SaaS. As described earlier, most of our interviewees had heard of either cloud computing or SaaS, but they did not really understand what the terms actually mean. One informant mentioned that an SaaS vendor had invited him to follow an SaaS seminar. However, in the view of this interviewee, these types of seminars are more about promoting products, than educating on SaaS:

“Yes, I received the seminar invitation, but I didn’t have time to go there..And they are just promoting their product anyway. They don’t explain what SaaS actually is.” (C4)

## 4 Discussion

In this section, we first discuss the influence of the organizational factors on the adoption of SaaS in Indonesia, and then elaborate on the limitations of this study.

## 4.1 Patterns of SaaS adoption in Indonesia

In our across-case analysis, we revealed three patterns concerning the adoption of SaaS in Indonesia: 1) top management support is an enabler for SaaS adoption; 2) SMEs are more likely to adopt SaaS than large companies; and 3) organizational readiness for IT innovation reduces the likelihood of SaaS adoption. We discuss each pattern and elaborate on the context in which the results were found, namely, Indonesia as a developing country.

## 4.1.1 Pattern 1: Top management support is an enabler for SaaS adoption

With regard to top management support, we found that the role of decision makers is crucial in deciding whether or not to adopt SaaS: All of our cases with sufficient management support adopted SaaS. We also found that how SaaS was perceived by decision makers potentially influenced the support given, which in turn was mostl affected by SaaS awareness levels. In other words, there is a connection between top management support and SaaS awareness. As we can see in Table 3, most of the nonadopting companies had a basic or very basic knowledge of SaaS. This is in line with Thong and Yap (1995), who found that the top management characteristics innovativeness, attitude toward the adoption of IT, and IT knowledge have a positive influence on the adoption of IT innovations in companies, and the work of Rogers (1995), who argues that the “attitude towards an innovation takes place before a decision to adopt is made.”

In several cases, we saw that decision makers were influenced by external pressures, such as from a professional community or the government. We do not underestimate the influence of these actors and recognize this as a form of institutional isomorphism (Greenwood et al. 2008), in which organizations adopt similar structures and ideas due to pressure from institutionalized ideas. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) identified three isomorphic pressures: coercive, mimetic, and normative. Coercive pressures come from the state or other power relationships, and banking regulations are a clear example of this In addition, we saw that several cases were pressured by mimetic forces, implying that when these organizations were uncertain about which course of action to take, they looked to their peers (in this case other hospitals) and imitated their behavior. In earlier studies, these types of mimetic forces have been found to influence top management in the assimilation of IT innovations. An example is the study by Liang et al. (2007), who found that mimetic pressures influence top management beliefs and participation in the ERP assimilation process.

Based on the existing literature, it is no surprise that top management support has a positive influence on the adoption of SaaS. Many studies show that top management support is a strong enabler for the adoption of IT innovations (e.g., Sabherwal et al., 2006; Scupola, 2009; Yen et al., 2013; Alshamaila et al., 2012). This has also been found in studies on the adoption of SaaS (Low et al., 2011; Oliveira et al. 2014). However, we were surprised to find that so few of our informants had tried to use or promote SaaS applications in their companies. In the recent literature, the consumerization of IT has been of increasing interest. Consumerization, which refers to the use of consumer technologies such as mobile devices and cloud services in the workplace (Harris et al., 2012; Niehaves et al., 2013), can be seen as an example of user-driven innovation. Tan and Kim (2014) found, for example, that prior experience with SaaS collaboration tools such as Google Docs has significantly influenced the introduction of these tools in organizations. In our cases, we did not find much evidence of this. One explanation might be that the Indonesian culture is characterized by a high power distance (Hofstede, 1994) in which organizations have strong hierarchies and Indonesian employees expect to be clearly directed by their executives. In these types of cultures, the opinions of top management may have a stronger effect on the adoption process than in cultures with a lower power distance, as found by Lee et al. (2009) in their research on the diffusion of knowledge management systems in Chinese enterprises and by Kurnia et al. (2014) in their research on e-commerce adoption within the Indonesian grocery industry. Furthermore, the collectivist nature of the Indonesian culture (Hofstede, 1994) might increase the influence of isomorphic pressures, indicating that top management is strongly influenced by the government and their peers in their decisions on IT innovation adoption, as also observed by Kurnia et al. (2014).

## 4.1.2 Pattern 2: SMEs are more likely to adopt SaaS than large companies

In terms of organizational size, we found that only one of the seven large companies adopted SaaS, whereas four of the 11 SMEs adopted it. This is surprising, as several studies on innovation adoption have shown that organizational size positively affects an organization’s willingness to adopt IT innovations (Khemthong & Roberts, 2006; Joe & Kim, 2004; Alam et al., 2011). However, this finding is also in line with some studies that argue SaaS as being particularly fitting for SMEs and organizations with tight resources (e.g., Alshamaila et al., 2012).

Our informants expressed several reasons for adopting or rejecting SaaS that can be traced back to the size of their organization. First, some informants expressed the need to control data by placing their own server inside the organization. The fact that large organizations have more resources in terms of finances, infrastructure, and IT workers implies that these companies can make choices that might not be the most cost-efficient. As Susarla et al. (2003) argue, for large companies, the willingness to adopt an innovation is related to, among others, how they handle the risks and uncertainties associated with this new innovation (Susarla et al., 2003). This motivation may be reinforced by the characteristics of Indonesia as a developing country. As discussed in the last part of our analysis, there is a lack of trust in the availability and stability of Internet connections. This is a concern specific to developing countries and has also been reported in other studies (e.g., Mujinga & Chipangura, 2011; Lechesa, Seymour & Schuler, 2011).

Another reason mentioned was that our informants had to use standardized applications in compliance with the parent company. Large companies usually have more data and more complex business processes, which means that the adoption of SaaS may involve major changes in organizational IT governance (Heart, 2010).

As mentioned earlier, according to Hofstede’s (1994) cultural dimensions, Indonesia has a culture with a high power distance, where hierarchy is important. In large organizations in particular, this may lead to a delay in the adoption of IT innovations, as lower management levels may be less inclined to make such adoption decisions without consulting the top management.

For the SMEs that adopted SaaS, decisions were motivated by the need to lower the investment costs in IT infrastructure and human resources, as well as the possibility of collaborating online with internal users and customers. The small amount of data owned by such companies and the simple structure of their business processes were also some of the considerations that made it easy to adopt SaaS. Conversely, the non-compliance of SaaS with existing business processes had also led some other SMEs to reject SaaS, similarly to some of the large organizations.

Based on our findings, we conclude that large organizations are not more inclined to adopt SaaS than SMEs. Moreover, we found strong indications that this relation is actuall inverse. This is in contrast to several studies that claim that the larger the size of an organization, the more likely it is to adopt SaaS, reflecting its ability to take risks and allocate the required organizational resources (i.e., Oliveira et al., 2014; Saedi & Iahad, 2013; Low et al., 2011). We believe that this contradiction is mainly caused by the nature of SaaS as an innovation, in line with the arguments of Alshamaila et al. (2012).

## 4.1.3 Pattern 3: Organizational readiness for IT innovation reduces the likelihood of SaaS adoption

Four of the 18 cases showed sufficient organizational readiness for adopting IT innovations, indicating that these organizations had sufficient financial resources, skilled IT employees, and a decent IT infrastructure. Based on the existing literature (e.g.,

#

Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Yen et al., 2013), we might expect these cases to be among the adopters of SaaS. However, the opposite is true: None of these companies actually adopted SaaS.

Based on the interview results, some companies delayed SaaS adoption due to concerns about the security and reliability of their own IT infrastructure, which actually supports the argument that higher organizational readiness leads to the adoption of SaaS. However, we also found evidence that limited IT infrastructure and unskilled IT personnel lead to the exact opposite: They were used as motivations to adopt SaaS. This is mainly due to the fact that SaaS adoption requires low investments in IT infrastructure, such as computer servers, and IT personnel for maintaining IT operations. This contradicts prior studies such as Gutierrez and Lumsden (2014), Hsu (2013), and Yen et al. (2013), but it is in line with the work of Mangula et al. (2014) and Erisman (2013), who found in earlier studies on SaaS adoption in Indonesia that organizational readiness was a barrier to adoption rather than an enabler. Based on our findings, we conclude that this contradiction is caused mainly by the nature of the innovation (e.g., low up-front investments).

Although there is an overlap between large organizations and organizations with sufficient organizational readiness, we believe it is important to distinguish these variables. Indeed this overlap is not unexpected because large organizations tend to have more resources (Damanpour, 1992; Brusha & Chaganti, 1999). However, organizational readiness is not comparable one on one with organizational size, which can also be seen in our large cases with insufficient organizational readiness and vice versa. Therefore, we followed the extant literature on IT innovation adoption, which treats these variables separately (e.g., Mehrtens et al., 2001; Zhu et al., 2006).

## 4.2 Limitations

Although our study resulted in interesting findings, it has some limitations to be discussed.

#

First, we focused our study on the three most commonly used organizational factors in the IT innovation literature. This was intentional, as we focused our research on the organizational aspects of adoption, but it also implies that we did not thoroughly explore other factors, such as technological or environmental factors. A broader scope might result in a more comprehensive analysis of SaaS adoption in Indonesia.

Second, our study was based on 18 Indonesian cases, divided over seven industry sectors. Although we used literal replication to strengthen the robustness and reliability of our study, the findings should be interpreted carefully. Several sectors were not included in our study, which might have influenced our results. Furthermore, although Indonesia has the typical characteristics of a developing country, it is not directly comparable with all other developing countries, as these might have different cultural dimensions. One solution to overcome the small amount of cases is to use survey-based research methods so more organizations can be included. Nonetheless, these methods have several limitations compared to qualitative research methods; for example, if interesting issues emerge, it is usually not possible to ask follow-up questions. We believe that our multiple-case study offers further insight into the adoption process.

Finally, we found in our research that top management support is extremely important in adopting SaaS. However, we do not know much about the personal characteristics of these managers. An interesting direction for future research would be to consider the personal characteristics of the top management and assess how this affects attitudes toward SaaS adoption.

## 5 Conclusion and implications

In this paper, we attempted to answer the research question “How do organizational factors influence SaaS adoption in Indonesian organizations?” In particular, we investigated SaaS awareness in Indonesian companies and how three organizational factors, namely top management support, organizational size, and organizational readiness, influence the decision to adopt SaaS.

This paper makes several contributions that have important theoretical implications. First, our study revealed that top management support is the strongest enabler for SaaS adoption. This is in line with the literature (Sabherwal et al., 2006; Scupola, 2009; Low et al., 2011; Oliveira et al. 2014). However, we also have indications that this influence is reinforced by the Indonesian culture, with its high power distance and strong hierarchies (Hofstede, 1994). Future research could investigate in more detail how the relation between top management support and IT innovation adoption is influenced by the Indonesian culture, or, in a broader context, by the characteristics of developing countries. Second, we investigated the relation between organizational size and SaaS adoption. The previously introduced IT innovation adoption literature assumes that organizational size has a positive influence on IT innovation adoption (Khemthong & Roberts, 2006; Joe & Kim, 2004; Alam et al., 2011). Contrary to this assumption, our research showed that SMEs were more likely to adopt SaaS than large companies, which is in line with the work of Alshamaila et al. (2012), although it contradicts the majority of studies on SaaS adoption (e.g., Low et al., 2011; Oliveira et al., 2014). This also holds for our third finding, namely that organizational readiness reduces the likelihood of SaaS adoption. Again, the existing studies on IT innovation adoption (e.g., Iacovou, Benbasat & Dexter, 1995; Low et al., 2011) and most studies on SaaS adoption (e.g., Gutierrez & Lumsden, 2014; Yen et al., 2013) assume that organizational readiness has a positive influence. We believe that this contradiction is caused in part by the nature of the innovation: SaaS is a service model that is highly suitable for organizations with few resources. Therefore, the extant theory regarding these variables should be revisited in light of recent IT innovations such as SaaS.

#

Future research on organizational size and organizational readiness in relation to IT innovation adoption should also explore the influence of different types of innovations. Finally, we found that several characteristics related to Indonesia as a developing country influenced the results, in particular, Indonesia’s collectivist culture with high power distance and the low reliability of its telecommunication infrastructure. This may explain why Indonesia is lagging behind in adopting SaaS. Although there are several studies on SaaS adoption in developing countries, more research into these and other specifi (cultural) characteristics is needed.

Beyond the theoretical implications, this study also has practical implications. First, our study findings highlight the importance of educating the Indonesian industry and making businesses aware of the possibilities of SaaS. The Indonesian government could play a large role in this, but also the SaaS providers in Indonesia and in similar developing countries may use this study to formulate better strategies for increasing SaaS adoption. Secondly, the strong positive influence of top management support on SaaS adoption suggests that appointment of managers with knowledge and favorable attitudes towards SaaS may be essential for adopting these IT innovations. Finally, multiple cases in our research with insufficient organizational readiness for IT innovation adoption have successfully adopted SaaS. Therefore, organizations can be advised not to necessarily reject adopting SaaS if they believe their organizational readiness to be low.

## Biographies

Inge van de Weerd is an assistant professor of the Knowledge, Information, and Innovation research group at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. She holds a PhD in information and computing sciences from Utrecht University, and she was a visiting scholar at the Tokyo Institute of Technology. Her research interests focus on the intersection of IT and organizations. Her work has been published in proceedings of conferences such as RE and CAiSE and appeared in international journals such as Information & Software Technology and Computers in Human Behavior.

Ivonne Sartika Mangula is a PhD student at the Department of Information and Computing Sciences of Utrecht University, the Netherlands. She has an interest in IT innovation adoption and diffusion in organizations, particularly in the adoption of software as a service. Her work has been published in several conferences such as PACIS and iiWAS.

Sjaak Brinkkemper is full professor of organization and information at the Department of Information and Computing Sciences of Utrecht University, the Netherlands. He leads a group of about 20 researchers specialized in product software development and entrepreneurship. The main research themes of the group are methodology of product software development, implementation and adoption, and business-economic aspects of the product software industry. Sjaak Brinkkemper has published about 10 books and more than 150 papers.
