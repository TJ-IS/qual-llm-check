ABSTRACT: Emerging economies present attractive opportunities to foreign firms. However, internationalization risk faced by foreign firms can have significant implications for their performance relative to local firms. Information Technology (IT) and IT-enabled capabilities help firms overcome internationalization risk and compete globally. Marketing Capability and Relational Capability also mitigate this risk through access to information related to markets and the business environment. We examine how foreign firms and local firms compare in leveraging synergies between such IT and firm capabilities. We focus on two kinds of IT-enabled capabilities: IT-enabled Flexibility in Customer Services, and IT-enabled Flexibility in Partner Services, and develop hypotheses for their complementary effects with Marketing Capability and Relational Capability respectively, to positively influence firm performance. We then draw on the firm-specific advantages framework to argue that foreign firms face a comparative disadvantage relative to local firms in leveraging the synergy between IT-enabled Flexibility in Customer Services and Marketing Capability. In contrast, foreign firms enjoy a comparative advantage relative to local firms in leveraging the synergy between IT-enabled Flexibility in Partner Services and Relational Capability. Empirical analysis using matched-pair survey and secondary data of 182 foreign and local firms in India supports our hypotheses. Our findings highlight important differences in how foreign and local firms benefit from IT, thus contributing towards a better understanding of how context and contingencies influence IT implications in emerging economies.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: emerging economies, foreign firms, internationalization risk, firm-specific advantage, firm performance, firm capabilities, IT-enabled capabilities, IT-enabled flexibility.

## Introduction

Firms from developed economies are striving to expand into emerging markets such as India, China, Brazil, and Russia. Emerging economies are attractive because of the revenue generating potential from a wide base of potential customers. For example, emerging Asian economies alone are projected to collectively account for 55 percent of global economic output by 2050 [75]. Recent economic developments in these emerging economies have increased consumers’ disposable incomes. For example, China and India have annual purchasing power of approximately \$13.5 trillion and \$5.8 trillion, respectively. In addition, the Internet and digital media have increased consumer awareness and raised aspirations for products and services available in developed economies. This untapped purchasing power and demand has lured foreign firms to emerging economies.

Despite these opportunities, emerging economies present salient challenges for both foreign and local firms. Their volatile operating environments pose more significant risks than developed economies due to complexities rooted in widely dispersed consumer segments, income disparities, differing consumer aspirations, and legacy settings [51, 68]. Many emerging countries suffer from poor quality infrastructure and distribution channels, inadequate regulations, and inefficient capital markets [104], which result in greater infrastructure, political, regulatory, customer, labor, and payment risks [60, 68]. Along with these issues, unique social institutions, power distances, and established relationships among local businesses raise additional risks for foreign firms [4]. Risks faced by foreign entrants when adapting to the ‘country specific economic, legal, political, institutional, and cultural differences’ of a host country’ [22, p. 195] arise because of lack of information about markets and environments, and are collectively termed internationalization risk in the international business literature [1].

Risk and uncertainty have a ubiquitous association with one another; reduction of uncertainty through access to information implies a reduction of risk [45].<sup>1</sup> Research demonstrates that when firms from more stable (less uncertain) and developed economies make downstream investments in less stable (more uncertain) and emerging economies, they face increased internationalization risk [50].<sup>2</sup> The fast-changing, complex business environment and consumer markets of emerging economies add to the uncertainty faced by foreign firms as standardized practices may not work in these environments. Instead, firm capabilities that reduce or manage uncertainty are critical to the success of both foreign and local firms [20]. These capabilities must enable firms to combat the complexities of institutional settings and subsequent internationalization risk associated with emerging economies [77]. The literature on international business suggests several capabilities used by firms to mitigate internationalization risk, out of which we focus on two salient capabilities: Marketing Capability and Relational Capability.

Marketing Capability helps mitigate internationalization risk as it enables access to information relevant to market practices, behaviors, and dynamics. Acquiring such information is not easy, and can be obtained only through presence, current activities, or current operations in a market [24, 102]. Thus, Marketing Capability reduces market uncertainty and thereby risk by acquisition of relevant information. Relational Capability addresses another aspect of internationalization risk, wherein business information that is not easily codified and predictable is “absorbed” by firms through intensive relationships with local partners [8]. Finally, the market efficiency perspective suggests that firms require flexibility to search freely and respond quickly to external changes when faced with uncertainty [37, 53]. Ergo, flexibility of the mechanisms through which market and business environment information are acquired or absorbed is also a critical capability in the emerging market contexts [1].

The internationalization risk faced by foreign firms can have significant implications for their performance relative to local firms in emerging economies. As such, studies in the academic literature report differences in performances of foreign firms and local firms [e.g., 20, 53].<sup>3</sup> Academic research in Information Systems (IS) recognizes the role that Information Technology (IT) plays in overcoming internationalization risks and helping firms compete globally [e.g., 62]. Thus, while Marketing Capability and Relational Capability may address salient aspects of internationalization risk and impact firm performance, considering the differing performance of foreign and local firms, IT may play different roles for the two types of firms. Ergo, an important question in the realm of IS is whether there exist differences between how foreign firms and local firms benefit from IT for firm performance. Although some studies suggest that foreign and local firms may differ in terms of how they benefit from IT [77],<sup>4</sup> extant literature provides little theoretical understanding regarding nuances regarding whether foreign firms face deficiencies or enjoy comparative advantages relative to local firms when leveraging IT. Our study helps fill this gap in the literature.<sup>5</sup> Identification of aspects of IT where foreign firms are either at a comparative disadvantage or advantage relative to local firms is insightful, because it will help both foreign and local firms to identify deficiency-reducing or strength-enhancing strategies using IT.

In this study, we begin to address this issue by focusing on one specific ITenabled capability: IT-enabled flexibility, defined as “the ability to quickly and economically adapt the IS applications to changing business requirements” [90, p. 326]. Earlier, we noted the importance of the flexibility of mechanisms for acquiring or absorbing market and business environment information [1]. We focus on ITenabled flexibility because IS applications enhance acquisition or absorption of information regarding the business environment and markets, and thus flexibility of IS applications is a critical enabler of adaptability in emerging economies and global environments [89]. Anecdotal evidence, such as the case of OlaCabs and Uber in India,<sup>6</sup> also supports the idea that foreign and local firms may differ in how they leverage IT-enabled flexibility.

Motivated by the challenges faced by firms in foreign markets and the identified gap in the literature, we ask the research question: How do foreign firms and local firms differ in leveraging their IT-enabled flexibilities and firm capabilities to improve firm performance? Because we examine how firms leverage IT to cope in foreign environments, we choose IT-enabled flexibilities that relate directly to how a firm interacts with key stakeholders in the environment - customers and partners [19, 26]. As such, reaching customers effectively and developing collaborations with partners are critical in foreign environments [2]. Thus, we define two kinds of IT-enabled flexibility. First, we define IT-enabled Flexibility in Customer Services (ITCS) as the firm’s ability to quickly and economically adapt its IT-enabled customer services to changing business requirements. For example, digital technologies used for data synchronization enable a retailer to flexibly adapt its customer services in its stores so that they are tailored to the needs of local customers [91]. Second, we define IT-enabled Flexibility in Partner Services (ITPS) as the firm’s ability to quickly and economically adapt its IT-enabled partner services to changing business requirements. For example, a firm can use an ITenabled marketplace to coordinate its transactions with multiple suppliers, enabling the firm to adapt to local business norms in its dealings with partners. Thus, ITCS and ITPS refer to the flexibility provided by IS applications in business functions of customer services and partner transaction services, respectively [90]. We posit that ITCS augments firm performance by complementing Marketing Capability, a firm capability that pertains to reaching out to customers. Similarly, we posit that ITPS augments firm performance by complementing Relational Capability, a firm capability that pertains to collaboration with external partners (e.g., suppliers) [9].

Finally, we use Rugman and Verbeke’s [83] Firm-Specific Advantage (FSA) framework from the International Business literature as the theoretical basis to offer our core argument - foreign firms have a relative disadvantage when it comes to leveraging the synergy between ITCS and Marketing Capability. In contrast, we argue that foreign firms enjoy a comparative advantage relative to local firms when it comes to leveraging the synergy between ITPS and Relational Capability. Specifically, we posit that the complementarity of ITCS with Marketing Capability is lower for foreign firms than for local firms because this is a locationbound FSA, whereas the complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability is higher for foreign firms than for local firms as it is a nonlocation-bound FSA. Location- and nonlocation-bound FSA here refer to the extent that capabilities and relevant advantages can be easily transferred across different “locations” of markets or business environments in which a firm is operating.

We test our hypotheses using primary matched-pair survey data as well as secondary data from 182 firms (both foreign and local) in India. Our empirical analysis includes using two-stage endogeneity correction modeling approaches and provides broad support for our hypotheses. Findings suggest that local firms are better able to leverage the complementarity of ITCS and Marketing Capability, whereas foreign firms are better able to leverage the complementarity between ITPS and Relational Capability. The study contributes to research by highlighting at a granular firm-capability level, how foreign firms and local firms enjoy different comparative advantages regarding synergies between specific IT-enabled flexibilities and firm capabilities. For managers of foreign and local firms, our study provides implications to help shape IT and capability portfolios effectively in emerging economies.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## Marketing and Relational Capabilities in Emerging Economies

Consumers in emerging economies are shifting their quality- and price-premiumbased purchasing behaviors to match those in developed countries. Underlying reasons are economic advancements, market transformations, and access to information through media and the Internet [67]. However, there is still a significant population of consumers in these countries who have a tolerance for low- or moderate-quality products and services, mainly ascribed to the prevailing indigenous vendors, suppliers, and intermediaries [70]<sup>7</sup>. In addition to the variance in quality and price aspirations of consumers, emerging economies are characterized by less formalized laws, lack of enforcement systems, corrupt regulatory bodies, and difficulty enforcing contracts [55, 60, 68].

Conditions in emerging economies often require businesses to have unique capabilities and complement them with appropriate strategies to be effective [70]. For example, to reach a highly dispersed low-income consumer base, firms need to be able to adapt marketing efforts to local languages and rural avenues such as local gatherings and village meetings. In other words, firms need to develop a Marketing Capability that facilitates access to markets and information regarding market practices. This capability thus addresses a key aspect of internationalization risk. In addition to market access and information, firms need to improve access to and affordability of products and services. For example, firms need to partner with local firms to sell products, employ local language-based support for helplines, and customize product distribution in line with local needs (e.g., transportation, logistics, and local sachet-based packaging firms). Thus, firms need to develop a Relational Capability that facilitates business-to-business relationships and access to business and information exchange regarding business norms, which helps overcome supply, support, and distribution challenges [38, 96]. This capability addresses the second aspect of internationalization risk.

In sum, we focus on marketing and relational capabilities because in emerging markets, reaching customers effectively and developing collaborations with external partners are critical components of success [2, 38, 96]. While customers are important sources of information related to product localization and market practices [91, 102], business partners provide resources and knowledge regarding operational details of local markets, and business practices and norms [53]. Prior research supports the assertion that marketing and relational capabilities are essential in emerging economies [47].

## Foreign Firms versus Local Firms

A large body of literature highlights that firms in foreign countries face a risk of internationalization (which local firms do not have to face) resulting from cultural, political, and economic differences between the host country and home country environments [1]. However, the literature conversely also suggests that foreign firms enjoy some advantages relative to local firms.<sup>8</sup> As such, studies in this stream argue that foreign firms possess firm-specific advantages over local firms that confer comparative advantages to foreign firms relative to local firms [79, 80]. The tension between these two contrary perspectives has been highlighted by studies which affirm that under certain environmental conditions, specific firm capabilities confer comparative advantages, whereas other environment-capability configurations result in comparative disadvantage [1, 20, 53]. In our study, we examine this dialectic tension in the purview of IT business value by using the framework of Firm-Specific Advantage (FSA) [83] as the edifice to build our theoretical arguments.

The FSA framework has been theoretically developed over a large body of work (e.g., [79-85]). Overall, the FSA framework is a resource-based interpretation that covers a broad set of unique firm competencies and capabilities which have been conceptualized in more detail through the resource-based view of the firm [83]. FSAs are of two types – location-bound and nonlocation-bound, and are defined as “knowledge bundles that take the form of intangible assets, capabilities, and relationships with outside actors” [84, p. 127]. FSAs can be combined with other location-bound FSAs or nonlocation-bound FSAs, or exploited on a standalone basis, without recombination with other FSAs [78]. Location-bound FSAs require significant adaptation to be used in other locations and hence are not easily transferrable. Hence, location-bound FSAs provide benefits only at the specific location (or set of locations), such as a national market, within which the FSA is located. In contrast, nonlocation-bound FSAs can provide benefits across multiple locations because they can be transferred at low marginal cost without substantial adaptation. Nonlocation-bound FSAs can lead to efficiencies derived from economies of scale and scope. Location-bound FSAs typically consist of infrastructure, local monopolies, or local know-how, whereas nonlocation-bound FSAs may reflect functional, production-related proprietary assets and knowhow or organizational capabilities for efficient control and coordination of assets [83-85].

FSAs have been used in prior research to develop organizing frameworks to explain internationalization processes and diffusion [83]. When applied to foreign and local firms, the FSA framework suggests that for a firm to achieve a satisfactory economic performance in a foreign country, it must possess at least one nonlocation-bound FSA that is easily transferable across borders as an intermediate product. Conversely, for a local firm to achieve a satisfactory economic performance in its host country, it must possess at least one location-bound FSA.

## IT Flexibility for Customer Services and Partner Collaborations

IT flexibility is a topic of importance in the IS research discipline. Due to the increasing structural and behavioral complexity of the environment and business systems, the flexibility of an information system is critical to its long-term viability.<sup>10</sup> Along with the functional (e.g., production, distribution) and nonfunctional requirements (e.g., data security, Business-IT Alignment, IT availability), flexibility is considered a major component of an information system. Presence of IT Flexibility ensures that the information system remains able to address and fit both the environment and business systems. Thus, the conceptualization of IT Flexibility anchors to the overall ideas regarding business flexibility, which is understood as the capability to adapt or respond to environmental changes. Some researchers have extended this conceptualization to incorporate specific kinds of flexibility, such as strategic flexibility, operational flexibility, scope flexibility, speed flexibility or structural flexibility, into their conceptualization of IT Flexibility [37, 65]. Overall, IT flexibility is the notion of a firm’s IT to help it be agile and adapt to contextual complexities [89].

The discussion on IT flexibility in the information systems literature has focused on understanding both antecedents and consequences of IT flexibility on a firm’s performance and market dynamics. The literature has often alluded to at least two perspectives of IT flexibility: functional IT flexibility and architectural (infrastructural) IT flexibility. Functional IT flexibility refers to IT configurations that support business process-focused flexibility and its potential impact on a firm’s crossfunctional and cross-organizational capabilities such as digital reach [89] and partner-enabled knowledge creation [58]. Architectural (or infrastructural) IT flexibility is enabled by the connectivity, compatibility, and modularity of IT infrastructure [74] as it supports a firm’s response to environmental demands (for a review, see [10]). The more modular and configurable the components of IT infrastructure are and technology-agnostic their connectivity is with the rest of the interfacing systems, the greater the architectural IT flexibility. The literature has argued that IT flexibility can enhance business flexibility in adapting to market place changes. For example, functional IT flexibility can influence both manufacturing flexibility and flexibility in service-oriented firms [103]. Architectural IT flexibility can help a firm develop options to meet differing customer needs, while providing an integrated framework for functional IT systems to influence organizational performance [100]. Firms can use IT to quickly sense change and adapt business priorities [10, 21, 49, 97]. Such flexibility can help achieve cross-functional capabilities [89], enhance the ability to meet differing customer needs [100] and build inter-firm relationships [90].

An underlying theme across this research is that the concepts used to explain flexibility (e.g., adaption, change, or response) lie at a high level of abstraction. However, some IS researchers define IT Flexibility concepts as applied to specific objects, contexts, and considerations, such as manufacturing flexibility, IT infrastructure flexibility, or business process flexibility [10, 21, 97]. We follow this approach to define and operationalize IT Flexibility across customer and partner services.

The flexibility of IT (through which market and business environment information are acquired or absorbed) is critical for firms facing internationalization risk [1]. Specific to emerging economies, research suggests that IT can compensate for poor infrastructure [7], and some examples of IT-enabled flexibilities indeed validate this notion. For instance, in many emerging markets, cell phones compensate for lack of landline telephone access in rural areas and are enabling unique services such as money transfers and agricultural intermediation services. Similarly, GPS (global positioning system) and cellular monitoring systems help manage delays due to local distribution and logistics issues. In addition, IT-based communication strategy is instrumental to operations of business process service providers (serving local needs) in many emerging countries. While IT has helped provide efficiency in transactions and communications, firms in emerging markets can also leverage IT to offset local challenges and more effectively manage customers, operations, and supply chains [42, 62].

We focus on two kinds of IT-enabled flexibilities. As noted earlier, ITCS refers to the flexibility and adaptability provided by the firm’s IT-enabled customer services. ITPS helps to orient partner related transaction services. These two IT-enabled flexibilities have important roles to play in emerging economies. Due to diversity and differentiated market scope, a single IT solution may not be adequate. For example, providing only a cell-phone based payment system by a bank is not sufficient to meet the needs of all customers. The bank needs to have kiosks, ATMs, and paper-to-digital hybrid payment systems—as a significant portion of the customer base would adhere to the paper-based system. Alternate solutions that suit local needs is the basis of ITCS—that how flexible the firm is in appropriating different IT channels and avenues to customers. Similarly, a big box store depends on a range of IT-enabled coordination systems for distributors’ trucks, such as fax, cell phone, and real-time monitoring systems. These coordination systems form essential elements of the flexibility dimension of ITPS for the store. How these kinds of IT-enabled flexibility complement firm capabilities to improve performance, and how foreign and local firms differ in leveraging these complementarities remains unexplored. Next, we conceptualize our research model and build our hypotheses.

## Moderating Effects of ITCS and ITPS

We posit that ITCS complements the link between Marketing Capability and firm performance. Three reasons support this argument. First, effective marketing processes and capabilities uncover latent needs of existing and potential customers. ITCS can enable quick and adaptable responses to customer inquiries and needs, thereby facilitating conversion of potential opportunities. Second, ITCS helps firms rapidly develop new scalable relationships with profitable customers. This complements the deep understanding of customer needs gained through marketing capability [64, 71]. For example, CEMEX, a cement manufacturer, opened up new unserved markets by implementing digital customer service systems that enabled flexibility in meeting customer needs and broadened the customer base [13].

Third, superior Marketing Capability enables firms to sense individualized needs of customers [41]. ITCS complements these targeted marketing efforts enabled by superior marketing capability by facilitating flexible and adaptable responses to individualized needs of targeted customers [47], thereby realizing greater benefit from marketing campaigns [74]. For example, when a marketing department runs an outbound campaign, flexible IT-enabled customer services can effectively field queries, provide support, and meet diverse requirements. In sum, we posit that ITCS complements a firm’s marketing capabilities because it enables firms to quickly respond to customer inquiries and needs, identify and cater to profitable customers, and meet individualized customer needs for targeted marketing. Hence, formally:

Hypothesis 1a: ITCS has a complementary effect on the relationship between marketing capability and firm performance.

We next hypothesize a complementarity effect of ITPS with Relational Capability. First, superior Relational Capability increases the visibility of changes in demand and environment conditions due to relationship-specific information, knowledge flows, and information sharing arising from collaboration with partners [72]. ITPS helps firms to meet new requirements arising from anticipated changes in demand and environment conditions in a scalable and rapid manner. ITPS also helps reduce process inefficiencies resulting from lack of process flexibility and transparency across business partners [12].

Second, benefits from Relational Capability can be enhanced and sustained if firms also possess an ability to continue these collaborative business relationships in the face of environmental changes. ITPS enables firms to continue and develop business relationships by facilitating changes in requirements of partner transaction services and scale and scope of delivery. Thus, ITPS fuels rapid development of new business relationships made possible by changes in environmental conditions. Third, strong relational capability provides an ability to offer customized solutions to value chain partners [95]. For example, a luxury clothing brand with strong relational capability can provide training services to field agents of its franchise partners customized to specific localized needs of each partner. ITPS complements Relational Capability by accommodating changes in customized solutions in a quick and scalable manner. For instance, training services can be quickly contextualized to time and geography. In sum, we argue that ITPS helps meet changes in demand and environmental conditions, build new business relationships, and develop customized solutions for business partners. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis 1b: ITPS has a complementary effect on the relationship between relational capability and firm performance.

## Foreign versus Local Firms in Leveraging ITCS and Marketing Capability

Our core argument is that firm-specific advantages of foreign and local firms will play out differently in the context of leveraging synergies between IT-enabled flexibility and firm capabilities, depending on the type of IT-enabled flexibility or firm capability in question. The underlying logic for our argument is founded on the premise that the origins and experiences of foreign firms and local firms, and the nature of their firm-specific advantages, affect their ability to respond to and address challenges in emerging markets. While local firms are intimately and indigenously acquainted with local settings, foreign firms start with developedcountry experiences (involving business models and operations). Thus, the context of emerging economies where the formulation of adaptive strategies is critical [27] is not only a fertile empirical context to test our hypotheses but also an excellent context to frame our arguments.

Recall that per the FSA framework, nonlocation-bound FSAs are easily transferable across locations; consequently, they are relatively easy to diffuse internally and can be exploited globally. Location-bound FSAs face high transfer costs and thus are difficult to diffuse internally, but can be exploited locally [83]. Note that the “location-boundedness” of an FSA is dependent upon both the context and the content of the FSA [78]. Three issues require attention: the “nature” of the competency, capability, or resource from which the advantage is derived, the “stickiness” of the source location, and the “receptiveness” of the target location [83]. For example, an FSA derived from a manufacturing facility is location-bound because the nature of the resource does not allow it to be transferred across geographical borders. In contrast, an FSA derived from relationships with government agents is location-bound as it is “sticky” to the host location and hence not transferable. Alternatively, an FSA derived from a mobile-based software application may be location-bound because the target location lacks an adequate mobile broadband infrastructure to support the application.

We assert that the complementarity of ITCS with Marketing Capability is a location-bound FSA in the context of emerging economies. Marketing Capability helps foreign firms overcome the market information and access aspect of internationalization risk [1]. Knowledge of the market comprises of objective knowledge and experiential knowledge. While the former is explicit and transferable, the latter is not transferable as it is tacit and market-specific because it concerns information regarding practices and behaviors required to succeed in a market. Acquisition of this information is primarily through current activities, is path dependent, and suffers from time compression diseconomies [24]. Similarly, access to a market and its consumers is market-specific due to contextualization requirements [85]. Hence the synergy of ITCS with Marketing Capability is locationbound due to its stickiness with the source location or location-specificity and low receptivity of the target location.<sup>11</sup>

Thus, we posit that the complementarity of ITCS with Marketing Capability is less pronounced for foreign firms than for local firms, for three reasons. First, cultural issues arise from lack of familiarity with the local market. A variety of unique customer needs, distinct from their home market, hinder the potential returns that foreign firms can achieve in emerging markets [31]. Local firms have a better understanding than do foreign firms of culture-based characteristics such as customer needs and value systems of local customers [3]. We propose that this lack of familiarity on the part of foreign firms concerning understanding of local consumers in emerging economies contributes to the location-boundedness of the FSA derived from synergies between ITCS and Marketing Capability. Furthermore, foreign firms are likely to face different kinds of customers due to different attitudes and lifestyles of consumers and consumer demand patterns in foreign markets [36]. The relative lack of understanding of local consumers compared to their more knowledgeable local competitors hinders foreign firms from fully leveraging the ITCS-Marketing Capability synergy.

Second, local firms may develop ITCS that is customized to the context and expectations of local customers because local firms are more knowledgeable about customers’ IT usage preferences and constraints. For example, local firms may contextualize their electronic stores or digital customer service channels by providing offline options because they know that local customers may want a more “physical” and “intimate” channel to interact. Thus, local firms take a more localized approach by taking into account the local environment [87] in aligning ITCS to complement their Marketing Capability and will be able to realize more benefits by better leveraging ITCS compared to foreign firms.

The more localized choices of ITCS have more influence in complementing Marketing Capability for local firms because they directly influence how customers respond to marketing efforts and are attracted to local firms versus foreign firms.<sup>12</sup> Likewise, foreign firms face challenges in bringing customers to IT-enabled customer service channels, such as chat-on-demand services, for which local customers may not be ready or may have difficulties with IT access. Such challenges for foreign firms in reaping benefits of ITCS imply that it is a location-bound FSA, which stems from a relative lack of understanding of the culture, mindset, and needs of local customers and consequent difficulty deploying appropriate customized IT to suit local markets [106]. Furthermore, the internal processes, organizational routines, communication and control systems, and organizational structures of local firms are developed within the unstable environment of the emerging economy [53]. Hence, local firms have higher capacity to process market information and manage change in comparison to foreign firms.

Third, local firms are more likely than foreign firms to develop deep relationships with customers. For example, India favors a clan culture entailing tradition, trust, commitment, and long-term orientation. Local firms share this long-term orientation and hence develop enduring relationships with customers.<sup>13</sup> On the contrary, foreign firms have different time expectations as they are market-oriented and competitive, resulting in a short-term, results-oriented culture [53]. Also, in emerging markets, customers rely more on experience-based than IT-based purchasing. They may prefer to spend more time in a store interacting with shopkeepers developing a bond that is crucial to the purchasing decision process. This “corner shop experience” goes beyond only shopping to encompass family-level or villagelevel connections [5]. Foreign firms tend to use market-based, impersonal transactions, and institutions [8] whereas local firms are more accustomed to coordinating market exchanges by developing connections with customers. Such differentiation helps local firms in having a greater understanding of customers relative to foreign firms that, in turn, helps local firms to leverage the synergy between ITCS and Marketing Capability better. Our reasoning resonates with prior research that it is difficult for firms to do well in emerging economies without understanding informal institutions or relationships that affect customers [109]. Moreover, because local firms are more likely to have developed such ties and social ties help firms overcome weak institutional infrastructures of emerging economies, local firms are better positioned than foreign firms to reap benefits of exploiting personal connections with customers [93].<sup>14</sup>

In sum, foreign firms have a comparative disadvantage when leveraging synergies between ITCS and Marketing Capability. Formally, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2a: Compared with local firms, foreign firms experience a lower complementary effect of ITCS on the relationship between marketing capability and firm performance.

## Foreign versus Local Firms in Leveraging ITPS and Relational Capability

We argue that foreign firms will have a comparative advantage relative to local firms in leveraging the synergy between ITPS and Relational Capability. The fundamental idea behind this assertion is that the complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability is a nonlocation-bound FSA in the context of emerging economies. Relational Capability helps foreign firms overcome internationalization risk by enabling the firm to “absorb” information regarding the business environment [53]. Similar to building ties with customers, tie building with business partners is also experiential and market-specific. However, emerging economies like India are highly receptive to this FSA due to the perceived opportunities of partnering with foreign firms. Because they use market-based transactions and institutions, foreign firms are perceived to pay better, pay more regularly, and offer access to foreign markets [53]. Thus, foreign firms are likely to extract favorable terms, protections, opportunities, and resources from Relational Capability. Furthermore, the global scope of foreign firms implies that they have greater experience with established global partnerships. International operations also enhance the ability to arbitrage operations across countries and leverage location-based advantages [98]. Firms that have a global scope would have developed skills in managing international partners. Such accumulated experience helps when operating in a foreign country. Hence synergy of ITPS with Relational Capability is nonlocation-bound due to its global applicability and receptivity of emerging economies.

Accordingly, we suggest three reasons why the complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability is greater for foreign firms. First, unlike developed economies where computers are considered essential for doing business, there is a relative lack of such culture in emerging economies [40]. Thus, although local firms may have deployed sophisticated ITPS, their ability to leverage ITPS to complement their Relational Capability may be hindered due to their inherent preference for non-IT based exchanges (e.g., in-person meetings, phone calls) with partners. For example, in India, while most foreign automobile manufacturers derive flexibility in transactional services by managing relationships with suppliers through online portals, most local firms do not leverage such technology.

Second, due to their pre-existing relationships with global suppliers, the synergy between ITPS and Relational Capability is a nonlocation-bound FSA for foreign firms. Foreign firms have established partnerships for sourcing inputs from other global firms. Such partnerships already have built-in efficient mechanisms that strengthen the ability of ITPS to complement Relational Capability. Furthermore, because importing inputs provides opportunities for transfer pricing which would be lost with local sourcing, foreign firms would prefer to continue importing inputs, unless the price of a local substitute is well below the import price [53]. Thus, foreign firms have higher bargaining power over their business partners and can enforce the use of IT and therefore leverage ITPS.

Third, compared to local firms, foreign firms have more global experience in managing business-to-business IT-based interactions and the global applicability of this experience results in the complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability being a nonlocation-bound FSA. Although foreign firms may be new to a specific emerging market, they may have already developed IT-based business-to-business transactional platforms in similar emerging economies, giving them the necessary insights to leverage such relationships through IT, enabling them to benefit from ITPS to complement their Relational Capability. For example, Hewlett-Packard (HP) has pre-established global partnerships that are more efficient than local partnerships. Hence HP can establish effective IT-based relationships with new suppliers’ due to its prior experience in doing so. Conversely, local firms may not be able to leverage ITPS to complement their Relational Capability as effectively due to their reliance on a traditional way of conducting business and existing loyal relationships to manage business-to-business transactions, which are often inefficient [93].<sup>15</sup>

In summary, foreign firms have a comparative advantage relative to local firms when it comes to leveraging synergies between ITPS and Relational Capability.<sup>16</sup> Formally, we posit:

Hypothesis 2b: Compared with local firms, foreign firms experience a greater complementary effect of ITPS on the relationship between relational capability and firm performance.

## Methods

## Data and Variables

We collected data for this study from multiple sources. We conducted a matchedpair cross-sectional field survey of foreign and local firms in India that was complemented with archival data from secondary sources. Our research context, India, suits our research purpose for at least three reasons. First, by virtue of being a highly complex and dynamic emerging economy, there is ex-ante reason to suppose that the level of internationalization risk for foreign firms in India is likely to be higher than that in most developed countries [50], attributable principally to nontransparent government policies, peculiar business culture, and differing societal forces [38]. Second, the role of complementary resources is particularly salient in the context of weaker market-supporting institutions typical of emerging economies that are characterized by less formalized laws and institutions [55]. Third, in recent decades India has seen substantial inflows of foreign direct investment, based in large part on government financial incentives for foreign firms to locate in India and to expand existing Indian production. Thus, India provides a rich context for examining our research questions. To minimize confounding factors due to uneven economic development, our sample drew from a verified compilation of 749 firms (identified in three industry and city directories) located near the commercial hub of Mumbai and Pune in western India.<sup>17</sup>

We collected multi-respondent matched data via a research firm that conducted a survey from December 2013 to February 2014 through in-person visits, phone calls, and e-mails.<sup>18</sup> The multi-respondent matched-pair design of data collected from distinct and independent sources reduces the chance of common method bias [69]. This design also allows us to use items that are suitable to each respondent’s domain knowledge [43]. We collected each control variable from both respondents;<sup>19</sup> these responses demonstrated significant positive correlations.

We collected our dependent variable (firm performance) from the Prowess database, which is a secondary data source compiled by the Centre for Monitoring the Indian Economy. Prowess contains data from income statements and balance sheets of firms in organized sectors in India. This database is often used for analysis of firms in India [44]. Specifically, we use Return on Assets (ROA), a widely used firm performance measure in prior studies in the IS literature [73] and studies of foreign and local firms in the Indian context [20]. ROA is also relatively resistant to financial manipulations of management [32]. We used ROA for the financial year 2015-2016, which is one year after the survey. <sup>20</sup> Consistent with similar approaches in prior IT business value studies [61], a oneyear lag between independent and dependent variables ensures temporal precedence and strengthens causal inference. To facilitate triangulation of results, our survey also collected firm performance data from executives in the strategy function, as executive perceptions have been argued to be good proxies of objective firm performance [99]. We collected control variables at the firm and industry level from the Prowess database.

Table A2 in the Online Appendix A describes all variables and survey questions.<sup>21</sup> Consistent with prior research [52], we defined a firm as a foreign firm (binary variable Foreign) if its corporate headquarters is located outside the host country (India). Marketing Capability measures the planning, resourcing, strategy, and programs related to the development of the firm’s marketing capability, using a six-item instrument from prior research [63]. Relational Capability is measured on a six-item instrument focusing on the firm’s collaboration with external partners [9]. Finally, ITCS and ITPS are each four-item instruments adapted from prior research, capturing the extents to which IT-enabled customer services and IT-enabled partner transaction services can be adapted quickly to changes in business requirements [90]. As noted by Saraf et al. [90], these measures broadly capture the operational (business requirements), tactical (scalability), and strategic (changing products or relationships) aspects of flexibility. All variables exhibited sufficient reliability, with Cronbach alphas above recommended thresholds [66].

We include a variety of control variables. Firm Size accounts for scale [43]. IT capital helps account for IT resources [18]. Firm Age captures the number of years since the firm established in India and accounted for learning-curve influences [43]. To the extent that Age represents the length of time that the firm has established in the country (India), it also captures the presence of long-lived assets in the country. Liquidity, Leverage, and Capital Intensity account for the firm’s well-being, debt structure, and capital structure, respectively [30, 76, 98]. Wagebill is a proxy for skill level of employees [15]. Prior Performance controls for effects of prior performance.<sup>22</sup> International Scope (number of countries in which the firm operates) controls for experience in operating abroad [98]. The variables Exports and Imports respectively measure the extent of exports and raw material (inputs) imported by the firm, and control for the effect that international strategies may have on firm performance [56, 94]. R&D Intensity controls for the possibility for R&D to influence firm performance [73]. Finally, we include industry-level controls: industry competition, industry-average performance, and industry sector [17, 59].

After dropping incomplete responses and outliers, our sample comprises 182 firms from a range of industries such as manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, and consumer goods. Of the 182 firms, 94 are foreign, and 88 are local. Table 1 shows descriptive stats and correlations.

We take two approaches to facilitate triangulation of our results. First, in addition to using ROA as the dependent variable, we tested our hypotheses using firm performance measure collected through the survey. Our survey captured financial performance, measured with a four-item instrument reflecting the performance of the firm over the past three years [71]. The results remain qualitatively similar when using the survey measure of financial performance, Second, we also tested our hypotheses using Marketing Intensity, collected from the Prowess database, as a proxy for Marketing Capability [46]. Results were unchanged.<sup>23</sup>

## Robustness Tests: Accounting for Endogeneity

A potential concern is that ITCS and ITPS might be endogenous due to unobserved factors that affect ITCS and ITPS as well as performance. For example, if managers of high-performing firms possess personality traits that make them prone to invest in IT that provides ITCS and ITPS, it could be a source of endogeneity. To address this concern, we re-estimated the models accounting for endogeneity, using three approaches. First, we use the Garen methodology [28] which is a residual analysis technique to correct for selection bias and is used in several other studies [e.g., 88]. The rationale for the selection mechanism is that ITCS and ITPS may be endogenous as firms may self-select into high values of these two variables based on factors, some observable to researchers (e.g., firm size), and others unobservable (e.g., managerial traits). If such potential endogeneity is not accounted for, estimates can be inconsistent. Garen [28] provides a generalization of Heckman’s two-stage estimator [35] and accounts for the continuous nature of the selection variable. Accordingly, following the approach of Bharadwaj et al. [6], we created a variable which is the sum of standardized ITCS and ITPS variables. Intuitively, this variable (labeled ITSum) represents the level of ITCS and ITPS in the firm. Then, we estimated the first stage estimation regressing ITSum on factors likely to impact ITCS and ITPS (equation 4). We used generalized least squares to account for heteroscedasticity [28]. We calculated residuals $\tilde { \eta }$ from the first-stage and included $\tilde { \boldsymbol \eta }$ and the interaction term $\tilde { \eta } \times I T S u m$ as endogeneity correction terms in the performance equation (equation 5). ῆ corrects for selection bias, and $\tilde { \eta } \times I T S u m$ accounts for unobserved heterogeneity over the range of the selection variable [28]. The equations are:

$$
\text { Stage   1 }: \mathrm{ITSum} = \beta_ {\mathrm{a}} + \beta_ {\mathrm{r}} \mathrm{W} + \eta\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Stage 2:} Y = \mathrm{f} (\text {independent variables, interaction terms}, \tilde {\eta}, \tilde {\eta} \\ \times \text {ITSum, control variables}, \varepsilon) \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where Y is ROA or Performance, W is the vector of variables in the first stage, η and ε are error terms, and $\tilde { \boldsymbol \eta }$ is an estimate of residuals from the first stage estimation. Firm and industry characteristics are used as regressors in the first stage.<sup>24</sup> Table A3 (in Online Appendix A) shows estimates of the first-stage equation. Table 3 shows estimates of the second stage of the model (equation 5). Endogeneity is addressed by including endogeneity correction terms calculated from the first stage [28]. Results are qualitatively similar to the main results, suggesting that they are robust to endogeneity.

Second, we followed the approach suggested by Heckman [35] and used in prior research [6]. Consistent with Shaver [92], we separate our sample firms into two groups: firms with scores above the mean on the sum of the standardized ITCS and ITPS variables, coded as 1, and firms below the mean on the sum, coded as 0. Intuitively, this binary variable (which we label HIGHIT) represents a high level of ITCS and ITPS in the firm. In this approach, endogeneity is addressed by calculating the inverse mills ratio (IMR) using estimates from the first stage and including the IMR term in the second-stage equation as an additional predictor [6, 92].

We compute the IMR variable using estimates from the first stage. We calculate the IMR as $\mathrm { I M R } = { \cal O } ( \beta _ { \mathrm { r } } { } ^ { * } \mathrm { W } ) / \Phi ( \beta _ { \mathrm { r } } { } ^ { * } \mathrm { W } )$ if $H I G H I T = 1$ and $\mathrm { I M R } = { - \theta ( \beta _ { \mathrm { r } } { ^ * \mathrm { W } } ) } / [ 1 - \Phi$ $( \beta _ { \mathrm { r } } { } ^ { * } \mathrm { W } ) ]$ if $H I G H I T = 0$ , where W and $\beta _ { \mathrm { r } }$ are, respectively, the vectors of independent variables and estimated coefficients from the first stage probit model, Ø denotes standard normal distribution function, and $\Phi$ denotes cumulative standard normal distribution function [6, 33, 92]. In the second stage, we include the IMR term as an additional control variable. This additional term appears because of potential endogeneity of ITCS and ITPS; namely, unobserved factors may influence ITCS and ITPS, and so there is potential for endogeneity [6, 33, 92].

## Additional Robustness Tests

Although we used the two-stage approach, which alleviates concerns of endogeneity, we quantitatively assess the concern of potential confounding variables impacting our results. We follow the method suggested by Frank [25] to calculate the threshold at which such a confounding variable would nullify the significance of our key focal variables of interest (the interaction terms). This approach has been applied in past studies to mitigate concerns of unmeasured confounding variables [39]. Frank [25] defined the impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) on an estimated regression coefficient as the level of impact necessary to invalidate the inference of the regression coefficient. The ITCV determines the minimum level of correlation required between the confounding variable and the focal variable, and between the confounding variable and the dependent variable in order to nullify the significance of the estimated coefficient [25]. A higher level of ITCV implies that the regression coefficient is more robust to omitted variable concerns. Using the estimates from our analysis and the ITCVapproach [25], the ITCV for the interaction of ITCS × Marketing Capability interaction is 0.11, with the threshold correlations of 0.39 and 0.28 between the confounding variable and the interaction term, and between the confounding variable and the dependent variable respectively before conditioning on the covariates. The ITCV for the ITPS × Relational Capability coefficient is 0.13 with threshold correlations of 0.43 and 0.31 between the confounding variable and the interaction term, and between the confounding variable and the dependent variable respectively, before conditioning on the covariates.

Likewise, the respective threshold correlations for the ITCS × Marketing Capability × Foreign coefficient are -0.41 and 0.42. The respective threshold correlations for the ITPS × Relational Capability × Foreign coefficient are 0.42 and 0.37. These values of thresholds for correlations are quite high. To put these correlation thresholds in perspective, among all the variables in our regression, the largest correlation between any of the independent variables and ROA is 0.38, and the largest correlation between any of the control variables with the focal interactions is 0.24. Therefore, a confounding variable that simultaneously exceeds these high correlation thresholds is very unlikely. Moreover, the estimates of the ITCV are conservative because their computation assumes that the confounding variable is not correlated with any of the existing covariates [25]. As a result, to the extent that any confounding variable is likely to also correlate with covariates other than the focal interaction terms, the ITCV of the interaction terms would be even higher [25]. In sum, the ITCV comparisons mitigate concerns of an omitted confounding variable that would significantly falsify effects of the focal interaction variables in our study.

In combination, the inclusion of an extensive set of control variables (12 controls plus industry dummies), the three approaches to address endogeneity, as well as the quantification of the impact threshold of confounding variable (which were found to be quite high), address potential endogeneity concerns in an elaborate way, given what is permissible in the context of the study.<sup>25</sup>

## Discussion

This study has two main findings, consistent across a variety of estimation approaches. First, we find that while ITCS has a positive moderating effect on the relationship between Marketing Capability and firm performance for all firms, the benefit of ITCS is weaker for foreign firms than for local firms. These findings suggest that the complementarity of ITCS with Marketing Capability is a locationbound FSA and foreign firms face a comparative disadvantage relative to local firms when it comes to leveraging IT-enabled flexibility for complementing firm capabilities on the customer side of the business. Second, and in contrast, we find that while ITPS has a positive moderating effect on the relationship between Relational Capability and firm performance for all firms, the effect is stronger for foreign firms than for local firms. This suggests that complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability is a nonlocation-bound FSA and foreign firms enjoy a comparative advantage relative to local firms in leveraging IT-enabled flexibility for complementing firm capabilities on the partner side of the business.

## Contributions to Research

Our study makes primary contributions to two broad thematic areas of research. First, the study contributes to the enduring, yet growing, research conversation around the role of IT in internationalization [42, 62] by overcoming internationalization risk [1] through access to market and environmental information. Our study validates that leveraging of global resources (ITPS) or customizing of local resources (ITCS) are alternative strategic choices that foreign firms should consider for competing in emerging economies [8]. Our findings extend prior research on foreign and local firm differences to the purview of Information Systems by uncovering how foreign and local firms differ in leveraging synergies between IT-enabled capabilities (ITCS and ITPS) and firm capabilities (Marketing Capability and Relational Capability). Specifically, our study brings together the foreign-local firm literature and IT-business value literature by employing the Firm Specific Advantage framework [83] to address the question of how IT payoff differs for foreign and local firms. The FSA framework itself is an important artifact that can serve as a theoretical edifice for future studies of cross-national IS issues, a rapidly growing stream of work [51]. Our study regarding neglected differences in synergies between IT and firm capabilities for local and foreign firms can spur future research to study how digital artifacts support business processes differently across different ownership categories. Most importantly, by integrating contextual logics and variations into our theory – an approach that has been advocated in the International Business literature [60], we develop nuanced theoretical ideas, and thereby demonstrate the importance of context-based IS theory in uncovering unique insights in emerging economies.

Second, our study contributes to the IT flexibility literature in two ways: a) by taking a nuanced approach to implications of IT-enabled flexibilities in specific business functions, and b) by showing implications of specific IT-enabled flexibilities in complementing specific firm capabilities are different for foreign firms and local firms. By examining the implications of IT-enabled flexibilities in specific business areas (customer services and partner services), this study builds on research that studied IT flexibility more generally [89, 90]. Prior research identified “a need to assess the impact of some contingent factors on the IT-agility relationship” [64, p. 163]. Our study is a step toward addressing this call by uncovering the effect of contingent factors (foreign versus local) on this relationship.

## Managerial Implications

Our findings are of significant managerial importance. As firms in a globalized world seek to operate in foreign countries, competing with local firms effectively is a key managerial concern. While relevant in all foreign markets, this concern is of salient importance in emerging economies where foreign firms are confronted with substantively more barriers. Research suggests that foreign firms can mitigate internationalization risk through astute hiring of host-country nationals with international experience who possess market and environmental information (as compared to expatriates lacking host-country experience and understanding) [53]. However, merely hiring local senior and top managers is inadequate. IT is a critical component of strategies to compete with local firms. Our findings imply that foreign firms need to be particularly vigilant when using IT to complement their firm capabilities on the customer side of the business because of the relative disadvantages they face relative to local firms. As such, this implies that foreign firms may need to take other remedial measures to develop an enhanced knowledge of local customers, cultural characteristics, deep relationships with customers, and contextualized localized approaches to customer-based IT systems. Samsung has applied such measures to achieve success across multiple product markets in India. These remedies can arguably help foreign firms put their Marketing Capability and ITCS to more fruitful use by attracting local customers.

Although our findings paint a gloomy picture for foreign firms using ITCS to complement firm capabilities on the customer side of the business, the picture is rosier on the partner side. Our findings suggest that foreign firms enjoy comparative advantages relative to local firms while leveraging ITPS. Thus, firms seeking to compete with local firms should build on their strengths in leveraging IT on the partner side. We had argued that these strengths stem from more global experience and networks with global partners, as exemplified by the case of Honda in India. Our findings suggest that foreign firm managers can build on the strengths that amplify the role of ITPS in leveraging Relational Capability.

For managers of local firms, the implications of our findings are a mirror image of those for foreign firms. A key implication for local firm managers is that they can use ITCS to build on their strengths of superior knowledge of local customers while marketing. This may help them to defend against the threat posed by foreign firms, as Amul has successfully defended against foreign entrants in the dairy products market in India. Local firms need to be cognizant of foreign firms entering these markets, as they may prove to be a disrupting force. For example, when cell phone providers enter emerging markets, they bring differentiated value-added services that provide them with a winning strategy against local competitors. Our findings suggest that local firms need to build up their strengths on the partner side of the business so that they do not fall behind foreign firms who have a comparative advantage in leveraging ITPS due to their generally higher global experience. Though prior research suggests that local firms should develop Relational Capability that is rooted in systems and processes, rather than personal connections, our findings suggest that their comparative disadvantage in enhancing performance through this route lies not in the Relational Capability itself, but in its synergy with ITPS. SBI Life can serve as an exemplar for local firms in this regard. Overall, our findings provide implications for both foreign and local firms to help develop IT strategy portfolios.

Finally, given the importance of foreign direct investment to various countries and its impact on industrial structure and competition, how the performance of foreign firms compares to local firms is of considerable interest to policymakers. Our findings show that IT is one explanation for different performance levels of foreign and local firms. More broadly, the study contributes to a better understanding of IT in emerging economies.

## Limitations

Our study suffers from three limitations, which must be considered when assessing our results. First, although India is good empirical context for our study, the generalizability of our result beyond emerging economies may be limited. Though our findings may hold in transitional or developed economies, the theoretical mechanisms and the contextual nuances therein will necessarily be different.<sup>26</sup> Second, our study surveyed respondents on only one side of inter-firm dyads. We are unable to assess how firms’ ITCS or ITPS influence customers or partners. Perhaps the effects on the other side of the dyads are different — an interesting possibility. Third, we are unable to draw causal conclusions through our analysis. Though we took care to measure the independent variable with a lag to the independent variables and found similar results across three approaches that account for endogeneity, enhancing confidence in our findings, our research design is essentially cross-sectional by nature. The lack of longitudinal data on ITCS and ITPS in emerging economies precludes us from making causal claims.

## Future Research

We highlight four avenues for future research. First, capabilities develop over time. Their synergies with IT-enabled flexibilities, IT architectures, and IT capabilities, also likely to develop, co-develop, and change over time [42]. These changes may affect firm performance — perhaps even differentially for local and foreign firms. A longitudinal research design is necessary to tackle this intriguing issue. Second, while emerging economies provide an interesting context to examine how foreign and local firms differ in their leverage of IT, other related opportunities abound. Theorizing about how emerging economy multinational corporations may differ in their leverage of IT as compared to other firms, such as emerging economy domestic corporations or developed economy multinational corporations, is one such exciting avenue of future inquiry. The FSA framework may provide a basis for developing theory along these lines. Third, future research can test the mechanisms that underlie the differential effects of ITCS and ITPS for foreign and local firms. While clearly beyond the intended scope of this paper, research along these lines may be accomplished by employing case studies or structural models with multiple theoretical perspectives. Fourth, how foreign and local firms differ in leveraging other IT capabilities (e.g., IT-enabled analytics), IT governance mechanisms [101], and information flows [14] are fertile questions for future research. Given that the political, social, and legal institutions in emerging economies are often complex, idiosyncratic, and dynamic, and technological gaps limit the efficacy of IT, addressing these issues in the context of emerging markets may provide valuable insights and help improve our collective understanding of the interplay of IT and firm capabilities.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, this study argued that in emerging economies, compared to local firms, foreign firms have a disadvantage in leveraging synergies between ITCS and Marketing Capability, but an advantage in leveraging synergies between ITPS and Relational Capability. Our empirical analysis found broad support for our hypotheses. The key takeaway for researchers is the applicability of the FSA framework, and theories from International Business literature in general, to IT business value issues in emerging economy contexts. Application of this and similar frameworks have the potential to enrich our understanding of the complex phenomena associated with the leverage of IT, by different categories of firms, under different environmental conditions. In an increasingly globalized and digitized world where IT is a key lever for success, our findings have important implications for foreign and local firms and can spawn further scholarly research on this important topic that has a rich history in our field [e.g., 42].

8. Samsung and Sharp are exemplars of better performing foreign firms. They leveraged superior manufacturing capabilities to reduce prices of flatscreen TVs and grow sales while competing against local Chinese firms producing conventional TVs [29].

9. Emerging economies provide a contextual and institutional environment that is often not experienced by foreign firms earlier. As a result, many foreign firms learn from one another and form a strategic reference group to mimic each other’s behavior, leading to consistent patterns of strategies and actions. Hence, differences in contextual origins and institutional settings provide another basis for the generalizability of strategic behaviors, such as FSAs, across foreign and local firms.

11. It is possible that location-boundedness arising from lack of context-specific knowledge about local cultures and ways of doing business may be mitigated to some extent through the hiring of senior and middle managers from the foreign country. Nonetheless, path dependencies and time-compression diseconomies will remain. Furthermore, in a large emerging economy like India, there is no “single version” of market knowledge. Distinct differences in cultures, sub-cultures, races, languages, religious practices and state regulations across geographies imply that India consists of multiple markets, with multiple versions of market knowledge. Such diversity would require the hiring of more than a few local leadership and management team members. Finally, the foreign firm would also require not only the means but also the will to incorporate local expertise into its marketing practices.

12. ITCS derived from IT deployed by local food chains in India is an example of localized ITCS. When presented with online customer service channels from foreign food chains (e.g., McDonalds) and competing local food chains (e.g., fast food segment of Haldiram’s in India), customers may be more attracted to the local firm because a local firm’s IT-enabled customer services are more adapted and flexible to their needs and contextual preferences. Specifically, websites and apps of foreign food chains often require customers to verify their purchase through email while local firms accept phone text messages and missed-call based verifications. Customers using low-bandwidth Internet connections or having no email accounts prefer this flexibility.

13. A significant percentage of local firms in India are majority family- or communityowned or influenced. This overlap between family and business systems results in risk aversion and long-term orientation. The alignment of long-term orientation of family and community firms with the cultural ethos of Indian customers leads to enduring relationships. This successful behavior, in turn, shapes the strategic choices of other non-family local firms, which thereby also share a similar long-term orientation.

14. An example that substantiates our arguments for why local firms are better positioned than foreign firms to leverage the synergy of ITCS and Marketing Capability is IndusInd Bank, a local firm based in Mumbai, India (see Table A1 in Online Appendix A for additional examples). The bank uses IT-enabled customer services, such as smartphonebased local language–enabled video chat that caters to local needs, in addition to multichannel facilities such as ATMs, and banking through Internet, mobile and phone channels. IndusInd is the first Indian bank to adopt a real-time gross settlement system (RTGS) that enables specialized real-time funds and securities transfer without a waiting period, and the transaction is settled on a one-to-one basis without bundling with any other transaction. Adoption of RTGS is an innovative step as the Indian banking system faces challenges due to socio-cultural factors and lack of awareness that hamper the spread of e-payments. ITCS helped IndusInd to identify opportunities, respond to customer needs, and synchronize local expertise to fulfill business needs, resulting in it becoming one of the best-performing bank stocks. HSBC exemplifies a foreign firm that has struggled to adapt its global IT platforms to the Indian context. Despite heavy investments in marketing, HSBC has decided to shrink its presence and focus on the higher-end of “globalized” Indian consumers. This is because HSBC has been unable to provide flexible banking services (e.g., missed-call based deposits) that local firms offer through mobile apps and online channels.

15. Honda illustrates our arguments that foreign firms have firm-specific advantages stemming from global scope of international operations and a global network of partners (see Table A1 in Online Appendix A for additional examples). Honda manufactures cars and two-wheelers in India. Honda set up a local base of supply chain firms to replace costly importing of parts and equipment. To achieve a local supply chain base that meets its standards while remaining cost-effective, Honda relies on an IT-enabled marketplace and portal to source, coordinate, and communicate with partners. Thus, Honda, a foreign firm, uses relevant IT to complement its expertise in building local relationships, enabling it to flexibly respond to vagaries in supplier capacity constraints due to information sharing and operational synchronization. The resulting visibility and flexibility provided Honda with a highly efficient “just-in-time” manufacturing process producing increasing volumes of market-leading products.

16. We can also examine counterfactual arguments to this hypothesis [23]. These local partners would have a high bargaining power and would enforce non-IT based exchanges on foreign firms. 21. Survey instruments were developed through a multi-step process. In Step 1, items were constructed using existing scales where available or else through the development of new multi-item scales. 25. We conducted several additional tests to assess robustness. First, because the independent variables, main dependent variable (ROA), and control variables are from different sources and the main dependent variable is objective (not perceptual), it mitigates concerns of common method bias [69]. Nevertheless, we performed Harman’s one-factor test [34] and marker-variable test [54]. In Harman’s test, nine factors with eigenvalues exceeding one were extracted, with the first factor explaining only 14.86 percent of the variance. In markervariable tests, correlations among variables did not change significantly after accounting for common method variance. Thus, our matched-pair data collection protocol and results of both tests suggest that common method bias is not a big concern. Moreover, because our hypotheses pertain to interaction effects, common method bias is even less of a concern because common method variance reduces the likelihood of detecting interaction effects [105]. Second, we checked for multicollinearity by examining variance inflation factors (VIFs). In all equations, maximum and average VIFs were below 2.76, which is well below suggested limits, indicating that multicollinearity is not problematic. Third, although tests did not suggest the presence of heteroscedasticity [108], we use robust standard errors in all our estimations [33]. To further test whether our data suffers from heteroscedasticity, we plotted the squared residuals against predicted values of the dependent variable, the idea being to test whether the estimated mean value of Performance is systematically related to the squared residual. We found no systematic pattern, suggesting no significant heteroscedasticity. We also plotted residuals against independent variables. No systematic pattern emerged, further suggesting a lack of heteroscedasticity. Fourth, we assessed the sensitivity of the alternative survey-based measure of firm performance to individual items. For example, we re-estimated the models after dropping the item related to sales growth. Results remain unchanged. Consistent with prior research, a holistic measure of performance is more appropriate [71]. Fifth, we performed several diagnostic checks including normality of residuals, and influential observations, and found no problems or violations of assumptions [33]. Sixth, as discussed earlier, we triangulated our results using Marketing Intensity collected from Prowess database to proxy for Marketing Capability. The results, shown in Table A5 in Online Appendix A, are unchanged.

26. Our findings are in the context of foreign firms in an emerging economy. We can expect similar contextual and contingent factors to influence the synergy between IT-enabled capabilities and firm capabilities in the context of a developed economy, with foreign firms hailing from emerging economies. However, since the location-bound nature of FSAs would plausibly be reversed in this context (complementarity of ITCS with Marketing Capability a nonlocation-bound FSA and complementarity of ITPS with Relational Capability a locationbound FSA), the findings and theoretical mechanisms would both be different.
