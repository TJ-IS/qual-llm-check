# Debiasing the framing effect: The effect of warning and involvement

## a b s t r a c t

This study reports on an experiment that examined the effect of message framing on the Internet buyers' attitude and purchase intention. The effect of two possible moderators in framing effect including warning about the possibilities of bias and the subjects' level of involvement were considered in the experiment. In addition, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the individual decision makers' involvement level on the joint effect of warning and framing was also examined to understand the individual differences in sensitivity to warning messages. The experimental results suggested a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant framing effect that participants in positive condition revealed more favorable responses than their counterparts in negative condition. Further, the debiasing effect of prompting the warning instructions was observed. Differences between responses in positive and negative conditions were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant when no warning message was prompted, and the magnitude of framing effect was attenuated and eliminated in weak and strong warning conditions respectively. In addition, the effect of framing messages on people's responses was moderated by their level of involvement. Less involved participants were more susceptible to framing effect than more involved subjects. Finally, the debiasing effect of warning on message framing depended upon the subjects level of involvement. The framing effect is diminished when high involvement participants were prompted with either weak or strong warning messages. For less involved participants, the prompt of weak warning message is not an effective debiasing mechanism to prevent the occurrence of framing effect. Only when they were exposed to strong warning messages can the framing effect be eliminated.

## 1. Introduction

A rational decision maker is expected to make an indifferent judgment no matter how an identical decision problem is described (or framed). However, humans usually lack the knowledge, computational skills, or resources necessary to make decisions in a manner compatible with economic notions of rational behavior [36]. In fact, it has been shown that judgments and decisions can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced greatly by the way information is presented or framed. For example, [41] described the same Asian disease problem in terms of either the likelihood of lives saved (positive frame) or the likelihood of lives lost (negative frame) to two groups of participants. The results indicated that the relative attractiveness of options varies when the same decision problem is framed in different ways. This phenomenon is referred to as the framing effect, or framing bias [41].

Decision support technologies or decision aids have been designed to provide decision makers with the additional capabilities to extend their bounds of rationality and thus, in turn, to eliminate or at least mitigate, the decision bias. [11] called the efforts to diminish the effects of biases “debiasing”. Various approaches to debiasing have been proposed in previous studies, one of which is warning about the possibilities of bias [3,13]. However, it appears that little research has explored countering the framing bias by warning.

In addition, although researchers have reported that the framing effect is a robust phenomenon [19,22,41] a contradictory <sup>fi</sup>nding that did not replicate the framing effect has been obtained [9,39,40]. Some researchers have suggested that there may be variables, such as individual level of issue involvement, that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the magnitude of framing effects [24,34]. Previous research has demonstrated that individuals who are highly involved with the issue have greater motivation to attend to and process the salient information than low involvement individuals [31]. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that people who obtain a higher score in the involvement inventory are less susceptible to the effect of framing. Further, little is known about whether the joint effect of warning and framing will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the individual decision maker's level of involvement. Individual differences in sensitivity to warning effects remain largely unknown.

The purpose of the current study is to contribute to framing research by investigating the debiasing effect of warning as well as the moderating role of individual involvement in decision making. This was done by examining: (a) the effect of the valence-based attribute framing; (b) the effect of warning in debiasing the framing effect; (c) the moderating role of the individual level of involvement in framing effect; and (d) the effect of individual level of involvement on the joint effect of warning and message framing.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Framing effect

Framing effect can be categorized into three different types: attribute framing, goal framing, and risky choice framing effects [21]. In attribute framing, a single attribute of a given object is framed positively or negatively. In goal framing, the persuasive message is framed to stress the positive (negative) consequences of (not) performing an act. Risky choice framing occurs when choices between a risky and a riskless option of equal expected value depended on whether the options are described in positive terms or in negative terms. This study focuses only on attribute framing because it is the simplest case of framing and it is especially useful for gaining a basic understanding of how descriptive valence in<sup>fl</sup>uences information processing [21].

Attribute framing effect occurs when individuals' judgments vary as a function of the labels used to describe speci<sup>fi</sup>c object attributes [15,20]. For example, [20] examined the effect of attribute framing in three tasks and observed a statistical reliable framing effect. More favorable ratings were produced when the attributes were expressed in positive terms than when they were expressed in negative terms.

[19] presented ground beef framed as either “75% lean” or “25% fat” to two groups of participants. The results indicated that the participants evaluations were more favorable when the beef was described as percent-lean than as percent-fat. Other studies [17,21–23] also demonstrated similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the same alternative was rated more favorably when a key attribute was framed in positive terms than in negative terms.

Based on the above discussion, the following hypotheses were proposed:

H1. Participants exposed to a positive framing message will have a more positive attitude toward the target product than their counterparts exposed to a negative framing message.

H2. Participants exposed to a positive framing message will reveal higher intention to buy the target product than their counterparts exposed to a negative framing message.

## 2.2. Debiasing techniques

Research on framing effects has been plentiful in the years since these effects were <sup>fi</sup>rst demonstrated. In view of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and persistence of framing effects, a natural question is whether they can be avoided. The selection of the method for reducing or eliminating the bias will depend on the particular bias and the particular decision maker [1,2]. [18] suggested that attribute framing effect occurs because positively or negatively framed information is believed to be associated with favorable or unfavorable encoding. That is, favorable associations in the memory of participants can be evoked by the positive labeling of an attribute. In contrast, when the information is labeled negatively, unfavorable associations in the memory are evoked, resulting in less favorable responses. In addition, the framing effect has also been attributed to a lack of attention and would occur less frequently if people thought more carefully about their choices [35,37]. Possible remedies for this bias include, but are not limited to, warning about the possibility of bias, and describing the direction of the bias that is typically observed.

The debiasing mechanism manipulated in our experiment is to prompt the participants with a warning message about the possibility of decision bias. This is a less intrusive debiasing strategy because it provides additional information to the participants, but does not require them to perform any additional tasks, such as writing down the decision processes. The characteristics of the decision maker may moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of positive versus negative frames [34]. Thus, the individual's level of issue involvement is also considered in current study in exploring the moderating role of involvement in framing effect, as well as the debiasing effect of warnings among individuals with different involvement levels. This individual characteristic has been considered an important issue in cognitive study. A brief review of these two variables is given below.

## 2.2.1. Warning

Previous research that investigates the debiasing effect of warning mainly focuses on such cognitive biases as hindsight bias [14,33], anchoring effect [3,13], and outcome effect [7]. To our knowledge, there has been little research examining the effect of warning in debiasing the framing effect. Thus, hypotheses regarding the debiasing framing effect of warning were taken from work that addresses other decision making biases and heuristics. This literature is described below.

The warning manipulation was found to successfully eliminate the hindsight bias in [14,33]. In these studies, the warning was manipulated by telling participants that the information already incorporated into memory is wrong [14] or by instructing participants to consider the worst possible outcomes before they received the outcome knowledge [33]. Other studies investigated the effect of warning in diminishing the anchoring and adjustment effect, and the results suggested that the debiasing effect of warning messages appeared to be less successful in anchoring bias. For example, [3] found that when participants were warned before they provided their <sup>fi</sup>nal estimates, the anchoring and adjustment effect was somewhat decreased but not completely eliminated. A similar result was found in [13] where warnings about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the anchor reduced the anchoring effect but did not completely eliminate it.

The prompt of a warning message has also been used to mitigate the outcome effect. For example, the results in [7] suggest that simply instructing or warning the evaluator about the potential biasing effects of outcome information was only weakly effective. However, strong debiasing instructions to evaluators were effective in signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly mitigating the outcome effect.

Results from the above studies indicate that the debiasing effect of warnings is mixed and inconclusive. The strength of warning arguments and the nature of the judgmental bias are possible reasons for the inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, a strong warning message appears to work better than a weak one in debiasing. Further, warning can successfully eliminate hindsight bias, but cannot completely mitigate the anchoring effect and outcome effect. However, the debiasing effect of warning in other cognitive biases, such as the framing effect, has not been examined. Thus, we propose that:

H3. The prompt of warning message will moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing on participants' attitude toward the target product. For participants with no warning, receiving a positive framing message will result in a more positive attitude toward the target product than their counterparts receiving a negative framing message. For participants with a warning, a positive or negative framing message results in a similar attitude toward the target product.

H4. The prompt of warning message will moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing on participants' intention to buy the target product. For participants with no warning, people receiving a positive framing message would have a higher intention to buy the target product than their counterparts receiving a negative framing message. For participants with a warning, receiving a positive or negative framing message results in a similar intention to buy the target product.

## 2.2.2. Involvement

The involvement is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a person's perceived relevance of the object based on inherent needs, values, and interests” [44, p.342]. Involvement is considered an individual difference variable and has been proposed to be associated with the individual motivation to process information. Results from prior research of involvement showed that people who are involved with an issue are more motivated to systematically process persuasive messages [4–6,26] and are more interested in acquiring information about the product [44] than people who are less involved with the issue.

Framing effect is a cognitive bias that causes decision makers to adopt heuristic principles (or cognitive “short-cuts”) that simplify the complex tasks of making judgments [41]. In addition, it has been argued that the occurrence of framing effect can be attributed to a lack of attention and that the framing bias would be mitigated or eliminated if individuals thought more carefully about their choices [35,37]. This argument is supported by the indirect evidence showing that induced elaborated thinking reduces or eliminates risky choice framing effects [35]. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that people who adopt heuristic information processing strategies (low involvement) are more vulnerable to message framing because they tend to exert less cognitive effort to comprehend and evaluate the message than people who adopt systematic information processing strategies (high involvement) [6]. Similarly, [38] reported several experiments where cognitive ability signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderates the descriptive-normative gap demonstrated in behavioral decision theory. Thus, decision making biases may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderated by individual cognitive differences, such as the level of involvement. Further, [8] argued that framing should have less impact for high involvement consumers because they exert the cognitive effort required to evaluate the arguments presented and will evaluate the argument from both positive and negative perspectives. Thus, the following hypotheses were proposed:

H5. Participants' level of involvement will moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing on participants' attitude toward the target product. A positive framing message results in a more favorable attitude than a negative framing message for low involvement participants. By contrast, no framing effect occurs with high involvement participants.

H6. Participants' level of involvement will moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing on participants' intention to buy the target product. A positive framing message results in higher buying intention than a negative framing message for low involvement participants. By contrast, no framing effect occurs for high involvement participants.

## 2.2.3. Warning, involvement, and framing

Warning and decision maker's level of involvement have been regarded as two independent factors that moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of message framing in decision making. Little research has focused on understanding the joint effect of warning and involvement in framing effect. Accordingly, the current study explores the possibility that the joint effect of framing and warning depends upon the participants' level of involvement, or the role of individual differences in sensitivity to the debiasing effect of warnings.

Previous studies have suggested that more involved people have a higher motivation to process information and actively think more about the decision problem, but this is not the case for less involved participants [26,27,44]. According to the Elaboration likelihood Model (ELM) [28,30], when individual involvement is high, the central route to persuasion should be particularly effective, while the peripheral route should be better when the involvement is low [31]. As positive/negative framing messages are possible peripheral cues [43], it is expected that the framing messages are more persuasive in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing people's attitude as well as their buying intention for less involved decision makers. Thus, the attempt to eliminate the framing bias by prompting the warning messages to less involved participants is expected to be less effective because the framing effect is expected to be more persistent for low involvement than for high involvement participants.

By contrast, high involvement participants exert considerable cognitive effort to actively attempt to comprehend and evaluate the message's arguments (the central route) and thus the positive/ negative framing messages, which are delivered through the peripheral route, should be less persuasive. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that the framing effect is less persistent when more involved participants were exposed to warning messages that indicate the possible cognitive biases. In other words, the prompt of warning message is more effective in debiasing the framing effect for high involvement than for low involvement participants.

Accordingly, the following hypotheses were proposed:

H7. The debiasing effect of warning on attitude will be moderated by participants' level of involvement. The framing effect on attitude is more persistent and the prompt of warning messages is less effective in debiasing for less involved participants than for more involved participants.

H8. The debiasing effect of warning on intention to buy is moderated by the participants' level of involvement. The framing effect on behavioral intention is more persistent and the prompt of warning messages is less effective in debiasing for less involved participants than for more involved participants.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research framework

The research framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. The independent variable, attribute framing message, was manipulated either positively or negatively. Participants' attitude toward the target product and their purchase intention served as dependent variables. The debiasing mechanisms, warning, and participants' level of involvement, were considered as moderators. Warning was manipulated in three levels: no warning, weak warning, and strong warning. Participants' level of involvement was divided into two levels, high and low, based on their involvement inventory scores.

## 3.2. Participants

Participants were recruited from a large pool of undergraduate students in Taiwan in partial ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of a class requirement and each participant received a NT \$100 (about USD \$3) McDonald coupon for their participation. A total of two hundred and ninety-seven students were recruited.

## 3.3. Experimental design

The experiment is a 2 (framing: positive/negative)×3 (warning: no/weak/strong)×2 (involvement: high/low) between-participants factorial design. The experimental stimuli, framing and warning were manipulated variables, whereas participants' level of involvement was measured after the experiment. Thus, the participants were randomly assigned to one of the six conditions resulting from a combination of positive/negative framing messages and no/weak/ strong warning instructions. All the participants were asked to answer questions measuring their attitude toward the target product and their intention to buy the product after they were exposed to the manipulated messages. Participants' level of involvement and demographic information was collected at the end of the experiment.

## 3.4. Materials

The target product is an electronic translator and the product information is provided as follows: “YiShen is the latest electronic translator equipped with all the functions that can be found in most of the powerful products on the market. In addition, it offers built-in English→Chinese (or Mandarin) and Chinese→English two-way fulltext translation. You can type in English in sentence and it will be translated into Chinese. You can also write Chinese in sentence and YiShen will translate it into English. This device will serve most social groups, especially students, foreign language learners, businessmen, secretaries, and travelers.”

## 3.5. Task and procedure

The key attribute of the electronic translator is the accuracy of its translation and whether it is framed either positively or negatively. In the positive frame, the translator was described as: “The translator offers built-in English→Chinese and Chinese→English two-way full-text translation with up to 80% accuracy.” In the negative frame, the translator was described as: “The translator offers built-in English→Chinese and Chinese→English two-way full-text translation with result only 20% error rate.”

The manipulation of weak warning condition is similar to that used in previous research [10] where the participant is warned about the potential biasing effect of the framing message and asked to be aware of its in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Participants receiving a weak warning message were exposed to the following message before they submitted their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, “Warning! Information to be described in different ways may in<sup>fl</sup>uence people's judgment and result in decision biases. Thus, please be aware of possible biases and be sure to avoid undue in<sup>fl</sup>uence from the way the information is described before you submit your <sup>fi</sup>nal judgment.” Further, the strong warning condition is designed to describe the framing effect and intended to encourage the participants to re<sup>fl</sup>ect more deeply on the messages they were exposed to. The strong warning message employed in the current study is “Warning! Product information presented in a positive (or negative) way may in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer preferences toward the product. Thus, please be aware that the product information that you have read in last web page may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced your <sup>fi</sup>nal judgment.” Finally, participants in the no warning group received no warning message before they made their decisions.

All the experimental stimuli were displayed through Web pages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst page was the general description of a synthetic online store selling different types of electronic appliances, including the electronic translator, the target product of this article. Message framing information and judgmental tasks were provided on the next page. Participants were instructed to decide whether to buy an electronic translator. Following the task instruction, participants were presented with either the positive or negative information described above. In addition, some of the participants were exposed to weak or strong warning instructions on the third page before they answered the attitude and buying intention questions. Participants in the no warning group did not receive any warning message and were asked to provide their attitudes toward the electronic translator as well as their buying intention right after their exposure to the framing messages.

Finally, all the participants completed the 10-item Revision of RPII (Revised Personal Involvement Inventory) involvement scale [25] which was derived from the PII (personal involvement Inventory) developed by [44]. Participants were asked to provide ratings on a seven point semantic differential scale. A composite score was created for each participant by averaging the items. Demographic information was also collected in the same page. The whole experiment took about 20–25 min.

## 3.6. Measurement

Participants were asked to rate their attitudes toward the electronic translator and their intention to buy it. First, attitude was measured by three-item semantic differential on a 7-point scale, which included three pairs of adjectives: bad/good, unattractive/ attractive, and unlikable/likable. Further, participants were asked to rate their intention to buy the electronic translator on a 7-point Likert scale in terms of the following three questions: (1) I intend to buy this electronic translator; (2) I will suggest to my friends that they buy this electronic translator; (3) I will buy this electronic translator even if I have already have one. For later analysis, ratings of the three items in attitude and intention to buy were averaged into a single item respectively for further data analysis.

## 4. Data analysis and result

Data analysis was divided into three segments to examine the following questions: (1) will the prompt of warning message mitigate the framing effect? (2) Will the participants' level of involvement moderate the framing effect? (3) Will the individual's level of involvement in<sup>fl</sup>uence the joint effect of warning and framing in decision making?

To answer the <sup>fi</sup>rst question, ratings from the full sample of two hundred and ninety-seven participants were submitted to data analysis. For the second and third questions, only ratings from high and low involvement participants were included. Participants were rank ordered by their Revised RPII scores and split into three equal segments. The top and bottom thirds were committed to further data analysis and the middle one third of responses were teased out from the experimental sample. This procedure resulted in two hundred and six participants. A similar practice has been followed in studies such as that of [44]. H3 and H4 suggested that participants' susceptibility to the framing messages depended on the prompt of warning messages. The ANOVA results illustrated in Table 1 showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interactive effect for framing by warning, suggesting that the warning message may interact with the effect of framing as hypothesized in H3 and H4. ## 4.1.3. Framing effect and the moderating effect of involvement

H5 and H6 posit that the participants' susceptibility to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a framing message depends on their involvement level. Low involvement participants are expected to be more susceptible to framing effect than high involvement participants as they exert less motivation to systematically process information and are thus more in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the peripheral cues such as a positively/negatively framed message. Ratings from two hundred and six participants were submitted to two independent ANOVA tests similar to that described above. Results are provided in Table 3.

The results revealed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main effect for message framing on participants' attitude and behavioral intention responses. In addition, the interaction effect of framing by involvement was also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. A further examination of response differences between positive and negative framing for high and low involvement participants showed that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant framing effect on both attitude and behavioral intention were detected with low involvement participants, but not with high involvement participants. This result provides support for hypotheses H5 and H6. Thus, we can conclude that individual differences in involvement moderate participants responses to framed messages.

4.1.4. The influence of involvement on the joint effect of warning and framing

H7 and H8 posit that the debiasing effect of warning on framing effect would depend on the participants' level of involvement. Unlike low involvement participants, high involvement participants are expected to be less in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by framing messages. Thus the framing effect should be diminished when they are exposed to warning messages. By contrast, framing effect is expected to persist even when messages that warned about the potential bias were given to low involvement participants. Thus, the warning message is expected to have less impact on the framing effect for less involved participants.

In order to examine the role of involvement in the joint effect of warning and framing, two independent 2 (framing: positive/negative) × 3 (warning: no/weak/strong) × 2 (involvement: low/high) ANOVA tests were conducted for statistical analysis. The results show a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant three-way interaction effect of framing, warning, and involvement (Table 4).

A further examination of means in each experimental condition (Table 5) showed that the framing effect was observed for both high and low involved participants when no warning message was given. Further, as expected, the debiasing effect of warning is observed for high involvement participants. The framing effect is diminished when high involvement participants were prompted with either weak or strong warning messages indicating the possible cognitive biases. For less involved participants, the weak warning message is not an effective debiasing mechanism to prevent the occurrence of framing effect. Only when participants were exposed to strong warning messages can the framing effect be eliminated.

## 5. Discussion

This study aimed at investigating the framing effect and the effect of warning messages in debiasing the framing effect in online purchasing context. In addition, the moderating role of individual's level of involvement was also considered. The study was conducted using a laboratory experiment. The results revealed a robust phenomenon: framing the product attribute in either positive or negative terms signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects individual attitudes toward the product and their intention to buy it. This effect of message framing can be mitigated or diminished by weak or strong warning messages. The debiasing effect of a strong warning message appears to be more effective than a weak one.

In addition, participants' level of involvement moderates the effect of message framing. The positively/negatively framed messages resulted in different responses only for less involved participants. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with the arguments of previous research that individuals high in involvement are more likely to intensively process information than individuals with low involvement [4]. Thus, high involvement participants were less in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by message framing. An indirect evidence supporting this <sup>fi</sup>nding is provided by [42] who found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant framing effects among undergraduates (low involvement) and no framing effect among experienced decision makers (high involvement). They argue that this is because that “experienced participants naturally think harder than undergraduates…” [42, p.1064]. Findings in the current study regarding the moderating role of involvement in framing bias can be further explained by the Elaboration Likelihood Model [28,30] which suggests that consumer attitudes in response to an advertisement are affected via the central route for high involvement participants, whereas the attitude is affected via the peripheral route when participants' involvement is low. Thus, positively/negatively framed messages (which are delivered through peripheral route) are more salient determinants of low involved participants in current study.

In addition, our experimental results showed that the debiasing effect of warning instructions depends upon participants' level of involvement. Framing effect was more persistent for low involved participants and a weak warning message that indicates the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing message mitigate but cannot eliminate the occurrence of framing bias. By contrast, a weak warning message is ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in eliminating the framing effect for high involvement participants. Finally, a strong warning message can eliminate the impact of message framing for both high and low involvement participants. Thus, encouraging decision makers to think more by prompting with a warning message has a maximal effect on more involved participants, and little effect on participants who are less involved with the decision problem. This <sup>fi</sup>nding again con<sup>fi</sup>rms the argument above that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of message framing on low involvement participants is more persistent as they tend to shape attitudes without engaging in any extensive thought about the message presented [29]. Less involved participants receiving the weak warning instruction, which merely indicates the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the messages presented, are less motivated to process the warning message. Hence, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the framing message persists. Until they are exposed to strong warning instruction, which indicates the framing bias directly and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these low involvement participants are not aware of the framing bias. By contrast, high involvement participants actively attempt to comprehend and evaluate the message's arguments. Thus, when they are exposed to warning instructions, even when it is weak, they tend to exert more cognitive effort to consider the decision problem from different perspectives and are more aware of the potential framing bias.

## 6. Implications

The present results have a number of implications. First, <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the current study suggested that the framing of product messages is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential for low involvement participants. Thus, it can be applied to the practical design of online stores. Focusing on the positive attribute of products (the peripheral route) should result in more favorable evaluations for less involved consumers. Although the message framing is less effective in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing high involvement consumers, web site designers are encouraged to provide more issuerelevant information about the product (the central route) to increase the attractiveness of the product.

Second, warning messages in an electronic commerce web site may be helpful in reducing the effects of framing on internet shoppers. Although this practice is rare, it does exist. For example, in one web site (http://ec.softking.com.tw/) that sells a variety of software, a paragraph of warning messages will pop-up when the Internet buyers proceed to checkout step. The warning messages ask the consumers to “Think twice before you buy. It is our deep hope that every customer gets right product to meet his or her own needs. So, don't be impetuous. We are willing to wait for you until you are sure the product is the one you need.” Although we are uncertain of the relationship between this warning message and the revenue of the above website, there is at least one lesson here for designers of e-commerce web sites.

Finally, web site design is a crucial determinant of whether visitors are willing to return to the site repeatedly. Unlike shopping in physical stores, Internet buyers cannot touch and actually see the products. Thus, the impact of environmental stimuli delivered by web site elements (messages) on consumer's feelings (trust) should be of critical concern for online retailers [12,16,32] Behaviors such as unfair pricing or conveying inaccurate information are harmful to buyer–seller relationships. It is expected that, although not empirically tested, giving warning messages before potential consumers make their buying decisions to forestall the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing messages should increase buyers' level of trust in sellers and thus result in better relationships. Further research may investigate this contention in an Internet shopping environment.

## 7. Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, the effect of warnings on framing effect has not been investigated, although warnings have been used to eliminate other kinds of cognitive biases. This study contributes to a better understanding of the effect of warning in framing. In addition, our experimental results provide support for the hypothesis that the participants' level of involvement moderates the effect of framing on their attitudes towards the target product and the buying intention. Low involvement participants are more susceptible to the effect of framing than high involvement participants. However, involvement is merely one of many possible moderators of the framing effect. Further research is encouraged to consider other possible moderators to provide more insight into the reasons for the framing effect. For example, the inclusion of participants' education level or gender might be meaningful as they are variables a manufacture can control due to the nature of the product, such as cosmetics for woman or ri<sup>fl</sup>es for men. Such promotional messages can be framed separately for different targeted markets and is expected to result in different outcomes.

Further, our results suggest that the prompt of warning instructions revealed different debiasing effects for high and low involvement participants. The warning messages are more effective in debiasing the framing effect for high involvement participants, as they are expected to exert more effort to process the messages presented. The framing bias is more persistent for low involvement participants. A strong warning, but not a weak warning, can eliminate the framing effect. As research that investigates the role of involvement in the joint effect of warning and framing is rare, the present research contributes by providing a starting point for investigations into these issues and for further research regarding the moderating role of other possible variables in the interaction debiasing techniques and cognitive bias.

Findings in this paper provide a better understanding of the debiasing mechanism, framing effect, and individual involvement in this type of decision bias. However, there are a few limitations to this study that should be noted.

First, the study measures participants' attitude and intention, but not the actual behavior. Since many studies have shown that attitude or intention may not be totally correlated to the <sup>fi</sup>nal action taken, future study is encouraged to include the measurement of actual behavior in framing effect. Second, the current study addresses only the attribute framing effect, but not the other two types, goal and risky choice, framing effects. To what extent warnings work in the two other types of framing contexts is not determined in this study. Thus, further research may apply this research model to other types of framing effects to provide better understanding regarding the warning mechanism in debiasing the various types of framing effects, as well as the role of individual involvement in debiasing the framing effect. Third, the current study only considered one type of debiasing technique in framing effect. It is hoped that future research will continue to explore other types of debiasing mechanisms to help people to overcome the framing effect. Finally, the current research focuses only on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of message framing in internet users' decision making in terms of attitudes and purchase intention. However, other important variables such as participants' risk taking attitudes may impact the framing effect. Though risk attitudes may be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure, future studies are encouraged to incorporate this variable into the research.
