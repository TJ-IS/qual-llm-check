# The three tiers architecture of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and management activities

Emad M. Kamhawi ⁎

Zagazig University, Egypt

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 24 February 2010 Received in revised form 9 September 2010 Accepted 10 September 2010

Keywords: Knowledge management Knowledge management activities Knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow Knowledge sharing Information sharing Innovation Bahrain

## a b s t r a c t

This study aims to develop a framework for Knowledge Management Activities (KMA) that reflects their multitier nature and accommodates the multilevel of <sup>fl</sup>ow that knowledge goes through in organizations. A survey method was emploved for this study in Bahrain to test the research model behind that architectural framework. The results showed that the classical three tiers of IS platforms, i.e. backend, processing, and frontend, can be used as an overarching distribution to devise corresponding KMA tiers related to managing knowledge resources, knowledge creation, and knowledge application respectively. Moreover, they highlighted the different sets of KMA at each tier as well as provided evidences that support the relationships between them. Research implications related to advancing the current path of studies on KMA modeling as well as practice implications concerning the development of knowledge based management approaches for organizations have been discussed at the end of the paper.

## 1. Introduction

The idea that knowledge related activities can be directed to make organizations more capable of continuously generating sustainable competitive advantages, has attracted many researchers and executives in the last decade. It is widely believed that the truly lasting advantage an organization may have is the ability to continuously generate ideas and innovations related to its products, services, markets, and processes (Eisenhardt and Santos, 2002; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Teece et al., 1997). This ability mainly depends on how knowledge is created, circulated, and applied in the organization (Garvin et al., 2008; Sher and Lee, 2004). Meanwhile, such activities play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in helping the organization understand and continuously adapt to its environment (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Zollo and Winter,

2002). Moreover, it is also accepted that knowledge-based core competencies cannot be easily copied by competitors, due to the idiosyncratic nature of knowledge (Dixon, 2000). Naturally, knowledge resources are embodied in human brains and cannot be easily accessed by competitors. Due to the wide recognition of this strategic importance of knowledge, research in Knowledge Management (KM) has recently grown exponentially.

On the other extreme, KM <sup>fi</sup>eld of study has been criticized of being surrounded by confusion, due to lack of maturity and clarity of its de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and frameworks (Beesley and Cooper, 2008). Some researchers are even wondering whether KM classical concepts, fundamentals, and models have any practical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Wilson, 2002).

The literature points at two main problems behind this lack of maturity in the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the confusion surrounding our understanding of the nature of knowledge and its relationship to information. We believe that this confusion mainly came from the classical distinction of tacit/explicit knowledge, which we often use to tell what knowledge is. This concept has been introduced in a way that confused knowledge, especially explicit one, with information. Moreover, this dichotomy seems to drive the research community in this <sup>fi</sup>eld to a corresponding dualistic and yet myopic view for the strategic alternatives an organization may take to manage its knowledge resources. Examples for these dualistic strategies are: personalization vs. codi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Choi and Lee, 2003; Hansen et al., 1999; Scheepers et al., 2004), network vs. repository (Alavi, 2000; Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2005), emergent vs. engineering (Hooff and Huysman, 2008), or human oriented vs. IT oriented strategies (Wilson, 2002).

The second problem is related to the inability to properly model knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and management activities in organizations. Although the current literature is replete of Knowledge Management Activities (KMA) frameworks, they all seem to be oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed and consequently unable to represent the multifaceted nature of KM.

Tackling these two problems, the purpose of this paper is to develop a framework that provides a better modeling for the different activities of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and management, based on better understanding of the nature of organizational knowledge. The study will use a multi-layering logic to better re<sup>fl</sup>ect the distributed and architectural nature of these activities and at the same time the different <sup>fl</sup>ow levels that knowledge go through in the organization. The multitier and multilevel architecture introduced will be statistically tested in order to provide empirical evidences to support its practice implications as well as give a new departure for a rather modi<sup>fi</sup>ed path for the current KM body of knowledge.

## 2. Literature review

KM is widely seen as a set of activities that can be deliberately and/or systematically used to direct knowledge resources, inherent in all the organization business processes, to create strategic value for the organization. Based on this idea, many researchers have tried to identify and model such activities. Maybe the earliest and the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential model is the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle introduced by Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995). This model was built on the classical description of knowledge as a construct of a tacit/explicit dichotomy, where tacit is embedded in human brain, and explicit can be easily codi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Based on this distinction, the model suggested that knowledge creation happens when one type of knowledge of an individual or a group is transferred or converted into other individual or group's same or different type of knowledge. Therefore, it introduced four possible knowledge activities corresponding to four possible interactions or conversions, i.e. from: tacit to tacit, tacit to explicit, explicit to explicit, and explicit to tacit. These four activities are called: Socialization Externalization, Combination, and Internalization (SECI) respectively.

However, three streams of studies can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for KMA. One of these streams introduced KMA as the activities required to manage intellectual assets or stocks in organizations (Bontis et al., 2002; Curado, 2008; De Carolis, 2002; Teece, 1998). This perspective made researchers introduce KMA as activities related to accumulating and maintaining stocks or amounts of different required components of knowledge at certain points in time, through consistent human resources management (HRM) and human investment initiatives

Following this line of thinking, Beijerse (2000) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nine activities required for knowledge management in organizations: determine necessary knowledge; indentify available knowledge; evaluate knowledge gap; develop knowledge; acquire knowledge; lock knowledge; share knowledge; utilize knowledge; and evaluate and feedback. Similarly, Cepeda and Vera (2007) and Dove (1999) used this idea, related to evaluating knowledge gaps through comparing knowledge desired and knowledge available, to suggest their KMA models.

A variety of this perspective viewed KM as a set of activities required for building and maintaining knowledge repositories and then orchestrating access to these repositories. For example, Rowley (1999) sees that KMA are mainly: creation of knowledge repositories, improvement of knowledge access, enhancement of knowledge environments, and management of knowledge as an asset. Also, simila models for this variety have been introduced by Ellingsen and Monteiro (2003). Adams and Lamont (2003), and Magnier-Watanabe and Senoo (2008).

However, the majority of researchers seem to see that KMA models better focus on how organizations generate rather than store knowledge. The difference between knowledge and any other type of tangible assets rests on how such resources are processed and/or created. It is widely known that the value of any knowledge asset is generally maintained and often enlarged by its application, while conventional assets are depreciated and/or replaced (Curado, 2008).

Following this creation/processing perspective, some KMA models focused on the internal creation of knowledge such as Lee et al.'s (2005) model which identi<sup>fi</sup>ed KMA as: creation, accumulation, sharing, utilization, and internalization. See also similar models reviewed by Tat and Hase (2007) and Soliman and Spooner (2000).

Others stressed the importance of showing both internal creation and external acquisition or adoption of knowledge. Bhatt's (2001) model of KMA had activities related to: creation, adoption, distribution, and review and revision. Many other studies (e.g., Beesley and Cooper, 2008; Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Holsapple and Jones, 2004; Holsapple and Joshi, 2000; Holsapple and Jones, 2005) have followed the same line of thinking

Finally, another recent trend has been more conscious towards the importance of the activity, or set of activities, responsible for turning KMA into value and consequently impacting the overall performance of the organization. Kalling (2003) introduced a model of three stages: development, utilization, and capitalization. The model used the utilization stage for explaining how organizations create knowledgebased dynamic capabilities, and then dedicated the capitalization stage for showing how these capabilities can be turned into pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Also, the model introduced by Zollo and Winter (2002) was based on the suggestion that linking KMA with performance should be obtained through managing knowledge as an idea/innovation generation process. A similar model has been introduced by Tat and Hase (2007) who concentrated on the role of KM in problem solving in the organization.

Although many models have been introduced for KMA, unfortunately no one of them has been recognized yet as a standard or reference model. They all seem to oversimplify KMA into some sequential activities. Some researchers explicitly termed these models as KM value chain models (Holsapple and Jones, 2004; Holsapple and Jones, 2005; Magnier-Watanabe and Senoo, 2008) and others called them KM cycles (Beijerse, 2000; Bhatt, 2001; Zollo and Winter, 2002), implying some development or engineering stages they usually take. These cycles or chains assume that knowledge has speci<sup>fi</sup>c undertakings or projects in organizations with start, end, and milestones to manage. Clearly, the ubiquitous and dynamic nature of knowledge resources and activities in the organization challenge such assumptions. On the other side, our review identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three perspectives for these models: storage, creation, and application. These perspectives reveal the multifaceted nature that KMA may have. We believe that this nature should be considered when modeling such activities.

Finally, another important consideration is to clarify what assumptions the KMA model adopts in relation to how knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow takes place in organizations. The most widely used <sup>fl</sup>ow model in KM literature is the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle of Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995), which has been briefed earlier in this section. The majority of KMA models, especially those related to the creation perspective rely on the classical SECI processes of that model to explain how knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation take place in organizations. Meanwhile, KMA models related to the storage perspective classify their knowledge strategies based on the dichotomy construct of knowledge used by that <sup>fl</sup>ow model, i.e. explicit vs. tacit oriented strategies such as codi<sup>fi</sup>cation vs. personalization (Scheepers et al., 2004).

Unfortunately, this model is not free of problems. In fact, many researchers believe that it is the main source of confusion in the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld (Beesley and Cooper, 2008; Diakoulakis et al., 2004; Wilson, 2002). The model confuses knowledge, at least the explicit type, with information. It assumes that knowledge can be codi<sup>fi</sup>ed and transferred using the classical information management tools and systems. Consequently, it fails to highlight the relationships between information and knowledge and the role information may play for knowledge creation processes. Also, although the model identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three levels of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow; namely, individuals, groups, and organization, it did not clearly show how knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow may take place from one level to the other.

Overall, although many research efforts in this <sup>fi</sup>eld have been directed towards understanding what activities need to be handled while managing knowledge resources in organizations, the resulting models seem to be unsatisfactory as they were mainly built on some confusing assumptions and/or unable to represent the multifaceted nature of KMA. Due to our belief that this point has the potential to make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on KM body of knowledge, we believe that KM research is in need for a new model for KMA, with a view of bridging three important gaps identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the current literature: the multifaceted nature of KMA; the multilevel view of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow; and the sophisticated nature of the relationships between KMA. The framework developed in this paper aimed at bridging these gaps. Moreover, the study will statistically test its research model and see whether enough empirical evidences can be obtained to support the study's main framework.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The layering approach, which is usually followed in establishing and managing complex information systems architectures, was used in this study to introduce a model for KMA. This approach was selected because it has the capability to provide guidance for distributing and organizing knowledge resources and activities into tiers, related to the different perspectives identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature. Having this distribution in hand, the extant literature can then be emploved to build a specific research model for the study. Three tiers distribution corresponding to the three perspectives introduced earlier for knowledge activities, i.e. storage, creation, and application, were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for our KMA architecture. Clearly, this is borrowed from the classical three tiers distribution of IS architectures which distributes a system's components along backend, processing, and frontend tiers. Also, the relationships between the KMA three tiers will be similar to those in IS architecture tiers. Consequently, the backend tier, which represents the storage perspective in KMA, will be the main source of resources required for the other tiers, especially the creation tier which holds the knowledge related processing activities. While in the other end, the frontend tier is the area where the organization realizes the output of the activities carried out in the previous 2 tiers and turns them into speci<sup>fi</sup>c applications.

Before identifying knowledge resources and activities related to each tier, an overarching model has been developed <sup>fi</sup>rst to explain how knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation take place in organizations. We believe that this model has the abilities to overcome the problems which Nonaka and Takeuchi's (1995) one has. The following subsection will brie<sup>fl</sup>y introduce our <sup>fl</sup>ow model. After that, each tier of the architecture will have another subsection to discuss the development of the research hypotheses.

## 3.1. The knowledge flow and creation cycle

Knowledge can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the intellectual properties accumulated from an individual's education, experiences, and skills and blended with other personal traits related to intuitions, beliefs, and emotions. This mixture collectively gain an individual a speci<sup>fi</sup>c cognitive structure or framework which enables him/her to comprehend, generate insights or ideas, and then be able to act on some situations (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Popadiuk and Choo, 2006; Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2005). Stating the relationship between knowledge and information, Wilson (2002) explained, “We express our knowledge using oral, written, graphic, or even gestural messages. Such messages do not carry ‘knowledge’, they constitute ‘information’, which a knowing mind may assimilate, understand, comprehend and incorporate into its own knowledge structures”.

Based on this opening, it was thought that relaxing the confusion related to the relationship between knowledge and information, our model for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow should consider three points. First, it should include a separate and explicit component for information, instead of relying only on the two types of knowledge, i.e. tacit and explicit. Second, it should use the dichotomy tacit/implicit instead of the classical tacit/explicit to describe the main two types of knowledge. Where the <sup>fi</sup>rst, i.e. tacit knowledge, is not expressible and/or cannot be shared with the others as it is buried deep in a person's memories, backgrounds, experiences, etc, and the second, i.e. implicit knowledge, is expressible or sharable. We chose not to use the term ‘explicit knowledge’ as it was introduced in the literature in many cases in a way that refer to ‘information’ items such as codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge, written procedures or manuals, and knowledge discovered in ‘databases’, or frequently called ‘knowledge bases’. Meanwhile, the term ‘implicit’, which was introduced first by Polanvi (1966), may help avoid such confusion, as it refers to this subsidiary part of a person knowledge that is active in the mind while in the act of knowing something (Mooradian, 2005).

This emphasizes the idea that both types of knowledge are mental properties and consequently avoids confusing the ‘information’, which might get documented or codi<sup>fi</sup>ed from people's experiences, ideas, etc, with ‘knowledge’. Third, the model should then provide an understanding, based on the extant literature, for the main <sup>fl</sup>ow activities between those three components; namely, tacit, implicit, and information.

Based on these arguments we now suggest the <sup>fl</sup>ow model shown in Fig. 1.

The model has three levels of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation: individuals in one side, organization in the other, and groups as a mixed zone between those two ends for exchanging, combining, and creating knowledge and information resources. Although both types of knowledge, tacit and implicit, are individuals' properties, the implicit type is shown in the groups' level to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the area where this type of knowledge can be more effectively shared and used for the organization. On the other side, information is an organization's properties, but it is shared at the group level to help individuals develop their implicit knowledge in order to perform their work duties.

The model suggests four activities for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation, as presented by the four arches in Fig. 1. Two of these activities have direct impact on knowledge creation and <sup>fl</sup>ow: knowledge sharing and information sharing. According to Fig. 1, groups' level provides the proper environment for stimulating individuals obtain part of their tacit knowledge into a usable and expressible form – implicit knowledge Knowledge sharing takes place when individuals exchange their implicit knowledge with the organization to help them conduct their work duties. This process may happen within groups when they formally perform some group work activities or informally communicate some ideas or understanding for some phenomena or information.

There is a strong support from the literature for the notion that knowledge is a social construct (Beesley and Cooper, 2008; Ellingsen and Monteiro, 2003; Lang, 2001). Knowledge sharing is regarded in that literature as the main driver not only for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow but for creation as well. For example, McDermott (1999) showed that when groups share their knowledge, new knowledge is created at the boundaries of old ones. Lang (2001) explained that “More generally, knowledge is created and recreated as practitioners see the logic of each other's thinking in communities brought together by common interests. Knowledge creation requires frequent, informal, direct, and face-to-face personal interaction”

In addition to knowledge sharing, information sharing is a critical type of activities that provides an important support for the thinking processes involved in knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation in organizations. In these processes, when individuals get information they re<sup>fl</sup>ect on them using their knowledge, or others knowledge, to draw insights in creative ways in order to solve a speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem. Previous studies have supported the notion that information increases groups' ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness on acting on problems and creating new knowledge (Lang, 2001; Magnier-Watanabe and Senoo, 2008).

In addition to knowledge and information sharing, the model shows two more important but indirect processes related to knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation; namely, learning and storage. Learning represents what individuals store and add to their tacit knowledge from their experiences in group work, knowledge sharing, and information sharing processes at the groups' level. The role of organizational learning in knowledge creation has been supported by many previous studies (Adams and Lamont, 2003; Dove, 1999; Kalling, 2003). Actually, it is generally accepted that KM literature is originally built on the organizational learning principals (Wilson, 2002).

On the other side, storage is what the organization keeps or codi<sup>fi</sup>es into information through its information systems, documents, and/or manuals from its groups' knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation processes. A big bulk of the literature addressing the role of IT/S for KM was mainly on how KM systems (KMS) can be built with the view of providing storage capabilities for the explicit type of knowledge (Choi and Lee, 2003; Magnier-Watanabe and Senoo, 2008; Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2005). Overall, these two processes, learning & storage, help continuously increase the organization's stocks of knowledge and information resources necessary for its groups' knowledge and information sharing activities.

## 3.1.1. The distinction between knowledge and information

One of the important points this cycle tries to clarify is how knowledge and information differ. We believe this clari<sup>fi</sup>cation relaxes the problematic assumption, which dominated the <sup>fi</sup>eld that knowledge can be stored, accessed, and then shared in the same way and using the same tools or systems we use for information. The cycle, instead, emphasizes the notion that knowledge can only be amassed within individuals, but once it is expressed, it immediately becomes an external object or information (Beesley and Cooper, 2008). Others may incorporate such information into their knowledge structures, and this process may result in different knowledge. Therefore, knowledge is not only the messages that individuals produce about what they know. It also involves the mental processes of comprehension, understanding, and learning that go on in the mind (Wilson, 2002). These activities and the produced messages get even in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by individuals' personal and emotional traits and modes. The so called ‘codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of knowledge’, which is central to the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld, is only about codifying the messages that people express out of the knowing process. Obviously, this codi<sup>fi</sup>cation can not store or codify knowledge, as we explained, as an activity.

Activities related to each type of resources, knowledge and information, differ accordingly. While traditional IS tools can be used for capturing information, learning is the main activity related to knowledge. Based on this understanding, knowledge creation in the organization relies on the contribution of both types of resources, through knowledge sharing and information sharing processes. Since knowledge only exists within individuals. knowledge sharing represents the internal part of the creation process. In this process, individuals use their minds to bring what they know, whether from their tacit or implicit knowledge resources by means of comprehension, understanding and/or learning, to this active area in the mind when they are doing something (Mooradian, 2005). On the other hand, the external part of the process is related to information sharing, where individuals bring external resources, i.e. information, and use their minds to bring, again by means of comprehension, an understanding of these external resources.

Although these processes are performed by individuals, knowledge utilized in the organization has a collective nature, as shown in the model. This is because no single person has all the knowledge required for a business or for the different business processes inside an organization. Besides, each business process is usually performed by a group of persons, who naturally have different minds and/or mental abilities (Lang, 2001).

This conceptualization has the abilities to avoid some of the confusion brought in many of the previous studies, due to using knowledge as a synonym or replacement term for information. This confusion drove some previous studies present KM as some applications or computer systems that have the capabilities of codifying knowledge, build knowledge bases, transferring tacit knowledge to the same or another type of knowledge, etc. The concepts used in the <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle introduced in this section will be used to develop the three KMA architecture suggested for the study as follows.

## 3.2. The backend tier: knowledge, culture, and information

The backend tier of the suggested architecture, as shown in Fig. 2. will deal with managing the activities related to the basic resources of KM. Unlike the previous studies representing the storage perspective of KMA models, knowledge is not the only type of resources included in our architecture model. This tier suggests three types of resources: knowledge, culture, and information, corresponding to the resources required for the three levels of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow introduced in the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle shown in Fig. 1.

Accordingly, activities required for this tier can be split into three as well. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is concerned with developing employees' knowledge. Clearly, HRM activities, such as staff selection, staff development, and training and learning programs, could be used to help build and develop the organization's intellectual assets.

The stream of studies that developed KMA models from a storage perspective introduced HRM activities as central for KM in organizations (Chen and Huang, 2009; Wong, 2005). For example, Wong (2005) has considered HRM policies as critical success factor for KM in organizations. Also, Diakoulakis et al. (2004) showed that KM has four domains of actions: strategic, organizational, technical, and HRM.

The second set of activities in the <sup>fi</sup>rst tier is related to aligning organizational culture to knowledge sharing activities in the organization. This set of alignment activities corresponds to the groups' level of <sup>fl</sup>ow, shown in Fig. 1. A knowledge sharing culture is one particular variety of organizational culture concerned with those characteristics that enable and motivate people to share, create, and utilize knowledge for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the organization (King, 2008; Park et al., 2004).

Some researchers considered knowledge sharing culture as a type of knowledge (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Popadiuk and Choo, 2006) or a type of intellectual capital (Curado, 2008; Hooff and Huysman, 2008). Moreover, the crucial role that culture plays in KM initiatives success is one of the topics that took much of the attention in KM writings (Alavi et al., 2006; Park et al., 2004).

Unfortunately, managing this type of resources is extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Activities related to changing a corporate culture to a knowledge friendly one, have been described by practitioners and researchers as time consuming, frustrating, and maybe impossible, especially if employees did not understand and/or accept such changes (McDermott and O'Dell, 2001; Park et al., 2004). A longer term and evolutionary approach might be more effective for such undertakings (Schein, 2000). On the other extreme, others suggested that such activities should be directed to align KM initiatives and systems with the organizational culture than to attempt to change that culture. For example, McDermott and O'Dell (2001) explained that corporate culture may stand like a wall that KM initiatives hit if they did not adjust to it.

However, between these two extremes, previous research studies have introduced speci<sup>fi</sup>c activities that can be used in this layer of the model. Examples of these activities are: using rewards systems to promote collaboration (Bennett and Gabriel, 1999; Goh, 2002; Lee et al., 2005), facilitating friendly work environment through social interaction (Connelly and Kelloway, 2003), building and nurturing communities of practices (Kimblea and Bourdonb, 2008; Martin et al., 2005), and demonstrating top management support for KM initiatives (Goh, 2002; Kimblea and Bourdonb, 2008; Kulkarni et al., 2007).

The last set of activities suggested in the backend tier is related to information management for the purpose of supporting knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation in the organization. This component of the architecture comprises the activities related to planning, building, and managing the information systems required for supporting information as well as knowledge sharing activities in the organization. Clearly, these activities will deal with IS components such as hardware, software, data, people, and processes that the organization utilize to facilitate communication, information processing, and information access for KMA.

The role of IT/S for KM is one of the important tracks in this <sup>fi</sup>eld of study and practice. The literature is replete of studies explaining how systems such as groupware, on-line databases, Internet, data warehouses and software agents can be used for KM (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Lee and Suh, 2003).

## 3.3. The processing tier: knowledge and information sharing

The processing tier is where the resources of the KM architecture are used for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation processes. As shown in the architecture model in Fig. 2, this layer introduces two sets of activities; namely. knowledge sharing and information sharing, Obviously, this selection was based on the arguments explained earlier in the <sup>fl</sup>ow model shown in Fig. 1. Due to their human and social nature, we cannot directly intervene in knowledge sharing activities. However, the three main sets of activities comprising the previous tier of the architecture are expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing in this layer. Due to the limited space of the paper and the review we already introduced in the previous tier, the literature explaining these in<sup>fl</sup>uences, and consequently supporting the related research hypotheses, will be only briefed in the following paragraphs.

Previous studies showed that the main source of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation in the organization is simply its employees' own knowledge. Studies in organizational learning assert that current knowledge representations in the organization create two important types of capabilities: absorptive and transformative capacities (Adams and Lamont, 2003; Cohen and Levinthal, 1990). While the former is mainly related to the ability to absorb new external knowledge, the later is related to comprehending the internal ones. Therefore, we expect that:

H1. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to developing their employees own knowledge and the effectiveness of knowledge sharing in these organizations.

Although individuals' knowledge is important for knowledge creation, the sharing process is clearly a social one (Connelly and Kelloway, 2003; Hooff and Huysman, 2008). The role of culture in enabling these social and reciprocal processes has been clearly supported in the literature as explained in the previous tier of this architecture. Therefore, we also hypothesize that:

H2. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to culture alignment and the effectiveness of knowledge sharing in these organizations.

In addition to the human and the social in<sup>fl</sup>uences on knowledge sharing, information sharing plays an important enabling role for knowledge sharing as well. The creation of knowledge involves the collation of observations, data, facts, and information as explained by many previous studies in this <sup>fi</sup>eld (Beesley and Cooper, 2008; Harrington and Guimaraes, 2005). Thus, we expect that:

H3. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to information sharing and the effectiveness of knowledge sharing in these organizations.

Also based on our arguments in the previous tier of this architecture, regarding the role information management plays for effective information sharing, we also hypothesize that:

H4. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to information management and the effectiveness of information sharing in these organizations.

## 3.4. The frontend tier: Knowledge application

The frontend tier is where the organization turns KMA and resources into a strategic value. The activities, required for this tier, involve establishing and developing capabilities related to KM strategy, innovation, and process change.

The research model has positioned innovation as the main outcome of the processing tier. Many studies have supported this link (Johannessen, 2008; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995) and others obtained related <sup>fi</sup>eld and statistical evidences (Darroch, 2005; Kalling, 2003). We, therefore, expect that both knowledge and information sharing, will in<sup>fl</sup>uence innovation in organizations as stated in the following hypotheses:

H5. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to knowledge sharing and their abilities related to innovation.

H6. There is a positive relationship between organizations' abilities related to information sharing and their abilities related to innovation.

However, being innovative, i.e. having the capability of generating ideas and concepts out of KMA, may not be enough for creating a strategic value for the organization. It may require a knowledge-based strategy as well as abilities for process change for implementing such strategies. A KM strategy may help focus the whole KM architecture towards some knowledge related vision or concept for the whole of an organization. Previous studies have emphasized the role of KM strategies for pursuing the organization's overall goals (Magnier-Watanabe and Senoo, 2008), providing guidance for its learning capabilities (Zollo and Winter, 2002), directing its KMA applications (Kalling, 2003), and eventually building an agile organization (Dove, 1999). In addition to strategies and visions, organizations should have the abilities to rapidly and continuously change and reengineer its processes to help operationalize such knowledgerelated strategies (Capuano et al., 2008; Carpenter and Rudge, 2003; Jung et al., 2007).

Finally, the main output of the model is the contribution, or strategic value, expected of KMA's whole architecture to the organization's overall performance. The link to performance, which is acknowledged in the architecture in Fig. 2, will not be investigated in this study. We came to a belief that going outside the boundaries of the architectural model, investigating the relationships of the frontend components with some organizational performance constructs, will most probably challenge the study's focus and increase its level of complexity, providing it is already loaded with two complicated models.

## 4. Method

A <sup>fi</sup>eld survey using a questionnaire was used for this research, with items grouped around each of the study constructs. A list of the top 100 Bahraini businesses, prepared by the Bahraini Chamber of Commerce and Industry (BCCI), was used as our study sample. Obviously, relying on this list will make the sample biased toward larger <sup>fi</sup>rms, which does limit the generalizability of the study. However, it is believed that the larger <sup>fi</sup>rm has the advantage of having a potentially broader pro<sup>fi</sup>le of knowledge activity (Gold et al., 2001).

In order to prepare a list of potential respondents to the questionnaire, we visited these companies and contacted their IS managers, relying on 4 research assistants. Obviously, the right sampling unit for this survey does not have to be the IS manager, although most KM initiatives in organizations are IT-related ones. Therefore, our visits to these companies' IS managers aimed to help us distribute our questionnaires to the most knowledgeable managers in his/her company in the topic of our study, regardless of their functional department or background.

Because the unit of analysis for the study is the business or the company level, we preferred collecting more than one response from each company to enhance the responses level of validity and reliability. Four copies of the questionnaire were handed to that contact manager after s/he speci<sup>fi</sup>ed who would be the most appropriate informants for such study. Consequently, 400 copies of the questionnaire were given to the contact persons (IS managers), who forwarded them to the targeted informants in their companies.

Only167 (42%) respondents completed the survey. However, these responses came from 81 (81%) companies. This shows a high response rate at the company level which is the level of analysis selected for the study and consequently the level used in performing its statistical analysis. Using a t-test, no nonresponse bias was found by comparing respondents with non-respondents on <sup>fi</sup>rm size.

The companies included in the study sample belong to 11 different business activities: 18 (22%) <sup>fi</sup>nancial services (including banks & insurance companies); 14 (17%) trading companies; 13 (16%) manufacturing; 10 (12%) services, 8 (10%) construction; 7 (9%) holding companies; 5 (6%) consultation & training; 2 (2.5%) telecommunication; 2 (2.5%) information technology industries as well as one company (1.2%) from the oil and Airlines industries. Also, among the responding organizations, there were 31 (38%) with a single respondent for each organization in our sample, 23 (28%) with 2 respondents, 18 (22%) with 3 respondents, and 9 (11%) with 4 respondents.

To check whether respondents were reporting about their organizations, not their own beliefs, a oneway between-groups multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) test was performed, using SPSS statistical software package, to investigate organizations' differences on KMA. Five dependent variables, representing KMA, were used: innovation, knowledge sharing, information sharing, individuals' knowledge development, culture alignment, and information management. The test indicated that the variation of respondents' answers concerning KMA activities were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than the variation due natural individual differences or beliefs concerning those KMA issues (F=1.78, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1$ , Wilks' $\lambda { = } 0 . 0 5$ and also F=1.71, pb0.001, Pillai's Trace=2.167)

On the other hand, at the individuals or respondents level, there were 46 (28%) females and 121 (72%) males; 12 (7%) chief executives 15 (9%) vice presidents; 87 (52%) chief of<sup>fi</sup>cers; and 53 (32%) mid-level managers. A majority of the respondents (64%) were in the 40–50 age category. Finally, IS managers represented almost 42% of the sample (71 respondents). Non-IS (96 respondents) managers had different functional backgrounds such as personnel, general management, marketing, operations, accounting, and <sup>fi</sup>nance. The responding managers of both groups had on average 10 years experience in management positions.

Different aspects of validity and reliability have been scrutinized for the research instrument. These aspects are presented in the following sections.

## 4.1. Content validity

The content validity of an instrument is the extent to which it provides adequate coverage for the construct domain or essence of the domain being measured (Churchill, 2000). Prior to data collection, the content validity of the instrument was established by grounding the study measures on the extant literature. All the items measuring the study constructs were developed based on previous related studies in: knowledge sharing and information sharing (Gold et al., 2001; Lee et al., 2005; Lin and Lee, 2005); culture (Harrington and Guimaraes, 2005; Park et al., 2004); individuals' knowledge development (Brelade and Harman, 2000; Chen and Huang, 2009; Robertson and Hammersley, 2000; Soliman and Spooner, 2000); information management (Broadbent et al., 1999; Kearns and Lederer, 2004; Lewis and Byrd, 2004); and innovation (Anantatmula and Kanungo, 2006; Gooijer, 2000).

The study constructs and their measurement items are presented in Appendix A. These items were measured with a seven-points Likert-scale ranging from 1=Very strongly disagree to 7=very strongly agree. The mean of the scores provided the composite score for each variable.

Three faculty members and two Bahraini managers were invited to pre-test the questionnaire for further content and face validity. These experts were asked to review the questionnaire for structure, readability, ambiguity, and completeness. The <sup>fi</sup>nal survey instrument incorporated minor changes to remove a few ambiguities that were discovered during this validation process. These tests indicated that the resulting measurement instrument represented the content of the study factors.

## 4.2. Internal consistency reliability

After data collection, responses to survey questions have been evaluated using the Partial Least Squares (PLS) approach to evaluate the research model. This approach has the same advantages known for all structural equations modeling (SEM) such as the items measuring the constructs, that is the measurement model, are assessed within the context of the theoretical model. This makes SEM approaches superior to traditional regression models, as they assume that the scores are portable – a rather impractica assumption.

Testing for internal consistent reliability was used to evaluate the consistency or precision of a measuring instrument. The internal consistency reliability of survey instruments is a measure of reliability of different survey items intended to measure the same characteristic. Cronbach's alpha (α) values and internal composite reliabilities $( p _ { \mathrm { c } } )$ were used for this test. The results produced by PLS for the study's constructs are summarized in the <sup>fi</sup>fth and sixth columns of Table 1. All scores (for both indicators) were greater than the typical adequate value, which is 0.70 as Hair et al. (1995) suggested.

Another technique used for testing reliability, is the average variance extracted (AVE) score which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the overall amount of variance in the indicators accounted for by the latent construct. As shown in column 4 in Table 1, all the extracted variances are greater than or equal 0.50, the acceptable cut-off value of this test. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the results of these reliability tests seem acceptable.

## 4.3. Construct validity

Construct validity is the extent to which the items in a scale measure the abstract or theoretical construct. Testing of construct validity concentrates not only on <sup>fi</sup>nding out whether or not an item loads signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on the factor it is measuring, or convergent validity, but also on ensuring that it measures no other factors, or discriminant validity.

Many techniques can be used to test a construct's convergent and discriminant validity. However, factor loadings similar to Varimax rotation in classical principal component analysis are the most widely used for both types of validity. In Table 1, construct (for both convergent and discriminant) validity was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed when items for each variable loaded onto single factors with loadings of greater than 0.5 (Hair et al., 1995). The table provides the loadings of each item on independent and dependent variables. Six factors emerged with no-cross construct loadings above 0.5, indicating good construct validity.

Moreover, for discriminant validity, the correlations among latent variables must be less than the square root of the AVE values for the two constructs. This test was also passed as shown in Table 2.

For examining the problem of common method bias we used Harman's single-factor analysis as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). Six factors emerged from the analysis, with the <sup>fi</sup>rst extracted factor accounting for 23.1% of the variance in the data. Since more than one factor was extracted from the analysis and the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounted for less than 50% of the variance, we concluded that common method variance is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem in our study.

## 5. Assessment of the structural model

Assessing the overall research model was facilitated by SmartPLS v2.0 M3 software program. It provides a graphical environment for running complicated path models for both con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory and exploratory modes. The model depicted in Fig. 1 has been entered to the program with all its 6 variables' data. Two main statistical indicators, generated by PLS, have been used to test the model hypotheses: (1) the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients represented as standardized betas (B) along with their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance indicators; (2) the explained variance in the dependent constructs represented as $R ^ { 2 }$ values as an indication of the overall predictive strength of the model.

As in Fig. 3, path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for individuals' knowledge development $\left( B = 0 . 2 2 \right)$ ; culture $( B = 0 . 2 9 )$ and information sharing $\left( B = 0 . 3 3 \right)$ , in their relationships to knowledge sharing, were all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant with pvalueb0.001. Also $R ^ { 2 }$ value indicates that these three variables explain 41% of knowledge sharing total variance. Meanwhile, information management $( B = 0 . 5 1 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , with $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 8 )$ was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant as well but for information sharing. These results support H1, H2, H3, and H4 respectively. Similarly, knowledge sharing $( B = 0 . 2 5 , p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and information sharing $( B = 0 . 2 1 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ were found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predicators for innovation, with $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 3 2$ . This supports H5 and H6.

## 6. Implications and discussions

The three tiers architecture, developed and supported by statistical evidences from a Bahraini business environment in this study, have the potential to play an important role for guiding the future research and practice in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of KM. Starting with the theoretical imputes the study introduces, two types of implications can be claimed. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is related to the theoretical gaps or problems in the current literature the study's architectural model tried to tackle, while the second is concerned with those implications derived from the creation and <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle the study introduced.

The literature of KM seems to have been trapped in the <sup>fi</sup>rst thoughts introduced with the advent of this <sup>fi</sup>eld in mid nineties. KMA modeling research efforts, as an example, got captive to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c path, which leads researchers to introduce speci<sup>fi</sup>c chains or cycles of activities that introduce little for the ability of evaluating and then inspiring management for developing knowledge-based approaches for their organizations. These chains do not have the abilities to make researchers address multiple perspectives for knowledge management. Even if one or more of these cycles included different activities from different perspectives, their oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed sequential and cycle like picture make the modeler biased towards organizing and interpreting them in relation to some speci<sup>fi</sup>c perspective, i.e. storage, creation, or application.

Moreover, the sequence of activities, suggested by these models, is not supported with empirical evidences. They seem to derive their logic from the classical engineering or project cycles. As an emerging <sup>fi</sup>eld, researchers have not had enough opportunities to empirically test such chains. Even if they had, the fact that the stages of these classical models cannot be easily planned, monitored and controlled as in project or engineering cycles, may challenge obtaining such evidences. Clearly, these challenges stem from the fact that KMA are human and social intensive processes. Consequently, management implications for such models might not be practical.

The architectural design introduced in this study breaks this chain mentality, relaxes the related life cycle assumption, frees the modeler from being biased towards single perspective of KMA, and consequently opens the door for a new path of research efforts in that <sup>fi</sup>eld. Future studies can build upon this layering approach, through examining the different possible distributions of these KMA layers, components of each layer, and relationships between them.

The second type of theoretical implications, this study claims, is related to the creation and <sup>fl</sup>ow model the study suggests as a basis for modeling KMA. The contribution this cycle introduces is not less important than what the architectural model suggests. In fact, it tackles a puzzling point that brought confusion to this <sup>fi</sup>eld. This point is concerned with the relationship between knowledge and information. The new cycle clearly relaxes the problematic idea, which dominated the <sup>fi</sup>eld, that knowledge can be implicitly treated as an object which consequently can be managed and/or captured, maybe by using information systems, and made available for later use by organizations. Moreover, we believe this cycle has the power to set future research efforts free from another <sup>fi</sup>rst thought bias, brought to the <sup>fi</sup>eld and dominated its research direction related to the dualistic view of knowledge and consequently the corresponding dualistic alternatives for K-based management approaches.

Alternatively, our model simply directs the focus for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation to the activities and resources related to the three levels of <sup>fl</sup>ow and creation in organizations. Manifesting this simple idea in this paper may help inspire researchers for better strategies and alternatives rather than the myopic dual alternatives publicized in this <sup>fi</sup>eld such as personalization vs. storage. We believe this issue comes to the heart of the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld as it addresses the meaning of knowledge, its relationships to information, and how it is processed and created.

On the other side, we think that the architecture may produce real practice implications, in despite of the challenges the KM body of knowledge has in practice. The model invites for a rather balanced knowledge-based management approaches for organizations. This balance, we see, comes from the fact that the reference architecture does not emphasize IT track, for example, on the expense of human one, or the storage perspective on the expense of the creation one. The architecture simply does not limit executives for such narrow mutual exclusive choices for KM as the previous KM frameworks do.

Meanwhile, the layering approach provides a ‘divide and conquer’ mind-set for establishing different building blocks for a comprehensive KM-based approach. The layers may inspire for building blocks related to managing knowledge resources, processing and/or application types of building blocks. This is while the vertical view of the architecture, or the multilevel perspective, may as well be used for devising strategies at the individuals, groups and/or organization's level. Obviously, the architecture provides a more <sup>fl</sup>exible basis for the development of KM approaches. It is expected then that different organizations will emphasize different building blocks, in the light of their corporate strategies and circumstances.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not elaborate on the link between the frontend tier and the organization's overall and/or strategic performance. We believe this issue requires an attention in the future, as many researchers in the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld assume that KM practices and activities automatically lead to higher corporate performance.

Second, the fact that the study introduced two models in order to meet the research objectives came on the expense of the space available for the theoretical background of the <sup>fi</sup>rst one, concerning the knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow cycle. This model requires separate richer theoretical research efforts. Moreover, we believe that the model in its current form represents only an initial version developed to meet some speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements related to the distinction between information and knowledge, information sharing and knowledge sharing, and learning and storage activities. Such focus may have resulted in bypassing some other details, as usually happen in modeling, of the original phenomenon we model. Future studies, which adopt wider objectives for abstraction or modeling, may revise the model and provide more advanced versions.

Third, the study was limited to KMA practices in Bahrain, which con<sup>fi</sup>nes our abilities to generalize results to other countries in the world. Moreover, the study sample size was limited to 81 Bahraini organizations. This size maybe relatively small comparing to other studies conducted in, for example, a Western country. However, it could be seen as satisfactory for a small country such as Bahrain, which its current population is approaching only 1 million residents. Another limitation for the study sample is that females' respondents represent only 28% of the sample.

Usually, study limitations may be used to provide future research with some opportunities for research ideas and projects. However on the other side, we believe that one of the important contributions to future research efforts this study introduces is that it may help lay the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld on the right track of being more practically feasible, away from the <sup>fi</sup>rst myopic thoughts dominated the <sup>fi</sup>eld so far.
