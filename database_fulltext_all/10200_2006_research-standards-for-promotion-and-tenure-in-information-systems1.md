## Abstract

What constitutes excellence in information systems research for promotion and tenure? This is a question that is regularly addressed by members of promotion and tenure committees and those called upon to write external letters. While there are many elements to this question, one major element is the quality and quantity of an individual's research publications. An informal survey of senior Information Systems faculty members at 49 leading U.S. and Canadian universities found 86 percent to expect three or more articles in elite journals. In contrast, an analysis of publication performance of Ph.D. graduates between the years of 1992 and 2004 found that approximately three individuals in each graduating year of Ph.D.s (about 2 percent) published 3 or more articles in a set of 20 elite journals within 6 years of graduation. Only 15 individuals from each graduating year (11 percent) published one or more articles. As a discipline, we publish elite journal articles at a lower rate than Accounting, yet our promotion and tenure standards are higher, similar to those of Management, Marketing, and Finance. Thus, there is a growing divergence between research performance and research standards within the Information Systems discipline. As such, unless we make major changes, these differences will perpetuate a vicious cycle of increasing faculty turnover, declining influence on university affairs, and lower research productivity. We believe that we must act now to create a new future, and offer recommendations that focus on the use of more appropriate standards for promotion and tenure and ways to increase the number of articles published.

Keywords: Tenure, promotion, publishing standards

## Introduction

Promotion and tenure is an important career milestone for most faculty members. It is also one of the most important decisions that faculty members make. The granting of promotion and tenure is quite literally a multimillion dollar decision. Tenure is traditionally granted not as a reward for past performance but instead on the promise of future work, thus the tenure decision often requires a careful assessment of past work and the promise it holds for the future.

Although each university has its own criteria for promotion and tenure, teaching, research, and service are often the major factors in the decision. Teaching and service often have well-established local benchmarks, both relative and absolute, and a reasonably large set of data to use for comparison. Judging research performance is a challenging task, as it must consider the quality and quantity of publications (as well as a host of other factors such as the overall nature of an individual's research portfolio) for which there may be fewer well-established local benchmarks. Each university is likely to have its own standards, but it is important to ensure that those standards are grounded in overall national and international research performance benchmarks and to understand what different levels of research performance imply about an individual's performance at the national and international levels.

The key question is: What constitutes appropriate research standards for tenure and promotion? The goal of this paper is to provide a set of empirical benchmarks that can be used as a foundation for establishing local standards for research performance in the promotion and tenure decision. We examine the total number of publications in elite journals published by information systems faculty members by their sixth year after graduation (when they are presumably ready to be considered for promotion and tenure) and calculate what percent of faculty members publish at each level. We then use these percentiles to develop our recommendations for different local standards. We conclude by drawing implications and recommendations for the Information Systems discipline as a whole.

## Research Performance of Junior Faculty Members

Journal publication is a primary consideration in the promotion and tenure decision for faculty in the United States and Canada. It plays a role, albeit a lesser one, in this decision in other parts of the world, which often take a broader, more inclusive, view of what constitutes research contributions (e.g., see Athey and Plotnicki 2000; Beckman and Forsman 2002; Chua et al. 2002; Huang and Hsu 2005; Im et al. 1998). Therefore, we focus most of our analysis on U.S. and Canadian faculty members, but also include global data.

## Journal Selection

The first step in our analysis was to select a set of IS journals to use in our discussion. Additionally, since some IS faculty publish in elite journals in other business school disciplines (e.g., Management, Accounting, Marketing), we developed a second, larger, set of business school journals designed to capture this broader definition of research.

When selecting journals to include in such an analysis, there are at least two approaches: select a set of journals that are perceived as being of “good quality” or select a set of journals that are “beyond reproach” in their reputation for quality (Trieschmann et al. 2000). Following Trieschmann et al., we chose the latter. By focusing on those journals that are unquestionably top quality research journals, we define research performance in very narrow terms, but terms that we believe are clear and understandable to all. We chose precision over inclusiveness. Table 1 presents the two IS journals and 18 additional business school journals we used in our analyses. Our rationale for selecting these IS journals is presented in Appendix A. In selecting the broader set of business school journals, we simply adopted the set used by Trieschmann et al., which has also been used by other researchers examining business school research (e.g., Swanson 2004). We discuss the limitations of these decisions in Appendix A.

## Analysis Approach

The decision on tenure and promotion to the rank of associate professor is typically made during a review conducted in a faculty member's sixth year of employment. This suggests that the critical set of data for understanding faculty research performance should be the number of articles published during their first 6 years after graduation. One might argue that 5 years might be more appropriate, but since articles often take up to a year to appear in print after acceptance, and it is the acceptance not the appearance in print that matters, we believe that 6 years is appropriate.

It is difficult to make compelling arguments for a specific number of time periods to study. Selecting a number too small risks the overweighting of an outlier year that is not representative. Selecting too large a number risks including data from eras that are no longer representative of the current era. We chose to study seven 6-year time periods from 1992 to 2004, because this includes a reasonable number of time periods and because this does not include the early years of Information Systems Research (founded in 1990), as the early years of any journal often include invited articles.

Appendix B provides details on the method we used for our analysis of the data. In short, we used the MISRC-AIS Faculty Directory (www.isfacdir.org), information in the article itself, and Web searches to classify authors of articles in MIS Quarterly or Information Systems Research as being IS faculty members or not (e.g., faculty in other disciplines, practitioners) (for this approach to identifying IS faculty members, see Athey and Plotnicki 2000; Chua et al. 2002). We then matched this set of authors and entries in the Faculty Directory to the authors of articles in the other 18 journals to produce a set of IS faculty authors. We then identified the graduation date for each of these IS faculty authors.

## Research Performance Results

U.S. and Canadian authors make up by far the largest proportion of authors in our sample, as they have in past studies (e.g., Athey and Plotnicki 2000; Beckman and Forsman 2002; Chua et al. 2002 Huang and Hsu 2005; Im et al. 1998). Therefore, we report our results both worldwide and just for U.S. and Canadian faculty. Worldwide, an average of 275 individuals per year accepted IS faculty appointments after graduating with their Ph.D. (of which 135 were at U.S. or Canadian universities).

Table 2 shows the results of our analyses. A quick glance at the numbers and percentages shows that elite journal publications are elusive. Worldwide, an average of 0.8 individuals out of each year's annual class of 275 people (0.3 percent) publish four or more articles in MISQ and/or ISR by the time they reach the promotion and tenure decision 6 years later. If we include the entire 20-journal set, this number rises to an average of 2.0 individuals (0.7 percent) out of each graduating class. The data in Table 2 are cumulative, so the average of 1.9 individuals (0.7 percent) who have published three or more articles in MISQ and/or ISR also includes those who have published four or more.

If we look only at U.S. and Canadian faculty, the numbers are, of course, smaller, because they are a subset of the worldwide authors. However, the percentages are higher because the size of each graduating class is smaller. On average, 1.9 individuals (1.4 percent) of each year's graduating class of 135 publish four or more articles in the 20-journal set, 2.9 (2.1 percent) publish three or more articles, 5.8 (4.3 percent) publish two or more and 15.2 (11.2 percent) publish one or more.

These results are somewhat similar to those of Athey and Plotnicki (2002) and Chua et al. (2002), who examined all IS researchers, not just those approaching tenure. Athey and Plotnicki found that only 345 IS researchers (regardless of graduation date) (about 4 percent of the worldwide population) published two or more articles over a 5 year period in their set of 10 elite and nonelite journals. Chua et al. concluded that about one third of all IS researchers (regardless of graduation date) had published three or more articles over an 11 year period in a set of 58 elite and nonelite journals.

## Research Standards for Promotion and Tenure

We were surprised by the results in Table 2. The criterion for research performance used at our institutions in the promotion and tenure decision turned out to be much higher than we expected compared to the performance of junior IS faculty members; we were holding the junior faculty at our institutions to a higher percentile standard than we realized. We concluded that our standards were high, relative to the actual performance of IS junior faculty members.

## Current Standards for Promotion and Tenure

We began to wonder if our expectations were similar to those held by faculty at other institutions, so we conducted an informal e-mail survey of senior faculty to benchmark our institutions' research performance expectations against those at a larger set of institutions. Appendix C describes the survey methodology in more detail. We received responses from 36 individuals, each drawn from a different leading U.S. or Canadian university. Eleven individuals (31 percent) reported that five or more "A" level articles were required for tenure and promotion at their university. Eight individuals (22 percent) reported that four articles were needed, bringing the cumulative percentage requiring four or more "A" articles to about 53 percent. Twelve (33 percent) required three articles, bringing the cumulative percentage for three or more "A" articles to 86 percent. From this informal survey, we concluded that the research performance expectations at our institutions were not atypical of expectations at other institutions. An important limitation is that what constitutes an elite "A" journal at each university is likely to not exactly match our set of 20 journals, so these responses should be used with care (see the additional limitations in Appendix C). Nonetheless, they provide a point of discussion.

## Research Performance Relative to Other Disciplines

One might attribute the differences between the current tenure standards and the actual performance of the junior faculty in our sample to a lower than expected rate of research productivity from the junior faculty in our sample. The question, then, is: Do IS researchers in general (not just junior faculty members) publish elite journal articles at the same rate as faculty in other disciplines? Swanson (2004) produced a metric used to compare research productivity across U.S. business school disciplines. He calculated the average number of authors per year on articles published in elite journals divided by the number of faculty members in each discipline at AACSB-accredited schools. This metric is, by definition, U.S.-centric (but includes international authors in the calculations), and also includes authors from outside the discipline (e.g., accounting faculty who publish in finance journals are treated as finance faculty). Nonetheless, it does provide a basis for comparison.

Table 3 presents Swanson's data for Accounting, Finance, Management and Marketing (the only four disciplines he examined) based on a 20-year data set (1980 through 1999) of articles drawn from the discipline-specific journals defined by Trieschmann et al. (2000) (i.e., the journals listed for each discipline in Table 1). This table also presents the same metric calculated using our data from 1992 through 2004 on authors of articles in $MISQ$ and $ISR$ (regardless of whether they were in their first 6 years or not). From the percentages in Table 3, we can see that IS faculty have, on average, produced fewer articles per capita per year than faculty in all other disciplines. We have conducted more detailed analyses examining research productivity over time which concluded that IS faculty members currently publish elite articles at a rate below faculty members in all other business school disciplines (see Valacich et al. 2006).

## Recommended Standards for Promotion and Tenure

We believe that the current promotion and tenure standards expressed in our informal survey are high for the level of research productivity that we as a discipline are capable of supporting. Promotion and tenure criteria differ from institution to institution, although the set of leading schools we surveyed had somewhat similar policies. All but one of the U.S. universities in the top 50 set from Trieschmann et al. that were included in our tenure and promotion survey were classified as research extensive universities by the Carnegie Foundation (2000): institutions that award 50 or more doctoral degrees per year across at least 15 disciplines. Thus in discussing promotion and tenure standards, we focus primarily on research extensive universities.

Simply put, we believe that current standards for promotion and tenure in IS used at many institutions are not appropriate. We believe that these standards are based on a conscious or unconscious use of management, marketing, and/or finance as the reference discipline for comparison, disciplines that have higher per capita rates of publication than IS. We believe that the appropriate discipline for comparison is Accounting.

Read et al. (1998) present data on promotion and tenure decisions for 495 Accounting faculty at AACSB-accredited business schools in the U.S. Analysis of their data shows that Accounting faculty were promoted with an average of 1.3 articles in the Trieschmann et al. set of top-three Accounting journals. $^{2}$ Their data are not broken out by the Carnegie classification of school, and given their use of all AACSB institutions, some proportion of their sample includes universities that are not classified as research extensive. If we use their data for just private universities, more of which are research extensive universities, we get an average of 2.0 articles in these elite journals.

Our recommendations for appropriate promotion and tenure standards are presented in Table 4. Our opinion reflects the unique nature, goals, and focus of research-focused universities and the approximate relative performance levels that these standards imply, based on current data. We believe that universities classified as research extensive by the Carnegie Foundation should strive to grant promotion and tenure to the top 5 percent of faculty. Given the current state of the IS discipline, this equates approximately to a publication level of two articles in elite business school journals by tenure time. Elite research universities (e.g., top 20 research schools) may wish to have higher standards and choose to grant promotion and tenure to only the top 2 percent of faculty, which, for IS faculty, would suggest three articles in elite business school journals. Research universities classified as research intensive by the Carnegie Foundation should allow their mission to help in setting their standards; we believe that those for which research plays a dominant role should strive to have faculty that are members of the top 10 percent, which equates to about one article in elite business school journals.

## Implications for the Information Systems Discipline

We believe that the current state of the IS discipline calls for change. We have presented our opinions on the appropriate standards for promotion and tenure. We encourage faculty members making promotion and tenure decisions to be cognizant of our data about the relative performance of junior faculty members and of our recommendations for standards in setting local standards and in assessing candidates at their institutions. We believe that these data also present two overarching implications for the discipline as a whole: the need to educate college and school promotion and tenure committees, and the need to increase the number of elite journal articles we produce.

## Communicating with Faculty in Other Disciplines

Promotion and tenure recommendations are made by both IS faculty committees and school/college committees whose members are drawn from other disciplines. It is not sufficient for IS faculty members to be aware of our data and the implications they have for assessing the relative performance of junior faculty members. It is critical that faculty outside our discipline are also aware. It is well understood at most schools that Accounting faculty produce elite articles at a much lower rate than other business school disciplines. It is not well understood that IS faculty members produce elite articles at even lower rates.

It is essential that we as a discipline educate our colleagues in other disciplines about the level of performance that our junior faculty can reasonably achieve. We call on department heads to educate colleagues in other disciplines. When we write external review letters for school/college promotion and tenure committees, we are routinely asked to assess the performance of the candidate relative to others at similar points in their careers. We call on those writing external review letters to be cognizant of our recommendations in Table 4 and to use Table 2 as a benchmark in evaluating the research performance of the individual being considered for promotion and tenure when considering the number of articles published in elite journals.

Of course, many IS faculty publish in IS journals that are not included in the set of journals that we included in our study. Each university may have a slightly different set of journals that its faculty consider to be elite. Very often, this set of IS journals is larger than the small set that we used in our analysis. These broader lists may accommodate topics that may not be perceived as a natural fit for either of the two elite journals used in our analysis (e.g., software engineering, data mining, artificial intelligence). Thus these benchmarks will need to be considered by the letter writer in light of local standards, journal lists, and the relative publication opportunities across disciplines (Valacich et al. 2006). For example, when writing external review letters, it is important to indicate journals that are well-respected for publishing topics that are not typically covered in the elite journals. Writers of external review letters should also indicate when an article has had critical impact to the discipline, either because of extensive citations or its ability to initiate or redirect research on a topic.

## Increasing the Number of Elite IS Journal Articles Published

Elite IS journals publish fewer articles per capita than do the elite journals in Accounting, Finance, Management, and Marketing. We believe that the information systems discipline needs additional elite publication opportunities by increasing the number of articles in existing elite journals, by recognizing additional journals as elite, and by changing the mission of MISQ.

## Increasing the Output of MISQ and ISR

Simply put, MISQ and ISR need to double the number of articles they publish each year to bring our discipline's per capita publication rate closer to that of other disciplines (Valacich et al. 2006). The editor-in-chief of MISQ (Saunders 2005a) and the editor-in-chief of ISR (Sambamurthy 2005) have both committed to increasing the number of articles published. We call on them to set a goal of doubling the number of articles published each year by the end of their tenure.

Expanding the number of articles published is both a push and pull problem. The journals need to receive more high quality submissions and to accept more high quality articles. It is our opinion, based on our experiences as authors and reviewers, and based on conversations with other senior researchers, that the review process at MISQ and ISR has changed for the worse over the past five years. Part of the reason that we as a discipline produce fewer elite research articles per capita is that many reviewers now expect papers to be “flawless” to be worthy of publication in MISQ or ISR—a take-no-prisoners style of reviewing $^{3}$ rather than the developmental style of reviewing sought by both MISQ and ISR.

We remind reviewers and associate editors at MISQ and ISR that the mission of these journals is to publish, not reject, papers. The sad truth is that it is literally impossible to produce flawless research (Dennis and Valacich 2001; McGrath 1982; Starbuck 2003). $^{4}$ We call on reviewers and associate editors to actively seek to publish papers that are not flawless; that is, to look beyond flawed methodology (because all papers will contain flawed methods) and seek to balance the theoretical contributions against the methodological flaws in papers.

We call on senior editors at these journals to educate reviewers and associate editors (see Bergh 2004). Unfortunately, in our experience, such education has little effect as the culture of reviewing induces a critical frame (Harrison 2002; Kachelmeier 2004); this is how we teach our Ph.D. students to examine research papers and this critical culture often continues into mid-career. By mid-career, many faculty members have learned to overcome the natural tendency to view the review process as a gate-keeping process and have come to view it as a publication process. We believe that senior editors need to educate reviewers and associate editors both a priori and in situ; that is, during the review process, senior editors need to educate and correct reviewers and associate editors when they adopt a critical frame. By making the review process more developmental, as MISQ and ISR both desire, we will publish more articles.

Expanding the number of articles is also a financial problem. Journal pages cost money, and the more articles a journal publishes, the more it costs to operate it. We call on the Association for Information Systems and INFORMS to increase their funding for MISQ and ISR to enable these two journals, as the elite IS journals, to publish more articles each year.

## Increasing the Number of Elite IS Journals

IS currently ranks lower than other business disciplines in elite journal publishing opportunities (Valacich et al. 2006). Inequities in publishing opportunities can have many potential impacts on not only individual researchers such as not getting tenure but also on the discipline as a whole. For example, lower promotion and tenure rates across the IS discipline as compared to other business disciplines may foster widespread perceptions that IS researchers are less skilled than researchers in other disciplines as well as cause greater faculty turnover within the IS discipline. Over time, this turnover will act to weaken programs and the discipline as a whole. Thus, gaining more elite publishing opportunities within the IS discipline may be paramount to the IS discipline's long term survival.

Given the need for additional elite publication opportunities, we do not believe that it is possible to bring the publication rate of IS faculty in line with that of other disciplines by simply increasing the number of the articles in MISQ and ISR. Instead, we believe that the IS discipline needs additional elite journals that are widely recognized by most IS faculty members as being elite. A quick glance at Table 1 shows that most disciplines other than IS have three or more elite journals (and those that have only two journals have journals that publish monthly). But what journals should be considered?

Numerous articles have been published identifying the “best” IS journals (for a summary, see Saunders 2005b). After MISQ and ISR (and omitting Management Science, which is already included in our 20-journal sample set), two journals have historically vied for third place, both in faculty opinion surveys and ISI impact factor rankings: Communications of the ACM (CACM) and Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS) (Lowry et al. 2004; Mylonopoulos and Theoharakis 2001; Peffers and Ya 2003; Rainer and Miller 2005; Saunders 2005b). CACM is no longer a research journal, having shifted its focus to applied, practitioner-focused articles (Peffers and Ya 2003). CACM is also not a pure IS journal, focusing more on computer science (Rainer and Miller 2005).

Based on ISI rankings and faculty opinion, JMIS is often considered the best candidate for becoming a third elite journal. However, its status is sometimes controversial since it is published by a private, for-profit organization (as opposed to other elite journals that are traditionally sponsored by academic societies), since a noticeable proportion of its articles come from special issues that fast track conference papers through review processes that prevent non-conference papers from consideration, and since its editorial board is relatively static.

Another candidate for elite journal status is the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS). JAIS is a relatively new journal and does not yet have a track record, ISI ratings, or high rankings in faculty opinion surveys (e.g., Lowry et al. 2004; Peffers and Ya 2003). JAIS is also an online-only journal and some individuals are prejudiced against such journals. Yet its strong editorial board and pedigree as an AIS journal suggests its promise.

Elevating JMIS and/or JAIS so that they gain widespread acceptance as elite journals, both within and outside the IS discipline, will require a concerted effort to overcome current perceived or real deficiencies. For example, JMIS might overcome some concerns by developing a more traditional editorial structure and limiting the number of annual fast track special issues it publishes. Likewise for JAIS, a first step is to gain ISI ratings status and a continued commitment to publish very high quality articles—articles that are widely cited by other ISI rated journals—in order to gain solid ISI impact ratings. JAIS might also produce a limited run print edition. It is impossible to outline all of the steps necessary to move a journal into elite status. Nevertheless, we believe that for the good of the discipline both journals need to focus on gaining elite status by identifying real and perceived deficiencies and then pursuing remedies for these shortcomings.

## Increasing Research Diversity in MISQ

Information systems is inherently a diverse discipline, drawing on a variety of reference disciplines (Banville and Landry 1989; Westin et al. 1994). $^{5}$ Unfortunately, this research diversity is not captured equally well by all elite IS journals: ISR and JMIS are as diverse as, and are representative of, the IS discipline as a whole, but historically, MISQ has been noticeably less diverse than, and not representative of, the IS discipline as whole (Vessey et al. 2002). $^{6}$

The lack of diversity at MISQ has been intentional. According to its mission statement, MISQ “publishes research ‘concerning both the management of information technology and the use of information technology for managerial and organizational purposes.’” (MIS Quarterly 2005). In contrast, ISR has adopted a very broad view of the field: ISR “will take a broad and open view about what it publishes and seeks to be an influential voice on both core and boundary oriented information systems phenomena” (Sambamurthy 2005, p. 1). JMIS has also intentionally taken a broad view of IS: JMIS intends “to provide an integrated view of the entire field of MIS” (Zwass 2005).

In the years since the Vessey et al. article appeared, MISQ has attempted to broaden its focus by including editors with an expertise outside the focus identified by its mission statement and by publishing a broader set of articles. However, the mission statement has remained unchanged. Although there is arguably value in a sharp, clearly defined mission statement (especially during a journal's early years), we believe it is now time to develop a broader editorial mission. In our opinion, this lack of diversity and representativeness in MISQ is a major structural problem facing the IS discipline. When one of the two (or three) elite IS journals is closed to a body of mainstream IS research, the IS faculty in those areas are disadvantaged in their pursuit of promotion and tenure, and the per capita productivity of elite journal articles of the discipline as a whole suffers.

Therefore, we call on MISQ to (1) change its mission statement and (2) increase its diversity to become more representative of the IS discipline as a whole. MISQ has in recent years worked to broaden its focus, and we call on MISQ to continue to work toward greater breadth by publishing more research

\- based on computer science, management science and economics,

\- employing the group/team and computer system levels of analysis,

\- focusing on specific problem domains (e.g., scientific computing, bioinformatics, education, entertainment), and on systems and software concepts (e.g., architecture, engineering, programming, development methodologies, security), and

• using conceptual arguments, pure theoretical analyses and mathematical methods.

## Conclusion

We believe that the IS discipline currently is on a dangerous course, at least at U.S. and Canadian universities. Less than three individuals (2.1 percent) out of every graduating Ph.D. class of about 135 individuals who seek employment at U.S. or Canadian universities will produce enough elite journal articles to receive promotion and tenure at a typical research extensive university using current standards. Therefore, we call on promotion and tenure committees to rethink what research standards are appropriate for IS faculty members at their institutions, given our data. We believe that we need to use new, lower, research standards for promotion and tenure, standards more akin to the standards used in Accounting. We also believe that it is critical that we communicate these standards to faculty in other disciplines.

This problem is not unique to our junior scholars. IS faculty members produce less than half as many elite journal articles per person per year than do our colleagues in Management, Marketing, and Finance. We believe it is essential that we as a discipline strive to increase the number of elite articles we publish. We believe that (1) ISR and MISQ should double the number of articles they publish each year to match the publication rates of other business school disciplines, (2) that we need to promote a third or fourth widely recognized elite journal, and (3) that the mission statement and research focus of MISQ should be revised to promote a broader, more diverse, view of research that is more reflective of the IS discipline as a whole.

If we fail to act now, we envision a rather gloomy future for the discipline. If current promotion and tenure standards remain, few faculty members will receive tenure at their first institution and faculty turnover will increase. Increased turnover will lead to less continuity and political power within leading institutions. Reduced power will diminish resources and influence and, ultimately, information systems programs will be in jeopardy. As programs diminish in stature within leading universities, the discipline as a whole will be damaged, possibly irreparably.

Fortunately, we have time to act. But the clock is running.

## Appendix A

## Methods for Journal Selection

What IS journals are “beyond reproach” as the top quality research journals? One approach is to survey faculty members for their perceptions of quality. Saunders (2005b) provides a good summary of previous journal ranking articles. Recent studies of faculty opinions show considerable differences of opinion among researchers in different parts of the world over what constitutes the top research journals (Lowry et al. 2004; Mylonopoulos and Theoharakis 2001; Peffers and Ya 2003; Rainer and Miller 2005; Saunders 2005b). The study by Lowry et al. (2004) shows researchers in all regions, on average, agree that the top two IS journals are MIS Quarterly (MISQ) and Information Systems Research (ISR) (respectively), but beyond this, there is less agreement. The third place journal differs by region, but even so, in each region, the third place journal is scored at less than half the quality of ISR, suggesting that the top two journals are clearly the best, with others noticeably lower in perceived quality than MISQ and ISR. The study by Mylonopoulos and Theoharakis (2001) places these two journals first and second in North America, but adds Communications of the ACM (CACM) as either first or second in other regions, with MISQ and ISR second or third. CACM publishes primarily short articles in a magazine format targeted at computer science practitioners, and is typically not considered a pure research journal (Peffers and Ya 2003). CACM is also not a pure IS journal. We therefore excluded it.

While faculty opinions matter, a second approach for judging journal quality is to examine the extent to which published research is incorporated in and influences subsequent research (Trieschmann et al. 2000). Two traditional measures of this influence are the total number of citations and the citation impact factor. The top two IS journals by citation count are MISQ and ISR (respectively) (Katerattanakui et al. 2003). The top two IS journals by citation impact factor are again MISQ and ISR (respectively) (ISI 2003).

Given that both faculty opinion and citation analysis agree that the top two IS research journals are MISQ and ISR, we choose these as the journals to use in our analysis. This is the same conclusion reached by Trieschmann et al. (2000), who also selected MISQ and ISR as the elite quality IS journals.

As with all empirical data collection decisions, these have some important limitations. First, some IS faculty publish in IS journals that are not included in this set. Our goal was to focus solely on the elite journals whose research quality was beyond reproach. Each university may have a slightly different set of journals that its faculty consider to be elite. Thus these benchmarks will need to be considered in light of local standards.

Second, some IS faculty publish in elite journals in other disciplines outside of the business school (e.g., psychology, computer science, engineering). We made no attempt to assess publications in nonbusiness school journals. By omitting these journals, we will tend to understate performance, thus making our counts and relative percentages smaller than they should be. Thus the data should be used with care; it represents only the body of elite quality IS research and business school research, omitting research produced by IS faculty in nonbusiness disciplines.

Despite these limitations, we believe that these data can provide useful empirical benchmarks as one element in assessing faculty research performance. We believe that these data may be particularly useful as we attempt to help our colleagues in other disciplines with different standards (Swanson 2004) who sit on school, college, and university promotion and tenure committees to understand what constitutes “outstanding,” “excellent,” “high quality,” etc. research performance in the IS discipline.

## Appendix B

## Methods for Calculating Research Performance

A parser was written that extracted bibliographic data (author, date, title, journal, and issue) from EBSCO's Academic Search Premier and Business Source Premier databases that contained published articles for the 20 journals in our set for the 12 year time period under study. Because most faculty are hired in the fall, and most promotion and tenure packets are submitted in the fall, we choose September 1992 to September 2004 as the 12 year period. We then broke this up into seven 6-year periods for analysis purposes (September 1992 to September 1998, September 1993 to September 1999,...September 1998 to September 2004).

After the parser was run, the data was manually examined to remove non-peer-reviewed articles, such as editor's comments and errata. The data was then examined to remove typographical errors in names and match full names to nicknames (e.g., Chris and Christopher). Finally, the data were examined to identify IS authors whose names had changed during these time periods and produce records using just one name, not multiple names for the same individual. Such matching relies on the personal knowledge of the individual authors in the data, which is by definition incomplete. Five faculty members, graduates of different universities with diverse research interests, examined the data to identify authors with multiple names. While we believe that many of these cases have been identified and consolidated, this remains a potential limitation.

The process resulted in 654 unique authors of articles in MISQ and ISR over this 12-year time period. We first matched these authors to entries in the MISRC-AIS Faculty Directory. Researchers who see themselves as IS faculty members list themselves in this Directory (Chua et al. 2002). This produced a set of 475 faculty members, leaving 179 authors not listed in the Faculty Directory. Because not all IS researchers are necessarily listed in the Faculty Directory, we attempted to determine if these 179 individuals were IS researchers who graduated within the time period of our analysis. We used the affiliation listed on the published article, the biographical information it contained, and Web searches to classify these 179 authors. We classified 130 authors as not being members of the IS discipline (e.g., faculty in other disciplines, members of industry); these were thus excluded from our analysis. We classified 18 authors as IS researchers and identified their graduation date or determined that their graduation date did not fall in our analysis period. We found that seven authors had retired or were deceased, and thus were unlikely to have graduated in the time periods covered by our analyses. We were unable to classify 24 authors. Thus out of the total of 654 authors of IS articles (475 found in the faculty directory and 179 authors of articles in MISQ/ISR not in the Faculty Directory), we were unable to classify 24 (3.7 percent) individuals. All of these 24 individuals were authors of only one article.

We then matched the authors of articles in the other 18 journals to listings in the Faculty Directory and our author data from MISQ and ISR. There were 32,149 author entries in these other 18 journals, so we did no intensive hand searches as we did for MISQ and ISR.

We began our analysis with those individuals who graduated in 1992, using the normal count approach by which each article is counted as one publication by each individual author (Athey and Plotnicki 2000; Chua et al. 2002; Huang and Hsu 2005) as this is the approach commonly used by promotion and tenure committees. The Faculty Directory lists 283 individuals who graduated in 1992, 134 of whom are currently on faculty at a U.S. or Canadian university. We then counted the number of articles published by each individual who graduated in 1992 in MISQ/ISR and in the entire set of 20 business school journals up until September 1998 (presuming that these individuals would be considered for tenure in the fall of their sixth year, i.e., 1997). For example, only 24 (8.5 percent) of these 283 individuals published one or more articles in our 20 journal set in the 6 years after their graduation. These are cumulative totals and therefore include individuals who published two articles, three articles, and so on.

We repeated these steps for each of the remaining six time periods for individuals who graduated in 1993, 1994, 1995, 1996, 1997, and 1998. We then calculated the weighted average number of individuals (and percentage) across these seven samples (weighted by the number of graduates in each year). We then repeated these calculations using just U.S. and Canadian faculty members.

## Appendix C

## Methods for Tenure and Promotion Survey

For our sample of leading U.S. and Canadian universities, we began by selecting the top 50 most research productive business schools from the Trieschmann et al. (2000) study. These schools were those listed in Table 2 (p. 1136) in Trieschmann et al. To this set, we added four Canadian universities selected for the quality of their business schools and the quality of the IS research: McGill University, Queen's University, the University of British Columbia, and the University of Western Ontario.

We then identified a senior IS faculty member at each school. We were unable to identify an appropriate individual at five universities and therefore dropped them from our analysis (Columbia University, Dartmouth College, Northwestern University, University of Chicago, and Washington University (St. Louis)). This resulted in a set of 49 universities.

The first two authors then e-mailed the selected individual at each school explaining the nature of our request and asking the number of A-level publications in MISQ, ISR, Academy of Management Journal, etc. required for tenure and promotion. Each institution likely has its own definition of elite journals which may or may not match the 20-journal set used in our publication productivity analysis. Providing a clear-cut answer to such a nuanced question is difficult, given that different journals have different values at different universities, and because even individuals with ostensibly the same research record may be viewed differently depending upon the exact nature of the articles and their individual contributions. Nonetheless, it is a question that senior faculty are routinely called upon to answer as part of the tenure and promotion process and for which some patterns have evolved at most universities.

This survey suffers from several limitations. We examined the espoused opinions of the respondents, rather than their actual behavior; our respondents may in practice use different standards than they reported to us. Since we surveyed only one senior faculty member at each university, the responses may not be representative of the full range of faculty opinions at each university. It is possible, even likely, that the definition of an elite journal differs from university to university, so our set of 20 elite journals may not match the journal sets considered by the respondents when they responded to our survey; their sets may have been larger or smaller than the set we used in our analyses. Finally, as one respondent who declined to answer our question pointed out, it is not the number of articles than matters, which is by definition backward looking; instead, what matters is what the impact of the candidate's work suggests about the candidate's potential for future research, which is a very subtle and nuanced question that cannot be answered by simple article counts.
