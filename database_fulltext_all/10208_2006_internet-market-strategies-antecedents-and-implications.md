# Internet market strategies: Antecedents and implications

Jaeki Song <sup>a,1</sup>, Fatemeh Mariam Zahedi <sup>b,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Box 42101, Lubbock, TX 79409, USA <sup>b</sup> School of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin Milwaukee, P.O. Box 742, Milwaukee, WI 53201, USA

Received 7 April 2004; received in revised form 14 September 2004; accepted 20 June 2005 Available online 15 August 2005

## Abstract

The Web is a new market channel that has become a serious alternative to traditional channels. At present, there is little strategy analysis available to guide a company’s decision about its involvement in the internet market. In this paper, we differentiate between the internet and traditional market channels and define two fundamental strategies for operating on the internet as: pure and mixed. Based on models of competition in microeconomic theory and resource-based perspectives, we develop a conceptual model for channel selection. We discuss the results and their implications for companies operating on the internet.

Keywords: Creative pricing policies; Mixed strategy; Pure strategy; Resource-based theory; Internet competition; Internet market; Traditional markets; IMS model

## 1. Introduction

Firms rely on the Web as an alternate channel for branding their products, transactions, and public relations. By doing so, they reduce their transaction and communication costs, increase the speed and quality of communication with potential customers and suppliers, and expand their markets beyond their geographical boundaries. Online publishers, for instance, have lower printing and circulation costs, maintain smaller inventories, and have better relationships with customers than conventional publishers. The rise of amazon.com to a prominent online company with US\$ 5.3 billion in net sales 2003 [2] as well as Dell’s 50,000 employees and its US\$ 34.4 billion annual revenue in 2003 [22] attest to the great potential of the internet. Intel reached US\$ 1 billion sales on e-business per month in the year it deployed its e-commerce system [60]. However, while the use of the internet can reduce costs, it also has the potential to reduce the profit of inefficient firms [6]. Furthermore, operation in the internet does not necessarily produce profit, as demonstrated by the number of internet companies that have failed.

It has long been recognized that strategic positioning is critical to the long-run survival of any firm [63]. The internet has created new avenues for creative rethinking of business models, processes, and relationships [16,44,55,69]. There is now a stronger demand for a sound strategy, which in turn requires an understanding of the differences between operating in the traditional markets and on the internet [18,61]. However, corporate decision makers need to understand the implications of each strategy and the factors associated with adopting it. Hence, identifying market strategies and understanding their driving forces and implications is necessary in making strategic decisions regarding Web investment and marketing channels.

In this paper, we investigate e-commerce strategy choices, their antecedents, and their implications. In doing so, we need to carefully delineate basic strategic choices. We compare the characteristics of marketing channels and categorize internet market strategies into two types: pure (only online) and mixed (or hybrid— those operating in both online and traditional markets). We argue that the choice of a market strategy does not take place in a vacuum. Business decision makers need to know the implications of selecting one strategy over another.

## 2. Internet market strategies and pricing policies

We distinguish between two types of market channels in general: traditional and internet. In the internet market, buyers and sellers rely on it to conduct some parts of public, market-related activities. We can differentiate this from traditional markets in three dimensions: access, competition, and information.

## 2.1. Market access

Traditional market channels have a location constraint that reduces consumers’ utility [36]. With its global reach, the internet market enjoys a lower level of this constraint [15] and requires less start-up resources. Furthermore, the difference in market structure for intermediation may also reduce transaction costs for consumers and suppliers. It has been observed that the internet has replaced the role of traditional intermediaries, a phenomenon called ‘‘disintermediation.’’ At the same time, a new intermediary type, called cybermediaries or infomediaries, has emerged to provide, process, and validate information on the increasingly large number of suppliers [5]. Successful examples of such online firms are yahoo.com (recently purchased by Kelkoo, its European counterpart) and google.com. The information provided by such firms helps reduce transaction costs on the internet by reducing the search cost and increasing information access.

Even though the internet increases suppliers’ and consumers’ access to the market, it constrains access for certain categories of consumers and suppliers. Participating in the internet market requires technology know-how and resources. Furthermore, substantial resources are needed to develop and maintain websites that engender consumer traffic, customer loyalty, profitability, and growth. There is some evidence that e-marketing has become increasingly resource-intensive, making firms with limited resources unable to reach their intended consumers.

## 2.2. Price information

There is a cost associated with searching for information on products and prices. The high cost of such searches creates inefficiencies in the market and reduces competition. The internet increases price competition. It enhances customers’ utility by improving the match between consumer needs and products, and moves this market closer to perfect competition as perceived in Walrasian competitive equilibrium theory. For example, the availability of book prices on amazon.com and barnesandnoble.com makes it easy to compare online book prices. Moreover, firms such as priceline.com give online shoppers a chance to compare prices.

On the other hand, internet information overload creates congestion, which taxes customers’ cognitive capabilities and increases their search cost. Information seekers rely on ‘‘infomediaries’’ such as search engines to reduce their search cost, which may reduce some vendors’ visibility. Furthermore, as online customers’ options increase, they rely on brand names (for websites and products) in order to reduce their search costs. Creating brand names is a costly option for online vendors. Moreover, for non-digital products not deliverable directly by downloading, a purchase can involve substantial shipping, handling, and processing costs. Hence, online vendors may recover part of their reduction in price by charging more for shipping and handling.

## 2.3. Competition

The internet may increase competition in two ways. First, it removes some of the monopolistic pricing powers due to market inefficiencies in traditiona markets by reducing barriers to entry—resources required to reach consumers in the internet market are relatively low. Second, the open-market nature of the internet increases the bargaining power of buyers by increasing their ability to search for the best price. However, slack resources are needed for collection of market intelligence and for timely moves to develop brand and mindshare dominance. For example, early moves by yahoo.com and etrade.com gave them a competitive advantage over later entrants such as infoseek.com. Furthermore, the technological and knowledge capital needed to sustain visibility and competition may serve as a major barrier for the survival of small firms with limited resources.

While the internet and traditional markets differ in market access and price information, there are few published studies on formalizing internet market strategies, investigating the factors influencing them, and the pricing policy implications of a strategy choice. In the pure internet marketing strategy, the firm does not have any physical location for business transactions with its customers (e.g., eBay.com and yahoo.com). In the mixed strategy, the internet complements and enhances the firm’s traditional marketing activities: customers reach it through a physical location or its internet site (e.g., Barnes & Noble bookstore). Some pure online stores, such as Dell, have begun setting up physical stores for examining and ordering products with orders delivered by mail. Our categorization of these internet market strategies corresponds to Brynjolfsson and Smith’s [14] grouping of the internet retailers into the internet and hybrid types, as well as to that of Subramani and Walden [68].

In this research, we argue that if the internet market is more competitive than the traditional market, the choice of pure strategy over mixed strategy should lead to the use of creative and flexible pricing policies.

Furthermore, companies do have free choice in their selection of an internet market strategy and their market and organizational constraints influence their choice of an internet market strategy and pricing policies as well. We draw on theories in microeconomics and strategic management to support our argument. First, however, we need to discuss the types of creative pricing policies needed for operating in a more competitive market.

## 2.4. Pricing policies

We identify three types of creative pricing policies: discount, bundle, and auction. These are in addition to the normal prices dictated by a firm’s cost structure and market forces. Creative and flexible pricing policies have been adopted by companies operating on the internet market. Lohse and Spiller [47] showed that 22% of 137 internet retail stores sold their goods at discount prices. Jarvenpaa and Todd [39] observed that the most influential factor that lures customers to the internet is discounted price: therefore, creative pricing is the core business model of many internet companies.

Bundle pricing is implemented by selling two or more products or services together. There are various types of bundling: pure, mixed, tie-in, and add-on. Bundle pricing can stimulate demand and increase profit. For large bundles, it has been shown that bundling of goods is highly profitable [7]. Another reason is to increase efficiency and reduce cost. Savings in transaction costs make selling products as a bundle more economical than selling them separately [58], especially in digital products and services [20].

Auction pricing is a market structure that has a set of explicit rules for setting prices and allocating resources based on bids from the market participants. Auction types are categorized as English, Dutch, firstprice sealed, second-price sealed, and reverse auction [51]. Auction has become a popular pricing policy on the internet. In 2003, eBay reported US\$ 2.17 billion in net revenue and US\$ 1.75 billion in net profit [25]. In the second quarter of 2004, it had 114 million users and 332 million listings. The phenomenal success of eBay (founded in September 1995) attests to the attractiveness of this method of creative pricing and service. Offering auction services may make a site more attractive, reduce the inventory cost of slowselling products, reduce transaction costs, and provide invaluable insight into customer preferences.

Other types of creative pricing policies include free shipping (with or without minimum purchase), conditional discounts, rebates, freebies, sweepstakes, and no-payment financing. For example, in May 2002, drugstore.com began testing the pricing policy of offering a 5% discount on every order above US\$ 200 to those customers whose annual purchases had been less than US\$ 200 [29]. During July 2002, Dell automatically entered all its PC buyers into daily sweepstakes and Gateway gave away free printers to its customers. Dell customers were also given a chance to win a US\$ 50,000 sweepstake and Apple offered a period of no-payment financing to its customers [26].

## 3. The research model

In formulating our internet market strategy (IMS) model, we synthesize competition theories in microeconomic and resource-based and contingency theories in management to argue that due to the differences between traditional and internet markets, the adoption of an internet market strategy has a major consequence in the choice of pricing policy. Furthermore, a firm’s market environment and organizational contingency factors influence its internet market strategy and its pricing policy, as shown in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Internet market strategy and pricing policy

In exploring the impact of the internet market strategy on the pricing policy, we draw on the game theoretic models in microeconomics used for studying firms’ behavior in the market. The Cournot model is one of the first models of non-cooperative duopoly by the famous French mathematical economist. In this model, each firm decides how much to produce, assuming that the quantity of its rival is fixed, in order to maximize its profit in a downward sloping demand function and a constant marginal cost. This model shows that the price falls as the number of competitors increases from one to two. In the Bertrand model, firms compete on price and the lowest price competitor takes the entire market. In the Bertrand model, the equilibrium price declines to the level of the competition price (marginal cost) faster than the Cournot model.

In modeling an oligopoly, Edgeworth added capacity constraints to the Bertrand model. The Bertrand–Edgeworth (BE) model does not yield a single optimum price. Firms need to use a ‘‘mixed strategy’’ of offering different (randomized) prices in order to balance between the desire to increase the price to enhance profit and to lower the price to increase market share [1]. One interpretation of the presence of mixed strategy for market equilibrium is that firms use them to differentiate between customers who are informed and uninformed about the price [21]. Although the Cournot and Bertrand models are different in their assumptions, it has been shown that prices move closer to competitive prices as the number of competitors increases. In the empirical investigation of these models, Bresnahan and Reiss [11] have found that prices decline as the number of retailers increases. This decline is not continuous and the price adjustment is more consistent with the results of the Cournot than of the Bertrand.

Put in the context of the internet market, we would expect that consumers who participate in the internet market would be more price conscious. Recent studies of pricing in e-commerce indicate that Web-customers have high price sensitivity. For example, in analyzing the perceived customer value in e-commerce, Chen and Dubinsky [19] show that higher prices significantly contribute to higher perceived risk and a lower perceived value. In a study of online sales of vacation packages, vendors had a two-to-one lead over their offline competitors [30]. In a study of online pricing for hotels, O’Connor reported on several studies (by Gomez.com, PhoCus-Wright, Travel Industry Association of America, and Joint Hospitality Industry Congress) in which price has been identified as a key motivating factor for travel purchases [56].

On the other hand, it has been argued that brickand-mortar retailers may better succeed in differentiating themselves and charging higher prices. Models using game theory have shown that firms can use location differences to charge higher prices for similar products [28]. Firms with a mixed strategy are bound by their brick-and-mortar prices and are less flexible in competing in price. Furthermore, the initial success of pioneering online vendors is attributed to their competitive pricing policies. Cao and Gruca [17] compared the price difference between pure and hybrid online stores and concluded that prices of pure e-tailers are lower that those of hybrid ones.

To investigate whether this pricing policy is still in effect, we compared prices for 20 hardcover books sold on amazon.com and barnesnoble.com with those sold in a Barnes & Noble store in a Midwest town during September 2004. Our comparison showed that the online prices for the hardcover books were below the in-store prices, even for the titles that were on sale in the brick-and-mortar store. Moreover, both online stores provided the opportunity for online shoppers to order used books for the same titles at much lower prices. Additionally, amazon.com offered a hardcover option for more books, and its prices were usually lower than those of barnesandnoble.com for the hardcover titles that we investigated. In order to compete, barnesandnoble.- com provided additional discounts for its club members, who pay a fixed annual membership fee of US\$ 25.

We therefore argue that sellers in the internet market need to have competitive pricing strategies that attract price conscious consumers and should expect their prices to be lower than those in the traditional markets. Based on these findings, we posit that since the internet is a more competitive market, companies with pure strategy need more flexible and creative pricing strategies than those who adopt mixed strategies. Mixed strategy permits vendors to operate in comparatively less competitive traditional markets, and they also need to be concerned about cannibalizing their own offline customers. Hence:

Hypothesis 1. A firm with a pure strategy is more likely to adopt creative pricing policies (H1 in Fig. 1).

## 3.2. Environmental factors

The choice of the internet market strategy and the subsequent pricing policy is dictated by the market environment of the firm. The importance of environmental factors has been recognized in the contingency theory of firms [23], which posits that a firm’s performance and choice of strategy are contingent on the environment in which it operates. We draw on the existing literature and empirical evidence to identify two of the most important environmental factors that could affect the choice: extent of competition and industry type.

## 3.2.1. Extent of competition

Porter emphasizes the importance of competitive forces and the number of relevant competitors. It is argued that the internet market is a great equalizer that has moved us closer to an efficient market and reduced barriers to entry. Access to online vendors’ prices is instantaneous, and online customers can use sites to compare online prices. Furthermore, the menu cost is lower for online vendors (menu cost is defined as the vendor’s cost for changing the posted price). Additionally, in online stores, product variety is higher than that found in any large offline store. Brynjolfsson et al. compared the variety of products on amazon.com with typical large brick-and-mortar stores and reported that amazon.com carried 10–50 times more products than typical offline stores. At the same time, online stores needed to change their prices more frequently in response to market conditions. However, their costs of changing prices were lower than their traditional counterparts.

Since the internet market is more competitive and dynamic, business models of pure and mixed strategies may differ. To survive in an online market, a company needs to have a low cost and agile business strategy. Furthermore, when a hybrid firm, with loyal offline customers, lowers its online price to attract new online customers, it must also lower the price for its offline customers. Consequently, the firm will have a lower profit in both online and offline markets. If it charges two prices, one for online and one for offline customers, there may be a loss of offline customers. On the resource side, mixed strategy may incur two non-complementary overhead types, such as skills needed for building and maintaining online stores and those to manage and maintain physical stores. Such non-complementarity may drive up business costs in a mixed strategy. Thus, in a highly competitive market, a company may not be able to sustain such costs and remain profitable, since such a company normally operates at low profit margins.

One can also argue that firms operating in a highly competitive market may be more willing to adopt new market strategies. For firms operating only in traditional markets, the adoption of a mixed strategy may be novel. And, those in a highly competitive market may be more likely to adopt a novel approach, as was Barnes & Noble. On the other hand, companies with pure strategy, such as Dell, may move to mixed strategy in order to compensate for online stores’ lack of face-to-face customer assistance and tactile experience.

In operationalizing competition, we use the number of relevant industry competitors, which is one of the common ways to measure a company’s extent of competition [62]. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis 2. A firm’s number of relevant competitors is associated with its choice of internet market strategy (H2 in Fig. 1).

Hypothesis 3. A firm with a higher number of relevant competitors is more likely to adopt creative pricing policies (H3 in Fig. 1).

## 3.2.2. Industry type

Miller et al. [53] considered industry type as a contingency factor and argued for the inclusion of more than one industry. They pointed out that using one industry sector prevents analyzing the variance across industries, reducing the external validity of results. McGahan and Porter [52] have analyzed the profitability of US public corporations within specific 4-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) categories and reported that industry effect accounts for 19% of industry aggregated variance in profitability.

A wide variety of products and services can be traded on the internet, but due to its nature, the internet does not provide a uniform opportunity for all product types and industries. This has been recognized by researchers. For example, Lederer et al. [46] divided the products offered in an internet mall into eight categories. Since many online vendors offer a variety of product types, the concentration on industry type could capture differences in competitive environments. Furthermore, as shown in Appendix A, the census data for 2002 indicated that the share of ecommerce revenue in various industry sectors varied significantly. Even in the electronic shopping and mail-order houses, the share of e-commerce in the total revenue ranged from 5.9% for drugs, health aids, and beauty aids to 44.9% for books and magazine.

Hence, we argue that industry type impacts the choice of internet market strategy. Additionally, the type of industry may influence the likelihood of adopting creative pricing policies. Hence, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4. A firm’s industry type is associated with its internet market strategy (H4 in Fig. 1).

Hypothesis 5. A firm’s industry type is associated with its adoption of creative pricing policies (H5 in Fig. 1).

## 3.3. Organizational factors

The resource-based theory addresses the question: how firms achieve and differ in sustaining competitive advantage? As conceptualized in this theory, resource heterogeneity is the basis of a firm’s competitive advantage [8]. Its resources—characterized as valuable, rare, difficult to imitate, and difficult to substitute— create distinct strategic choices that it could exploit in order to improve market position. Although there are criticisms of this view as a theory [64], it has been used in various contexts to examine resources contributing to a firm’s strategy for competition.

The resource-based theory finds a firm’s unique organizational processes to be most influential in its profitability, the theory has been used in examining the role of IT as a competitive weapon [50] and the relationship between IT-related resources and firms’ financial performance [9]. In the context of the use of IT for strategic ends, it has been observed that strategic resource differences among firms are important outcome predictors of strategic applications of IT [40,67]. We therefore argue that firms’ resources and capacities impact their choice of internet market strategy. Size, employee productivity, IT use and assets, and age are identified as proxies for such resources.

## 3.3.1. Size

Firms possess heterogeneous resources and capabilities, and the differences may be long-lasting resulting in resource immobility, which could evolve from three roles: the role of history, the role of causal ambiguity, and the role of social complexity. Size develops over a period of time and represents the social complexity that comes to exist in a firm. It has been shown to be an important factor in organizational and strategy studies [43], and it could be a major factor in the organizational structure [41]. In the study of diversification strategy, size was found to impact strategy either through a mediating effect [32] or direct impact [48]. Moreover, larger companies had the ability to create and maintain economies of scale for operating in national and regional markets [3]. A large company is usually more capital intensive; it can efficiently assign its resources, hence increasing its productivity [38]. Furthermore, Barney classified firms’ resources into: (i) physical resources referring to the physical technologies used in a firm, (ii) human resources including experience, judgment, and intelligence, and (iii) organizational resources due to the firm’s formal and informal structure to plan, control, and coordinate systems. Size could be considered a proxy for the firm’s organizational resources and capacities, representing its structural complexity, operational scope, and extent of inertia.

We, therefore, posit that size is one of the factors associated with the choice of market strategy and since pure strategy is a relatively new approach in business, the inertia and complexity of large firms may limit innovative initiatives necessary for adopting such a strategy. Hence:

Hypothesis 6. A firm’s size is positively associated with the adoption of the mixed strategy (H6 in Fig. 1).

## 3.3.2. Employee productivity

In resource-based theory, one of the three critical resources is the company’s workforce. More valuable human resources are, by definition, more productive. Employee productivity measures the relationship between employees’ input and the company’s output; it is defined as output per worker [37]. Furthermore, it has been shown that high-productivity businesses have more highly skilled workers and adopt a higher level of technology [34]. By nature, the structure of a firm with pure strategy is technology- and skill-biased. For such firms are structured to delegate routine, welldefined tasks to computers, thereby increasing the productivity of employees. Furthermore, a pure strategy does not require as many lower-skilled workers (e.g., cleaners, cashiers, and security guards) needed in a physical business site. The purely internetbased operation requires a more skilled work force. We, therefore, expect companies with a higher employee productivity to be in a better position to adopt the pure strategy. Hence:

Hypothesis 7. A firm with high employee productivity is positively associated with the adoption of the pure strategy (H7 in Fig. 1).

## 3.3.3. IT–asset ratio

We use IT to represent the firm’s general level of production technology since it is the most important technology for operating in the internet market. The effective use of more advanced IT provides competitive advantage [57]. Extensive use of IT could reduce transaction costs and make the internet more attractive [33]. IT allows firms to change the way of interacting with customers by improving market reach, customizing products, and increasing efficiency in payment systems, and shortening time to market for new products [49].

Therefore, we assert that the level of IT is one of the resource factors impacting the choice of internet market strategy and a pure strategy requires a relatively high level of IT technology and knowhow. A firm with a pure strategy would have a high reliance on its IT, and thus IT would have a higher share in its total assets. Hence, we posit that:

Hypothesis 8. A firm with a high IT–asset ratio is more likely to adopt the pure strategy (H8 in Fig. 1).

## 3.3.4. Age

Another historical contingency in creating resource immobility and increasing the cost of imitating successful firms’ capabilities is a firm’s age. Older firms are conservative with respect to change [54]. In the contingency theory, it has been shown that the age of a firm is positively correlated with its strategy and structural complexity. Furthermore, older firms have a higher probability of survival in the manufacturing industry [27]. On the other hand, Dunne and Hughes [24] have found that in the United Kingdom, smaller and younger firms have a higher rate of growth. Age is particularly important in our analysis because the internet market started with the introduction of Web technology in 1993. This historical imperative implies that companies with pure strategy are younger than companies with mixed strategy.

Hypothesis 9. An older firm is more likely to adopt the mixed strategy (H9 in Fig. 1).

## 4. Measurement, data collection, and analysis

## 4.1. Measurement

The measures in our IMS model were direct measurements obtained from a combination of primary and secondary sources.

## 4.1.1. Strategy

Internet market strategies were represented as a binary variable (mixed = 1, pure = 0). The data for the strategies were collected directly by visiting firms websites. If websites contained clear information such as ‘‘we are an internet-only company,’’ its strategy was coded as pure. If we could not determine the company’s market strategy from its website, we established their market strategy by asking via e-mail if they sell their products (or services) outside the internet. Less than one-quarter in the data set required the e-mail contact.

## 4.1.2. Creative pricing policies

The data for pricing policies were also collected directly by visiting firms’ websites. We did not differentiate between cases where a firm provided an auction service (customer-to-customer) or when it sold its products by auction (business-to-customer). Having an auction service enables the seller to include its own products in the auction list as well, benefit from customer traffic, and provide the image that its site offers good deals. This variable was defined as a binary variable, with 1 representing the presence of creative pricing policies.

## 4.1.3. Competition

In order to measure a company’s competition, we used the company’s 6-digit SIC category and counted all its competitors in that category. If a company belonged to more than one 6-digit SIC category, we counted competitors in all categories to which it belonged.

## 4.1.4. Industry type

We used the first two digits of the ABI 6-digit SIC to determine a company’s industry. It is possible for a firm to belong to more than one. We categorized industry types into manufacturing, wholesale, retail, and services. Services also included the other categories. Each industry was represented by a dummy variable.

## 4.1.5. Size

There are many ways to measure the size of a company, such as the number of employees, sales, net assets, value-added, and total cost [12,42]. Since labor intensity of outputs differs across product types and industry, we chose two measures: number of employees and sales. We tested the model using each measure separately.

## 4.1.6. Employee productivity

One can measure the productivity of a firm in different ways, such as value added and value added per employee [65]. Since our focus was the human resource factor, we used the sales productivity of employees, and measured it by the ratio of total sales to the total number of employees.

## 4.1.7. IT–asset ratio

IT investment and use can be measured using different sources and at different aggregation levels [35]. We computed the ratio (multiplied by 100) of IT investment and use to the total assets of the firm.

## 4.2. Data sources

In identifying business firms operating on the internet (as opposed to personal or very small operations), we used internet.com from the Wall Street Research Net (WSRN). In 2001, approximately 300 companies listed on this site were traded on the NASDAQ stock exchange. We were able to complete data for 281 such firms.

In order to reduce the possible bias in the sample, making it more representative of the general population of businesses operating in the internet market, and increasing the number of observations, we augmented the data set with data collected from sample sites in the 1999 World Wide Web Internet Yellow Pages that were still operational in the first quarter of 2001. This was done to ensure that the selected firms had been in operation on the internet for at least one year, and were more likely to have survived the dot.com crisis. Our selection procedure was based on random sampling. We selected more than 25% of the Yellow Pages sections that, we believed, had a higher probability of containing business firms. For example, we avoided those offering alternative living, cheerleading, and geography. Overlaps with the first sample were eliminated. A total of 199 companies were thus randomly selected, of which 138 were still operational in 2001; for these, we obtained complete records. Hence, the data set consisted of complete observations for 419 business firms operating on the Web.

For market strategy and pricing policy, we collected most primary data by visiting company websites. For the independent variables, we collected data from secondary sources, as reported in Table 1. Using different data sources (as opposed to questionnaire-based subjective data) is a common practice (see, for example, [59]). In such cases, as experienced by Brynjolfsson and Kemerer [13] in their spreadsheet market analysis, having a single source for a variable is highly desirable but often not possible.

For publicly traded internet companies, we used Moody’s company data source for number of employees, total sales, IT–asset ratio, and age. Such data were not available for privately held firms, so we used data from American Business Information (ABI at Info USA) and Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA at the US Department of Commerce) to complete observations for the latter firms.

For privately held internet companies, we used the IT–asset ratio computed at their 4-digit SIC industry level. Our source of data for IT use was the U.S. Input– Output Table published by BEA. The latest input– output data available at the time were from 1992 and were published in 1997. The input–output tables have 97 rows and columns that show the Make and Use of 97 commodities by 97 industries at 4-digit SIC industry level [45]. Of the 97 commodities, two in the ‘‘Use’ table were of interest to us: computer and office equipment and computer and data processing services. For each 4-digit SIC industry level, we added these two categories to measure IT use. We then divided the ITuse of each 4-digit SIC industry level by the total input of that industry. For those companies that belonged to more than one SIC, we added the relevant SIC-level data. To control for the difference in data sources, a dummy variable was added to the estimation equations. Furthermore, to account for possible interaction effects between the data source and independent variables, we added an interaction term (dummy multiplied by the variable) for every independent variable.

There are relatively noticeable correlations among some of the independent variables (such as size as measured by number of employees with IT and employee productivity, and also among industry types); this could create collinearity problem in model estimation. Collinearity could cause overestimation of the standard error of coefficients and lead to underestimation of the significance of important variables. For this reason, estimations were carried out by introducing the collinear variables one at a time and examining the collinearity diagnostics for the presence of collinearity.

Logit regression in SAS was used for estimating the probability of adopting creative pricing policies and internet market strategy. We tested the IMS model by three logit regressions: adopting creative pricing policy as a function of strategy (H1 in Fig. 1) and as a function of environmental factors (H3 and H5). This was done to avoid collinearity due to the associations between strategy and environmental factors. A third logit regression estimated the associations between adopting an internet market strategy and environmental and organizational contingencies. Examination of the condition index and variance proportions did not indicate the presence of noticeable collinearity in the final logit estimations.

As the results shown in Table 4 show, the adoption of creative pricing policy is strongly associated with internet market strategy, indicating a strong support for H1. Furthermore, the sign of the coefficient shows that the adoption of creative pricing policy is negatively associated with mixed strategy and positively associated with pure strategy as hypothesized.

The model fit test is based on the analysis of deviance and the p-value for the model deviance indicates a good model fit. The second logit estimation shows that adoption of creative pricing policies is associated with industry type (as in H5), in this case it associates positively with the retail industry. We did not find any support for the association between pricing policies and competition, H3. The p-value for scaled deviance indicated a good model fit.

The third logit estimation reports on the results of tests for H6–H9. We did not find evidence supporting an association between strategy and size (H6) or employee productivity (H7). To ensure that the lack of significance is not due to collinearity, we introduced these variables one by one into the estimation model and found no statistical significance for the variables. Thus, our results do not support H6 and H7. On the other hand, we found strong statistical significance for the association between strategy and competition, industry type (retail), IT–asset ratio, and age. This provided support for H2, H4, H8, and H9. Furthermore, the pvalue for the model fit indicated a good fit for the model estimation. Thus, we found evidence in support of the impact of environmental (industry type and competitors) and organizational contingencies (IT–asset ratio and age) on the selection of internet market strategy.

The negative coefficient of the IT–asset ratio indicated that firms that have adopted pure strategy have a higher asset ratio. In contrast, the positive sign of competition shows that firms with more competitors are more likely to adopt a mixed strategy. This supports the argument that established firms that operate in a highly competitive market adopt a mixed strategy as a new competitive strategy.

## 5. Discussion and implications

## 5.1. Pricing policy and market strategy results

The results of testing the IMS model showed that the adoption of creative pricing policy was strongly associated with internet market strategy in internet companies with pure strategy, which are more likely to adopt creative pricing policies (including discounting, bundling, auction, and other flexible policies). Furthermore, firms in the retail industry are more likely to adopt creative pricing policies. This finding is in line with a report that 89% of online shoppers surveyed chose ‘‘best price’’ as the reason for returning to a website to make additional purchases [70]. Bailey [4] noted that amazon.com quickly changed its pricing when Barnes & Noble entered the internet market. Furthermore, in a study of CDs and books, internet prices have been found to be lower than those in the traditional markets. Smith et al. [66] conjectured that the internet market might have become more efficient in the past few years, when the conflicting results were obtained.

## 5.2. Antecedents of internet market strategy

We found no evidence supporting the association of size or productivity with the choice of internet market strategy. The lack of significant association indicated an absence of compelling economies of scale as a reason for the choice of one strategy over the other. On the other hand, our results show that the environmental contingencies in the form of industry type and industry competition play a role in the choice of strategy. Firms operating in the retail industry are more likely to adopt a pure strategy, whereas those in wholesale and manufacturing are more inclined to use a mixed strategy.

An interesting finding is the positive association between mixed strategy and number of competitors. This finding is in line with anecdotal evidence that well-known sites with pure strategy trade in nongeneric products, such as books, airline tickets, and vacation packages.

Furthermore, established firms with many product lines that operate in a competitive environment face a high number of competitors. They are more flexible and willing to adopt new market strategies. Earlier movements of many established brick-and-mortar companies to a hybrid business model are in line with this argument. On the other side, some established pure online companies, such as dell.com and amazon.com, have recently started a gradual shift toward a mixed strategy—more anecdotal support for our finding.

## 5.3. Implications

The positive relationship between the adoption of pure strategy and creative pricing policies implies that those firms which operate solely in the internet market have to compete more intensely in price, pointing to the more competitive nature of the internet market. The vague assertions about the competitiveness of the internet are not specific regarding areas of competition. Our findings provide evidence in support of the industry-specific nature of competitiveness in the internet market. Specifically, they show that the internet market is highly competitive for retail products, but not so for products in other industries.

1. Managers and CEOs of online firms in the retail industry should be aware that the survival of their companies requires them to set creative and flexible pricing policies for their products; they must balance their savings in capital expenditures for physical locations against the need for innovation and flexibility in their pricing policies and lower product costs. Furthermore, they need to be far more aware of the pricing policies and marketing activities of their competitors.

2. The internet firms with pure strategy in the service industry are not as susceptible to creative pricing policies, indicating that the internet may not be a suitable channel for all types of service deliveries. For example, internet firms in the service industry could compete on non-price factors, such as quality and timeliness of service.

3. As they design their websites for e-commerce, etailers need to pay particular attention to pricing information and Web-design elements. There is a rich body of marketing research on the ways vendors can increase the impact of price information by enhancing buyers’ perception of it [31], through price comparison cues and semantic cues [10].

4. Firms with a pure strategy have higher IT– asset ratios, indicating their technical capabilities and sophistication. Therefore, firms planning to enter the internet with pure strategy need to have above-average IT resources and technical knowhow.

5. Older firms are more likely to select a mixed strategy. A brick-and-mortar company that plans to enter the internet market by creating a new division that uses a pure strategy should consider forming an independent subsidiary that has all the flexibility and creativity of a start-up firm.

6. The size and employee productivity of a company do not play an important role in influencing the choice of its internet market strategy. Hence, our results do not indicate the presence of inertia or slack resources due to size that may inhibit or encourage the adoption of the pure policy. In contrast, although size is not correlated with internet-market strategy, it is negatively correlated with creative pricing policy. Since such a relationship was not hypothesized in our IMS model, we did not include size in the pricing policy estimation function.

7. The significance of the impact of industry type, industry competition, IT–asset ratio, and age raises the question of strategy determinism in the choice of the internet market strategy. The question here is how much choice exists in the selection of internet market strategy. Our findings support the inescapable presence of strategic determinism due to age. Moreover, although IT asset could be shaped to some extent as a result of the adoption of an internet market strategy, its effect is usually not immediate and could require a long time to materialize. Furthermore, unless a firm is a clear leader in shaping the structure of an industry, its industry type and competition constitute significant environmental contingencies. Thus, our results provide evidence in support of strategic determinism.

## 6. Conclusions

The rapid growth of the internet market has made it a significant part of the business strategy of any company that aims to grow its markets and maintain its competitive advantage. Although there are ample assertions regarding the nature of the internet market, there has been little scientific testing of the competitive nature of the internet market and the types of market strategies suitable for operating in this market. Furthermore, because e-business is a relatively new concept, there is little insight regarding factors driving internet market strategies. Our study is one of the first to investigate the antecedents and pricing implications of internet strategy choices.

More specifically, we examined the difference between the internet and traditional markets to formulate two basic internet market strategies: pure and mixed. We synthesized duopoly and oligopoly theories in economics and resource-based and contingency theories in strategic management to develop a conceptual model—the internet market strategy model. The IMS model hypothesizes that if the internet market is more a competitive market, internet firms with pure strategy would be more creative and flexible in their pricing policies and that internet firms are not completely free in their choice of an internet market strategy—market and organizational contingencies constrain and influence their choice.

In testing the IMS model, we obtained a sample of more than 400 firms. The statistical analysis was based on logit regression. Our results supported the hypothesis that the internet market strategy is strongly associated with pricing policies, in that retailers operating solely in the internet market have creative, flexible pricing policies. Also, the environmental contingencies (in the form of industry type and industry competition) and organizational factors (IT assets and age) constrain and influence the adoption of an internet market strategy.

Our work had a number of limitations. Our categorizations of strategies, pricing strategies, and industry types are relatively coarse, and the necessity to combine data from multiple sources limited our findings. Finally, our findings indicate that the internet may be more a competitive and efficient market, which means the internet offers consumers better prices. We then need to ponder what this means for consumers who, due to limited resources, education, or culture, are left out of this market. It is important to find ways to bridge this divide and bring all citizens together to benefit from the internet.
