# Strategizing for mass customization by playing the business networking game

Martijn R. Hoogeweegen <sup>\*</sup>, Diederik W. van Liere, Peter H.M. Vervest, Lorike Hagdorn van der Meijden, Ido de Lepper

Rotterdam School of Management / Erasmus University, P.O. Box 1738, 3000 DR Rotterdam, The Netherlands

Received 3 February 2004; received in revised form 11 November 2005; accepted 16 November 2005 Available online 4 January 2006

## Abstract

This paper introduces a multi-player simulation game to let players experience how the trend of mass customization and product personalization changes stable business networks into dynamic networks. The game, called Business Networking Game, simulates the changing dynamics of business networks and stimulates the players’ thinking on whether and how to develop a strategy to cope with the mass customization trend within their own businesses. The game can be tailored to a specific context. In this paper, we introduce the game by means of an application within the insurance industry. This paper describes the theoretical background, the design of the game, how it is played and how students of business administration and professionals from the insurance industry evaluated it.

## 1. Introduction

This paper introduces a multi-player simulation game to let players, both students of business administration as well as business managers, experience how the trend of mass customization and product personalization changes stable business networks into dynamic networks. The game aims to encourage business managers to start strategizing the opportunities and threats within their own changing business network environment.

Stable business networks consist of a stable set of organizations that collectively produce and deliver goods and services to the market. Forced by changes in customer demand (e.g., customers ask for more service and more personalized products) and the rise of enabling internet technologies, organizations have the opportunity to redesign their business networks and form new alignments with those parties that are needed to fulfill personalized customer requirements. This is called dynamic business networking: forming temporary alignments with those parties that are qualified to produce and deliver customized goods and services [11].

The move towards dynamic business networks demands from its members – the individual organizations – a clear strategy on how to survive the change, or even on how to benefit from it. Upfront there is no single clear answer and it is difficult to assess which courses of action will have which kinds of outcome. In the literature, many stress the strength of gaming-simulation to enhance the understanding of complex systems (see for instance [5,10,17]). Therefore, we have developed a game, which should offer a safe experimental environment to teach and test alternative courses of action. It should enhance a comprehensive understanding of the move towards more dynamic networks and how a sound strategy to cope with this move could look. In this respect, the game should serve as a Decision Support System to enable the thinking and decision-making of business managers, regarding their strategy, cope better with the move towards dynamic business networks.

The game, called Business Networking Game, serves two specific aims: (1) to support the teaching of students on how dynamic networks will arise and what kind of strategies can be developed to win in a dynamic network; and (2) to provide a safe learning environment to business managers in order for them to experiment with those strategies.

This paper discusses the theoretical background of the game, the design of the game, how it is played and how a first set of 64 students (during 6 playing sessions) and 60 managers from a large Dutch insurance firm (during 5 playing sessions), evaluated the game. Finally, we draw conclusions and identify directions for further research.

## 2. The rise of modular dynamic networking

Mass customization forces organizations and business networks to move from a market-push towards a market-pull situation: the customer is no longer the last party in the value chain to consume what has been produced, but the first party in the value chain to formulate his or her requirements. Pre-formed stable business networks of organizations are less suitable since an actually formulated customer order determines which organizations are required to fulfill this order. As Jarvenpaa and Ives [9] stated, organizations should <sup>d</sup>think in reverse<sup>T</sup>, meaning that they first should determine individual customer demand, and then organize a chain through the network of organizations for order fulfillment and delivery. In doing so, the participants form temporary alignments dedicated to the fulfillment of a single customer order by engineering the required customer value (see also [16]). After order fulfillment, the temporary alignment often dissolves again, with the participating organizations being ready to form other, new alignments [11].

The rise of dynamic networking challenges organizations to develop sound strategies to mobilize their networks in such a way that they become part of the right temporary alignments and/or start up the right temporary alignments (see also [8]). Many examples are described in the literature of how business networks are reconfigured to more dynamic ones that focus on better serving the end-customer, such as IKEA [13], Benetton [2], and the Media and Communications Market [18].

The three strategies as defined by Treacy and Wiersema [14] could be considered as potentially interesting strategic options available to dynamic networking organizations. They propose that organizations that have taken leadership positions within their industries or business networks have focused on <sup>b</sup>. . . delivering superior customer value in line with one of three value disciplines – operational excellence, customer intimacy, or product leadership<sup>Q</sup> (p. 84). Operational excellence refers to providing reliable products and services to customers against competitive prices and convenience. Customer intimacy refers to the ability to meet customized demand by tailoring production to the exact requirements of individual customers or market niches. Product leadership refers to the offering of innovative, leading-edge products and services to customers that enhance the use or application of the product or service; this should offset the value of the competitors’ products and services.

Depending on the position and relations within a network, all three strategies are potentially interesting in order to create a sustainable and valuable business model for a dynamic business network situation. For instance, by specializing production, an organization could become a main node within the network and be part of virtually every temporary alignment formed within the network (operational excellence). Another focus could be to approach the end-customer and act as a network coordinator to fulfill its customized demand by forming the right temporary alignment (customer intimacy). Yet another option could be to innovate and develop new capabilities to meet the customers’ demand for state-of-art products and services (product leadership).

All three strategies contribute to the efficient and effective forming of temporary alignments to fulfill customized demand and therefore support dynamic networking. One specific task in a dynamic network is the coordination of the formation and execution of these temporary alignments. The network coordinator is the organization that receives the customer order and is responsible for the forming of the right temporary alignment that will fulfill the order as requested by the customer (see also [3]). The network coordinator’s primary responsibility is to identify the requirements of customers, to identify appropriate satisfiers, such as resources, skills, semi-finished products, to produce the requirements, and to match requirements to the satisfiers (based on the theory of the virtual organization of Mowshowitz [12]).

Hoogeweegen et al. [7] propose a modular approach to support the matching task of the network coordinator to allocate the right satisfiers to the right requirements. Customer requirements are modeled in modular service elements and satisfiers are modeled in capability elements. A service element describes a specific feature of a total product and/or service range. By mixing and matching specific service elements, a customer is able to specify exactly his or her requirements. A capability element refers to a skill, resource or capability to produce a specific service element. By linking the service elements to the capability elements, only those capability elements are activated which are actually requested by the customer by selecting a specific set of service elements. The network coordinator is responsible for collecting customer orders, in terms of sets of service elements, and the right translation of service elements into capability elements and for the choice of the appropriate organizations within the dynamic network who own these capability elements. In a dynamic market with many customized requests for products, each customer order consists of a different set of service elements, therefore a different set of capability elements is needed, which results in a different set of organizations which together form a temporary alignment to fulfill this specific customer order. Each order could result in a unique path through the dynamic network.

## 3. Gaming-simulation

Gaming-simulation is an experiential learning method to provoke behavioral changes. Players need to analyze problems and opportunities, make decisions, implement them and interpret the feedback on these decisions. The main objective of gaming-simulation is <sup>b</sup>. . . to enhance a comprehensive understanding of complex systems and to develop learning skills<sup>Q</sup> [1, p. 90]. Simulation-gaming can be very effective in actionoriented disciplines like economics, medicine, and law. Larre´che´ [10] indicates that the <sup>b</sup>. . . rapid feedback on the value of [the player’s] decisions, together with the motivating nature of the competitive situation, provides the context and the means for an effective learning experience<sup>Q</sup> (p. 562). This should foster the motivation and confidence to act in the real life context (see also [17]).

Our aim is suitable for gaming-simulation since we want to teach players (both students and business managers) how dynamic networks will emerge, how a modular approach may be a key enabler, and to offer a safe experimental environment in which to develop and test what kinds of strategies are, or could be, valid in coping with the move towards dynamic networks. By playing the Business Networking Game, students will experience what impact the changing business network environment will have on single organizations and experience how to respond to this. Moreover, business managers can formulate and test strategies for their own business context to prepare themselves and their organizations for changes within their business networks.

Angelides et al. [1] classify gaming-simulation models in three ways. First, there are systematic games versus role-playing games. Systematic games focus on the structure and relations of elements within a specific system, while role-playing games focus on a particular position within a system. Second, there are specific games opposed to general games. Specific games are industry specific, while general games are not, or can be tailored to any industry. Third, there are total enterprise games versus functional games. Total enterprise games cover a wide range of disciplines (such as marketing, production, finance, etc.), while functional games focus on one particular discipline. The Business Networking Game can be classified as a general, role-playing game, focusing on the total enterprise.

## 4. The business networking game

The Business Networking Game is a multi-player computer-based management game and is developed by the Erasmus University Rotterdam in cooperation with A.T. Kearney. We will describe the game based on the twelve basic elements as proposed by Angelides and Paul [1], and based on Greenblatt [6]. These twelve elements are: (1) scenario – describing the plot of the game; (2) pulse – referring to the event in the game triggering the players to react; (3) cycle sequence – referring to the cycles of the game designed to achieve the intended outcome; (4) steps of play – describing the progression of activity in the game; (5) rules – referring to the available options to players to interact with the game; (6) roles – describing the different roles assigned to players; (7) models – describing the logic modeling of the processes and parameters used; (8) decision sequence and linkage – referring to the decisions to be made by the players and how they are sequenced and linked; (9) accounting system – referring to the financial model of the game; (10) indicators – referring to the main indicators that are used as the overall criterion to win the game; (11) symbology – referring to the physical representation of the main indicators; and (12) paraphernalia – referring to everything else that is required to successfully run the gaming-simulation application. The last element will not be described in the context of our game – all aspects for the game to run successfully are covered by the other eleven elements.

## 4.1. Scenario

The first element is the scenario of the game, which describes the outline of the plot of the game. The scenario of the Business Networking Game is to simulate the transition from a stable business network to a dynamic business network (see Fig. 1). A business network is modeled as a set of organizations and their relations, which collectively fulfill customer orders. Each organization owns one or more capabilities and contributes to order fulfillment by forming alignments with other capabilities of other organizations. Market demand is modeled in service elements (based on the modular approach of Hoogeweegen et al. [7]). The market is the only role played by the computer; all other roles in the network are played by one player each.

During a game session, market demand will change – more variations in products (by changing the set of service elements requested) and lower prices asked – and players will be forced to strategize how to cope with these changes. By developing and implementing a strategy, the network will change and will become more dynamic. In the end, the question is who has won the game by realizing the highest gross margin improvement (in the hypothetical example of Fig. 1, this is organization 5) and why, i.e. what was his or her strategy?

The Business Networking Game can be tailored to any specific industry using scenarios. Currently, we have developed a scenario for the insurance industry. This is an exemplary industry where business networks are changing and in which firms are struggling with maintaining and/or expanding their network positions [15]. The industry is facing the trend of customization and therefore it is collectively developing ICT standards. This will enable the ability to form quick temporary alignments within the dynamic network. A second trend is the move towards modular product architectures. The primary process of the production and sale of an insurance policy involves 5 generic activities: advice, sales and marketing, customer acceptation, claims settlement and customer service. By standardizing, especially the processes that are involved in the customer acceptation and claims settlement activities, it becomes possible to achieve economies of scale and re-use these processes for more than one type of insurance policy. The back offices of the insurance firms are increasingly more standardized, insurance professionals refer to their back offices as <sup>d</sup>policy factories<sup>T</sup>. These two trends illustrate how a business network changes and therefore this industry is an accurate setting for the Business Networking Game.

## 4.2. Pulse

Pulse refers to an event or problem introduced during the course of the play to trigger the players to react, which is to analyze the situation, decide what to do, take action and monitor feedback to check whether the decision(s) lead to the desired outcome. The Business Networking Game is based on two types of pulses. First, the market demand will change forcing the players to reconsider their role and position within the network. They will develop and implement a strategy leading to changes in the structure and relations within the business network. These amendments made by players refer to the second type of pulses. This type is not predictable and requires from the players that they stay alert in case things happen, which requires an adaptation of the implemented strategy.

## 4.3. Cycle sequence

The cycles of a game and their sequence describe how a game has been set up to achieve its intended outcome. The Business Networking Game consists of a macro cycle composed of a number of consecutive cycles, and a micro cycle, which describes how players operate within the game. The macro cycle of the Business Networking Game is the transition from a stable business network to a dynamic network. In order to simulate this transition, four major cycles are defined. The first cycle is the introduction. Players become acquainted with the interface of the game, how they can participate in order fulfillment and, therefore, how they can make a profit. The second cycle is the analysis cycle. Market demand will change (the first type of pulse) and players are challenged to strategize how to keep their business profitable. The third cycle is the decision and action cycle. Players will develop and implement a strategy (the second type of pulse). The fourth cycle is the evaluation cycle. Players will verify whether their strategy is effective and, if not, they can adapt their strategy. The micro cycle of the Business Networking Game refers to the <sup>d</sup>daily<sup>T</sup> operations of a player, which is order acquisition, sourcing capabilities to others and capability fulfillment. Every player receives feedback on performance and the effects of decisions taken every half a minute played (one single working day is simulated as half a minute).

Game comprises 5 main steps (see Fig. 2). First, the game is introduced – the aim of the game, how the interface works, etc. Second, the game is started and players can familiarize themselves with the game and the role they play within the business network. Third, the first pulse, which is the change in market demand, is introduced and players should start to develop a sound strategy based on the three main strategic options as proposed by Treacy and Wiersema [14]. Fourth, players will implement their strategy, monitor whether their strategy brings about the desired results and then possibly adjust their strategy when required. Fifth, the results of the game are debriefed: who won the game, why and how does this experience relate to the real world?

## 4.5. Rules

The main rule of the Business Networking Game refers to the investment options available to players to implement their strategy. First, players can develop new capabilities by buying capabilities they currently do not own. Second, players can aim for specialization by upgrading already owned capabilities (this will increase fixed cost and decrease the variable unit cost). Third, players can establish new relations – either with other organizations within the network or with the market. Should one aim for product leadership (s)he should invest in new capabilities and possibly in specializing in one or more capabilities. Should one go for operational excellence (s)he should specialize its capabilities and develop relations with all organizations within the network that need these capabilities. Should one go for customer intimacy, then developing a relation with the customer is required plus developing relations with those organizations that collectively cover the whole product portfolio (all service elements).

## 4.6. Roles

Roles are characters assigned to players with a prescribed set of capabilities and relations. Here we illustrate the roles of the Business Networking Game by means of the insurance industry application (see Fig. 3). This particular application consists of two chains of organizations that collectively participate in the production and delivery of insurance policies.

![](/api/attachments/T4FTSS5C/fulltext/images/b87e458227ed481df9e8bfa93dbc15a8fe4019e5e2f80f715832beaebeb06a36.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Steps of play in business networking game.

The customers ask for car, home and travel insurance policies. An insurance policy is offered at three different levels: product line bronze, product line silver and product line gold. The insurance network consists of ten organizational units grouped into five separate organization types and these units are represented in the game as separate organizations. The two agents (<sup>b</sup>Apollo<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>Jupiter<sup>Q</sup>) have direct access to the market and therefore are the (current) network coordinators. Apollo is the network coordinator for the car insurance policies and Jupiter is the network coordinator for the home insurance policies. The second business unit is called <sup>b</sup>Miracle<sup>Q</sup> for the car insurance policies and <sup>b</sup>Diamond<sup>Q</sup> for the home insurance policies and they both offer product line bronze. These two business units are responsible for the branding and marketing of the policies. The third business unit is called <sup>b</sup>Phoenix<sup>Q</sup> for the car insurance policies and <sup>b</sup>Rainbow<sup>Q</sup> for the home insurance policies, and they are responsible for the under-writing of policies, i.e. the allocation and spreading of the involved risks of the issued policies. The fourth business unit is called <sup>b</sup>Archimedes<sup>Q</sup> for the car insurance policies and <sup>b</sup>Blazer<sup>Q</sup> for the home insurance policies, and they are responsible for the settlement of all damage claims of insured customers. The fifth business unit is called <sup>b</sup>Cosimo<sup>Q</sup> for the car insurance policies and <sup>b</sup>Crystal<sup>Q</sup> for the home insurance policies, and they take care of all administrative processes of handling policies and offer customer service either by using a call center or a website.

The capability elements modeled in this application are: providing generic customer advice (1) or specific (2); the branding of the insurances, in bronze (3), silver (4) or gold (5); the underwriting of car (6), home (7) or traveling (8) policies; the claims settlement of car (9), home (10) or traveling (11) policies; and the services used of a call center (12) or online (13). By means of offering their capabilities, each organization will participate in the fulfillment of customer orders and will generate a certain margin. The margin is based on the price organizations receive for executing their capability elements minus the variable costs of executing these capability elements minus the fixed costs of owning these capability elements.

Customer orders are defined in service elements (see Fig. 4). Based on this set 2-3-3-1-2=36 types of orders can be asked for by the customer. The service elements are translated by the network coordinators (<sup>b</sup>Apollo<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>Jupiter<sup>Q</sup>) into the mentioned capability elements and distributed within their networks for fulfillment. When market demand changes, the type and combinations of service elements asked for by customers will vary more and more. In this way, we simulate the trend of mass customization and indirectly ask for new (temporary) alignments within the network.

## 4.9. Accounting system

The accounting system is based on the financial model described earlier. Players are shown the development in cash, sales, costs (both fixed and variable), profit, and gross margin. Investments are deducted from the players’ cash position. This overview is updated every day (=half a minute).

## 4.10. Indicators

Indicators are the main aspects of the accounting system that are stressed by the game master/operator to the players. As mentioned before, here we stress the (growth in) gross margin since this is the overall criterion to win the game.

As Fig. 7 illustrates, all players apply one or more of the three investment options available to implement a developed strategy. For instance, <sup>b</sup>Phoenix<sup>Q</sup> follows a product leadership strategy and buys all capabilities to be able to settle claims himself without partnering with others and therefore aims to exclude <sup>b</sup>Crystal<sup>Q</sup> from performing these capabilities. At the same time, <sup>b</sup>Crystal<sup>Q</sup> decides to pursue operational excellence and specializes all his elements and therefore increases his fixed costs, but at the same time lowers the variable costs of executing these capabilities. In this way, <sup>b</sup>Crystal<sup>Q</sup> is able to lower its price on the market and will therefore be able to increase orders that will more than compensate the increased fixed costs. The third strategy, customer intimacy is followed by for instance <sup>b</sup>Miracle<sup>Q</sup>. This player bypasses <sup>b</sup>Apollo<sup>Q</sup> and establishes additional relations to cover the whole capability and therefore product portfolio.

All the investments made lead to a very different, more dynamic, business network (as is shown in Fig. 7). Fig. 8 shows the result of this particular playing session. It shows the improvement of gross margins made per organization. The player <sup>b</sup>Archimedes<sup>Q</sup> appears to be the winner of the game. This particular player wins because he specialized his capabilities, became the cheapest provider of settling claims, and others in the network were able to find his low cost service (see relations established by <sup>b</sup>Cosimo<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>Diamond<sup>Q</sup>). The <sup>b</sup>loser<sup>Q</sup> of the game, player <sup>b</sup>Blazer<sup>Q</sup>, learned that buying new capabilities was not a successful strategy in this particular game session.

## 6. Evaluation

Based on the simulation evaluation framework developed by Feinstein and Cannon [4], we developed a questionnaire to let students and managers evaluate a playing session. Until now, we have 64 completed forms based on 6 playing sessions with students and 60 completed forms based on 5 playing sessions with business managers from insurance companies. All players were asked to rate 18 statements based on a Lickert scale (1 = totally disagree and 6 = totally agree). Fig. 9 shows the overall average scores for the 6 most important statements.

Both students and manages judged the model to be quite accurate. They acknowledge that the game indeed adequately illustrates how relatively stable business networks could move to networks that are more dynamic. Unfortunately, the students seem to be more critical regarding the actuality of the model than the managers are. A possible explanation could be that students are less aware of the current developments in the insurance industry because they do not have any working experience. Overall, the students seem to have confidence in the validity of the results, while the managers have reservations. Students and managers agree that playing the Business Networking Game is a valid and a fun learning experience.

## 7. Conclusion

The trend of mass customization forces whole business networks to redefine their strategy in terms of the kind of capability to improve, acquire or outsource, and to redefine in terms of with whom in the network to establish relationships. The game illustrates that changing customer demand will lead to a changing business network, caused by all the strategic actions taken by the individual organizations. Whether a specific strategic action taken by one of the players will be a winning one depends on the player’s current position in the network and the type of strategic actions taken by the others in the network.

The first playing sessions and the students and managers evaluations indicate that the game is a meaningful tool to allow players experience and learn how to deal with the ongoing trend of mass customization and personalization. The evaluation scores on <sup>b</sup>enhancement of learning<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>fun of playing<sup>Q</sup> indicate that we are succeeding in our aim to support the teaching of students in how dynamic networks will arise and evolve and to provide a safe learning environment in order for business managers to experiment with strategies.

Our first experiences with this game strengthen us to continue developing this game and make it a strong research instrument in order to investigate further dynamic business networking. Such an instrument should allow us to research questions such as <sup>d</sup>when should a capability be made in-house or bought externally<sup>T</sup>, <sup>d</sup>what is the relation between a network structure and its performance<sup>T</sup>, and <sup>d</sup>how will information transparency impact network performance<sup>T</sup>, etc.
