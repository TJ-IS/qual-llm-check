# Measuring e-government readiness

Chang E. Koh <sup>a,</sup>\*, Victor R. Prybutok <sup>a</sup>, Xiaoni Zhang

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 May 2004 Received in revised form 15 July 2008 Accepted 22 August 2008 Available online 30 October 2008

Keywords: E-government readiness E-government transformation Internet Information technology Public sector Instrument development

## A B S T R A C T

We proposed a way of assessing readiness of a government organization to transform itself into a provider of fully integrated e-government services. We identified major components of e-government and discussed how it could evolve from a simple website into a fully integrated portal that delivers services to the public. Drawing upon strategic IS theory, we proposed three levels – strategic, system, and data – at which the governmental office’s readiness for e-government should be addressed. We empirically tested our model of e-government readiness with data collected from a U.S. municipal government. However, the results supported only a two-level model rather than the three we had theorized. This finding had important implications because it supported our contention that discrepancies can occur when e-government initiatives are at a relatively early stage and some critical e-government issues have not yet emerged.

## 1. Introduction

We conducted a comprehensive study of the way that a municipal government utilizes IT in general and e-government in particular and from this developed a conceptual socio-technical model of e-government readiness. We were trying to understand the practice of technology management within a governmental unit and thus determine how major components of e-government evolved from a simple online brochure-like site to an integrated portal. The model was developed to assess the readiness of an organizational unit for e-government.

We used Churchill’s [5] approach in the development of the survey instrument to collect data; the instrument was then validated by using data collected in cooperation with the city of Denton, Texas, which is located 35 miles north of Dallas, Texas and has a population of 110,000 with an annual growth rate of six percent. The city government employs about 1400 people and has an annual budget of \$400 million.

In our conceptual model of e-government readiness, we included major components (functions) of e-government to describe how it evolved as e-government matured and thus determined how a governmental organization prepared to transform itself into a successful e-government.

## 2. Background

According to a study that analyzed more than 1500 websites in 70 metropolitan areas of the United States, the majority of local governments used their sites as directories, providing phone numbers and address information; only 11 percent of the sites in the study provided sophisticated automated online services [18]. Nevertheless, internal pressures, such as budget reductions and increased public demand are forcing local and national governmental units to implement better e-government sites. We developed an e-government readiness model that integrates several theories and concepts of IT, e-commerce and e-government, including technical, organizational and social issues. It addresses the nature of e-government in terms of its functions and transformation.

## 2.1. E-government functions

Several studies have recognized the services and features that e-government should deliver [2,15,17]. At a municipal level, Deshazo et al. [6] identified 51 different e-government features and examined the extent to which small city governments utilize the Internet. They organized these features into 12 categories: online payments, registration, permits, customer service, communication, license, images, audio/video, documents, applications, procurement, and miscellaneous.

Koh and Balthazard proposed a simple, intuitive and yet comprehensive framework to organize an ever-increasing array of features of the Internet [12]. A Three-Ring Model captures all Internet applications in three categories of Internet use (see Fig. 1):

![](/api/attachments/J5F9BKW5/fulltext/images/cbc0e40ffbf107784f6ea51ed7a96a8f60b8e38b868e38e6913b14970766b66e.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Three-Ring Model—three primary uses of the Internet.

\- Informational use: in which organizations disseminate information to educate, entertain, influence, or reach their citizens;

\- Transactional use: where they support a coordinated sequence of user and system activities to provide service and transfer value; and

\- Operational use: when an agency provides a new mechanism for conducting business operations by integrating IS, human intellect, and other resources into synergistic networks.

Classifying e-government applications using a concise framework allows e-government planners and administrators to take a broader view of an increasing and changing array of Internet applications. The model serves as a tool that allows e-government managers to recognize and focus on a set of critical issues specific to each category of e-government functions and to consider the critical issues that should be addressed to reduce its cost and provide information and services more effectively and efficiently [13].

## 2.2. E-government transformation

The stage theory of IS was introduced in the late 1960s. It refers to the steps in system maturation and the problems associated with each stage [4,7]. An organization can better utilize its IS and increase the chance of its success while controlling costs and other potential barriers.

Extending their Three-Ring Model, Koh and Balthazard argued that organizations follow an evolutionary path as shown in Fig. 2. In the earliest stage, an organization uses the Internet primarily for informational purposes, and returns are perceived as quick and large. As organizations incorporate technology, they expand their Internet applications to selling products or providing services. At this point, integrating Internet with existing applications and databases becomes effective.

While there is consensus on ultimate e-government needs, little has been written on how to achieve it. But there are several fundamental issues that required attention to ensure success.

## 3. Research model

Transforming an agency into a fully integrated, automated digital establishment is a daunting task and it increases with the size of the agency. While its focal point is the Internet, successful egovernment, like all other implementations, requires planning and coordination of goals, policies, processes, and technologies. We therefore devised our e-government readiness model to investigate the issues at three levels: strategic, system, and data.

## 3.1. SIS and government

Agencies are required to improve efficiency by both legislative mandate and budget and taxation income constraints. In 1996, congress enacted the Clinger-Cohen Act; requiring the heads of federal agencies to link investment in IT to agency accomplishments and establish a process to select, manage and control IT investment.

SIS and SIS planning continue to be one of the most critical issues for many IS executives [11,14]. They are organizational issues and should be conceptualized, operationalized, and evaluated as such [9,10]. Also, they are complex, multifaceted topics that must be addressed at a many levels [16]. Some of the factors that affect its success (or failure) depend on an effective organization, implementation, and database. Naturally, success requires alignment of IT and business strategies [1].

## 4. Methodology

In order to understand the e-government needs of the city of Denton, we conducted 6 personal interviews and 13 focus groups involving 73 participants. We used the feedback from these to help design the survey instrument. In addition, interviews with the city managers allowed us to determine how best to administer the survey and gain participation.

## 4.1. Instrument development

The instrument consisted of two sections: one that measured the employee perceptions of different e-government functions and the other that assessed critical success issues. All items were constructed using a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

For the first part of the instrument, we reviewed two previous studies; Koh and Balthazard had measured Internet practices in business while Deshazo et al. measured e-government activities in 131 municipal governments having 52 e-government service items organized in 12 categories. Items from these instruments were carefully examined for inclusion in our instrument. The second part was designed to measure readiness using the constructs discussed previously.

We conducted three rounds of pilot tests with panels of experts, including IS researchers, doctoral students, and some key city managers. Feedback from these pilot studies was incorporated into the final version of the instrument.

## 4.2. Data collection

The data collection process began with two letters sent by the city manager to all employees. It announced the intention to perform the survey and encouraged employee participation. In the letters, he also explained the purpose of the survey and its voluntary nature.

The survey was delivered in a multiple page format so that employees could respond to as many or as few pages at a time as they liked. A unique access code was assigned to each employee for security and tracking purposes. The survey web server kept track of progress and guided the employee through the survey. In addition to the initial e-mail message sent by the research team to provide the user access code, three follow-up messages were sent at 1-week intervals to encourage participation of those employees who had not completed the survey.

Out of 1104 employees eligible to participate, a total of 339 responded over a period of 5 weeks for a response rate of 30.7 percent. However, only about 800 employees had easy access to computers and, given this, our response rate could be considered as about 42 percent. Those employees without access to computers (some field service personnel, such as the trash service employees) were provided an option of using a hardcopy version of the survey but none were returned. However, they were obviously not directly involved in the design and delivery of e-government services.

## 5. Study findings

## 5.1. Demographics

The survey participants represented a broad range of employees in terms of department, tenure length, job, education, gender and age. All 21 departments were represented. The average tenure of the survey participants with the city was 8.4 years and on average, they had held their current position for about 4.4 years. About 54 percent held a position of an operational nature (field service, office and clerical, technical and supervisory) whereas the rest held a managerial or professional position. About half had a degree from a 4-year college or graduate school, whereas 13 percent were high school graduates. The average age of the participants was 40.2 and the number of male and female participants was almost equal.

## 5.2. Uses of the Internet

The city of Denton and most of its departments had various Internet applications to provide information to citizens and to its own employees; it had also automated and enhanced certain business transactions. Citizens can use the Web to download the city council meeting agenda and past meeting minutes, examine city budgets, download updates on taxes, and view civil service test scores. They can also watch and/or listen to city council meetings and planning and zoning meetings broadcast over a local community cable channel via the web. The city’s municipal court allowed citizens to search its citation records and pay fines online. On average, the city’s web server provides about 40,000 sessions with over a million hits on over 800 pages of web documents each month. Internally, e-mail has become an indispensable tool for employees to use in communicating with one-another and with citizens.

Despite these Internet initiatives, the use of the technology remains primarily in the presence stage of the four phase egovernment model [3] or the first stage of the e-readiness model in which the organization uses the Internet primarily for informational purposes.

We presented employees with a comprehensive list of egovernment functions and asked their opinions on them. The city had already implemented some of the functions, but many were still unexplored. The objective of this question was to help the city prioritize the overwhelming array of functions. Table 2 shows how employees viewed the importance of each function on a Likert scale 1 (least important) to 7 (most important).

Those e-government functions that employees considered very important with a score of 6.0 or higher included e-mail communication, posting city information, processing service requests online, processing permit applications and renewals online, and processing online job applications. Employees also thought that processing some transactions such as utility and fee payments would be important.

## 5.3. E-government readiness at three levels

Asked how well the city was prepared for e-government, employees responded that the city was ill-prepared at the data level. As shown in Table 3, the average score of items at the strategic level was 4.9, system level 4.5, and data level 4.2. The results suggest that the city management had a reasonable understanding of the strategic importance of e-government and the resolve to push e-government initiatives, but lacked the necessary data infrastructure to support its resolve. Without data infrastructure, e-government will remain fragmented and isolated, rendering little synergistic value. In fact, respondents rated all three data-related items lower than any other item. This is not only a technology issue but also of organizational and cultural concern. Like most government organizations, the city has historically neglected technology investment to create an environment in which data flows across functional boundaries. In fact, the bureaucratic nature and stove-piped structure of organizations has created a culture in which data sharing is often regarded as a relinquishing of power and authority.

## 6. Discussion

The findings of our research provided potentially transferable recommendations for enhancing the practice and concepts of egovernment. The city made significant inroads into e-government by implementing some advanced applications, such as allowing citizens to pay fees and fines online. Nevertheless, the city’s use of the Internet remains at an early stage. The city is embarking on a transaction stage but a fully automated online transaction system integrated with legacy applications and common enterprise databases is not yet being considered. For the city to achieve such a goal, the e-government readiness model suggests that it must recognize and focus on the critical issues at both the strategic planning and system implementation levels. The city must enhance its technical infrastructure before it can be successful and cost-effective in deployment of sophisticated e-government initiatives.

In system implementation, the agency must establish a mechanism to coordinate and integrate various e-government initiatives and to set up a data infrastructure that seamlessly connects different databases: a task that has been proposed for more than 40 years and is still unfulfilled in most of the industry and the private sector. As the e-government services provided by the city are expanded and enhanced, the possibilities and advantages will result.

## 7. Summary and conclusions

E-government is necessary at all levels to meet the needs of constituents. In our study, we explored and provided an egovernment readiness model. We used interviews and focus groups to obtain insights into government operations and issues related to e-government requirements. The feedback from these interviews was used in developing the survey instrument and to help us understand its results. Our analysis suggested that a twolevel e-government readiness model was more appropriate than the three-level model we originally posited.

Despite the concerns and issues specific to the city, our work allowed us to test and validate a conceptual framework that helped to explain the mechanism and dynamics of e-government as it evolves from a simple website into a fully integrated portal.

## Limitations

As with any exploratory study. our results must be assumed to be limited. The study was based on a single organization and the survey left out some important stakeholders such as citizens, elected officials, suppliers and contractors, and other government organizations. However, the pre-survey focus group and prior efforts by the city had already addressed others and established the need and basis for our survey effort. In addition, the city had conducted a survey with external stakeholders about the effectiveness of its e-government website.
