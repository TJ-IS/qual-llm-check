# Examining the efects of social influence in pre-adoption phase and initial post-adoption phase in the healthcare context

Zheng Lu<sup>a</sup>, Tingru Cui<sup>b</sup>, Yu Tong<sup>c,⁎</sup>, Wen Wang<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Computer Science, University of Nottingham Ningbo China, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Information Systems and Technology, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Pre-adoption phase Initial post-adoption phase User acceptance Social influence Healthcare information technology

## A B S T R A C T

Despite the widespread adoption of healthcare information technology (HIT) in hospitals, fostering physicians acceptance of this system is a challenging task. This study focuses on and compares two critical phases for user acceptance of HIT, namely, pre-adoption phase and initial post-adoption phase. Based on the psychological attachment theory, we propose that social influence factors exert diferentiated efects on physician acceptance of HIT in these two phases. Survey results show that reward, sanction, and informational influence afect physicians’ HIT acceptance in the pre-adoption phase, whereas reward and image are significantly associated with HIT acceptance in the initial post-adoption phase.

## 1. Introduction

Investment in enterprise systems continues growing. A recent fore cast for worldwide information technology (IT) spending shows that the spending on enterprise software will grow at the fastest rate among all categories of IT spending in organizations and reaches \$387 billion in 2018 [1]. In the healthcare sector, hospitals have substantially invested in the implementation of healthcare information technology (HIT) to enhance care quality and eficiency [2,3,4,5]. Despite the increasing adoption of HIT by hospitals, fostering physicians’ acceptance of the new system is an enduring challenging [6,7]. Often served as the designated system users, physicians tend to anticipate or experience sig nificant work changes because of the new system [8,7,9].

Prior evidence reports dificulties and importance of gaining users buy-in during the transition period of new system, including pre-adop tion phase (i.e., the short period prior to the system rollout) [10,11] and initial post-adoption phase (i.e., the short period immediately after the system rollout) [12]. Lapointe and Rivard’s [7] qualitative study demonstrated that the lack of involvement and acceptance in the preadoption stage can result in the resistance of physicians escalating in the later stages. In the initial post-adoption phase, physicians tend to resist the new system because of various reasons, such as changes in work and loss of status/power [13,14].

This research hence focuses on understanding and comparing phy sicians’ acceptance of a new HIT in these two phases. In hospitals, physicians possess high job autonomy. Their system acceptance and usage are found to be less influenced by the hospital mandates or system-related perceptions [15,16]. Instead, as knowledge workers, physicians rely on professional peers to gather information and form perceptions related to a new system [17]. Social influence from their peers or professional supervisors plays a salient role in inducing their acceptance [15,17,16].

Physicians’ experiences of a HIT are likely to difer in the preadoption and initial post-adoption phases. In the pre-adoption phase, physicians as potential users typically lack suficient hands-on experi ence with the new system. Their understanding and judgment toward the new system tend to be associated with the functional and symbolic value of the system [18,19]. Hence, they can be uncertain toward the actual value of the new system [9,20] or may underestimate the dificulty of system-induced work changes [8,7].

A change of physicians’ perception can occur when they initially interact with the new system. Users in the initial post-adoption phase experience drastic changes and rely on their system interaction to understand the new system [21,22,12]. They typically encounter considerable challenges to become familiar with new system functions, and changed work routines [22]. Many users experience dificulties in reconciling their initial hope with their experience of actual usage of a system. As a result, new users tend to generate a torrent of complaints, experience performance loss, feel anxious because of inexperience, or even regard the system as a threat to their work [8,9,12].

With diferent user experiences, the same social influence factor might result in diferent consequences in these two phases. For instance, physicians subject to the influence from peers may compare with their symbolic understanding of the system in the pre-adoption phase, but with their actual experience in the initial post-adoption phase. Hence, we focus on answering the following question in the present study: How is physicians’ acceptance in the pre-adoption phase (for potential users) and in the initial post-adoption phase (for new users) af fected by diferent types of social influence factors?

Early research analyzing the efect of social influence on system usage presents varied findings. While some literature identified a significant positive association (e.g., [23], others failed to detect so (e.g., [24]. Several researchers suggested that these mixed findings may be a result of a unidimensional conceptualization of social influence [25,26]. Accordingly, we observe an increasing number of studies taking social influence as a multidimensional construct (e.g., [27,28,29]. Drawing on psychological attachment theory [30], we posit that the HIT experiences of physicians tend to difer in the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases; hence, their acceptance in these two phases is afected by various social influence factors. We validate the model through a field survey from a public hospital shortly before and after the rollout of an electronic medical record (EMR) system. Our results provide empirical evidence to support the conjecture and add to the extant literature by advancing the in-depth understanding of system-induced changes in organizations [14,12,31,32]. By understanding factors influencing physicians’ acceptance at diferent stages, hospital management can deploy efective managerial interventions accordingly.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

## 2.1. Psychological attachment theory

Developed by [30], [33], psychological attachment theory proposes three social influence mechanisms afecting the behavior of an individual: compliance, identification, and internalization. This theory assumes that individuals can accept an induced behavior via these three conceptually diferent mechanisms. Compliance occurs “when an individual accepts the induced behavior because he hopes to achieve a favorable reaction from another person or group” [30], p. 53). Identification takes efect when an individual accepts social influences in establishing or maintaining a positive image in a group [30] because “it is perceived to enhance one’s. . . status in one’s social system” [34], p. 195). Internalization happens when performing the suggested behavior is deemed congruent with the value system of individuals. They may perceive the induced behavior useful for solving an issue or may determine this behavior consistent with their needs [35]. Psychological attachment theory has been applied in the organizational setting to explain how individual information system use may be afected by diferent social influence processes (e.g., [28,32]. Despite the valuable insights, prior literature lacks clear understanding on how diferent factors could take efects in the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases.

## 2.2. Social influence in the pre-adoption phase and initial post-adoption phase

Based on the psychological attachment theory and the contextual diferences between the pre-adoption phase and the initial post-adoption phase, we propose a research model (Fig. 1) that describes the influences of social influence factors on physicians’ acceptance of the EMR system in these two phases. Reward, sanction, image, and informational influence are identified important in this context and are proposed to influence physicians’ system acceptance diferently at these two phases. Control variables include demographic factors (i.e., age, gender, and education), perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, personal innovativeness, and self-eficacy.

## 2.2.1. Compliance

According to psychological attachment theory, the influence of expectations from significant others in terms of rewards or sanctions represents compliance [36,30]. Reward and sanction are deemed as key drivers of behavior change [37], and they are extensively used to induce the desired employee behavior [38,39]. In the healthcare setting, hospital management often employs reward and sanction to foster the acceptance of EMR system [16]. Employees such as physicians who are afected by a compliance process tend to engage in the induced behavior for the expectation of receiving explicit rewards (or approval as implicit rewards) or avoiding sanctions (or disapproval as implicit sanctions) from the superior [40,41].

Reward (compliance)

Rewards denote positive outcomes that subordinates perceive to gain (e.g., salary increase, bonus, and supervisor recognition) when they adopt the desired behavior [37,42]. During the pre-adoption phase, the judgment of potential adopters is chiefly based on the information gained from training and supervisor advocacy [18,41,43]. A lack of direct experience in using the new system makes potential adopters uncertain about the actual consequences of using the system. Reward from hospital management or supervisors could legitimize and hence reduce the uncertainty of the adoption behavior [44]. Without the aggravations of actual usage, the expectation of being rewarded or receiving approval can efectively foster the acceptance of subordinates [45,37,46]. Therefore, we expect that reward is positively associated with the EMR system acceptance of potential adopters.

In the initial post-adoption phase, physicians typically experience challenges in using the new system. Rewards can serve as external re inforcements that help strengthen the force and of the induced behaviors [47]. This reinforcement mechanism of rewards is helpful when individuals experience negative events [48]. In the context of this study, rewards can empower physicians as new users in resolving challenges during the initial usage of an EMR system. Therefore, we posit that in the initial post-adoption phase, reward can enhance the EMR system acceptance of new users.

H1a. In the pre-adoption phase, reward positively afects the EMR system acceptance of potential adopters.

H1b. In the initial post-adoption phase, reward positively afects the EMR system acceptance of new users.

## Sanction (compliance)

Sanction in the form of punishment, coercion, or disapproval is another key type of compliance often employed in organizations [49,50,37]. Such social influence factor takes efect because subordinates want to avoid the risks of receiving negative outcomes or withdrawing positive consequences, such as withholding pay raises, delaying promotions, or any form of disapproval from supervisors [51,42]. In the pre-adoption phase, physicians lack a direct experience with an EMR system. Providing tangible or intangible sanction is a strong signal of the attitude of management toward the new system, which could reduce the risks and uncertainties of adoption through legitimatizing the behavior [44]. Potential adopters may decide to comply when potential adopters perceive that the inability to meet supervisor expectations can generate the feeling of disapproval from supervisors or can potentially induce explicit sanctions [37]. Therefore, we expect that sanction is positively related to the EMR system acceptance of potential adopters in the pre-adoption phase.

H2. In the pre-adoption phase, sanction positively afects the EMR system acceptance of potential adopters.

While the preceding discussion suggests that sanction can positively afect the system acceptance of potential users in the pre-adoption phase, we posit that this positive relationship may not hold for sanction in the initial post-adoption phase. In the initial post-adoption phase, physicians tend to encounter significant changes in work routines and substantial challenges in using the new system [8]. Such adverse cir cumstances generate negative emotions [52]. Research on expectationdisconfirmation theory in post-adoption behaviors contends that users attitudes toward the system often change when they start to obtain firsthand experience, which deter them from continuing using the system [53,54]. A recent qualitative study has found that imposing tight sanctions in organizations could lead to employees’ resistant behaviors [49]. Unlike providing rewards that ofer positive gain for users when experiencing negative events, further imposing the sanction in this setting does not provide them a constructive direction and may not be efective in fostering the desired attitude and behavior [48]. Therefore, we do not expect that sanction to be an influential factor during the initial post-adoption phase.

## 2.2.2. Image (identification)

Identification takes efect when an individual perceives that the induced behavior could help him/her obtain a favorable relationship with another person or group [30]. Such influence is often associated with gaining image in a group [26]. Image denotes the extent to which a certain behavior can enhance the status of an individual in the social system [55,34]. In a work setting, an individual’s increased status in a group can be reflected by the enhanced power and influence [26]. Therefore, the individual typically has the tendency to enhance his/her image within a reference group [56]. Although physicians’ experiences tend to difer between pre-adoption phase and initial post-adoption phase, they are generally situated within the same set of reference groups. Hence, we expect image could be an efective motivator for both potential adopters who are aware of basic system operations, and for new users who just gain actual experience of using the new system.

H3a. In the pre-adoption phase, image positively afects the system acceptance of potential users.

H3b. In the initial post-adoption phase, image positively afects the EMR system acceptance of new users.

While image is important in both phases, we further propose that the salience of the efect difers. Physicians often treat them as healthcare professionals with core responsibility of taking care of patients. They do not naturally associate EMR system use as central to their professional image [16]. In the pre-adoption phase, potential users learn the basic operations of the new system primarily through training sessions and advocacy by hospital management [8]. Their sense of importance of gaining EMR-specific image is likely to be weaker as compared with new users, who are immersed in and required to use the system for the daily work. Therefore, we propose,

H3c. The efect of image on EMR system acceptance is more salient in the initial post-adoption phase than in the pre-adoption phase.

## 2.2.3. Informational influence (Internalization)

When an individual is in the internalization process, the individual, through others’ persuasion or behavior, believes that the induced behavior is consistent with his/her value system. Consequently, the individual decides to accept the external influence [30]. Informational influence is defined as an “influence to accept information from another as evidence about reality” [57], p.629). It emphasizes on the process that individuals internalize external information [32]. In the hospital setting, physicians who work in the cooperative work environment have frequent interaction and chance of observing their peers’ behavior [8]. To the extent that physicians tend to identify with their peers [16], internalization is likely to be triggered. These users may be influenced to accept the system because of their intention to interpret the reality of the situation accurately or to behave appropriately [56]. Hence, we theorize that informational influence is positively related to EMR system acceptance in both the pre-adoption phase and the initial postadoption phase.

H4a. In the pre-adoption phase, informational influence positively afects the EMR system acceptance of potential adopters.

H4b. In the initial post-adoption phase, informational influence positively afects the EMR system acceptance of new users.

While informational influence helps physicians recognize the im portance of EMR system use to facilitate their healthcare work, we propose that such efect is more salient in the pre-adoption phase. In this phase, physicians tend to under-estimate the dificulties of the new system. However, in the initial post-adoption phase, healthcare provi ders often experience a decline in their work performance [8,12]. Given the primary responsibility of taking care of patients, physicians in this phase may be less firm about the short-term value of using the system. Hence, we propose,

H4c. The efect of informational influence on EMR system acceptance is more salient in the pre-adoption phase than in the initial post-adoption phase.

## 3. Research methodology

Survey was used to test the hypotheses as this methodology ofers a basis in ascertaining generalizability of our findings to the population; supports result replication in future studies, and provides statistical power [58]. We used individual physician as the unit of analysis. We aimed to explain the distinct social influence antecedents of physician acceptance in the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases.

Our research setting was a large public general hospital in China. We conducted a field survey during the period when this hospital was rolling out an EMR system in all inpatient units. A notable evidence of health IT proliferation in hospitals is the extensive adoption of the EMR system [4]. An EMR system typically serves as an electronic infra structure for healthcare providers (e.g., physicians) to record, store, and process various types of patient-related data. The majority of the comprehensive EMR system packages support the computerized viewing of patient health information (e.g., previous diagnosis, medi cation list, and test results), documentation of diagnosis and progress notes, prescription of medication, ordering of laboratory and radiology tests, decision support, and generation of billing information [59,16]. An EMR system is touted to provide significant cost savings to hospitals by reducing paper usage and physical storage, improving the capacity to identify the least expensive drugs, and minimizing the occurrence of redundant tests [60,4]. Moreover, the service quality of a hospital can be improved by the EMR system features, such as care management reminders and alerts, safety standards embedded in prescription templates, and quality control [60,61].

The focal system included the core functions of a typical EMR system package, such as health information and data (e.g., previous diagnoses, medication, and test results), test results, computerized order entry, and clinical decision support (e.g., reminders). It was expected that the physicians would use the system to a certain degree, to perform their daily work directly or indirectly. During the time of the study, approximately half of the medical units adopted the EMR system, whereas the other half was expected to use the system within six months. This context provided an excellent setting in comparing the system acceptance between potential and new users. Our research setting at a single hospital minimized the influence of organizational-level variables (i.e., hospital size, hospital managerial support, and diferent EMR systems in use) on individual behavior.

## 3.1. Scale development

Our constructs were measured by adapting the items, which have been validated in prior literature. If this approach was impossible, then new items were developed after a comprehensive review of related studies and validity tests were then used to check the quality of these items. In order to ensure that the results in these two phases could be compared, we minimized the diferences of the items for each construct used in the two phases, i.e., the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases.

Four social influence constructs are formative constructs from multiple sources of influence. These multiple sources were adapted from [62] to fit into this research context. They included the hospital management, supervisor, departmental peers, hospital peers outside the department, and professional peers<sup>1</sup> . In particular, hospital management and supervisors who hold power were identified as sources for reward and sanction. Departmental peers, hospital peers outside the department, and professional peers were recognized as important sources for image and informational influence. The formative nature of the constructs led us to select one representative item based on the existing scale. For sanction, we developed the items based on the definition because the extant scale for compliance primarily focused on the reward aspect. Our rationale of selecting diferent salient sources was that these sources might have diferent efects on physicians. For example, physicians may be eager to receive recognition from their supervisor but may not be considerably keen on achieving rewards from hospital management.

The dependent variable EMR system acceptance was operationalized by the intention to use (continue using) the EMR system and intention of physicians to allow other people to use (or continue using) the EMR system on their behalf. Prior studies recognize that direct and indirect uses of an information system provide a comprehensive view of user–system interaction in a hospital setting [66,67]. Physicians, whose work involves immense pressure and time constraints, often indirectly use the health IS by asking others to interact with the system on their behalf [68,16]. Indirect IS use is also observed as a common phenomenon across diferent countries (e.g., the West and the East), industries (e.g., service and manufacturing industries), and work positions (e.g., supervisors and physicians) [69,70,71,72]. To allow an easy comparison of social influence efects between the pre-adoption phase and post-adoption phase, we chose intention to use/continue using the system to operationalize the acceptance. Such way of operationalization is used in prior literature on user acceptance of IS (e.g., [73,74] and behavioral intention in initial post-adoption phase (e.g., [54].

We measured the age of physicians by asking the participants to specify a range rather than to input the actual number. This could help to minimize the discomfort when respondents answer sensitive questions. The operationalization of the constructs is shown in Table 1.

## 3.2. Conceptual validation

To enhance conceptual validity, the questionnaire was first checked by several IS researchers to detect and correct potential issues rooted in the wording. After that, we conducted two rounds of item sorting through postgraduate IS students, which was comprised of one unlabeled and one labeled sorting sessions (six IS student for each ses. sion). Based on their feedback, minor amendments were made on a number of items to clarify the concerns. We also adopted back-translation to ensure the meanings were comparable and equivalent. Two independent postgraduate students were recruited, and they translated the questionnaire into Chinese. The Chinese questionnaire was then retranslated back into English by another two postgraduate students. The translated version and the original were compared and some changes were made when needed. To obtain face validity, we also invited the chief information oficer in the hospital and two senior physicians to provide comments on the survey questionnaire. We used their suggestions as basis in modifying he EMR system description and in the framing of the survey questions.

## 3.3. Survey administration

We comprised a cover letter specifving the research obiective in the survey package. We highlighted the target respondents on page 2 of the survey questionnaire to make sure that the survey respondents belong to the appropriate categories (i.e., potential users/new users). Understanding that the focal hospital had multiple health IT systems (e.g., administration system, picture archiving, and communication system) in addition to the EMR system, we included a description of a typical EMR system to enable the respondents to relate to the focal system used in the hospital.

Subsequently, the authors distributed the survey package to the respondents in the surveyed hospital. For each completed questionnaire, 10 yuan (equivalent to US \$1.50) donation to a Chinese charity foundation was used as an incentive. The chief information oficer of the hospital and two physicians who gave comments on the previous version of the questionnaire were not included in the actual survey. The questionnaire did not ask personally identifiable information, including respondent’s name or e-mail address to protect respondents’ privacy. To increase the response rate, the authors made follow-up phone calls and physical visits to the hospital. Our questionnaire was distributed to potential and new users of an EMR system. Among the 300 questionnaires sent out, 175 responses were returned, which thereby yielded a response rate of 58.3%. Considering the tight schedules of the physicians, our response rate was satisfactory. Of these responses, 85 were from potential users of an EMR system, whereas 90 were from new users of an EMR system. Several survey responses were excluded because of missing data. The final sample was 80 and 71 for potential adopters and new users, respectively.

## 4. Data analysis

The demographic information of the respondents is presented in Table 2. In contrast to that in Western countries, the entry-level qualification of a physician in the Chinese healthcare system is a bachelor’s degree. Older physicians typically lack higher degrees than a bachelor’s degree because of a previous lack of opportunities for a high-level of education in healthcare. Considering structural equation modeling (SEM) allowed for indicators to have diferent weights for construct estimation, it was used in our hypothesis testing [79]. Covariance-based SEM generally requires sample size over 200 and at least two reflective items or reflective constructs per each formative construct to achieve model identification [80]. Compared with covariance-based SEM, partial least squares (PLS) is found to generally more suitable for studies with small sample size and with multiple formative constructs [81]. Given that our study has relatively small sample size for potential and new users and with multiple formative constructs, we used PLS via SmartPLS version 2.0 M3 for the data analysis. Table 3 shows that the mean values of the studied variables for potential users are higher than the values for new users. Prior evidence indicates that potential users tend to form a positive attitude toward the new system. For example, qualitative evidence from Boudreau and Robey’s [18] study shows that users are initially excited about the arrival of a new system, and they expect that its implementation is a significant improvement that can make their work considerably easy, fast, and eficient. Other studies also reported a similar phenomenon in which potential users are enthusiastic about the new system and eagerly participate in training sessions [8,7].

## 4.2. Evaluating the structural model

After verifying good measurement properties, we subsequently validated the hypotheses using PLS bootstrapping procedure with 300 random samples. A two-tailed, 5% level of significance level was used for the statistical tests. The results of three models for potential and new users: control model, full model, and full model plus nonhypothesized relationships are given in Table 8. We employed F-statistic to assess whether the change in $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ between the two steps of hierarchical multiple regression analysis was significant. The change in $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ between Step 1 Control Model and Step 2 Full Model of the regression was 30.8% $( \mathbf { p } _ { \mathbf { \lambda } } < 0 . 0 1 )$ for potential users and 24.9% $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ for new users. Hence, the outcomes of the hypothesized relationships could be interpreted. The results of Steps 2 and 3 for new users in Table 8 indicated that adding the nonhypothesized relationships did not change the significance of paths.

Our test results revealed that our hypotheses are largely supported. Reward, sanction, and informational influence afect physicians’ acceptance of EMR system in the pre-adoption phase. Hence, H1a, H2, and H4 are supported. In the initial post-adoption phase, reward is significantly associated with system acceptance. As a result, H1b is supported. Image is partially significant for system acceptance (H3b is partially supported). H4b is not supported as informational influence does not have a significant efect on system acceptance. Given that image is not significant in the pre-adoption phase but is marginally significant in the initial post-adoption phase, H3c is partially supported. H4c is supported as informational influence is significant in the pre adoption phase but not significant in the initial post-adoption phase.

When assessing the measurement model, two items are found with negative weights in the initial post-adoption phase. To further evaluate the impacts of these negative items, we conducted further analyses by removing the items individually and together. As shown in Table 9, the results remain robust except for the last model when removing both items.

In the current study, we depict the diferent nature of user experi ence in the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases, which serves as a basis to theorize that user acceptance can be afected by various social influence factors. The findings confirm our research model in general. As hypothesized, reward, sanction and informational influence significantly afect the EMR system acceptance of potential users, whereas image is not associated with the EMR system acceptance in the pre-adoption phase. For new users, reward is significant and image is marginally significant associated with the EMR system acceptance, but sanction is not associated with the EMR system acceptance in the initial post-adoption phase. When comparing two phases, sanction and in formational influence are more important in the pre-adoption phase, while image is more salient in the initial post-adoption phase.

Contrary to H3a, image was not found to significantly influence EMR system acceptance in the pre-adoption phase. This may due to the nature of healthcare work for physicians. Physicians often treat them as healthcare professionals with core responsibility of taking care of patients. They may not perceive that the EMR system work is central to their professional image [16]. Thus, potential adopters, without handson experience to understand the value of EMR system, may not be at tracted by the image enhancement related to an EMR system.

In contrast to H4b, the relationship between informational influence and the EMR system acceptance is not significant for the new users in the initial post-adoption phase. A plausible explanation for this finding is that recognizing the value of the system may not serve as a strong motivation to induce the EMR system acceptance in the early postadoption phase. In this phase, physicians experience a steep learning curve and they are overwhelmed with challenges in the initial postadoption phase. Several extrinsic benefits, such as rewards and image enhancement, can be more efective in this phase to compensate for the challenges and eforts expended in this phase.

As shown in Table 9, removing constructs with single negative weight do not change the significance of hypothesis testing. However, we also notice that when removing both negative items, the efect of image becomes insignificant from marginal significant. Future research should be cautious when multiple negative weights exist in the measurement model.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research

This study contains several limitations. First, this study surveyed only physicians from a public hospital in China. While less studies focus on healthcare IS in China, which makes this study valuable, the result may not be generalizable to all care providers in other countries. Culture, economy, and institutional pressures can difer for hospitals in diferent countries. For example, physicians in Western countries typically hold high power in the workplace. Specific social influence fac tors, such as sanction, may be less salient in this context. However, the Western healthcare system (e.g., the US) experiences a paradigm shift from fee-for-service to employment mode [65]. Many physicians can experience an employer–employee relationship in hospitals [63,64], thereby making the present study valuable in understanding this trend. Hence, future research can replicate this study to explore the importance of social power in other care providers or in hospital from Western countries.

Second, we measured physician acceptance through the survey items on intention of direct and indirect use. We did not collect usagerelated factors to facilitate the comparison between two phases. Archival data such as system log-ins and use logs in the initial postadoption phase was not feasible in this study given the confidentiality concern from the hospital management. In addition, we observed that some physicians used the accounts of others to operate the system (i.e., indirect use behavior) in the focal hospital. Hence, system logs may not accurately capture the actual usage behavior/acceptance in our study. Although not applicable in this study, future research can collect the archival measure of acceptance when appropriate and feasible. Furthermore, future research can extend this study by measuring the two constructs (direct vs. indirect use) separately with existing scales. This allows a deeper theoretical understanding on the efects of social influence.

Third, given the limitation of sample size and the formative con structs, we employed PLS analysis in this study. It should be noted that the weights of indicators for formative constructs could be context specific. The limited sample size might also to weaken the chance of detecting significant efects. Should more data points be collected in the future research, researchers could employ other methods such as covariance-based SEM and adopt multiple indicators–multiple causes (MIMIC) approach to develop measures for social influence constructs.

Fourth, the importance of the pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases can be further explored in future studies through the collection of longitudinal data to explore the direct relationship between these two phases. This will allow the authors to draw a more concrete conclusion on whether user beliefs vary over time, as well as use more sophisticated analytical technique such as polynomial modeling [54] to draw further insights.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the literature on IS, social influence, and psychological attachment theory. First, this research contributes to the IS adoption/implementation literature by conceptually distinguishing the two critical phases for user acceptance, namely pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases. Previous literature conceptually or qualitatively reports diferent beliefs and attitudes before and after the system rollout (e.g., [7,86,21]. Our study adds to the extant literature by theoretically and empirically comparing the antecedents of user acceptance in these two phases. The focus on social influence also complements prior literature that largely examines system-related characteristics as the antecedents of technology acceptance. Thus, thi research is an exploratory study in this area that analyzes how organizational systems users (e.g., in this study, physicians) behave and are influenced diferently in these two phases.

Second, the current study adds to the literature on social influence [15,32] by recognizing that the multidimensionality of social influence can possibly explain the prior inconsistent efects of social influence. Our findings demonstrate that not all of the social influence factors can efectively foster the EMR system acceptance of physicians in the pre adoption phase or initial post-adoption phase. Multiple dimensions should be employed to comprehensively and reliably represent social influence. We further add to the related literature by theorizing the diferent efects of a social influence factor in these two phases.

Third, this study contributes to psychological attachment theory [30] by first applying this theory in the context of hospital IS in China. We also identify the boundary condition of this theory by demon strating that the characteristics of the pre-adoption and initial postadoption phases can afect the efectiveness of various social influence factors, which are theorized from this theory.

## 5.3. Practical implications

The findings of this research also make valuable practical implications for the EMR system implementation and hospital management. First, managers should note the distinction between the EMR system acceptance of physicians in the pre-adoption phase and that in the initial post-adoption phase. Our findings indicate that the EMR system acceptance of physicians can difer before and after the EMR system rollout. Thus, managers should anticipate this change, and dificulties should be considered when applying the strategies to facilitate system implementation.

Second, the findings of this study can guide hospital management that holds relatively strong power over physicians in deploying efective social influence mechanisms to potential users during the preadoption phase and to new users during the initial post-adoption phase. Reward can efectively enhance user acceptance in both the pre-adop tion phase and the initial post-adoption phase; thus, managers should recognize its importance and should deploy this mechanism to attract potential users and new users. However, managers should note that other social influence factors are only efective in one of these two phases. On the one hand, sanction and informational influence are effective motivators for potential users in the pre-adoption phase. On the other hand, image is only efective for users in the initial post-adoption phase. While sanction and informational influence can help get users on board before rollout, once the system is in use, users cannot be coerced into accepting it through fear of sanction. Thus, in the initial stage of post-adoption, managers need to focus on rewarding user acceptance and stressing the connection between user acceptance and positive user image. Hence, managers could selectively employ the social influence tactics when they face time or resource constraints.

Third, our research findings based on physicians in the China context can ofer important implications for healthcare practitioners in the West. Given the recent trend of Western physicians shifting from feefor-service to hospital employment mode, certain social influence factors (e.g., reward and sanction) can become feasible strategies for hospital management in Western countries to facilitate the system implementation.

## 6. Conclusion

Gaining system acceptance from organizational users can be challenging yet critical to the eventual success of system implementation. This study adds to this stream of research by systematically analyzing the two critical yet under-scrutinized stages of IS acceptance, namely pre-adoption and initial post-adoption phases. The findings advance the existing literature of how multidimensional social influence factors (i.e., reward, sanction, image, and informational influence) can diferently afect the physician acceptance of the EMR system in these two phases. Accordingly, practitioners can mindfully deploy efective mechanism to achieve the desired outcomes.
