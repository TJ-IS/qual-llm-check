RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Does ethical ideology affect software piracy attitude and behaviour? An empirical investigation of computer users in China

Ricky Y.K. Chan and Jennifer W.M. Lai

Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong

Correspondence: Jennifer W.M. Lai, Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Hong Kong SAR, Kowloon 852, Hong Kong. Tel: 852 2766 7370; Fax: 852 2765 0611

## Abstract

This study empirically examines Chinese computer users’ ethical ideology and its relationship to their software piracy attitude and behaviour. The investigation reveals several important findings. First, cluster analysis results show that Chinese computer users can be divided into four ethical ideology types (i.e., situationists, absolutists, subjectivists, and exceptionists) reasonably consistent with Forsyth’s taxonomy. Second, when compared with situationists, absolutists, and exceptionists, subjectivists are found to have the least unfavourable attitude towards software piracy and are most frequently engaged in software piracy. Third, Chinese computer users’ ideological relativism exerts a stronger influence on their software piracy attitude and behaviour than does their ideological idealism. The findings suggest that the government, and authentic software developers and vendors should focus on subjectivists as their target audience of anti-software piracy communications. These policy makers and practitioners should also make concerted efforts to help subjectivists better realise how their software piracy act may damage parties ‘proximate’ to themselves so as to lower the rate of software piracy. ## Introduction

In the global business environment that relies heavily on information technology, there is a compelling need for the proper protection of digital assets. Software piracy, or the production of unauthorised copies of software by individuals or businesses for resale or for use in the workplace, at school, or at home, is an unethical and illegal behaviour pervading today’s society (Limayem et al, 2004). This phenomenon is economically destructive for companies that develop and market software worldwide (Bhattacharjee et al, 2007). According to the Business Software Alliance (2009), the worldwide average piracy rate in 2008 was 41%, resulting in annual revenue losses of US\$53 billion for the entire software industry. The Alliance also estimated that for every 2 dollars spent on legitimate software purchases, around 1 dollar’s worth of software was obtained illegally (Business Software Alliance, 2009).

Accompanying the worldwide prevalence of software piracy has been an increasing research interest in understanding the factors that account for computer users’ engagement in such activity (Tan, 2002). Despite this interest, Holsapple et al (2008) have pointed out that most of these investigations were confined to college students from developed countries (primarily the U.S.), and did not control for respondents’ potential social desirability bias. The first limitation highlights the restriction of previous studies to only younger and more educated respondents from developed nations. Consequently, research on ordinary (non-student) computer users from rapidly developing or emerging countries (e.g., China) remains scant.

## Justifications and objectives of the study

According to estimates of the Business Software Alliance (2009), software piracy in developing countries is far more severe than that in developed nations. China, the world’s largest and fastest-growing emerging economy (Zhou et al, 2005), has been specifically dubbed ‘the capital of piracy’ due to the prevalence of counterfeits in all of its industries, particularly in the software sector (Yang et al, 2004). In 2008, China’s software piracy rate was 80%, compared with the worldwide average of 41%. Suffering from an annual financial loss of US\$6.7 billion, the country was ranked as the second most affected nation for software piracy in 2008 (Business Software Alliance, 2009). As over 70% of China’s software products are imported from overseas (mostly the U.S.), the prevalence of software piracy there also has a significant negative impact on the business performance of foreign genuine software developers and vendors (US Commercial Service China, 2010). The severity of software piracy in China warrants the conduct of more thorough research on this activity there.

Given that decisions to pirate software are likely to involve ethical considerations (Moores & Chang, 2006), this investigation is aimed at examining how the ethical ideology of ordinary Chinese computer users is related to their attitude towards software piracy (software piracy attitude) and to their behavioural engagement in software piracy (software piracy behaviour). This broad research aim is translated into three specific objectives. First, with reference to the dimensions commonly adopted in previous ethical ideology research (i.e., ‘relativism’ and ‘idealism’; see discussion below), it explores the major types of ethical ideology exhibited by Chinese computer users. Second, it examines whether Chinese computer users having different ethical ideologies differ in their software piracy attitude and behaviour. Third, it examines if different ethical ideology dimensions (relativism vs idealism) differ in their influence on Chinese computer users’ software piracy attitude and behaviour. This study also addresses Holsapple et al’s (2008) concern by specifically controlling for the potential problem of social desirability bias. By employing ordinary computer users as respondents and controlling for their potential social desirability bias, this study should render more representative and valid findings than have been provided by previous investigations.

## Studying ethics in China

In addition to enriching the extant software piracy literature, the use of China as a research setting can provide insights into how computer users from Asian cultures actually respond to software piracy. While Western cultural values stress the importance of protecting intellectual property rights, Asian cultural values emphasise social harmony and co-operation, and thus may cast doubt on the legitimacy of a monopoly claim over such rights (Husted, 2000). Relating specifically to Asian countries (e.g., China, Singapore) that are heavily influenced by Confucian philosophy, these cross-cultural researchers have further contended that people there often subscribe to the concept of sharing and believe that individual developers or creators should share their works with others (Ang et al, 2001). Consistent with these scholarly opinions, prior empirical studies have also reported that U.S. students exhibit less permissive attitudes towards computer-use ethics than do Hong Kong, Singaporean (Whitman et al, 1998), and Thai students (Kini et al, 2004). To summarise, owing to the possible conceptual divergence in intellectual property rights between Western and Asian computer users, and the scant research attention to the latter, it is considered justified to adopt the present research setting to investigate software piracy issues. By advancing authentic software vendors’ understanding of the mindset of software pirates in China, this study can help them devise better measures to crack down on software piracy there.

In examining individuals’ ethical perspectives, previous studies often referred to Kohlberg’s (1976) theory of cognitive moral development to guide the analysis (Trevino et al, 2006; Davison et al, 2009). Despite its popularity, this theory focuses on describing individuals’ moral maturation over time based primarily on a single moral principle, the social justice philosophy (Johnson, 2007). Although this theoretical focus renders a detailed description of individuals’ moral development over time, it neglects the very fact that individuals may refer to different moral principles or ethical ideologies to arrive at their ethical decision (Smith, 2009). So far as software piracy research is concerned, this focus has hampered a thorough investigation on how an important individualspecific variable, that is, a computer user’s ethical ideology, would affect his or her software piracy attitude and behaviour.

Hence, this study refers to another popular ethical theory, Forsyth’s (1980) ethical ideology theory, to conduct the analysis. Unlike Kohlberg’s theory, this theory focuses on examining the ethical ideologies that individuals would apply to arrive at their judgments on an ethically questionable issue. It is hoped that the application of Forsyth’s theory in this study can offer a new perspective to examine software piracy, and consequently advance understanding of the relationship between ethical ideology, and attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy. This application also provides authentic software vendors and developers with a new basis to differentiate software pirates from nonpirates, and ultimately helps them concentrate their resources on tailoring educational/communication programmes to rectify the pirates’ software piracy attitude and behaviour.

## Relating piracy attitudes and behaviour to ethical ideology

## An overview of their relationships

This study focuses on investigating how individuals with different ethical ideology types may vary in their software piracy attitude and behaviour. This research focus is considered appropriate as the constructs of ‘ethical ideology’, ‘software piracy attitude’, and ‘software piracy behaviour’ are theoretically and closely related to one another.

According to the value-attitude-behaviour hierarchy (Homer & Kahle, 1988), individuals’ fundamental values affect their attitudes towards a specific behaviour, and finally the behaviour itself (i.e., value-attitude-behaviour). According to this hierarchy, a value is defined as ‘an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct is personally preferable to its opposite’ (Homer & Kahle, 1988, p. 638). This value is ‘responsible for the selection and maintenance of the goals towards which individuals strive, while simultaneously regulating the manner in which this striving takes place’ (Shim & Eastlick, 1998, p. 142). Logically, as ethical ideologies can be construed as ethical beliefs or beliefs that constitute the basis on which moral decisions should be made (Robertson et al, 2008), they should fall within the above definition and thus be construed as some ethically related values. Following this logic and the proposed value-attitude link of the hierarchy, it is thus expected that individuals with different ethical ideologies would vary in their attitude towards software piracy.

In addition to the direct influence of value on attitude, the hierarchy postulates a direct influence of attitude on behaviour. This postulated attitude-behaviour link shares similarity with the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980) and the theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen, 1991); both of which assert that attitude towards a specific behaviour predicts the corresponding behavioural intention, which in turn, predicts the corresponding actual behaviour.

Moreover, the present conceptual framework, as shown in Figure 1, proposes a direct influence of value (ethical ideology) on behaviour. This proposed influence follows the view of some earlier psychologists (e.g., Allport, 1961; Rokeach, 1973). It also echoes some more recent empirical findings that suggest a significant direct impact of values on the corresponding behaviours (e.g., Bardi & Schwartz, 2003).

## Ethical ideologies

Following Sharp’s (1898) contention that ethical judgments tend to vary between individuals, Forsyth (1980) advocated that individuals’ ethical ideologies or approaches to making ethical judgments depend on two dimensions: relativism and idealism. Relativism refers to the extent to which an individual rejects universal moral rules in favour of subjective or situational approaches. This implies that individuals with higher relativism will have a stronger tendency to reject the use of universal moral rules when making decisions, and vice versa. Idealism refers to the extent to which an individual thinks that morally right courses of action will always yield desirable outcomes. It stresses the inherent goodness of certain natural laws and a commitment not to harm others despite any situational urgency (Ramsey et al, 2007). This implies that individuals with higher idealism will have a stronger desire to avoid actions that will endanger others, and vice versa. By dichotomising relativism and idealism into high and low levels, and then crossing these dimensions, Forsyth (1980) further proposed his 2 - 2 taxonomy to delineate four major types of ethical ideologies, namely situationists, absolutists, subjectivists, and exceptionists.

This concern may be particularly relevant for Chinese people who have long been regarded as relying on a relativist (Ralston et al, 1994) but less idealistic approach (Whitcomb et al, 1998) to making ethical decisions. ## Ethical ideology and software piracy

As discussed, an individual’s attitude towards and behavioural involvement in ethically questionable consumer practices are likely to be determined by his/her ethical ideology, or levels of relativism and idealism. Studies pertinent to this view include the investigation of Winter et al (2004), who examined the relationship between software piracy attitudes and consumers’ ethical ideologies. The researchers extended earlier results on idealism and relativism to unethical computer-related issues. Likewise, Suter et al (2006) advanced understanding of the copying behaviour by examining the relationship between computer users’ ethical philosophies and their judgments concerning illegal downloading activities. Recently, McMahon & Cohen (2009) conducted an exploratory study to investigate IT users’ ethical judgment and behavioural intention concerning a number of computer and Internet use behaviours.

Relating specifically to the influence of relativism, it is likely that computer users with low levels of relativism will tend to judge ethically questionable acts more critically due to their heavy emphasis on moral absolutes. They usually make their ethical judgment without regard to the specific contextual conditions that may justify the performance of these acts (Forsyth, 1980). The strong adherence to moral absolutes and the neglect of contextual considerations further imply that these computer users are likely to view an act as ethically unacceptable so long as it violates pre-established ethical standards (Bass et al, 1998).

As regards the influence of idealism, it is expected that individuals with high levels of idealism will tend to exhibit a strong desire to avoid actions that will endanger others. As such, they will be less tolerant of activities having such endangering potential. So far as software piracy is concerned, a previous investigation has revealed that Chinese people tend to agree with statements that ‘software piracy damages interests and rights of software manufacturers’ and ‘software piracy damages the software industry’. On a 7-point scale (1 ¼ ‘disagree or not important’ and 7 ¼ ‘agree or important’), their average agreement scores on these two statements were 5.99 and 5.38, respectively (Wang et al, 2005). These findings highlight that although it is generally believed that Chinese people may not realise the negative impacts of software piracy as fully as their Western counterparts do, they are still able to perceive this activity as harmful to others. This ability, in turn, suggests that Chinese computer users with high idealism are likely to be less tolerant of software piracy than those with low idealism.

In short, the above analysis suggests that if an individual’s ethical ideology is characterised by a low level of relativism and a high level of idealism (i.e., absolutist), he/she will judge ethically questionable practices as most unacceptable compared with those ascribing to other ethical ideologies. Likewise, if his/her ethical ideology is characterised by a high level of relativism and a low level of idealism (i.e., subjectivist), he/she will judge the same practices as least unacceptable compared with those ascribing to other ethical ideologies. On the basis of this, two hypotheses are offered:

H1: Among Chinese computer users adhering to different ethical ideologies (i.e., situationists, absolutists, subjectivists, and exceptionists), absolutists will exhibit the least favourable attitude towards software piracy (H1a), and be least frequently engaged in software piracy (H1b).

H2: Among Chinese computer users adhering to different ethical ideologies (i.e., situationists, absolutists, subjectivists, and exceptionists), subjectivists will exhibit the most favourable attitude towards software piracy (H2a), and be most frequently engaged in software piracy (H2b).

Although the above discussion suggests that absolutists and subjectivists are likely to exhibit the most and least favourable (attitudinal and behavioural) responses towards software piracy, respectively, the relative ethicality of situationists’ and exceptionists’ responses is far from unequivocal. Specifically, as idealism refers to one’s tendency to avoid causing harm to others, individuals with high levels of idealism should respond negatively to an unethical issue (e.g., software piracy), and vice versa. Conversely, as relativism concerns one’s tendency to reject universal moral rules, individuals with high levels of relativism should respond positively to an unethical issue, and vice versa (Barnett et al, 1994). Taken together, the above analysis suggests that the influence of idealism (ve) and relativism (þ ve) on individuals’ ethical judgment is opposite in direction.

Hence, if a person has high levels of both idealism and relativism, as in the case of a situationist, his/her resultant ethical judgment will be difficult to predict due to the interplay of two opposite ideological influences. Likewise, it will also be difficult to predict the resultant ethical judgment of an exceptionist who is low in both idealism and relativism.

Despite this difficulty, Henle et al’s (2005) investigation of the impact of ethical ideology on deviant acts in the workplace offers some insights into the issue. In the study, these researchers reported that exceptionists are more inclined to commit organisational deviance than situationists. Moreover, Barnett et al’s (1998) study on marketing professionals showed that exceptionists are more tolerant of ethically questionable marketing practices than are situationists. Tentatively, this identified difference may be due to the pure utilitarian approach exceptionists employ to reach their ethical judgments. Subscribing to the principle of ‘the greatest good for the greatest number’, exceptionists typically employ an additive evaluative process by simply accepting an action whose overall positive consequences are perceived as outweighing its overall negative consequences (Barnett et al, 1994). This implies that when compared with situationists who are high in idealism or restrained from causing harm to others, exceptionists may still be more prone to jeopardise the interests of others if they perceive their action as capable of maximising the net benefit for the majority (Marques & Azevedo-Pereira, 2008). On the basis of this analysis, the following hypothesis is offered:

H3: When compared with Chinese computer users adhering to the situationist ethical ideology type, those adhering to the exceptionist ethical ideology type will exhibit a more favourable attitude towards software piracy (H3a), and be more frequently engaged in software piracy (H3b).

## The relative importance of relativism and idealism for the Chinese

With regard to Forsyth’s two ethical ideology dimensions, relativism and idealism, previous research suggested that their relative influence on ethical judgment varies between cultures. For example, previous studies found that both South African (Singhapakdi et al, 1999) and Middle Eastern marketers (Attia et al, 1999) are more idealistic and less relativistic than their American counterparts. As for Asian countries, Singhapakdi et al (1994) reported that Thai people are significantly higher on both idealism and relativism scales than American people. Lee & Sirgy (1999) also found that Korean executives are higher on the idealism scale than their American counterparts.

Relating specifically to the Chinese, there is a general consensus that they are highly relativistic with moral principles varying according to the situations they face (e.g., Ralston et al, 1994). Chinese people’s high relativism seems to be rooted in the Taoist Doctrine of the Way, which states that: ‘A man must follow his instinct, y for there is no principle that is right in all circumstances, or any action that is wrong in all circumstances’ (Shively & Shively, 1972). Yau (1994) further argued that the Chinese practice of having children brought up in an extended or joint family setting also contributes to Chinese people’s high relativism. With such rearing practices, Chinese children have ample opportunities to be exposed to different points of views, thus allowing them to realise that circumstances have an important bearing on what course of action they should take.

As regards the idealism dimension, Redfern & Crawford (2004) contended that this dimension has a stronger prevalence in Western rather than Asian nations. Moreover, some prior empirical research demonstrated that Chinese students display less concern for others’ welfare than do their Western (mostly American) counterparts when facing an ethical dilemma (Whitcomb et al, 1998; Lin, 1999). In the business-to-business context, Tian (2007) also found that relativism rather than idealism is a significant predictor of Chinese business managers’ perception of bribery and kickbacks. On the basis of this literature, a hypothesis is proposed:

H4: Relativism rather than idealism exerts a stronger influence on Chinese computer users’ software piracy attitude and behaviour.

As mentioned, this relationship is explicitly proposed by the value-attitude-behaviour hierarchy. The following hypothesis guides the testing of this proposed relationship in the context of software piracy:

H5: Chinese computer users’ software piracy attitude is positively related to their software piracy behaviour.

## Measures

The measures used in the survey were first developed based on a thorough literature review. They were further fine-tuned in accordance with inputs from a pre-test of 30 Chinese subjects in Guangzhou and from three academics knowledgeable about the topic under investigation (i.e., expert assessment). English was initially used to develop the survey questionnaire, which was subsequently translated into Chinese. Linguistic equivalence was ensured by the back-translation technique (Bhalla & Lin, 1987). The finalised measures are described below.

## Ethical ideology

Forsyth’s (1980, p. 178) Ethics Position Questionnaire was adapted to measure respondents’ levels of relativism and idealism. As shown in Appendix A, the questionnaire comprises ten 9-point items (1 ¼ ‘completely disagree’ and 9 ¼ ‘completely agree’) for each of relativism and idealism.

## Software piracy attitude

In generating items for measuring software piracy attitude, the 10 attitude statements proposed by Wang et al (2005) were first referred to. Overall, these statements covered five aspects of software piracy:

1. infringement of intellectual property (‘software piracy infringes intellectual property’);

2. damage to the software industry and manufacturers (‘software piracy damages interests and rights of software manufacturers’; and ‘software piracy damages the software industry’);

3. good quality of pirated software (‘pirated software has similar quality as the legal version’; ‘pirated software provides similar functions as the legal version’; and ‘pirated software is as reliable as the legal software’);

4. the perceived benefit associated with software piracy (‘without pirated software, many people will not be able to enjoy listening to music’); and

5. software piracy as a wrong act and the perceived risk of punishment associated with software piracy (‘purchasing pirated software is illegal’; ‘purchasing pirated software is unethical’; and ‘there is little chance of being caught when purchasing pirated software’).

On the basis of inputs from the aforementioned pretest and expert assessment, the statement of aspect 1 (software piracy infringes intellectual property; reversely coded) was considered appropriate and thus retained. However, these inputs also suggested several modifications for the remaining statements to further improve the validity of the measure in the present research context.

First, as the pre-test revealed that the two statements of aspect 2 were similar, they were combined into one to avoid respondent fatigue (software piracy damages the interests of software developers; reversely coded). Second, the pre-test also highlighted that the three statements of aspect 3 were similar and could be combined into one. On the other hand, although these statements all focused on the comparable quality of pirated software (vs genuine software), they did not cover an important perceived advantage of pirated software, namely its (low) price advantage. On the basis of these views, a more general statement reflecting the value-for-money benefit or cost effectiveness of pirated software was thus developed to replace the original three statements (software piracy is a cost-effective means to obtain quality software).

Third, the pre-test respondents and experts also considered that the statement of aspect 4 was confined to the benefit of music downloading rather than the transfer of digital data in general. From a broader perspective, they believed that the prevalence of software piracy was attributed to its provision of a quick and economical (and often complimentary) means for computer users to acquire the needed software for either utilitarian (e.g., unauthorised copying of programmes for work or study) or hedonic (e.g., unauthorised downloading of music for entertainment) purposes. On this basis, they considered that the major benefit of software piracy, as perceived by software pirates, would relate more to facilitating the sharing of various kinds of copyright-protected digital information (broadly defined as any forms of information contained in software) among people. This view consequently led to the development of the statement without pirated software, information sharing among many people will be deterred.

Last, owing to China’s insufficient enforcement efforts and resources (International Intellectual Property Alliance, 2009), individuals engaging in software piracy there are hardly punished by law. The pre-test respondents and experts thus considered that the legal implications covered by the second and third statements of aspect 5 might not be particularly relevant to the context under investigation. Consequently, only one more general statement was employed to capture the perceived wrongness of software piracy (software piracy is wrong; reversely coded). After making these modifications, the five finalised statements (as described above), which were coded on a 7-point scale anchored with 1 ¼ ‘completely disagree’ and 7 ¼ ‘completely agree’, were then employed in the survey.

## Software piracy behaviour

According to the Software Publishers Association, software piracy refers to the unauthorised duplication of computer software and includes unauthorised: (1) copying of disks; (2) electronic downloading; (3) electronic transfer of programmes and data; and (4) purchase of pirated software (Douglas et al, 2007). In this study, the construct of software piracy behaviour was aimed at discovering how often respondents had been engaged in these four software piracy activities within the previous 6 months. The instrument used to measure this behaviour read as follows:

Please indicate, on the provided 7-point scale (1 ¼ ‘never’; 7 ¼ ‘always’), how often you were engaged in each of the following activities within the past 6 months:

1. unauthorised copy of disks;

2. unauthorised electronic downloading;

3. unauthorised electronic transfer of programmes and data;

4. unauthorised purchase of pirated software.

As respondents were likely to find it difficult to recall exactly how many times they had been engaged in these activities within the previous 6 months, the aforemen tioned perceptual measure was employed to capture their software piracy behaviour. The same measure was employed in previous investigations to operationalise such behaviour as eco-friendly purchase (Chan, 2001), food purchase (Homer & Kahle, 1988), and software piracy (Douglas et al, 2007).

In this study, a respondent’s overall score on software piracy behaviour (ranging between 1 and 7) was computed by averaging his/her scores on all four software piracy activities. One potential problem of this method is that respondents might assign high scores on some activities but low scores on the others, thereby undermining the reliability or internal consistency of this behavioural measure. However, as described in the ‘Basic statistics’ sub-section below, when the scores on these four activities were subject to the Cronbach’s reliability test, they rendered an a coefficient of 0.86 and well exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Hair et al, 1995). This suggested that the scores for the four software piracy activities were similar to one another, thereby ensuring the satisfactory reliability of the adopted measure (Nunnally, 1978).

## The control variable of social desirability bias

This study also included the Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale (form C) (Reynolds, 1982, pp. 121–123) to control for respondents’ potential social desirability bias. The scale comprised 13 true/false statements with 0 ¼ ‘false’ and 1 ¼ ‘true’ (see Appendix B). A respondent’s overall social desirability bias score (ranging between 0 and 13) was computed by summing his/her individual scores on all the statements, with a larger score denoting the presence of a stronger bias.

## Administration of the survey

A survey was conducted through door-to-door personal interviews in Guangzhou. Among all the Chinese cities, Guangzhou has been regarded as the most IT-advanced city in terms of various indicators such as broadband coverage in urban areas, household computer ownership, home broadband connection, and percentage of enterprises connected to the Internet (Hong Kong Trade Development Council, 2004).

With the assistance of a domestic research agency, a sampling frame listing all the city blocks in the urban areas of Guangzhou was first compiled. On the basis of this information and having regard to the resource constraints, two-stage area sampling (Churchill, 1996, pp. 522–525) was employed to select 30 city blocks. Within each selected city block, 20 households were then randomly selected and approached in order to solicit their participation in the survey. This sampling procedure resulted in the contact of a total of 600 households.

Within each selected household, a member who reported that he/she had regular access to computers and the Internet for the past 1 year was further randomly chosen. To encourage cooperation, potential participants were assured of anonymity and the strict academic use of the collected data. They were also offered a supermarket gift coupon of approximately US\$7 on their completion of the survey interview. This process resulted in 266 valid responses and constituted a response rate of 44%.

The present survey is confined to a major Chinese city with the most advanced IT development in the country.

Although this confinement can yield important insights into understanding how Chinese computer users with considerable exposure to computer and Internet usage perceive and engage in software piracy, the findings so derived may not necessarily be generalisable to all Chinese computer users. In particular, owing to the low computer ownership (5.4 computers/100 households) and Internet penetration rates (28.3%) of China’s rural areas (China Internet Network Information Centre, 2009; China Statistical Yearbook, 2009), the psychological and behavioural patterns of the relatively inexperienced and light computer users there may not be completely reflected by the present findings. Despite this caveat, this survey should still be reflective of those more experienced and heavy Chinese computer users who have ample opportunities to engage in software piracy. As the difference in the diffusion of new communication technologies across different Chinese regions is merely a function of time with major cities leading other localities by a few years, some of the most developed Chinese cities such as Guangzhou can serve as a barometer of changing trends in China (Zhu, 1997). On this basis, it is reasonable to believe that the findings from computer users in these cities will still be ‘generalisable to the rest of China in the near future’ (Zhu & He, 2002, p. 6).

## Analysis and results

## Sample profile

Overall, 57% of the 266 respondents were male and 40% of the respondents were unmarried. The median educational level, age, and monthly personal income of the respondents were high school completion, 25–29 years, and RMB2,001–3,000 (US\$1 ¼ RMB6.82), respectively. Approximately 70% of the respondents were working adults, 28% were students, and 2% were either retirees or unemployed. While 90% of the respondents reported that they had access to computers and the Internet at home, the others indicated that they had some form of regular access to these at work, at school, or somewhere else. On average, respondents spent 19.2 h per week on the Internet.

## Validation of measures

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted on the collected data to validate all the constructs under investigation using EQS6.1. Overall, the computed fit indexes showed an acceptable fit of the measurement model: w<sup>2</sup> ¼ 1864.99 (Po0.001); normed fit index ¼ 0.91; comparative fit index ¼ 0.94; goodnessof-fit index ¼ 0.93; and root mean square error of approximation ¼ 0.07 (Hair et al, 1995).

The CFA results further revealed that all measurement items had significant standardised factor loadings (ranging between 0.52 and 0.88) on their corresponding theoretical constructs at Po0.001. This indicated satisfactory convergent validity of all the constructs (Hair et al, 1995). To assess discriminant validity of the constructs. this study followed Fornell & Larcker's (1981)

guideline, which involved a comparison of the shared variance (i.e., squared correlation) between any pair of constructs with the average variance extracted (AVE) by the items measuring the constructs. This comparison revealed that all the shared variances were less than the corresponding AVEs, thus demonstrating adequate discriminant validity of all the constructs.

## Applicability of Forsyth’s (1980) taxonomy to the Chinese

As mentioned, cluster analysis was employed to first examine if Chinese computer users actually exhibited four different ethical ideologies under Forsyth’s (1980) taxonomy. Following the suggestions of Punj & Stewart (1983), both hierarchical and non-hierarchical clustering methods were used. Specifically, Ward’s hierarchical cluster analysis was first performed on the relativism and idealism scores to identify the optimal number of clusters. This number was determined by analysing the clustering coefficients (the within-cluster sum of squares) in the agglomeration schedule. Overall, the analysis supported a four-cluster solution as it generated the largest difference among clustering coefficients.

On the basis of the optimal number (i.e., 4) of clusters so derived, non-hierarchical K-means cluster analysis was performed to assign respondents into clusters. For further verification, three-cluster and five-cluster solutions using K-means cluster analysis were also run. Overall, the four-cluster solution was found to outperform the other two in terms of distance between cluster centres and ANOVA-based F-values. Moreover, multiple discriminant analysis for the four-cluster solution showed that 97.4% of the respondents were correctly classified into their assigned clusters. Details of the four-cluster solution are summarised in Table 4.

Table 4 shows that the four clusters corresponded roughly to Forsyth’s four ethical ideology types in terms of mean relativism and idealism scores. On the basis of their scores, clusters 1–4 were labelled as ‘situationists’, ‘absolutists’, ‘subjectivists’, and ‘exceptionists’, respectively. Their respective proportions were 40, 19, 26, and 15%. In summary, although Forsyth’s (1980) taxonomy was originally proposed in the U.S., the present findings provide empirical support for its applicability to Chinese computer users.

## Testing of H1 to H3

One-way ANOVA was employed to examine if respondents from the four clusters differed in their mean software piracy attitude scores and mean software piracy behaviour scores. Once the ANOVA test identified a significant difference among the four clusters, the posthoc Scheffe test was then performed between all possible pairs of clusters to see where the difference lay. The relevant findings are summarised in Table 5.

Overall, ANOVA showed that both the mean software piracy attitude and behaviour scores differed significantly across the four ethical ideology clusters. The post-hoc Scheffe test revealed that both subjectivists (5.78) and exceptionists (5.34) exhibited a more favourable software piracy attitude than situationists (4.74) and absolutists (4.56). Between subjectivists and exceptionists, the former were found to have a more favourable attitude than the latter.

Regarding software piracy behaviour, the post-hoc test also detected similar differences across the clusters. Specifically, it indicated that subjectivists (6.14) and exceptionists (5.57) were engaged in software piracy activities more frequently than situationists (5.09) and absolutists (4.85). Subjectivists were found to be more frequent software pirates than exceptionists.

In short, the above findings suggested that both situationists and absolutists (rather than just absolutists) exhibited similar levels of unfavourable attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy. As such, H1a and H1b concerning absolutists’ least favourable responses were not supported. On the other hand, the findings indicated that subjectivists held the most favourable attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy compared with the other clusters, thus providing support for H2a and H2b.

The more favourable software piracy attitude and more frequent software piracy behaviour of exceptionists compared with situationists also rendered support for H3. These findings echo those documented in the Western business ethics literature (Henle et al, 2005).

## Discussion

Although prior research advocated the essential influence of individual ethical beliefs on software piracy decisions (Wagner & Sanders, 2001), it failed to examine how computer users with different ethical ideologies might differ in making such decisions. By employing Forsyth’s (1980) ethical ideology taxonomy, this study represents the first attempt to investigate the differential influence of various types of ethical ideology on software piracy attitude and behaviour. This attempt offers a new perspective to explore the decision process underlying software piracy, an increasingly prevalent and economically destructive activity worldwide. Moreover, by investigating ordinary computer users in China and explicitly checking for social desirability bias, the study helps bridge the existing research gaps and furnish more valid findings to understand how Chinese computer users arrive at their software piracy decisions. On the basis of various statistical analyses of survey data, this study has generated the following implications.

## General implications

In studying Forsyth’s taxonomy, Western researchers often took its validity for granted by force-classifying respondents into the four proposed ideological types (e.g., Bass et al, 1998). Although this may be justified for investigating respondents from Western developed nations, the validity of the taxonomy is yet to be fully tested especially among subjects from a culturally and economically different setting. To fill this void, this study has employed cluster analysis to examine its applicability to Chinese computer users. Overall, the examination shows that these users can be classified into four ideological types that reasonably correspond with the taxonomy, thus rendering empirical support for its external validity.

Although the pioneering nature of this examination does not allow a direct comparison of the present findings with those derived from prior Western software piracy research, some reference to Western ethics research in other fields is still feasible and worth making. For instance, in studying ethics involved in sales management, Sivadas et al (2003) also employed cluster analysis to validate Forsyth’s taxonomy in their U.S. sample and found that the proportions of situationists, absolutists, subjectivists, and exceptionists were 21, 63, 9, and 7%, respectively. These proportions vary significantly from those derived from the present study (w<sup>2</sup> ¼ 108; d.f. ¼ 3; Po0.000). Most notably, the present Chinese sample, as shown in Table 4, has a lower proportion of absolutists (19 vs 63%) but a greater proportion of subjectivists (26 vs 9%). As absolutists are more likely to act in an ethical manner than are subjectivists, the identified difference further supports an ethical explanation for the much higher prevalence of software piracy in China than Western developed nations.

Despite the popularity of Forsyth’s taxonomy to study various ethical issues in Western developed countries, software piracy researchers there seldom investigated the influence of ethical ideology on software piracy decisions. It is hoped that the present study can remind them of the potential of invoking this long-established Western ethical theory to better understand the ethical reasoning of their home computer users. Moreover, by demonstrating the applicability of this taxonomy to computer users in China, this study provides software piracy researchers at large with a validated theory and a new direction to pursue their research endeavours there. These endeavours are of great practical significance to foreign software developers and vendors as over 70% of China’s software products are imported from overseas, and particularly the U.S. (US Commercial Service China, 2010).

In summary, by examining the relationship between ethical ideology type, and software piracy attitude and behaviour in a theoretically driven manner, this study provides empirical findings to corroborate and enrich the extant software piracy literature. The findings also help policy makers, and genuine (mostly foreign) software developers and vendors to better understand the ethical reasoning of Chinese software pirates and ultimately devise more effective measures to combat their wrongdoing. This last issue is further discussed below.

## Specific implications

Overall, three more specific implications have been derived from this study. First, the cluster analysis results reveal that situationists represent the single largest ethical ideology cluster (40%), exhibiting high levels of both relativism and idealism. This suggests that although a considerable proportion of Chinese people tend to refer to contextual factors to make their decision, they still like to consider whether their decision will have any negative impacts on others. This pragmatic yet considerate philosophy represents the most popular approach to guiding Chinese computer users’ practices.

Table 5 reveals that situationists exhibit similar levels of unfavourable attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy to those exhibited by absolutists. Although this finding does not support what has been proposed in hypothesis one above, it is consistent with that observed in a prior investigation on American sales managers’ ethical decision making (Bass et al, 1998). In that study, sales managers classified as absolutists and situationists were found to arrive at similar judgments on the three ethically questionable scenarios under investigation.

Overall, an understanding of the ethical ideologies ascribed to by the surveyed Chinese computer users has an important practical implication for authentic (mostly foreign) software developers and vendors. Specifically, although situationists are found to express similar unfavourable attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy to those expressed by absolutists, these situationally oriented computer users may eventually become more receptive to software piracy if they perceive some circumstantial factors that demand them to do so. This concern appears even more grounded when it is judged along with the finding that relativism rather than idealism exerts a much stronger influence on Chinese computer users’ software piracy attitude and behaviour (Table 6).

Among all the circumstantial factors, the wide availability and low price of pirated software in China appear to be the two most important in facilitating individuals software piracy activities (Zhang, 2008). Hence, to prevent situationists from becoming more receptive to software piracy, the Chinese government should make concerted efforts to crack down on those entities and individuals that supply pirated software either online or off-line. Owing to the lack of financial and human resources at the National Copyright Administration of China and local copyright bureaus, China has long been criticised for its ineffectiveness in enforcing legislations for protecting intellectual property rights (International Intellectual Property Alliance, 2009). To rectify this, the government needs to devote more resources and cooperate more closely with authentic software developers and vendors to enhance its enforcement competence.

As regards the low-price factor, both policy makers and practitioners should place more emphasis on the hidden ‘costs’ associated with software piracy in their relevant educational and communications programmes. These costs may include such potential detriments as the lack of follow-up technical support, computer viruses, and legal liabilities associated with the use of pirated software. Relating specifically to authentic software developers and vendors, they may also take the cost factor into account when setting prices so as to neutralise the perceived financial benefits of using pirated software. This price cut is likely to be a viable strategy for acquiring customers and reducing rates of software piracy in countries with significantly lower per capita GNP (e.g., China) (Peace et al, 2003).

Second, consistent with what has been hypothesised above, the results also reveal that among the four ethical ideology types, subjectivists exhibit the most favourable attitudinal and behavioural responses towards software piracy (Table 5). This echoes the work of Barnett et al (1998) who, in their study of the professional members of the American Marketing Association, found that those classified as subjectivists judged ethically questionable actions more leniently than those classified as situationists, absolutists, or exceptionists. Moreover, it should be noted that subjectivists in the present study account for more than one-quarter (26%) of the sample (Table 4). Given that subjectivists tend to reject universal moral rules and pay little attention to others’ interests, their substantial presence clearly highlights the seriousness of software piracy in China.

To rectify the prevalence of software piracy at the enduser level, the Chinese government, and genuine software developers and vendors are advised to focus their resources on changing the favourable software piracy attitude of the subjectivists. Although it is by no means easy to change their attitude, it is believed that proper educational initiatives can significantly contribute to individual moral growth, and consequently deter various IT-related abusive behaviours (Siponen & Vartiainen. 2004).

In order to reach and consequently educate subjectivists effectively, the policy makers and practitioners need to first find some practical ways to identify computer users who are subjectivist in nature. For example, one way to identify such users would be to examine the particular demographic characteristics of subjectivists. On the basis of some follow-up analyses of the demographic data of this study, it is observed that subjectivists are significantly younger than other clusters (at Po0.05). Specifically, the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test reveals that 85% of the subjectivists are below the age of 30, whereas the corresponding percentages of situationists, absolutists, and exceptionists are 65, 70, and 66%, respectively. These supplementary results furnish useful inputs for efficiently identifying subjectivists as the target audience for relevant antisoftware piracy campaigns.

Third, the regression analysis further reveals that relativism (compared with idealism) exerts a stronger influence on Chinese computer users’ software piracy attitude and behaviour. This stronger influence of relativism among Chinese subjects contrasts with findings derived from ethical research in a Western context. For example, when examining the respective influences of relativism and idealism on the occurrence of workplace deviance in the U.S., Henle et al (2005) found that only idealism exerted a direct effect on such deviance. This cross-national difference further corroborates Robertson et al’s (2008) contention that Chinese people are culturally more relativistic than their Western counterparts. In short, the present finding suggests that unlike their Western counterparts, culturally relativistic Chinese computer users tend to emphasise far more situational necessity than preserving others’ interests when making their software piracy decisions.

Apart from their cultural proclivity, Chinese computer users’ heavy reliance on relativism (vs idealism) to make their software piracy decisions may be due to the remoteness they perceive with regard to the impacts of software piracy. Specifically, although Chinese computer users are able to appreciate various negative impacts of software piracy on others, they may unconsciously feel less pressed to rectify these impacts as they perceive that the affected parties are mainly those involved in developing and selling authentic software rather than someone proximate to themselves (Chan & Ma, 2010). This inference is consistent with Jones’ (1991) ethical decision making theory that people will consider their act to be morally more imperative if they perceive those being adversely affected by this act as socially, culturally, psychologically, or physically closer to themselves.

From the foregoing analysis, it is thus recommended that the government, and the genuine software developers and vendors should again work closely to help Chinese computer users better recognise how their piracy may damage parties proximate to themselves. For instance, in their anti-software piracy campaigns, both the parties could place more emphasis on how domestic software employees may lose their jobs due to software piracy. Moreover, these campaigns should draw Chinese computer users’ attention to the risks that the country may suffer, such as image problems or even political sanctions at the international level if they continue their piracy. In the longer run, such education will help

Chinese computer users better appreciate the proximate losses that they or their ‘important others’ (e.g., family members, relatives, friends) may suffer due to software piracy.

## Conclusion

Software piracy is a prevalent and serious problem, especially in rapidly developing countries. However, insufficient research has been devoted to understanding this problem, particularly from the perspective of ordinary (rather than student) computer users in such countries. To remedy this deficiency, and given that ethical considerations are likely to affect decisions underlying software piracy, this study has drawn on Forsyth’s (1980) taxonomy to study how Chinese computer users’ ethical ideologies are related to their software piracy attitude and behaviour. It is hoped that the study can encourage cross-fertilisation between related subject fields, and supplement the extant literature on various IT-related abusive behaviours.

Overall, the findings of the present study reveal that Chinese computer users exhibit four different ethical ideologies roughly corresponding to Forsyth’s (1980) taxonomy.
