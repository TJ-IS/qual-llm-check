# DIGITIZATION OF MUSIC: CONSUMER ADOPTION AMIDST PIRACY, UNBUNDLING, AND REBUNDLING

Byungwan Koh Korea University Business School, Korea University, Seoul, KOREA {byungwan@korea.ac.kr}

Il-Horn Hann Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {ihann@rhsmith.umd.edu}

Srinivasan Raghunathan Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75083 U.S.A. {sraghu@utdallas.edu}

Digital music formats and the Internet as a distribution mechanism have fundamentally disrupted the music industry by altering the way music is packaged, distributed, and consumed. This disruptive innovation has come in two stages. First, it enabled music to be purchased as an individual song (digital single) or as an album (digital album) or to be enjoyed without paying for it (unlicensed digital music). More recently, music has become available as a streaming service (streaming music). Prior to these innovations, music was primarily distributed as an album using a physical medium such as the CD. Building on multi-generation diffusion models, we identify and quantify different types of concurrent demand migration in the music industry such as generational substitution, unbundling, and piracy effects in the first stage and streaming effects in the subsequent stage. Measuring the relative contributions of factors that drive each of these different migration types, we find that the introduction of licensed digital downloads (digital single and digital album) has weakened the piracy effect. Since the introduction of licensed digital downloads, the piracy effect on the demand for CDs has decreased about 15 percent every year. At the same time, unbundling, rather than piracy, has become the dominant factor in the decline of industry revenue. More recently, streaming music services such as Pandora have moved demand from digital albums to streaming music. However, demand has not yet migrated from digital singles to streaming music. The introduction of streaming music has further weakened the piracy effect by about 7 percent every year.

Keywords: Disruptive innovation, demand migration, generational substitution, unbundling, attrition of demand, multi-generation diffusion, music sales, digital music, piracy, streaming music

## Introduction

New technologies replacing old ones is ubiquitous in many industries. Products based on new technologies often have higher quality or lower cost than those based on old technologies, which induces consumers to migrate from the earlier generation products to their successors. The new technology is sometimes disruptive in the sense that it fundamentally redefines the characteristics of the industry or the market (Christensen 1997). One often-cited example of such a disruptive force in the music industry is the Internet along with digital sound recording formats (e.g., MP3) (Cellan-Jones 2013). It has fundamentally changed the way music is packaged, distributed to, and consumed by consumers. Before the proliferation of the Internet, music was distributed primarily using physical media such as LP (long play), cassette, and later CD (compact disc). The Internet, assisted by the development of digital sound recording formats, has made digital music<sup>2</sup> the consumers’ preferred choice in recent years.

While the digital sound recording formats and the Internet as a distribution mechanism may seem innocuous, it has had a dramatic impact on the music industry. First, the Internet facilitates sharing of digital content at minimal cost and provides consumers with various options to consume music without paying for it although many of those are illegal (online music piracy). Consumers download music from peer-to-peer (P2P) and Torrent networks and rip music from various online music channels. Second, it has transformed the industry from one that predominantly distributed and sold music as a bundle of songs in a single medium (i.e., album) to one that distributed music also as an individual song (i.e., single) (unbundling of music). On the supply side, the negligible marginal cost of distribution of digital music compared to the significant marginal cost of distribution for earlier music formats such as LP, cassette, and CD, has made such unbundling of music viable. On the demand side, there has been a clear preference for individual songs over albums (Amberg and Schröder 2007; Burgess 2014; Silverthorne 2009). Third, more recently, the rise of streaming services such as Pandora and Spotify has made a much wider array of artists, albums, and genres available to consumers (rebundling of music in a different form). Altogether, the digitization of music has substantially changed the way music is consumed by making it possible to obtain it without paying for it (unlicensed digital music), to download music as an album (digital album) or as an individual song (digital single), or to stream music (streaming music). A key difference between licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single)

or streaming music and unlicensed digital music is that the former is a direct revenue source for the music industry while the latter is not.

Although unlicensed digital music, licensed digital downloads, and streaming music are all distributed through the Internet, as opposed to a physical medium such as a CD, streaming music is fundamentally different from downloadable digital music (unlicensed digital music and licensed digital downloads). Further, the demand migration from CDs to downloadable digital music is of a different nature than the demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music in terms of technology and consumer behavior. The demand migration from CDs to downloadable digital music is associated with significantly improved portability of music. The emergence of the broadband Internet along with that of digital music formats and portable digital music players led to the emergence and explosion of the demand for downloading and unbundling of music, whether legally or illegally. The demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music, on the other hand, is characterized by instantaneous and ubiquitous availability of any kind of music. The increasing proliferation of Wi-Fi and wireless Internet connection propelled the emergence of streaming music and required consumers to accept the renting access to a large library of music, rather than owning a selective set of songs. Accordingly, we define the demand migration from CDs to downloadable digital music as the first stage disruption of digitization of music and the demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music as the second stage disruption of digitization of music.<sup>3</sup> For clarity and tractability, we focus on the first stage disruption of digitization of music for our base model and subsequently discuss the second stage disruption of digitization of music.

Digitization of music is noteworthy because, unlike typical migration found in many industries where one product of the successive generation replaces a product of the previous generation, the digitization of music has given rise to a phenomenon where several new products replace the old one or one new product replaces several old ones. In the first stage disruption of digitization of music, the demand for music has migrated from CDs to digital albums (which we call generational substitution), to digital singles (which we call unbundling), and to unlicensed digital music (which we call attrition from CDs). In addition, the demand for different downloadable digital music formats has been interdependent. The demand for licensed digital downloads (digital single and digital album) has been influenced by the demand for unlicensed digital music (which we call sampling) and vice versa (which we call attrition from digital albums and digital singles). In the second stage disruption of digitization of music, the demand for digital albums and/or digital singles has migrated to streaming music. Furthermore, streaming music has reduced the piracy effects of unlicensed digital music on digital albums and/or digital singles (which we call streaming effects altogether).

The factors that drive each of these migration types in each stage are unique and different. Understanding the relative contributions of these factors is of interest to both academics and practitioners. From an academic perspective, the examination of the music digitization phenomenon necessitates a new diffusion model that allows for a product migration from a single product to multiple successive products or from multiple products to a single successive product. Existing multi-generation diffusion models assume that the demand for the previous generation product migrates to a single product of the successive generation. Consequently, existing diffusion models do not adequately capture the complex demand migration patterns observed in the music industry.

For practitioners it is imperative to gain a better understanding of the sales implications that are due to the demand migration from CDs to downloadable digital music and, more recently, from downloadable digital music to streaming music. The music industry is keenly aware that the revenue generated from music sales has declined significantly with the proliferation of the Internet. Over the past 15 years, music sales have declined by more than 50 percent yet music consumption has steadily increased (Covert 2013). Although the music industry is justifiably arguing that piracy has been a major culprit for the sales decline, product design decisions such as the introduction of digital single (i.e., unbundling) might also have played a significant role. While the proliferation of various unlicensed digital music options, such as FTP (file transfer protocol) and IRC (internet relay chat), began in 1997, legitimate digital music distribution channels, such as iTunes, became available only in 2003. The music industry’s delayed introduction of licensed digital music was not due to technical reasons. Rather, it was grounded in the fear that selling individual songs could hurt revenue (Smith and Wingfield 2008). Consequently, one can argue that during the time period 1997–2003, some consumers might have migrated from CDs to unlicensed digital music not because it was free but because it was the only available digital music option. Apparently, the subsequent introduction of licensed digital downloads had more than one effect. The introduction of the digital single has reduced sales of CDs but also piracy.

The relative contributions of each of these effects, however, is not apparent. A similar development continues with the introduction of streaming music. Streaming music has reduced piracy yet it might have cannibalized sales of licensed digital downloads as well.

In this paper, we identify and model various types of demand migration in the music industry at a micro level not found in the existing literature. As noted, for clarity and tractability, we do this in two stages:

The first stage investigates the demand migration from CDs to downloadable digital music. This includes the demand migration from CDs to unlicensed digital music, to digital albums, and to digital singles as well as the demand migration from digital albums and digital singles to unlicensed digital music. We also investigate the sampling effects of unlicensed digital music on digital albums and digital singles. By quantifying each of these migration types, we answer the following questions:

What are the relative contributions of generational substitution, unbundling, and piracy effects in the demand migration from physical to downloadable digital music?

How does the introduction of licensed digital downloads affect the piracy rate?

What are the overall impacts of unbundling and piracy on the music industry?

In the second stage, we investigate the demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music. This includes the demand migration from licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single) to streaming music and the decrease in piracy effects of unlicensed digital music on licensed digital downloads due to the introduction of streaming music. This allows us to address the following questions:

How does the introduction of streaming music affect the demand for licensed digital downloads?

How does the introduction of streaming music affect piracy effects of unlicensed digital music on licensed digital downloads?

We adopt the multi-generation diffusion model approach for our analysis. One of the main advantages of using the diffusion model approach (over other empirical techniques) is that the model works well with a relatively small number of observations (Moutinho and Hutcheson 2011) and without decision variables such as price and advertising (Bass et al. 1994). It has been noted that the Bass model (Bass 1969) can be applied even when as few as four data points are available.<sup>4</sup> Furthermore, it has been shown that if the percentage changes of decision variables over time are approximately constant, then the estimation results of the Bass model without decision variables are observationally identical to those with decision variables. In the music market, CD prices were surprisingly unresponsive to piracy (Mortimer et al. 2012) and the price trends of each music format have been relatively stable.<sup>5</sup>

## Literature Review

Our study contributes to the literature on the impact of the Internet on the music industry and to the literature on diffusion models. The literature on the impact of the Internet on the music industry is extensive. Many studies (Andersen and Frenz 2010; Hong 2007, 2011; Liebowitz 2008; Michel 2006; Oberholzer-Gee and Strumpf 2007; Rob and Waldfogel 2006; Zentner 2005, 2006) in this literature focus on the impact of online file sharing on CD sales (i.e., the piracy effect). Bhattacharjee et al. (2003) examine the role of price of music and available bandwidth in the relationship between online file sharing and CD sales, while Waldfogel (2010) and Koh et al. (2014) explore how the introduction of licensed digital downloads affected this relationship. Chellappa and Chen (2009) and Dewan and Ramaprasad (2014) include licensed digital download sales in their analysis of the relationship between (aggregate) music sales and social media. Danaher et al. (2010) investigate the impact of legitimate digital distribution channels on physical sales and online piracy, but for the DVD market. The unbundling effect is not present in the DVD market. The unbundling effect in the music industry is investigated in Zhu and MacQuarrie (2003), Elberse (2010), and Danaher et al. (2014). Zhu and MacQuarrie examine the impact of unbundling on the music industry structure and Elberse examines the impact of unbundling on music sales. Danaher et al. (2014) explore optimal pricing and marketing strategies for digital music using price elasticities for individual songs and albums derived from a quasi-random experiment. However, these studies do not explore the piracy effect. As the disruptive forces in the music industry (i.e., the generational substitution, unbundling, piracy, and streaming effects) have evolved simultaneously, examining a subset of these forces, as the prior research has done, provides only a partial view of the impacts of these forces on the music industry. In contrast to prior work, the first stage analysis in this paper investigates generational substitution, unbundling, and piracy effects as well as interactions between the different downloadable digital music formats simultaneously and provides a more comprehensive and richer set of insights about the relative significance of these effects in the demand migration from physical to downloadable digital music and how different downloadable digital music formats affect each other. Further, our second stage analysis, which includes streaming music, complements and provides new insights into Aguiar and Waldfogel (2015), who investigate the impact of streaming music on music sales and piracy using the top 50 songs at Spotify.

On the theoretical side, our model contributes to the diffusion literature in the following way. We develop a multigeneration diffusion model that captures the complex demand migration in the music industry. The single-generation diffusion models approximate the bell-shaped growth of product sales using the market potential and a cumulative adoption probability, which is a function of innovation (the communication of product through mass media) and imitation (the word-of-mouth) parameters (Bass 1969; Chow 1967; Floyd 1962; Fourt and Woodlock 1960; Mansfield 1961; Rogers 1962). Several studies have extended single-generation diffusion models to multi-generation diffusion models by including generational substitution (Chu and Pan 2008; Danaher et al. 2001; Hann et al. 2016; Islam and Meade 1997; Jun and Park 1999; Kim et al. 2000; Jiang and Jain 2012; Mahajan and Muller 1996; Michalakelis et al. 2010; Norton and Bass 1987). Existing multi-generation diffusion models assume the demand from a previous generation product migrates to a single new generation product. Extending these multigeneration diffusion models and drawing inspiration from codiffusion models (Bucklin and Sengupta 1993; Dewan et al. 2010; Niculescu and Whang 2011), our model allows (1) the demand from a previous generation product to migrate to multiple new generation products (i.e., from CDs to digital albums, digital singles, and unlicensed digital music), (2) the demands from multiple previous generation products to migrate to a single new generation product (i.e., from digital albums and/or digital singles to streaming music), and (3) interaction among different new generation products (e.g., the sampling effects of unlicensed digital music on digital albums and digital singles).

## Model Development

We extend the multi-generation diffusion model introduced in Norton and Bass (1987) (the NB model) and examine the various types of demand migration in the music industry. We first briefly review the NB model. We then conceptualize the first stage disruption of digitization of music and introduce our base model. Finally, we conceptualize the second stage disruption of digitization of music and discuss how we extend our base model.

## Review of the NB Model

Let there be two successive generations, G1 and G2, of a product which are introduced in the market at time 0 and $\tau _ { 2 } >$ 0 respectively. $F _ { i } ( t )$ is the cumulative adoption probability of generation i by time t. In the NB model, the sales of generation i in time period $t , S _ { i } ( t )$ , is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} {S _ {1} (t) = m _ {1} F _ {1} (t) - \Phi (t)} \\ {S _ {2} (t) = m _ {2} F _ {2} (t - \tau_ {2}) + \Phi (t)} \end{array}
$$

where the migration, referred to as generational substitution, from G1 to G2 in time period t is defined as

$$
\Phi (t) = m _ {1} F _ {1} (t) F _ {2} (t - \tau_ {2})\tag{1}
$$

and $F _ { i } ( t )$ is derived from the following hazard rate function

$$
\frac {\partial F _ {i} (t)}{\partial t} = [ p _ {i} + q _ {i} F _ {i} (t) ] [ 1 - F _ {i} (t) ]\tag{2}
$$

with $F _ { i } ( 0 ) = 0$ . Solving equation (2) yields

$$
F _ {i} (t) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \frac {1 - e ^ {- [ p _ {i} + q _ {i} ] t}}{1 + \frac {q _ {i}}{p _ {i}} e ^ {- [ p _ {i} + q _ {i} ] t}}, & \text { if } t \geq 0 \\ 0, & \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right.
$$

$p _ { i }$ and $q _ { i }$ are referred to, respectively, as the coefficient of innovation and the coefficient of imitation for generation $i .$ $m _ { 1 }$ is the market potential (peak sales) of G1 and $m _ { 2 }$ is the increase in (or unique) market potential for G2 over and above that of G1; that is, the market potential for G2 is $m _ { 1 } +$ $m _ { 2 } .$ As G2 diffuses, more of the demand for G1 migrates to G2 and the demand for G1 becomes zero when the demand for G2 is saturated $( \mathrm { i . e . , } F _ { 2 } ( t - \tau _ { 2 } ) = 1 )$

## The First Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music: Digital Downloads

## Conceptualizing the First Stage Disruption

Since its introduction in 1983, the CD has been the dominant music recording and distribution format. Starting in 1997, when the broadband Internet (simply the Internet hereafter) access started to become mainstream, however, digital music began to spread on the Internet, and in 2003, the music industry started selling digital music through online channels such as the iTunes music store (often simply called iTunes). We note that while the proliferation of various unlicensed digital music options began in 1997,<sup>6</sup> digital music for purchase (i.e., licensed digital downloads) became available only in 2003. That is, during the time period 1997–2003, unlicensed digital music was the only available digital music option and there was no legitimate way to purchase digital music. When the music industry started selling licensed digital music, it offered digital music in two formats: digital album, which is a bundle of songs similar to a CD, and digital single, which is an individual song. Accordingly, the demand for music has migrated in different ways, as illustrated in Figure 1.

In Figure 1, the arrows labeled “a,” “b,” and “c” represent three different types of migration from the old (physical) generation music format to the new (digital) generation music formats. Each of these migration types is different in character.

(a) Although the initial sound quality of digital music was inferior to that of CDs, there is now little difference between the two (Atkinson 2008). Further, downloadable digital music provides several benefits over the CD. A consumer can instantly get (download) digital music from online stores, carry more songs in a smaller device, and play them on a number of different devices. For such reasons, a part of the demand for CDs has migrated to digital albums. This migration can be characterized as generational substation, which is similar to that defined in the multi-generation diffusion literature (Chu and Pan 2008; Danaher et al. 2001; Hann et al. 2016; Islam and Meade 1997; Jiang and Jain 2012; Jun and Park 1999; Kim et al. 2000; Mahajan and Muller 1996; Michalakelis et al. 2010; Norton and Bass 1987).

Figure 1. The First Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music

(b) Downloadable digital music distribution channels such as iTunes allow consumers to purchase an individual song, as opposed to an album, which is a bundle of several songs. It is commonly known that a significant portion of consumers in the music market prefer to consume only a subset of the songs that are bundled as part of an album (Elberse 2010) and some of these consumers may hesitate to buy an entire album for the sake of consuming only one or two songs they like. According to a survey conducted by Amberg and Schröder (2007), 69.4 percent of interviewed persons indeed preferred to buy an individual song over buying an entire album. Due to this unbundling, in addition to the benefits of downloadable digital music discussed in (a), another part of demand for CDs has migrated to digital singles. We call this migration from CDs to digital singles unbundling.

(c) The remaining part of demand for CDs has migrated to unlicensed digital music because, in addition to the benefits of downloadable digital music we discussed in (a) and (b), for some consumers, it is relatively “free.”

Although there are some cognitive and annoyance costs associated with consuming unlicensed digital music (Danaher et al. 2010), a direct monetary payment is not required to consume unlicensed digital music and, therefore, some consumers find unlicensed digital music more attractive than CDs as well as digital albums and digital singles. As unlicensed digital music is not a direct revenue source for the music industry, we call the migration from CDs to unlicensed digital music attrition from CDs.

Unlicensed digital music has affected the demand for licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single) in two different ways: migration (attrition) and sampling. The arrows labeled “d1” and “d2” represent the attrition of demand from licensed digital downloads to unlicensed digital music and the arrows labeled “e1” and “e2” represent the sampling effects of unlicensed digital music on licensed digital downloads.

(d) Unlicensed digital music has affected the demand for licensed digital downloads in a similar way as it has affected the demand for CDs. As discussed in (c), due to the “free-of-charge” nature, some consumers prefer to consume unlicensed digital music over licensed digital downloads. Accordingly, a part of the demand for licensed digital downloads has migrated to unlicensed digital music. For the same reason given in (c), we call this migration from licensed digital downloads to unlicensed digital music attrition from digital albums (d1) and attrition from digital singles (d2).<sup>7</sup>

(e) As shown in Andersen and Frenz (2010), some consumers consume unlicensed digital music to sample it before they make purchase decisions and may eventually purchase licensed digital downloads of some of those songs they sampled; that is, a part of demand for licensed digital downloads is due to the cross-format imitation of unlicensed digital music. Consistent with the literature, we call this cross-format imitation of unlicensed digital music the sampling effect on digital albums (e1) and sampling effect on digital singles (e2). We note that while migration is the substitution, the sampling is the duplicate consumption of the same songs.

In summary, in the music industry, multiple types of migration—from CDs to three different formats of downloadable digital music as well as from licensed digital downloads to unlicensed digital music—and sampling have occurred simultaneously. The factor that drives each of these migration types is unique and different. The generation effect has induced a part of the demand for CDs to migrate to digital albums, the unbundling effect (on top of the generation effect) has induced another part of the demand for CDs to migrate to digital singles, and the piracy effect (on top of the generation and unbundling effects) has induced the remaining demand for CDs to migrate to unlicensed digital music. Similarly, the piracy effect has induced some demand for digital albums and digital singles to migrate to unlicensed digital music, yet at the same time unlicensed digital music has stimulated the demand for digital albums and digital singles through the sampling effect. Because these distinctions in types of migration, which have a significant impact on the industry’s revenue, are not considered in existing multi-generation models, we formulate a diffusion model that captures complex dynamics of demand migration in the music industry.

## Modeling the First Stage Disruption

We extend the NB model to quantify different types of demand migration in the first stage disruption of digitization of music. We use subscripts CD, DA, DS, and UM to denote, respectively, compact disc, digital album, digital single, and unlicensed digital music. CD represents the old (physical) generation music format and DA, DS, and UM represent the new (digital) generation music formats. We adjust the timeline such that CD is introduced at time zero. Licensed digital downloads—digital albums and digital singles—are introduced at time $\tau _ { L M }$ and unlicensed digital music is introduced at time $\tau _ { U M } < \tau _ { L M } .$ Building on the NB model, we write the demand (measured by units shipped) for each music format in time period $t \mathrm { a s } ^ { 8 }$

$$
S _ {C D} (t) = m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) - \Phi_ {G S} (t) - \Phi_ {U B} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t)\tag{3}
$$

$$
S _ {D A} (t) = m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \Phi_ {G S} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t)\tag{4}
$$

$$
S _ {D S} (t) = m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \beta_ {D S} (t) \Phi_ {U B} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t)\tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} S _ {U M} (t) = m _ {U M} F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) + \beta_ {U M} \Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t) + \\ \beta_ {U M} \Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) + \Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

$m _ { C D }$ is the market potential for CD and $m _ { D A } , m _ { D S } ,$ and $m _ { U M } ,$ respectively, are the increase in (or unique) market potential for digital albums, digital singles, and unlicensed digital music. The migration of demand from CDs to digital albums is measured by $\Phi _ { G S } ( t )$ and it quantifies the generational substitution. Analogously, the unbundling and attrition from CDs are quantified by $\Phi _ { U B } ( t )$ and $\Phi _ { A C D } ( t )$ respectively. $\Phi _ { G S } ( t )$ $+ \Phi _ { U B } ( t ) + \Phi _ { A C D } ( t )$ is the overall decrease in demand for CDs due to the first stage disruption of digitization of music. $\Phi _ { A D A } ( t )$ and $\Phi _ { A D S } ( t )$ measure the attrition from digital albums and the attrition from digital singles respectively.

$\beta _ { D S } ( t )$ is the set of parameters that accounts for the differential impact of album and single formats on the demand migration from CDs to digital singles. Our model allows one unit demand for CDs to migrate to $\beta _ { D S } ( t )$ units demand for digital singles. As a CD album has on average 12 songs,<sup>9</sup> if unbundling has not affected the consumption behavior of consumers we would likely have $\beta _ { D S } ( t ) = 1 2 ;$ otherwise, we would have $\beta _ { D S } ( t ) < 1 2$ . Further, as the price of a digital single relative to that of a CD could have affected the consumption behaviors of consumers (i.e., as the price of a digital single relative to that of a CD goes down (up), consumers might purchase more (less) units of digital singles), in order to control for this price effect, we define $\beta _ { D S } ( t )$ as

$$
\beta_ {D S} (t) = b _ {D S} \left[ 1 - \log \left[ \frac {\nu_ {D S} (t)}{\nu_ {C D} (t)} \right] \right]\tag{7}
$$

where ${ \boldsymbol { \upsilon } } _ { i } ( t )$ is the real price of music format i in time period $t . ^ { 1 0 }$

Similarly, $\beta _ { U M }$ is the parameter that accounts for the differential impact of album and single formats on the demand migration from CDs and digital albums to unlicensed digital music. We assume the price of unlicensed digital music is sufficiently low compared to that of a CD and a digital album, and hence, consumers who migrate from CDs or digital albums to unlicensed digital music consume all 12 songs bundled in a CD album or a digital album $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \beta _ { U M } = 1 2 )$ . We discuss the case in which the price of unlicensed digital music is not sufficiently low, and thereby, $\beta _ { U M }$ is less than 12 in the section “Robustness Checks.”

We model the demand migration from CDs to each digital music format in time period t as

$$
\Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t) = e ^ {- \gamma \bullet I _ {L M} (t)} m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})\tag{8}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {G S} (t) = [ 1 - \alpha (t) ] [ 1 - e ^ {- \gamma \bullet I _ {L M} (t)} ] m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M})\tag{9}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {U B} (t) = \alpha (t) \left[ 1 - e ^ {- \gamma \cdot I _ {L M} (t)} \right] m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M})\tag{10}
$$

where $I _ { L M } ( t ) = t - \tau _ { L M } \mathrm { i f } t \geq \tau _ { L M }$ and 0 otherwise. Until licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single) became available $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \tau _ { U M } \leq t < \tau _ { L M } )$ , unlicensed digital music had been the only digital music option for consumers; hence, for $\tau _ { U M } \leq t < \tau _ { L M } ,$ we have $\Phi _ { G S } ( t ) = \Phi _ { U B } ( t ) = 0$ and $\Phi _ { A C D } ( t ) =$ $m _ { C D } F _ { C D } ( t ) F _ { U M } ( t - \tau _ { U M } )$ , which is similar to the migration defined in the NB model (equation (1)). After the introduction of licensed digital downloads $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t \ge \tau _ { L M } )$ , some demand for CDs that would have migrated to unlicensed digital music in the absence of licensed digital downloads has migrated to digital albums or digital singles instead. Hence, we hypothesize and later empirically test that for $t \ge \tau _ { L M }$ the attrition from CDs $( \Phi _ { A C D } ( t ) )$ has decreased over time at the rate of $\cdot _ { \gamma , }$ and a part of this decrease (weighted $a ( t )$ fraction) has switched to unbundling $( \Phi _ { U B } ( t ) )$ and the remaining has switched to generational substitution $( \Phi _ { G S } ( t ) )$ . In order to control for the price effect on the relative preference for digital singles over digital albums, we define α(t) as

$$
\alpha (t) = a \left[ 1 - \log \left[ \frac {v _ {D S} (t)}{v _ {D A} (t)} \right] \right]\tag{11}
$$

Following the NB model (equation (1)), we model the attrition from digital albums and digital singles as

$$
\Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) = m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})\tag{12}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) = m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})\tag{13}
$$

Finally, $F _ { i } ( t )$ are derived from the following hazard rate functions

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \frac {\partial F _ {i} (t)}{\partial t} = & \left[ p _ {i} + q _ {i} F _ {i} (t) \right] \left[ 1 - F _ {i} (t) \right] \\ & \text { for } i = C D \text { and } U M \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \frac {\partial F _ {i} (t)}{\partial t} = \left[ p _ {i} + q _ {i} F _ {i} (t) + \lambda_ {i} F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) \right] \\ \left[ 1 - F _ {i} (t) \right] \text { for } i = D A \text { and } D S \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

with $F _ { i } ( 0 )$ for all i. The hazard rate functions for CDs and unlicensed digital music (equation (14)) are identical to that in the NB model (equation (2)). The hazard rate functions for digital albums and digital singles (equation (15)) have an additional term $\lambda _ { i } F _ { U M } ( t - \tau _ { U M } )$ that captures the cross-format imitation (the sampling effect) of unlicensed digital music. Equation (15) is similar to the hazard rate function used in the co-diffusion literature (e.g., equation (1) in Dewan et al. (2010)).

A consumer generally does not $\mathrm { \Delta ^ { * } p a y ^ { * } }$ to consume unlicensed digital music; hence, it is difficult to track the transactions of unlicensed digital music and a direct measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music $( S _ { U M } ( t ) )$ is not readily available. Prior studies, therefore, have used a proxy measure such as the Internet penetration (Bender and Wang 2009; Liebowitz 2008;Zentner 2005), Internet use or access (Hong 2007, 2011; Zentner 2006), and computer ownership (Michel 2006). Following this prior literature, we use Internet penetration as a proxy measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music assuming

$$
S _ {U M} (t) = \delta N _ {I} (t)\tag{16}
$$

where N (t) is the number of Internet subscribers in time period t. δ is the parameter that captures the average per Internet subscriber consumption of unlicensed digital music. In order to check the robustness of using the Internet penetration as a proxy measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music, we also estimate the model using other proxy measures—Internet penetration weighted by the piracy rate and the number of consumer complaints received by the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)—and discuss the results later in the section “Robustness Checks.”

In summary, our base model is given by equations (3)–(16) collectively. This base model reduces to the NB model with three successive generations if we adjust the data into the album-level (by defining the demand for licensed digital downloads in time period t as $S _ { L M } \left( t \right) = S _ { D A } \left( t \right) + \frac { S _ { D S } \left( t \right) } { 1 2 } \mathrm { \Omega } )$ and consider licensed digital downloads as a successive generation of unlicensed digital music. Please note that in our base model, digital albums, digital singles, and unlicensed digital music are three different (downloadable digital) music formats of the same generation. By modeling in this way, we are able to identify and quantify various types of demand migration and sampling effects as well as the overall impact of the unbundling and piracy on the music industry.<sup>11</sup>

## The Second Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music: Streaming

## Conceptualizing the Second Stage Disruption

Since Pandora launched its streaming service in 2005, streaming music has become a big trend in the music industry. Following Pandora, Spotify was introduced in the U.S. market in 2011 and Apple launched its streaming service, Apple Music, in 2015. In 2015, for the first time, the largest portion portion (34.3 percent) of the industry revenue came from streaming.<sup>12</sup> In this section, we extend our base model by incorporating the demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music, as shown in Figure 2.

The second stage disruption of digitization of music includes (a) the demand migration from licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single) to streaming music (solid arrows labeled “f1” and “f2”) and (b) the decrease in attrition from licensed digital downloads to unlicensed digital music due to the introduction of streaming music (dotted arrows labeled “f1” and “f2”). The salient features of the extension are as follow.

(a) Although streaming is an advanced way of delivering digital content over the Internet compared to downloading, the benefits of streaming music over licensed digital downloads (digital album and digital single) are unclear. A streaming music subscriber has access to an almost unlimited library over the Internet, yet she cannot own songs. She needs to be connected to the Internet to play streaming music. Some streaming music services allow offline listening for paid subscribers but the number of songs that can be played offline is limited and subscribers lose the access to these songs when their subscription expires. Further, although some streaming music services allow on-demand streaming, they allow only for paid subscribers. In many cases, free users are not allowed to choose songs that they want to play. Instead, streaming music services recommend songs based on each subscriber’s selection of songs or shuffle the playlist. Studies also have found mixed results about the impact of streaming music on music sales. While Aguiar and Waldfogel (2015) and Mulligan (2014) show that streaming music cannibalizes music sales, Page (2013) shows that it does not. Accordingly, we hypothesize and empirically test that there is demand migration from digital albums and/or digital singles to streaming music (solid arrows “f1” and “f2”).

(b) Although both streaming music and unlicensed digital music are mainly free,<sup>13</sup> cognitive and annoyance costs associated with consuming unlicensed digital music for some consumers are higher as consuming unlicensed digital music is illegal. Consequently, as shown in recent studies (Aguiar and Waldfogel 2015; Page 2013), streaming music has reduced the demand for unlicensed digital music; that is, a part of the demand for digital albums and digital singles that would have migrated to unlicensed digital music in the absence of streaming music have migrated to streaming music instead (dotted arrows “f1” and “f2”).

## Modeling the Second Stage Disruption

Incorporating two types of demand migration from downloadable digital music to streaming music discussed in the previous subsection, we extend our base model (equations (3)–(16)) in the following way: We use subscript SM to denote streaming music. We first revise the equations for the demand for digital albums and digital singles in time period t (equations (4) and (5)) as

$$
\begin{array}{c} S _ {D A} (t) = m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \Phi_ {G S} (t) \\ - \Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) - \Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) \end{array}\tag{17}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} S _ {D S} (t) = m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \beta_ {D S} (t) \Phi_ {U B} (t) \\ - \Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) - \Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) \end{array}\tag{18}
$$

and write the equation for the demand for streaming music in time period t as

$$
\begin{array}{c} S _ {S M} (t) = m _ {S M} F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M}) + \zeta_ {D A} \Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) \\ + \zeta_ {D S} \Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) \end{array}\tag{19}
$$

where $\tau _ { S M }$ is the time at which streaming music is introduced and $m _ { S M }$ is the increase in (or unique) demand for streaming music. $F _ { S M } ( t )$ is derived from the same hazard rate function defined for CD and unlicensed digital music (equation (14)). $\Phi _ { S D A } ( t ) \mathrm { a n d } \Phi _ { S D s } ( t )$ quantify, respectively, the streaming effects on digital albums and on digital singles. While the demand for downloadable digital music is measured by the number of downloads, the demand for streaming music is measured by the number of streams (plays). $\zeta _ { i } , \ i \in \{ D A , D S \}$ are parameters that account for this differential impact of downloading and streaming formats on the demand migration from licensed digital downloads to streaming music; that is, our model allows one unit demand for downloadable digital music to convert to $\zeta _ { i }$ units demand for streaming music.

Incorporating the impact of streaming music on the attrition from digital albums and digital singles, we revise the equations for the attrition from digital albums and digital singles (equations (12) and (13)) as

$$
\Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) = e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})\tag{20}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) = e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})\tag{21}
$$

and model the streaming effects on digital albums and digital singles as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) & = \left[ 1 - e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} \right] m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) \\ & + \Phi_ {G S} (t) F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M}) \end{array} \tag {22}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) & = \left[ 1 - e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} \right] m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) \\ & + \beta_ {D S} (t) \Phi_ {U B} (t) F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M}) \end{array}\tag{23}
$$

where $I _ { S M } ( t ) = t - \tau _ { S M } \mathrm { i f } t \geq \tau _ { S M }$ and 0 otherwise. For $t < \tau _ { S M } ,$ $\Phi _ { A D A } ( t )$ and $\Phi _ { A D S } ( t )$ are identical to those defined in equations (12) and (13). Analogous to how we modeled the impact of licensed digital music on the attrition from CDs, we hypothesize and later empirically test that for $t \ge \tau _ { S M } , \Phi _ { A D A } ( t )$ and $\Phi _ { A D S } ( t )$ have decreased over time at the rate of η and these decreases have switched to streaming music. All other aspects of the model remain the same as specified in equations (3), (6)–(11), and (14)–(16). We summarize our extended model which includes streaming effects in the Appendix F.

## Data

As a measure of demand for CDs, digital albums, and digital singles, we collect the annual units shipped for these three music formats for the time period 1982–2014 from RIAA (Recording Industry Association of America) U.S. Music Industry Shipment and Revenue Statistics. As the first CD album was released in 1983, the demand for CDs in 1982 is set to zero. Further, although the licensed digital music distribution channels (e.g., iTunes) were introduced in the U.S. market in 2003, RIAA began to report digital album and digital single sales from 2004. Assuming the sales of licensed digital downloads in 2003 were negligible, we set the demand for digital albums and digital singles in the time period 1982– 2003 to zero. We also collect the list prices of CDs, digital albums, and digital singles for each year for the time period 1982–2014 from RIAA U.S. Music Industry Shipment and Revenue Statistics and convert it to real prices using the annual average consumer price index using 1982–1984 as the base.

As a proxy measure of demand for unlicensed digital music, we collect the number of (broadband) Internet subscribers in the United States from the World Bank Databank on a yearly basis for the time period 1998–2014.<sup>14</sup> Although the (broadband) Internet connection became available in the United States in 1997, World Bank Databank began to report the number of (broadband) Internet subscribers from 1998. Assuming the number of (broadband) Internet subscribers in 1997 was negligible, we set the demand for unlicensed digital music in the time period 1982–1997 to zero. We show the time series of our main variables (units shipped of CDs, digital albums, and digital singles and Internet penetration) in Figure 3.

Our dataset includes other categories of data that are not used for the estimation of our base (main) model. As instruments for GMM estimation, we collect the units shipped of other physical music (other than CD such as LP, cassette, and DVD music) and mobile music (e.g., ringtones and ringback tones, which are in general not full-length songs) from RIAA U.S. Music Industry Shipment and Revenue Statistics. For the estimation of our extended model, we collect the listener hours from Pandora’s annual report. Finally, in order to check the robustness of our estimation results to alternative proxy measures for the demand for unlicensed digital music, we collect the piracy rate from BSA (The Software Alliance) Software Piracy Study and the number of consumer complaints received by the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) from IC3’s Internet Crime Report. Some of these data have missing observations. We interpolate missing observations using a linear time trend. Details about the interpolation of missing observations are available in the Appendix D.

## Model Estimation and Results

## First Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music: Digital Downloads

In Norton and Bass (1987), the coefficients of innovation and imitation are assumed to be constant across generations (i.e., p = p and $q _ { i } \neq q$ for all i). However, Islam and Meade (1997) and Danaher et al. (2001) show that the assumption of constant imitation coefficient can be rejected and the recent multi-generation diffusion literature (Jiang and Jain 2012) assumes that while the coefficient of innovation is constant, the coefficient of imitation differs across generations. Accordingly, for the estimation of our base model we assume $p _ { { C D } } = p _ { { D A } } = p _ { { D S } } \neq p _ { { U M } }$ and $q _ { i } ~ \neq q$ for all $i \in \{ C D , D A , D S ,$ UM}. Since we use a proxy measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music, we allow the coefficient of innovation for unlicensed digital music to be different from that for other music formats. Further, following Van den Bulte and Joshi (2007), we impose the constraints that the coefficients of innovation and imitation are nonnegative.

Unfortunately, we cannot estimate both $m _ { U M }$ (in equation (6)) and δ (in equation (16)) on one model. Hence, for our base model, we assume $m _ { U M } = 0$ and later relax this assumption and check the robustness of our results. Since $m _ { U M } = 0$ implies that there is no unique demand for unlicensed digital music (i.e., the entire demand for unlicensed digital music has migrated from CDs), this assumption allows us to capture the upper limit of the piracy effect of unlicensed digital music on the demand for CDs. We discuss the case in which $m _ { U M } \neq 0$ in the section on “Robustness Checks.”

As there is no closed form solution for the system of differential equations given by equations (14) and (15), we numerically solve it while we estimate equations (3)–(16) simultaneously using nonlinear seemingly unrelated regression (NLSUR) at the same time. This estimation technique is similar to the one used in Van den Bulte and Joshi. Seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) has been used in prior diffusion literature (Dewan et al. 2010; Libai et al. 2009) and it has been shown that SUR can be substantially more efficient than ordinary least square (OLS) regression when estimating multiple time-series equations simultaneously (Creel and Farell 1996).<sup>15</sup> Further, we use heteroscedasticity-consistent estimates as White’s general test for heteroscedasticity rejects the null hypothesis of homoscedasticity (White 1980).

Generalized method of moments (GMM) estimation is another method for improving efficiency when heteroscedasticity is present in the model (Wooldridge 2001).<sup>16</sup> Hence, to check the robustness of our results, we also estimate the model using nonlinear system GMM (simply GMM hereafter) while we numerically solve equations (14) and (15) at the same time. Following Menon and Kohli (2013) and Suarez et al. (2013), we use lagged dependent variables as well as non-GMM exogenous variables as instruments. A constant term is also included in the instrument set. For non-GMM exogenous variables, we use two variables that are approximately correlated with music sales yet exogenous: units shipped of other physical music and mobile music. The validity of instruments is tested using Hansen’s J test of overidentification (Hansen 1982).

The Marquardt-Levenberg method is used in NLSUR estimation and the Gauss-Newton method is used in GMM estimation. In both estimations, 0.001 is used as a convergence cut-off value. We use a combination of grid search and iterative process for starting values in NLSUR estimation and the parameter estimates we obtained from NLSUR estimation as starting values in GMM estimation. We present the estimation results and model fit from NLSUR along with GMM estimations of our base model in Table 1.

The adjusted R-squares from both NLSUR and GMM estimations are noticeably high and the estimation results from NLSUR and GMM are not significantly different. Further, all parameter estimates (except imitation coefficient for digital singles $( q _ { D S } )$ and the parameter that captures the sampling effect on digital albums $( \lambda _ { D A } ) )$ are statistically significant. The insignificant Hansen’s J statistic suggests that the instruments for GMM estimation are valid. These suggest that there is a strong overall statistical support for our model to identify and quantify the different types of demand migration and the sampling effects in the music industry.

A detailed examination of the model parameters provided in Table 1 reveals the following observations. For brevity and clarity, we use the estimation results from NLSUR to present our analysis hereafter.

The effect of the introduction of licensed digital downloads: It is estimated that licensed digital downloads increases, the size of the music market (in terms of units shipped). The increase in (or unique) market potential for digital albums $( m _ { D A } )$ and digital singles $( m _ { D S } )$ respectively are estimated to be 0.6 billion units and 0.9 billion units. It is also estimated that the attrition from CDs has diminished by about 15 percent $( \gamma = 0 . 1 4 5 2 )$ every year since licensed digital downloads were introduced. This suggests that the introduction of licensed digital downloads has converted some non-paying consumers to paying consumers in the music market.

The sampling effects: While the coefficient for the sampling effect on digital singles $( \lambda _ { D S } )$ is significant, that on digital albums $( \lambda _ { D A } )$ is not. Further, the imitation coefficient for digital singles $( q _ { D S } )$ is bounded by the nonnegative constraint, suggesting that it is not significant. These results suggest that while the diffusion of music in an album format (digital album) is likely driven by the imitation (word-of-mouth) effect, the diffusion of music in an individual song format (digital single) is likely driven by the sampling effect.

The unbundling effects: The average real prices of digital singles and CDs in the time period 2004–2014 were, respectively, \$0.50 and \$6.60. Hence, from equation (7), $b _ { D S }$ suggests that given the prices of digital singles and CDs, during the time period 2004–2014, on average one unit demand for CDs migrated to about 2.9 units demand for digital singles, that is, consumers who migrated from CDs to digital singles purchased, on average, about 2.9 songs among 12 songs bundled in a CD album (Figure 4(a)). This estimate is somewhat consistent with that in Elberse (2010) that estimates an overall reduction of onethird of the music sales due to the unbundling.

The average real price of digital albums in the time period 2004–2014 was \$4.76. Hence, from equation (11), a = 0.1968 suggests that given the prices of digital singles and digital albums, during the time period 2004– 2014, on average, 91 percent of the demand that migrated from CDs to licensed digital downloads chose digital singles and the remaining 9 percent chose digital albums (Figure 4(b)). Our model also suggests, perhaps a little surprisingly, that even if there is no price difference between digital singles and digital albums $( \mathrm { i } . { \bf e } . , \nu _ { D S } ( t ) =$ $\nu _ { D A } ( t ) )$ and both diffuse at the same rate (i.e., $F _ { D S } ( t ) =$ $F _ { D A } ( t ) )$ , a rather significant 20 percent of those that migrate from CDs to licensed digital downloads might choose digital singles; that is, like many information goods with the no free disposal property (Chellappa and Mehra 2013; Chellappa and Shivendu 2010; Dewan and Freimer 2003), the utility of a consumer from consuming music might not be monotonic and some songs could incur disutility.

## Quantification of Migration and the Impact of the First Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music on the Music Industry

In this section, we examine the impact of the digitization of music on the music industry by quantifying various types of demand migration that occurred in the first stage disruption. Using parameter estimates (reported in Table 1) and equations (8)–(10), (12), and (13), we compute the generational substitution $( \Phi _ { G S } ( t ) )$ unbundling $( \Phi _ { U B } ( t ) )$ attrition from CDs $( \Phi _ { A C D } ( t ) )$ , attrition from digital albums $( \Phi _ { A D A } ( t ) )$ , and attrition from digital singles $( \Phi _ { A D S } ( t ) )$ in time period t. We report each of these demand migration types in Figures 5 and 6.

A detailed examination of Figures 5 and 6 reveal the following observations:

(1) Over the 1998–2014 period, an estimated 7.26 billion units of demand for CDs migrated to downloadable digital music: digital albums, digital singles, and unlicensed digital music (Figure 5). About 5 percent (0.4 billion units) of this migration occurred when unlicensed digital music was the only digital music option (i.e., during the time period 1998–2003). Among the migration that occurred after licensed digital downloads were introduced, 45 percent (3.1 billion units) was due to the piracy effect (i.e., migration to unlicensed digital music), 47 percent (3.2 billion units) was due to the unbundling effect (i.e., migration to digital singles), and 8 percent (0.6 billion units) was due to the generation effect (i.e., migration to digital albums). This suggests that the piracy and unbundling effects dominated the generation effect in the music industry.

(2) The attrition from CDs has steadily decreased whereas the unbundling and generational substitution have increased since licensed digital downloads were introduced (Figure 5). The rate of increase was clearly higher for the unbundling than the generational substitution. In 2014, 58 percent of demand migration from CDs was to digital singles, 18 percent was to digital albums, and 24 percent was to unlicensed digital music. This indicates that the music industry’s strategy of providing consumers with an option to “purchase” digital music, especially an individual digital song (digital single), was successful in stemming the piracy effect.

(3) While the attrition from CDs has decreased since the introduction of licensed digital downloads (Figure 5), the attrition from licensed digital downloads has increased (Figure 6). Over the 2004–2014 period, the attrition was 1.15 billion units of the demand for digital albums and 5.87 billion units of demand for digital singles.

In 2014, compared to 2013, the attrition from digital albums increased by 21 percent yet the attrition from digital singles increased only by 1.7 percent. In summary, the comparison of the different types of demand migration in the music industry suggests that while the demand migration from CDs to unlicensed digital music was substantial, it is now the unbundling effect that contributes most to the demand migration from physical to digital music. However, the piracy effect still remains a challenge for the music industry. Instead of luring demand away from CDs, unlicensed digital music has been taking demand away from licensed digital downloads. The piracy effect has been emerging as a significantly bigger challenge for digital albums.

## Robustness Checks

Due to the “illegal” nature of piracy, limited data about the price and consumption of unlicensed digital music are available. Hence, for the estimation of our base model, we had to make two assumptions: the price of unlicensed digital music is sufficiently low (and thereby, $\beta _ { U M } = 1 2 )$ and the entire demand for unlicensed digital music comes from CDs $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } _ { . , } m _ { U M } = 0 )$ . Further, we use the Internet penetration as a proxy measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music by assuming each Internet subscriber consumes on average a constant amount of unlicensed digital music.

Internet penetration is widely used as a proxy measure for online piracy (Bender and Wang 2009; Liebowitz 2008; Smith and Telang 2010; Zentner 2005). Further, the estimate of the demand for unlicensed digital music we obtained using Internet penetration as a proxy measure and aforementioned assumptions is somewhat similar to the one made by RIAA. RIAA estimates that during the time period 2004–2009, approximately 30 billion songs were illegally downloaded (Owsinski 2013). In our estimation, during the same time period, about 26 billion units of unlicensed digital music were consumed. Nevertheless, in order to further check the robustness of our estimation results, in this section, we discuss the sensitivity of our estimation results presented earlier to the assumptions we made and the proxy measure we used.

The Price of Unlicensed Digital Music: For the estimation of our base model, we assumed the price of unlicensed digital music is sufficiently low and thereby $\beta _ { U M } = 1 2 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , consumers who migrate from either CDs or digital albums to unlicensed digital music consume all 12 songs bundled in a CD album or a digital album). However, as there are some cognitive and annoyance costs associated with consuming unlicensed digital music (Danaher at al. 2010), the effective price of unlicensed digital music may not be sufficiently low and $\beta _ { U M }$ could be less than 12. In order to check the robustness of our results to the assumption that $\beta _ { U M } = 1 2$ , we estimate the model for the cases $\beta _ { U M } = 1 0 $ and $\beta _ { U M } = 8$ . The estimation results (reported in Appendix E, Table E1) show that the estimates for most parameters are very consistent across different values of $\beta _ { U M } .$ One exception might be $\delta ,$ which measures per Internet subscriber consumption of unlicensed digital music. As expected, δ decreases in $\beta _ { U M } .$ However, altering the assumption about the value of $\beta _ { U M }$ does not qualitatively change any of our findings reported in the previous sections.

The Market Potential for Unlicensed Digital Music: For a technical reason, for the estimation of our base model, we assumed that there is no unique demand for unlicensed digital music $( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . , m _ { U M } = 0 )$ Since this assumption implies that the entire demand for unlicensed digital music has come from CDs, it allows us to capture the upper limit of the piracy effect on the demand for CDs. Nevertheless, this is a strong assumption. In order to test the robustness of our estimation results to the assumption that $m _ { U M } = 0$ , we estimate the model using an alternative assumption: unlicensed digital music has demand over and above the demand that migrated from CDs. In particular, we estimate the model assuming the unique market potential for unlicensed digital music is 50 percent of that for CDs (i.e., $m _ { U M } = 0 . 5 m _ { C D } )$ and 100 percent of that for CDs $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , m _ { U M } = m _ { C D } )$ The estimation results (reported in Appendix E, Table E2) show that the parameter estimates are noticeably consistent across different assumptions for $m _ { U M } ,$ suggesting that the results are rather insensitive to the value of $m _ { U M } .$ As expected δ increases in $m _ { U M }$ yet the change is relatively minimal.

Other Proxy Measures for the Demand for Unlicensed Digital Music: As a direct measure of the demand for unlicensed digital music is not readily available, for the estimation of our base model, we used Internet penetration as a proxy measure assuming each and every Internet subscriber consumes on average δ units of unlicensed digital music. However, not all Internet subscribers might have consumed unlicensed digital music and the fraction of Internet subscribers that consumed unlicensed digital music might have changed over time. Accordingly, relaxing the assumption, we revise equation (6) as $S _ { U M } ( t ) = \delta ^ { \prime } \psi ( t ) N _ { I } ( t )$ , where ψ(t) is the piracy rate in time period t (i.e., among $N _ { I } \left( t \right)$ number of Internet subscribers, ψ(t) fraction of them consume on average $\delta ^ { \prime }$ units of unlicensed digital music). δ' measures per pirate consumption of unlicensed digital music. We use the annual piracy rate estimated in the software industry as a similar measure for the music industry is not available. However, we would like to note that the average software piracy rate (21 percent) is of same magnitude as the estimate made by IFPI (The International Federation of the Phonographic Industry). IFPI estimates that 20 percent of Internet users regularly consume unlicensed digital music.<sup>17</sup>

We also estimate our model using the number of consumer complaints received by the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), which is likely correlated with the diffusion of unlicensed digital music consumption. We revise equations (6) as $S _ { U M } ( t ) = \delta ^ { \prime \prime } N _ { C } ( t )$ , where $N _ { C } ( t )$ is the number of complaints received. $\delta ^ { \prime \prime }$ is per complaint consumption of unlicensed digital music. Because the estimation of our model if we use IC3 data as a proxy measure does not converge, in order to reduce the complexity of the estimation problem, following prior diffusion studies (e.g., Dewan et al. 2010), we estimate the other parameters assuming the market potential for each music format is exogenously given. The estimation results (reported in the Appendix E, Table E3) show that although there are some differences in magnitude, the parameter estimates are fairly consistent across different proxy measures. Compared to when Internet penetration or weighted Internet penetration is used, when IC3 data is used as a proxy measure, we obtain a smaller value of $\mathit { \Delta } b _ { \mathit { D S } }$ and a larger value of α, which imply a stronger preference for an individual song format (digital single) over an album format (CD and digital album). Also, a larger value of $\gamma$ when IC3 data is used compared to when Internet penetration or weighted Internet penetration is used as a proxy measure suggests a stronger piracy dampening effect of licensed digital downloads. Nonetheless, the main implication of our findings reported in the previous section remain unchanged across different proxy measures.

## Second Stage Disruption of Digitization of Music: Streaming

For the estimation of our extended model discussed earlier, we use units streamed, converted from listener hours, which we collected from Pandora’s annual report as a measure of the demand for streaming music. For the conversion, we use the average length of songs on the radio which is 3 minutes 30 seconds.<sup>18</sup> In addition to Pandora, other streaming services such as Spotify and Apple Music also exist in the market; however, unfortunately, the data from these services are not available because they are either relatively new or relatively small. Pandora has dominated the U.S. streaming music market, with about 70 percent of the market share (Pandora Media 2012). Further, the study of RBC Capital Markets shows that Pandora’s user base in 2014 (51 percent of Internet users) was four times larger than that of Spotify (14 percent of Internet users) (eMarketer 2015).

Because the estimation of the extended model does not converge, in order to reduce the complexity of the estimation problem (i.e., the number of parameter needs to be estimated), we use the following strategy for the estimation of our extended model. Following prior diffusion studies (e.g., Dewan et al. 2010), we estimate the other parameters assuming the market potentials for CDs, digital albums, and digital singles are exogenously given (i.e., by feeding $m _ { C D } , m _ { D A } ,$ and $m _ { D S }$ which we obtained from our base model, into the estimation). We also assume that the demand for unlicensed digital music (i.e., consumption of unlicensed digital music per Internet subscriber) is exogenously given (i.e., by feeding δ, which we obtained from our base model, into the estimation). For the rest of the estimation, we use the same strategy that we used for the estimation of our base model. We present the estimation results and model fit from our extended model along with that from our base model in Table 2.

We make the following interesting and important observations from the estimation results of our extended model reported in Table 2. First, while $\zeta _ { D A }$ is significant $\zeta _ { D S }$ is not, suggesting the presence of demand migration from digital albums to streaming music and a likely absence of demand migration from digital singles to unlicensed digital music. This result is plausible because Pandora, like many streaming music services, does not allow subscribers to choose songs that they want to play.<sup>19</sup> The demand for digital singles from consumers that have strong preference for an individual song is not substituted by Pandora’s streaming music. The demand for digital albums, which is a seller-defined bundle, on the other hand, is substituted by Pandora’s streaming music, which is another form of bundling. Second, $\zeta _ { D A } = 1 . 1 8 9 7$ suggests that one digital album download converts to 1,200 streams of individual songs.<sup>20</sup> Finally, the attrition from licensed digital downloads (i.e., the piracy effect) has diminished by about 7 percent (η = 0.0686) every year since streaming music was introduced. That is, streaming music has induced a rather significant portion of the demand for licensed digital downloads to migrate to streaming music that would have migrated to unlicensed digital music in the absence of streaming music.

## Implications and Discussion

Several implications for the music industry emerge from our estimation results. First, our result confirms that the attrition of the demand for music due to unlicensed digital music (i.e., the piracy effect) has been substantial. However, it could have been worse if the industry did not provide consumers with the option to legitimately purchase digital music (licensed digital downloads) and focused only on stopping people from consuming unlicensed digital music. Since channels for licensed digital music were introduced, about 3.8 billion units of demand for CDs that would have migrated to unlicensed digital music in the absence of licensed digital downloads has migrated to either digital albums (0.6 billion units) or digital singles (3.2 billion units) instead. Further, streaming music also has dampened the piracy effect (the attrition from licensed digital downloads) about 7 percent every year. These findings support the suggestions of prior studies (Chellappa and Shivendu 2005; Khouja et al. 2008; Sundararajan 2004) that industry’s strategy needs to be focused on converting the attrition of the demand to profitable migration, rather than stopping the attrition, by offering new innovations.

Second, our estimation results confirm that consumers prefer only a small fraction of songs bundled in an album. First, in our estimation, one unit demand for CDs migrated to only on average 2.9 units of demand for digital singles; 9.1 other songs bundled together in an album were not consumed. Second, about 91 percent of the demand that migrated from CDs to licensed digital downloads was to digital singles and the remaining 9 percent was to digital albums. Third, like many information goods, music might exhibit the no free disposal property (i.e., the utility of a consumer from consuming songs might be non-monotonic and some songs might incur disutility). Because of such strong preference of consumers for an individual song over an album, unbundling is now the dominant factor that derives the migration of the demand from physical to digital music. In 2014, 58 percent of demand migration from CDs was to digital singles, 24 percent was to unlicensed digital music, and the remaining was to digital albums.

Third, due to this unbundling effect, although consumers are purchasing more music than ever (in terms of units shipped) (Covert 2013), the music industry’s revenue is still down from when revenue reached its peak in 2000. In 2014, the industry generated \$1.2 billion from 118 million units of digital album sales and \$1.4 billion of revenue from 1.2 billion units of digital single sales in the U.S. market. The combined revenue from digital albums and digital singles in 2014 was only about 20 percent of that from CDs in 2000 when revenue reached its peak (\$13.2 billion of revenue from 943 million units of CD sales in the U.S. market). This finding suggests that the industry likely benefitted tremendously by implementing the seller-defined bundling strategy, although this bundling strategy does not seem to be a working strategy anymore due to the digitization of music. The demand for the sellerdefined bundle (album) format was substituted by the individual song format (unlicensed digital music and digital single) in the first stage disruption of digitization of music and is now substituted by another form of bundling (streaming music) in the second stage disruption of digitization of music.

Finally, although preliminary, our results suggest that while streaming music will likely be a substitute for digital albums it may not for digital singles. This might be because the radio-type streaming music format, which does not allow subscribers to choose songs that they want to play and thereby is akin to a seller-defined bundle found in the CD or digital album format, has so far dominated the streaming music market (Ingham 2015). One possible strategy to convert the demand for digital singles into that for streaming music can be by providing features that allow subscribers to have more control of their own playlists. In fact, recent anecdotal evidence seems to suggest that the streaming music market may shift in favor of an on-demand streaming music format that allows subscribers to choose songs they want to play. iHeart-Radio and Pandora, who previously focused on radio-type streaming music services rolled out on-demand tiers in December 2016 and March 2017, respectively. Amazon also launched on-demand streaming music services in October 2016. In 2016, on-demand streaming music grew by 76 percent compared to the previous year.<sup>21</sup> On-demand streaming music format that preserves the salient features of bundling might be able to mitigate the music industry’s revenue loss caused by unbundling.

## Conclusion

Many industries have been disrupted by technological innovations and not all disruptive technologies improve the industry’s (or firm’s) profitability (Adner and Zemsky 2005). In the music industry, the digital music formats and the Internet as a distribution mechanism have fundamentally altered the way music is packaged, distributed, and consumed. The major contribution of our research lies in identifying, isolating, and quantifying the different types of demand migration in the music industry and the relative weight of factors (the generation, unbundling, piracy, and streaming effects) that derive each of these migration types. The different migration types have different implications for the music industry. The model developed for and the understanding gained from examining different types of migration in the music industry provides important implications in terms of product design and piracy not only to the music industry but also to other industries that are undergoing disruption caused by the digitization of content and distribution channels, such as publishing, television, and movies.

Despite the strong statistical results we obtained in our study, there are some limitations. First, assuming each Internet subscriber consumes on average a constant amount of unlicensed digital music, we used Internet penetration as a proxy measure for unlicensed digital music. Internet penetration has been widely used as a proxy measure for piracy in prior studies. Further, we tested the robustness of our estimation results using other proxy measures. However, it would be worthwhile to look at a direct measure and verify the results. Second, for a technical reason, we estimated our model assuming there is no unique demand for unlicensed digital music $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , m _ { U M } = 0 )$ . Although our robustness test showed that relaxing this assumption does not qualitatively change any of our findings, once a direct measure for the demand for unlicensed digital music becomes available, one can relax this assumption and measure more precisely the impact of unlicensed digital music on the music industry. Third, despite its advantage, the diffusion model approach has some limitations. Since the model uses aggregate market-level data, the model does not consider user heterogeneity (Susarla et al. 2012) and provides limited individual-level implications (Mahajan et al. 1990). In addition, the model does not capture the direct effects of decision variables such as price and advertising (Chandrasekaran and Tellis 2007). As an alternative estimation technique, a log-logistic distribution accelerated failure time model (Xue et al. 2011) and the decomposed diffusion model (Susarla et al. 2012) were used in prior studies. Nonetheless, the diffusion model has been successfully applied to estimate sales in many different industries, including retail service, telecom, information technology, agriculture, pharmaceutical, and consumer durable goods (for the review of diffusion models, see Bass 2004; Mahajan et al. 1990; Meade and Islam 2006). In particular, Bass (1995), Bewley and Griffiths (2003), Boswijk and Franses (2005) , and Pae and Lehmann (2003) apply the diffusion model and estimate the sales of sound recording formats (e.g., LP, tape, and CD). As shown in Chen et al. (2014), given that communication is a significant factor that derives music sales, the diffusion model, which is built upon innovation and imitation (word-of-mouth) factors, would be a good technique to measure the diffusion of and the demand migration among different music consumption options. Finally, available data about streaming music are limited. Estimation of our model using more data when they become available will help refine our findings.

## The Extended Model

Our extended model that includes streaming effects (given in equations (3), (6) – (11), (14) – (16), and (17) – (23)) can be summarized as the following. We write the demand (units shipped) for each music format ݅ in time period ݐ as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & S _ {C D} (t) = m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) - \Phi_ {G S} (t) - \Phi_ {U B} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t) \\ & S _ {U M} (t) = \delta N _ {I} (t) = m _ {U M} F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) + \beta_ {U M} \Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t) + \beta_ {U M} \Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) + \Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) \\ & S _ {D A} (t) = m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \Phi_ {G S} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) - \Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) \\ & S _ {D S} (t) = m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) + \beta_ {D S} (t) \Phi_ {U B} (t) - \Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) - \Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) \\ & S _ {S M} (t) = m _ {S M} F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M}) + \zeta_ {D A} \Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) + \zeta_ {D S} \Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) \end{array}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { \beta _ { D S } ( t ) = b _ { D S } \left[ 1 - \log \left[ \frac { v _ { D S } ( t ) } { v _ { C D } ( t ) } \right] \right] } \end{array}$ and $F _ { i } ( t )$ are derived from

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\frac {\partial F _ {i} (t)}{\partial t} = [ p _ {i} + q _ {i} F _ {i} (t) ] [ 1 - F _ {i} (t) ] \mathrm{for} i = C D, U M, \mathrm{and} S M} \\ & {\frac {\partial F _ {i} (t)}{\partial t} = [ p _ {i} + q _ {i} F _ {i} (t) + \lambda_ {i} F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) ] [ 1 - F _ {i} (t) ] \mathrm{for} i = D A \mathrm{and} D S} \end{array}
$$

We define each demand migration type as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\Phi_ {A _ {C D}} (t) = e ^ {- \gamma \cdot I _ {L M} (t)} m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {G S} (t) = [ 1 - \alpha (t) ] [ 1 - e ^ {- \gamma \cdot I _ {L M} (t)} ] m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {U B} (t) = \alpha (t) [ 1 - e ^ {- \gamma \cdot I _ {L M} (t)} ] m _ {C D} F _ {C D} (t) F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {A _ {D A}} (t) = e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {A _ {D S}} (t) = e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {S _ {D A}} (t) = [ 1 - e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} ] m _ {D A} F _ {D A} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) + \Phi_ {G S} (t) F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M})} \\ & {\Phi_ {S _ {D S}} (t) = [ 1 - e ^ {- \eta \cdot I _ {S M} (t)} ] m _ {D S} F _ {D S} (t - \tau_ {L M}) F _ {U M} (t - \tau_ {U M}) + \beta_ {D S} (t) \Phi_ {U B} (t) F _ {S M} (t - \tau_ {S M})} \end{array}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { \alpha ( t ) = a \left[ 1 - \log \left[ \frac { v _ { D S } ( t ) } { v _ { D A } ( t ) } \right] \right] , I _ { L M } ( t ) = t - \tau _ { L M } \mathrm { i f } t \geq \tau _ { L M } } \end{array}$ and 0 otherwise, and $I _ { S M } ( t ) = t - \tau _ { S M } \mathrm { i f } t \geq \tau _ { S M }$ and 0 otherwise.
