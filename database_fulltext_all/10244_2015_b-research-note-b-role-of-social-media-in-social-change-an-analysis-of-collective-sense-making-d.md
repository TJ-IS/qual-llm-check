# Role of Social Media in Social Change: An Analysis of Collective Sense Making During the 2011 Egypt Revolution

Onook Oh

Information Systems Program, Business School, University of Colorado Denver, Denver, Colorado 80202, onook.oh@ucdenver.edu

Chanyoung Eom

Department of Finance, Hanyang University Business School, Seoul, South Korea 133-791, cyeom73@hanyang.ac.kr

his study explores the role of social media in social change by analyzing Twitter data collected during the 2011 Egypt Revolution. Particular attention is paid to the notion of collective sense making, which is considered a critical aspect for the emergence of collective action for social change. We suggest that collective sense making through social media can be conceptualized as human-machine collaborative information pro cessing that involves an interplay of signs, Twitter grammar, humans, and social technologies. We focus on the occurrences of hashtags among a high volume of tweets to study the collective sense-making phenomena of milling and keynoting. A quantitative Markov switching analysis is performed to understand how the hashtag frequencies vary over time, suggesting structural changes that depict the two phenomena. We further explore different hashtags through a qualitative content analysis and find that, although many hashtags were used as symbolic anchors to funnel online users’ attention to the Egypt Revolution, other hashtags were used as part of tweet sentences to share changing situational information. We suggest that hashtags functioned as a means to collect information and maintain situational awareness during the unstable political situation of the Egypt Revolution.

## Introduction

The Egypt Revolution and Arab Spring in 2011 have opened forums for debates on the role of information and communications technology (ICT) and social change. Recent literature on ICT and social movements can be categorized into three broad discourses: social media as sites for political contestation, social media as tools or resources to achieve human emancipation, and social media as machine-mediated collective crisis communication (Ghonim 2012, Aouragh and Alexander 2011, Starbird and Palen 2012, Howard et al. 2011).

An example of these discourses is reflected in Wael Ghonim’s book, Revolution 2.0 (Ghonim 2012). As one of the organizers of the 2011 Egypt Revolution, he argues that “this leaderless revolution”

was the “Revolution 2.0,” echoing the participatory and people-centric characteristics of Web 2.0 technologies (Ghonim 2012, p. 294). In his CBS interview (CBS 2011), he emphatically emphasizes, “if there was no social network, it [the 2011 Egypt Revolution] would have never been sparked0 60 0 07 Without Facebook, without Twitter, without Google, without YouTube, this would never have happened.” Thus, he stresses the emancipatory nature of social media technologies. Such discourse frames social media technologies as creating “a social space where power is decided” (Castells 2007, p. 238) or as “a main site of political contestation” (Russell 2011, p. 1241). Other crisis communication scholars have approached social media technologies as intermediary machines that mediate human communications in social crisis situations and present varying patterns of crisis communication (Starbird and Palen 2012). For example, Lotan et al. (2011) reported that, during the Tunisia and Egypt Revolutions, situational news was coconstructed and rapidly distributed to the globe through Twitter. Howard et al. (2011) identified the sudden rise of revolutionary conversations in overall social media websites such as Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter.

In this paper, we set the scope of our research to the notion of collective sense making, which is considered an important aspect to explain the emergence of social movements (McPhail 1991, Shibutani 1966, Turner and Killian 1987), and expand it to the new context of the 2011 Egypt Revolution in which the use of social media was prevalent (Ghonim 2012, Howard et al. 2011, Lotan et al. 2011, Oh et al. 2012, Starbird and Palen 2012). To do that, we explore how the inseparably entangled dynamics of sociotechnical communication developed into a form of collective sense making in Twitter space. Specifically, we trace the changing communication dynamics by explicating how the selective and collective use of hashtags developed in Twitter space during the 2011 Egypt Revolution.

This study makes a twofold contribution.

(1) It explores how the collective sense-making process developed over time during the 2011 Egypt Revolution. We consider various Twitter hashtags as indicators of collective sense making during the 2011 Egypt Revolution and explore the sense-making process using a quantitative Markov switching analysis. We find that the phenomena of milling and keynoting occurred in various contexts during the Egypt Revolution.

(2) It excerpts characteristics of Twitter hashtags that influenced the collective sense-making process during the 2011 Egypt Revolution. We explore different hashtags through a qualitative content analysis and find that the hashtags embody technolinguistic grammar that helps explicate the collective sensemaking process. Hashtags were found to have dual uses—as symbols or as words. Although many hashtags were used as symbolic anchors to funnel online users’ attention to the Egypt Revolution, other hashtags were used as words—parts of tweet sentences to share changing situational information.

The succeeding sections are organized as follows. We first review the literature on collective behavior, social movements, and sense making and subsequently focus on the concepts of milling and keynoting. We discuss the concept of hashtags in the context of technolinguistic grammar. Subsequently, we discuss the context of the Egypt Revolution and outline the data collection method and research methodology. After that, we perform two analyses: a time series analysis to explore the changing patterns of hashtags and a qualitative content analysis of the hashtags. We conclude this study with a discussion, contributions, limitations, and future research topics.

## Collective Behavior, Social Movements, and Sense Making

Collective behavior has been a common theme in varying definitions of social movements (Morris and Herring 1987). We argue that the use of social media technologies allows inseparable technosocial collective communication and fosters collective communicational behavior. For this study, we adopt the collective view of social movements (Turner and Killian 1987). Turner and Killian define a social movement as follows.

A social movement is a collectivity acting with some continuity to promote or resist a change in the society or group of which it is a part. As a collectivity, a movement is a group with indefinite and shifting membership and 0 0 0 whose position is determined more by the informal response of the members than by formal procedures for legitimizing authority. (p. 223)

In a similar vein, Tilly (2004) suggests that a social movement shows ordinary people’s “collective claims on others” and that the success of a movement relies on whether ordinary individuals successfully translate collective grievance claims into a public issue (Kim and Miranda 2011, p. 9). Thus a social movement is an improvised and nonroutine form of collective action to achieve a common goal of social change (McPhail 1991, Morris and Herring 1987).

Social movements result in protestors’ use of nonroutine social action and nonstandard communication systems. As shown from the recent political uprisings in Moldova (Mungiu-Pippidi and Munteanu 2009), Iran (Bruns and Eltham 2009), Egypt, and other Arab nations (Ghonim 2012), proregime mainstream media are commonly silent about the demands of protester groups, and government’s panoptic surveillance systems hinder potential protesters from using standard communication channels. Therefore, to promote interactions for social movements, people must improvise alternative communication channels to construct and expand a shared understanding among groups of potential protesters. For this reason, the collective behavior of a social movement is very different from routinized social behavior. Collective behavior is an improvised, emergent, self-organized, and adaptive group behavior that occurs when standard social routines (e.g., standard communication channels and established norms) are not functioning in the favor of the actors involved (McPhail 1991). In this regard, recent social movements in Arab nations have demonstrated that social media technologies have emerged as new media by which activists can perform collective sense making because they enable immediate connections among previously disconnected groups of people.

The social movement literature has consistently reported that collective sense making normally precedes collective action and that it is primarily communicational (DiFonzo and Bordia 2007, Shibutani 1966, Turner and Killian 1987). Collective sense making is manifested as the communication behaviors of active information seeking, offering, and sharing among like-minded groups of people. It helps reduce the level of situational ambiguity and collectively defines the unfolding situation in ways that can assist collective action toward a uniform goal. Therefore, it is no wonder that sudden high traffic of tweeting, retweeting, linking, online posting, texting, and so on accompanied the 2011 Egypt Revolution (Howard et al. 2011, Oh et al. 2012, Starbird and Palen 2012).

## Sense Making Through Social Media: Milling and Keynoting

Social movements normally bring about an informational uncertainty and situational ambiguity at the societal level. They force people to make sense of ambiguous situations and construct a shared meaning among heterogeneous people with diverse backgrounds (Aguirre 1994, Aguirre et al. 1998, Turner and Killian 1987). Turner and Killian (1987) call this dynamic physical and verbal interaction process the “milling” and “keynoting” process, two subcomponents of collective sense making in social movement contexts. The main argument is that collective action is preceded by collective sense making, which continuously evolves from chaotic milling interactions into organized keynoting interactions. The main function of those large-scale communicational interactions is to collectively understand ambiguous external situations, expand shared awareness, and create a course of collective action to achieve the goal of social change (Aguirre et al. 1998, Rose 1982).

Turner and Killian (1987) submit that, facing crisis situations, “people become highly suggestible and turn to others to find cues. This restless search for cues is analogous to cattle milling, except in the case of humans, the milling is primarily verbal” (Bordia 1996, p. 8; Turner and Killian 1987). The restless milling phenomenon includes such behaviors as watching TV, calling friends, or gathering in the street to share information and interpret the situation together. In that regard, “milling, 0 0 0 , is essentially a communication process,” and it involves different voices from diverse backgrounds in a chaotic manner (Turner and Killian 1987, p. 64). At this stage of uncertain and ambiguous situations, group behavior is spontaneous and adaptive, and no norms or standardized communication channels have yet been developed.

For the milling process to develop into collective action, Turner and Killian (1987) argue that some strong and coherent voices, themes, events, or symbols should emerge to override the divergent and chaotic opinions of the crowd milling in the frame of uncertainty and ambivalence. The presentation of positive suggestions in a unified frame of reference is called keynoting (Turner and Killian 1987, p. 117). Keynoting indicates the emergence of new lines that have a strong and coherent theme and differentiate themselves from the multiple lines of milling voices and chaotic interactions. As specific voices differentiate from the muddled sounds of a milling crowd, keynoting needs to be strong and focused enough to be noticed among the conflicting and cacophonous voices. As keynoting evolves, cohesive symbols (e.g., pictures or banners), unified slogans, division of labor, structures, crystallization of norms, and clear directions to act toward a revolutionary goal also emerge (Tilly 2004, Turner and Killian 1987).

New technologies have produced “smart mobs, [0 0 0] who are able to act in concert even if they don’t know each other” (Tilly 2004, p. 97). In 1999, people used mobile phones, websites, and laptops to coordinate the protest against the meeting of the World Trade Organization in Seattle. In 2000, thousands of British citizens used mobile phones, short message services (SMS), and email to coordinate protests against rising gasoline prices. In 2001, Filipinos mainly used text messages of “Go2EDSA, Wear blck” to organize the march to demand that President Joseph Estrata step down (Tilly 2004, pp. 95–97). A common denominator in these protests is that “cellular phones and text messaging begin to look like serious alternatives to fixed-line communication,” and they afforded protestors the flexibility of “not falling easily under government control” (Tilly 2004, p. 97).

In 2011, social media in combination with mobile phones established themselves as essential tools that can bring about new modes of connecting and coordinating. Ghonim (2012), a key activist who coordinated multiple protests during the Egypt Revolution, maintains that mobile phones and social media were the main tools when he was on the streets. To facilitate keynoting out of a chaotic milling situation, he compiled into a document all information relevant to the protest of January 25th, such as “the reasons for protesting and for choosing this day and these locations, [0 0 0] the unified chants, [0 0 0] phone numbers for activists,” etc. He then “uploaded the file to Google Docs, where more than 50,000 people accessed [it],” and its content was disseminated through various “online forums, political websites, Facebook pages, and Twitter accounts” (Ghonim 2012, p. 164). Also, while he was on the streets, “[he] began tweeting like a madman” to propagate information about the changing situation and to urge everyone to come out and join the protest in Tahrir Square in Cairo (Ghonim 2012, pp. 183–184).

In this paper, we hypothesize that if the role of social media technologies was significant during the Egypt Revolution, and if the emergence of keynoting is a critical aspect for impending collective action, then the emergence of keynoting should be detected through an analysis of Twitter data.

## Hashtags in Twitter: A Technolinguistic Grammar Hashtags in Twitter: A Technolinguistic Grammar

Twitter is a social media service that allows messages to be sent and received via twitter.com, SMS text messaging, and numerous other computer and cell phone applications. Users can also “follow” other users, seeing their comments (tweets) in nearly real time. Limited to 140 characters or less, users are able to immediately broadcast local messages to the world. A key characteristic of Twitter is the hashtag—a word prefixed with the hash (#) symbol. It is an executable grammar of Twitter hypertext that enables retrieval of all messages containing the specific hashtagged word with one click. When a hashtagged word is written in a Twitter message, it automatically changes into a clickable hyperlink. In that regard, writing a hashtag is not just posting a message to spread or exchange information. It involves multiple possibilities of drawing other users’ attention, supporting the hashtagged ideas, expanding the level of situational awareness, coordinating collective behavior, and helping information aggregation and retrieval for Twitter systems (Kwon et al. 2012).

We suggest that the hashtag in Twitter is an executable technolinguistic grammatical feature for collective sense making. It helps identify clusters of focal themes and makes them rise to the surface among a whole population of chaotic tweets. Because hashtagged keywords automatically turn into clickable hyperlinks to retrieve all thematically relevant tweets, they can also easily present multifaceted views on the unfolding situation surrounding them. The power of the hashtag is contingent on the number of people who opt to write specific hashtags in their tweets. That is, as more people write specific hashtags, the size of Twitter space occupied by those hashtags increases, which strengthens the signal and makes it more collective while increasing the probability of its exposure. Indeed, the collective and frequent uses of hashtags were significant during the Egypt Revolution. A trend report published by Twitter reveals that the Egypt hashtag (i.e., #Egypt) was the most frequent, and the Jan25 hashtag (i.e., #Jan25) was the eighth most frequently used hashtag of 2011,<sup>1</sup> suggesting that online users were keen to make sense of and remain tied to the unfolding political situation in Egypt in 2011 (Pous 2011).

In the social movement context, collective writing practices anchored by specific hashtags can be understood as people’s expression of interest in the ideas implied by the hashtagged keywords, and its aggregated result processed through Twitter systems represents collective sentiment on the unfolding situation of a social movement. For instance, as we will detail in the following section, our hashtag frequency analysis shows that, during the Egypt Revolution, Twitter space was dominated by and structured around a few powerful hashtags (such as #Jan25, #Tahrir, #Mubarak, and #Egypt). The domination of those hashtags can be interpreted as support for the collective desire of the Egyptian people to gather in #Tahrir Square on #Jan25 to dispel the dictatorial #Mubarak regime from #Egypt. Although some of these hashtags might have been used with opposite or neutral meanings, their quantity was too small to change the probable meanings in the entire Twitter space. Therefore, we posit that observing the evolving patterns of hashtags and understanding their implications can permit detection of keynoting as it emerges, thereby anticipating the emergence of the collective action of the Egypt Revolution.

Collective sense making through social media and the production of shared meaning through hashtags give an important insight to the diffusion of the serial social movements that started in Tunisia and cut across national borders into Egypt and other Arab nations. Whereas previous face-to-face communication might have enabled milling and keynoting at the local and territorial levels, social media technologies and the collective writing practice of hashtags have opened the possibility of deterritorialized milling and keynoting in virtual space with unprecedented speed, scale, and complexity. The deterritorialized collective sense-making process will be described in the next section through an analysis of hashtag data from the 2011 Egypt Revolution.

## Research Method

To determine the emergence of keynoting processes out of milling communications, we analyzed the changing patterns of hashtagged keywords. This section comprises five subsections: (1) background description of the Egypt Revolution, (2) data collection methods, (3) descriptive frequency analysis of hashtags, (4) quantitative analysis of a time series of hashtagged keywords using a Markov-switching model, and (5) qualitative content analysis of those hashtags.

## Background of the 2011 Egypt Revolution

Prior to January 25, 2011, small and large protests (e.g., “Silent Stands” events from June to August 2010) with different political agendas sporadically occurred in different Egyptian cities. The active engagement of Ghonim (2012) in the Egypt Revolution began on June 10, 2010, when he anonymously created a Facebook page, “We Are All Khaled Said.” His first post was a horrifying picture of a murdered young man, along with a blunt description: “Today they killed Khaled. If I don’t act for his sake, tomorrow they will kill me” (Ghonim 2012, p. 60). The Facebook page was made to reprimand the notorious secret police officers who had beaten to death the young Egyptian because his blog post criticized the Mubarak regime. Reflecting anger, frustration, and a desire for freedom for Egyptians, the response was immediate, and more than 36,000 people joined the page on the first day (Ghonim 2012, pp. 60–62). Through the Facebook page, Ghonim coordinated “Silent Stands of Prayer for the Martyr Khaled Said” in major Egyptian cities from June through August 2010 (Ghonim 2012). Using social media, many young participants shared pictures and videos of the events, including reports on ill treatment of peaceful participants by police. As time went by, the “We Are All Khaled Said” page became a virtual meeting place to charge the corrupt government, express solidarity, encourage protesters, perform online polls, etc.

Fueled by the Silent Stands events and inspired by the Tunisian Revolution on December 17, 2010, that successfully deposed President Ben Ali from his 23 years in power, the Egyptian people planned a large-scale nonviolent civil resistance at Tahrir Square in Cairo on January 25, 2011. The gathering at Tahrir Square on January 25th was the largest and best-coordinated uprising, clearly different from the previous small-scale uprisings. Through it, the Egyptian people collectively demanded that their president step down from his presidency. The demand was realized on February 11th when President Mubarak was forced to resign from his 30-year dictatorship.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, the time period from January 25 to February 11, 2011, is recorded as the 2011 Egypt Revolution (Aouragh and Alexander 2011, Russell 2011).

Social media played a significant role in planning and coordinating all of the demonstrations until President Mubarak announced his resignation. Shocked by the snowballing number of angry protesters and the power of social media technologies, the Mubarak regime responded with rubber bullets, tear gas, pro-Mubarak thugs, and a disconnect of Internet and national cellular wireless services. However, the resistance of the protesters became more intense, and international criticism increased against the Mubarak regime. Finally, on February 11, 2011, President Mubarak resigned from the presidency and delegated power to the Supreme Council of the Egyptian Armed Forces, and the revolutionary protesters celebrated the overthrow of his 30-year dictatorship.

## Data Collection

Our research aims to (1) explicate how collective sense making developed by analyzing hashtags during the 2011 Egyptian Revolution and (2) explore the characteristics of the hashtags that contributed to collective sense making. To do so, we conducted an analysis of various Twitter hashtags as indicators of collective action during the 2011 Egypt Revolution. We collected hashtag data from January 12, sufficiently before the beginning of the Egypt Revolution on January 25, to March 10, sufficiently after the removal of President Mubarak on February 11, 2011.

Twitter does not allow keyword searches for historical data older than five days. The only way to access historical Twitter data is to backtrack each user account to retrieve a maximum of that user’s most recent 3,200 tweets. Therefore, we had to use an unconventional data gathering strategy: (1) identify and collect Twitter user IDs that tweeted during the relevant time period, (2) backtrack all identified user IDs to retrieve their past tweet messages, and (3) clean out irrelevant tweets.

On January 25, 2011, the first day of the revolution, we began data collection eight times per day for an hour per each data collection. This step enabled us to obtain a sufficient number of Twitter user IDs that could be used later to backtrack all their tweets. During the 17 days from January 25th to February 11, 2011, we collected a total of 50,778 Twitter user IDs. Subsequently, we used an automated Twitter API data collection tool to track back the entire Twitter user IDs and retrieve all of their tweets between January 12th and March 10, 2011. Through this process, we collected a total of 1,915,429 tweets from the 50,778 Twitter user IDs. We stored all data in a structured query language (SQL) relational database system. From the database, we extracted tweets that contained the keyword “Egypt.” After cleaning, we retained for analysis 343,581 tweets sent by 15,636 users between January 12th and March 10, 2011.

## Analysis of Hashtags: Detecting Patterns of the Keynoting Process Using a Markov-Switching Model

This section investigates the time series properties of hashtags during the 2011 Egypt Revolution and uncovers how the collective sense-making process is characterized in the top seven hashtags. From the definition of collective sense making, the emergence of keynoting indicates that people suddenly pay greater attention to specific hashtags and voluntarily adopt them in writing and sharing messages. This means that if keynoting occurs, the frequencies of hashtags should increase suddenly on the specific date at which the keynoting ideas emerge out of many milling ones. In a statistical sense, thus, keynoting is equivalent to a structural change in the parameters governing the underlying process of hashtag frequency, thereby causing new patterns of hashtag frequency starting from a specific date.

To conceptualize the occurrence of keynoting, we presume that the hashtag frequency prior to the emergence of keynoting is described by the autoregressive (AR) model of order one with $E [ \dot { y } _ { t } ] = \mu _ { 1 }$ of the form

$$
y _ {t} - \mu_ {1} = \phi (y _ {t - 1} - \mu_ {1}) + \varepsilon_ {t},
$$

and that, after the emergence of keynoting, hashtag frequency follows the AR(1) with $E [ \boldsymbol { y } _ { t } ] = \mu _ { 2 }$ of the form

$$
y _ {t} - \mu_ {2} = \phi (y _ {t - 1} - \mu_ {2}) + \varepsilon_ {t},
$$

where $y _ { t }$ represents a log-transformed hashtag frequency at time $t , \ \mu _ { 1 } \neq \mu _ { 2 } ,$ , and $\varepsilon _ { t } \sim \mathrm { i . i . d }$ $N ( 0 , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ The proposed model allows $y _ { t }$ to evolve with two different states in that the mean $\mu _ { 1 }$ governs the series of $y _ { t }$ in state 1 and the mean $\mu _ { 2 }$ governs the series in state 2. If $\mu _ { 1 } < \mu _ { 2 } ,$ for instance, the hashtag frequency is wandering around the low mean $\mu _ { 1 }$ in state 1 but has the high mean $\mu _ { 2 }$ in state 2. Importantly, there is little ex ante information on the date at which the mean changes from $\mu _ { 1 }$ to $\mu _ { 2 }$ . This suggests that a complete model should consider the turning point as being endogenous and include an explicit description of the probabilistic process governing the change in population means.

Based on a seminal work by Hamilton (1989), we introduce a state-dependent Markov regime switching model, in which the structural break is treated as endogenous. As the most complete model, we propose the AR(1) with a first-order two-state Markovswitching mean model of the form

$$
y _ {t} - \mu_ {S _ {t}} = \phi (y _ {t - 1} - \mu_ {S _ {t - 1}}) + \varepsilon_ {t},
$$

where the state-dependent mean $\mu _ { s _ { t } }$ equals $\mu _ { 1 } \mathrm { i f } S _ { t } = 1$ and $\mu _ { 2 } { \mathrm { ~ i f ~ } } S _ { t } = 2$ . It is assumed that the discrete state variable $S _ { t }$ evolves like a first-order Markov switching process satisfying the property $\mathrm { P r } [ S _ { t } = j | S _ { t - 1 } = i ] = p _ { i j } ^ { \prime }$ for $i , j = 1 , 2 ,$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { 2 } p _ { i j } = 1 } \end{array}$ . The transition probability $p _ { i j }$ represents a conditional probability that the next state will be $j ,$ given that the current state is $i .$ In the proposed model, the turning point, determined endogenously by the data, can be interpreted as a point at which the structural break occurs in the population mean of $y _ { t }$

When implementing the model, we first estimate the parameters of $\mu _ { 1 } , \ \mu _ { 2 } ,$ , $\sigma ^ { 2 } , \ p _ { 1 1 } ,$ and $p _ { 2 2 }$ by maximizing the log-likelihood function of $y _ { t } .$ Based on the estimated values of transition probabilities, we next calculate the filtered transition probabilities $\operatorname* { P r } [ S _ { t } = j \mid \Psi _ { t } ]$ by running the Hamilton filter (Hamilton 1989). Along with the filtered transition probabilities, we also compute the smoothed transition probabilities $\mathrm { P r } [ S _ { t } \ = \ \hat { \ j } \ \mid \ \Psi _ { T } ]$ following Kim’s smoothing algorithm (Kim 1994). The filtered transition probabilities are formed based on partial information up to $t ,$ whereas the smoothed transition probabilities use the full information up to T and are thus considered more accurate.

Table 1 reports the estimated parameters of the AR(1) with a first-order two-state Markov-switching mean model along with the corresponding standard errors in parentheses. The estimated AR coefficients $\phi$ in all hashtag series suggest that the hashtag frequencies are highly persistent over time, which is consistent with the graphical illustration in Figure 2. The fifth and sixth rows show that the estimated mean values are state dependent in that the hashtag frequency $y _ { t }$ is characterized with two different state mean values for $\mu _ { 1 }$ and $\mu _ { 2 }$ . In the case of the hashtag #Egypt, for instance, the estimated means equal 5.5900 in a low mean state and 8.1486 in a high mean state, both of which are significantly different from zero at the 5% level. This indicates that the dynamics of the hashtag #Egypt are quite different from one subsample period to another in terms of frequency; i.e., the frequency jumps up at a certain point. Therefore, the observation of distinct low mean and high mean states suggests the presence of a structural break in the moving dynamics of hashtag frequencies. In other words, the degree of attention to the hashtag #Egypt in writing messages has sharply increased at threshold, which is consistent with the notion of keynoting. The same observations are made for other hashtags, thereby concluding that keynoting has occurred in the top seven hashtags over the sample period.

Figure 3 offers information about when the structural break arises. For each hashtag, Figure 3 illustrates the filtered (depicted as a dotted line) and smoothed (depicted as a solid line) probabilities of switching from a low mean state to a high mean state during the sample time period, both of which are constructed using the estimated values of transition probabilities. Notice that the y-axis of each plot represents the probability of a low mean state<sup>3</sup>; i.e., how likely $y _ { t }$ is to have a lower value of population mean between $\mu _ { 1 }$ and $\mu _ { 2 } .$

Figure 3 shows that, except for the hashtags #Tunisia, #Libya, and #Mubarak, the probabilities are close to one (low mean state) before January 25th but remain zero (high mean state) thereafter. In other words, the low mean state for the hashtags #Egypt, #Jan25, #Tahrir, and #Cairo prevailed before January 25th and the high mean state became suddenly dominant thereafter. Combined with Table 1, Figure 3 argues that the patterns of the hashtags #Egypt, #Jan25, #Tahrir, and #Cairo were governed by the low mean state before January 25th and that the means of the frequencies of those hashtags significantly and suddenly increased thereafter. Given that January 25th (#Jan25) was the first day when a large-scale and well-coordinated protest occurred, the significant and sudden breakpoint occurring on January 25th makes sense.

Therefore, we take this consistent structural change in the pattern of those Egypt-related hashtags including #Egypt, #Jan25, #Tahrir, and #Cairo as empirical evidence for the emergence of keynoting ideas through collective writing practices using those hashtags.

In the case of the hashtag #Mubarak, the probability radically swings between low and high mean states before January 25th and shows marginal fluctuation in the area of the high mean state thereafter. Unlike the other Egypt-related hashtags (i.e., #Jan25, #Cairo, #Tahrir, and #Egypt), although both $\mu _ { 1 }$ and $\mu _ { 2 }$ of the hashtag #Mubarak are significantly different from zero, the transition probability $p _ { 1 1 }$ is not significant at the 5% level. This insignificant probability for hashtag #Mubarak would explain its highly fluctuating probability before January 25th. Indeed, the different pattern could arise from the different purposes of using hashtags: whereas the hashtag #Mubarak was used mainly as a part of Twitter messages that spread specific information about events surrounding President Mubarak, the other Egypt-related hashtags were mainly used as symbols to sustain people’s attention on the Egypt Revolution.

Thus, although major keynoting processes occurred surrounding January 25th (as represented by the hashtags #Egypt, #Jan25, #Tahrir, and #Cairo), the foundation for the protests had been laid before the mass outpouring of opposition, which is represented by the fluctuating hashtagged topics of #Tunisia and #Mubarak before January 25th. More detailed analysis on different uses of hashtags and its varying implications will be made in the content analysis section.

We report interesting findings regarding the hashtags #Tunisia and #Libya based on a reading of Figures 2 and 3. The emerging pattern of those two hashtags is important for two reasons: (1) those two hashtags do not have any direct relation with the Egypt Revolution and (2) they show very different breakpoints from the other hashtags. In the case of #Tunisia, it initially stays at a high mean state from

January 13th, decreases to a low mean state until January 22nd, and then returns to a high mean state again around January 23rd and 25th, the dates just before the explosion of the Egypt Revolution. In the case of #Libya, the change occurs gradually from a low mean state toward a high mean state from January 26th to February 10th, and then it suddenly moves to a high mean state on February 11th. In other words, keynoting with regard to the hashtag #Libya emerged much later than January 25th.

We interpret this sequential breakpoint pattern starting from the hashtag #Tunisia (on January 14th) through the other Egypt-related hashtags (#Egypt, #Jan25, #Cairo, and #Tahrir on January 23rd to 25th) to the hashtag #Libya (on February 10th) as the spreading effect of social movements, anticipating the serial revolutions cutting across national borders from Tunisia to Libya through Egypt. The Tunisian Revolution, which started on December 17, 2010, made

President Ben Ali resign, and the news of the successful protest in Tunisia stimulated the desire of the Egyptian people to overthrow the Mubarak regime on January 25th. The news of protests in Egypt then ignited the desire of the Libyan people to unseat their autocrat, Muammar Gaddafi. In this regard, it is not coincidental that hashtags of many other Arab nations (such as #Yemen, #Syria, and #Bahrain) appeared, as presented in the hashtag cloud in the left column of Figure 1. Those hashtags can be seen as representing an infectious effect of information following the successful revolutions in Tunisia and Egypt that stimulated the desires of people in neighboring countries, thus anticipating the ensuing serial revolutions in Syria on January 26th, in Yemen on January 27th, and in Bahrain on February 14th of 2011. This interpretation is supported by prior empirical research as well. For example, the Cornell and Cohn (1995) empirical study on serial coal mining strikes in France shows evidence that (1) news of one strike produces imitative strikes and (2) news of a successful strike stimulates more imitative strikes than news of unsuccessful strikes. The Cornell and Cohn study assumes an active role for information in stimulating other strikes where “the information carried in the stimuli strike induces workers to think about their own strategic opportunities” (Cornell and Cohn 1995, p. 369), and the information effect reaches “to a greater extent than would be expected from social structural factors” (p. 374).

## Content Analysis: Symbol versus Word

To have a more nuanced understanding on the different uses of hashtags during the 2011 Egypt Revolution, we performed a content analysis. The primary purpose of the content analysis is to explicate why only the frequency of the hashtag #Mubarak fluctuates unstably over time even before January 25th, when other Egypt-related hashtags (#Egypt, #Jan25, #Cairo, and #Tahrir) do not (see Figure 3). A close reading of the tweets shows two different hashtag usage patterns: (1) online users insert hashtags as a symbol in tweets and (2) online users write a hashtag as a word to constitute a sentence. A few examples of hashtags, inserted as a symbol and written as a word, are as follows:

It’s going to be a bloody night in Cairo. And no one will hear them scream. May their strength save them. Long Live Egypt! #jan25 (the hashtag #jan25 as a symbol).

EGYPT IS FREE! EGYPT IS FREE! crowds cheer after Suleiman says #Mubarak is stepping down after 30 yrs. (the hashtag #Mubarak as a word).

#Tahrir Square is trending again in spite of internet blockage in Egypt. #Cairo (the hashtag #Tahrir as a word, and the hashtag #Cairo as a symbol).

A close reading of the tweets allows us to suggest the following. First, the more a hashtag is inserted as a symbol, the more likely it is to result in a stable pattern over time. Second, the more a hashtag is written as a word in a tweet sentence, the more likely it is to show unstable fluctuation over time. Third, whereas the symbolic hashtags tend to function as an anchor to draw people’s attention to the Egypt Revolution, the word hashtags tend to be used to update unfolding situational information over time. For example, in the first tweet message, the hashtag (#jan25) is inserted as a symbol next to the tweet message. The symbolic hashtag does not constitute itself as a part of the tweet nor does it have any semantic referent. It is simply used as a symbolic marker to (1) send a signal to the Twitter community that January 25th is important, (2) express solidarity for the causes of the Egyptian protestors, and (3) help the Twitter system categorize and retrieve relevant tweet messages. Whatever motivations were intended by the hashtag authors, as far as it is inserted as a symbol, its implications cannot be interpreted from the tweets themselves. Rather, we can understand it only as a symbolic marker to hold people’s attention to the issue of the Egypt Revolution.

In the second tweet, the hashtag (#Mubarak) is written as a word primarily to constitute a part of the tweet sentence. In this message, the hashtag has the semantic referent of President Mubarak and offers situational and timely information about him. Unlike the symbolic hashtag, the primary focus of the word hashtag, #Mubarak, is to share situational and timely news about President Mubarak. That is, the hashtag (#Mubarak) as a word refers to the specific event described in the tweet of President Mubarak stepping down as president. In this sense, we maintain that the word hashtag (#Mubarak) is more responsive and sensitive to external events than the symbolic one (#jan25) insofar as it reflects the fluctuating external situation.

From this understanding, we suggest that the stable symbolic hashtags (e.g., #Egypt, #Jan25, #Tahrir, and #Cairo) develop subsequent to unstable milling word hashtags (e.g., #Tunisia and #Mubarak), not vice versa. Symbolic hashtags as markers of stable keynoting cannot be made without aggregating and sharing sufficient amounts of milling information on external events as represented by the highly and/or marginally fluctuating word hashtags of #Mubarak and #Tunisia before January 25th. That is, although #Jan25 was the historic moment when #Egypt protesters flooded #Tahrir Square in #Cairo, its foundation had been prepared before the mass outpouring of opposition on January 25th through milling revolutionary conversations about #Tunisia and #Mubarak under the surface of Egyptian society (Ghonim 2012, Howard et al. 2011). That is, before January 25th, people connected the news on the successful revolution in Tunisia with the Egyptian people’s desire to overthrow the Mubarak regime. The following tweets show the simmering conversations before stable keynoting hashtags were established.

Come on #Mubarak 0 0 0 take a hint and follow the lead of Ben Ali [the dispelled president of Tunisia, author added] (a tweet posted on January 14th, 2011).

A call for #Revolution—Down with #Mubarak: Huge protest inspired by #Tunisia to be held in #Egypt on January 25th (a tweet posted on January 22nd, 2011).

From our readings of Figure 3 and the hashtagged tweets, we argue that the two fluctuating word hashtags (#Mubarak and #Tunisia) before January 25th can be seen as seeds of an impending revolution in Egypt. The difference is that, whereas the hashtag #Tunisia rapidly took on the role of a symbolic hashtag right after the explosion of the Egypt Revolution on January 25th, the hashtag #Mubarak remained primarily a word hashtag to report unfolding situations about President Mubarak until the goal of the Egypt Revolution, the resignation of #Mubarak from his 30 years of presidency, was accomplished.

## Content Coding, Interrater Reliability Test, and Its Result

To verify how many hashtags were inserted as a symbol hashtag and how many were written as a word hashtag, we carried out a content analysis. For the analysis, we followed the content coding and analysis procedure suggested by Krippendorf (1980) and Landis and Koch (1977). Three Master’s students were hired and trained to code the sample tweets. The content coding was carried out in two stages: pilot coding with 100 sample tweets and actual coding of 3,500 sample tweets. We ensured that study authors were excluded from coding.

For the pilot coding, two authors and three Master’s students met. The first author explained the idea of symbolic and word hashtags. The same author demonstrated how to code each tweet using three categories: symbol, word, and both (symbol and word). Given that the coding scheme was simple and straightforward, the three coders independently performed pilot coding with a 100-tweet random sample. The coded sample tweets were tested for interrater reliability to verify that the coders adequately and consistently understood the coding rule. The kappa values for the pilot coding results were significantly high: kappa value of (1) 0.94 for student coders 1 and 2, (2) 0.94 for coders 1 and 3, and (3) 0.92 for coders 2 and 3.<sup>4</sup>

## Discussion of the Content Analysis Results

One visible difference in Figure 4 is that, whereas the hashtag #Mubarak was extensively used as a word hashtag (69.9% of 500 tweets), other hashtags were mainly (#Jan25, 85.20%; #Egypt, 66.4%; #Cairo, 61.80%) or slightly more often (#Tahrir, 51.80%; #Tunisia, 51.80%; #Libya, 49.4%) used as symbolic hashtags.

From Figure 4, it is easy to infer why only the #Mubarak hashtag pattern fluctuated unstably in Figure 3: because the hashtag #Mubarak was mainly used as a word hashtag to share situational news about President Mubarak, its frequency changed in response to uncertain external events. For example, when President Mubarak’s resignation was announced on February 11th, the frequency of the hashtag #Mubarak increased right away. Also, on February 28, 2011, when the General Prosecutor of Egypt issued an order to ban Hosni Mubarak from leaving Egypt, the frequency of the hashtag #Mubarak again increased, as visible in Figures 2 and 3. On the other hand, the symbolic hashtags were used primarily to anchor a large number of people’s attention to the Egypt Revolution in general. Their primary purpose was not to deliver specific messages, but to bring tweets about the Egypt Revolution to the surface.

In sum, we submit that both symbolic and word hashtags were part of the collective sense-making processes, but they served different functions. The word hashtags delivered situational news about temporal events in a timely manner, and the symbolic hashtags sustained people’s attention on general issues during the Egypt Revolution to prevent distraction from a massive slew of Twitter messages.

## Discussion

The 2011 Egypt Revolution, dubbed Revolution 2.0 (Ghonim 2012), and the following Arab Spring movements showed technosocial dynamics unprecedented in speed and scale. Sparked in Tunisia in December 2010, the social movement moved to Egypt (January 25, 2011) and then spread to Algeria (February 11, 2011), Yemen (February 11, 2011), Bahrain (February 16, 2011), and Libya (February 16, 2011). During that two-month period, the Tunisian president stepped down from his 24-year rule, the Egyptian president stepped down from his 30-year rule, and the Libyan ruler stepped down from his 40-year rule.

Earlier research, for example, in the International Journal of Communication special issue papers on “The Arab Spring and the Role of ICTs,”<sup>5</sup> and the Journal of Communications special issue papers on “Social Media and the Arab Spring,”<sup>6</sup> have focused on anecdotal descriptions based on preliminary data analysis or descriptive statistics. Through this study, we suggest the utility of the emerging epistemology of sociomateriality in information systems (IS) research because it permits consideration of how inseparably entangled sociomaterial dynamics can shape rapid, large-scale technosocial collective sense making (Orlikowski 2007, Orlikowski and Scott 2008). The main contribution of this study lies in demonstrating how social media technologies inseparably engaged with users to shape collective sense making with unprecedented speed and scale. Traditional sense-making literature has assumed that technologies and human beings exist as separate entities and that the sense-making capability belongs exclusively to human beings (Aguirre 1994, Turner and Killian 1987, Weick et al. 2005). As a result, that literature focused mainly on analyzing people’s interpretative interactions to investigate collective sense making in organizations and society. That literature has tended to view technology as at best a contextual variable or ancillary tool; hence, the agential capability of technology in shaping the sense-making process has remained unexplored. In fact, this kind of humancentric perspective is assumed in the interpretive research method and its applied empirical research that “attempts to understand phenomena through the meanings that people assign to them” (Klein and Myers 1999, p. 69).

Our study shows that the collective writing of hashtags differs from the human-centric production of meaning and sense making (Derrida 1973). We have found that collective sense making through Twitter should be understood in the matrix of technolinguistic grammar (such as less than 140 characters input, #, RT, @, etc.) with its communicating media as its enabler (Oh et al. 2012). Following Derrida (1973), we believe that sense making and “[m]eaning can never be isolated or held in abstraction from its context, e.g., its linguistic, semiotic, or historical context” and “we stand within language, not outside it” (p. xxxviii). That is, collective sense making developed through Twitter, requires recognition of the existence of Twitter’s grammatical rules and its connective computing powers as its constraining enabler. In this regard, it is reasonable that Derrida (1973) prefers the term sign-ification to the human-centric “intended meaning” in that sense making develops through inseparable interplays of signs, grammar, humans, and social technologies. We agree that through the notion of sign-ification, we can better understand the nature of the human-machine entangled collective sense making whose scale was large and whose speed was fast in leading to the Egypt Revolution and the Arab Spring. Seen from the Derridian notion of sign-ification, the collective sense-making processes through Twitter can be rephrased as human-machine collaborative information processing that builds and maintains orderly structures through collective suggestions and selective writing practices using hashtags to ensure that messages are not drowned in a massive and chaotic slew of tweets.

In practice, our approach has a potential use during uncertain situations in extreme events. For example, during the crisis situation of a natural disaster such as Hurricane Sandy in 2012, rumors abounded<sup>7</sup> (Oh et al. 2013). Identification of ideas that have keynoted could allow governments to focus on them and develop response strategies to mitigate an extreme situation.

## Conclusion

This study has limitations, which give direction for future study. The first limitation is that this study focuses only on the analysis of hashtags to understand the development of collective sense making. We recognize that hashtag analysis is just one of many analyses that can explicate the emergence of collective sense making. For example, one can investigate whether power users directed the collective sensemaking process. In addition, if one confirms the existence of power users, one can further analyze them and their characteristics. To operationalize that analysis, power users could be defined as Twitter users who receive the highest number of retweets by many others (Oh et al. 2012).

A second limitation of this study is that we focused on the macrolevel time series view of the moving hashtag pattern. Given the complexity of humaninvolved collective sense making, we suggest that this study should be complemented by a microlevel analysis of the changing moods of tweet content. Given the large number of tweet messages, the cost of a microlevel content analysis might be high, especially if it involves manual human reading and coding. To overcome the high cost, one could (1) extract multiple samples from the same large data set, (2) analyze the changing mood for each different sample, and then (3) compare each content analysis result to obtain reliable and robust findings.

Motivated by the fact that the 2011 Egypt Revolution involved a large-scale social media campaign (Aouragh and Alexander 2011, Ghonim 2012, Howard et al. 2011, Lotan et al. 2011), we analyzed the collective sense-making processes by observing how people collectively and cooperatively used hashtags. Among 9,071 hashtags submitted by voluntary online citizens, we found that the top seven hashtags emerged as a proxy for the keynoting process that contributed to collective sense making. We also found that some hashtags were used mainly as symbolic anchors to funnel people’s attention to the Egypt Revolution, whereas other hashtags were used as words to share information about temporal situations. We suggest that the collective sense-making process can be understood as large-scale, real-time human-machine collaborative information processing to build and maintain orderly structures from a massive and chaotic volume of tweets so people can maintain a high level of awareness during an unstable political situation, such as during the 2011 Egypt Revolution.
