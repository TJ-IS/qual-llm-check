# A hermeneutic interpretation of a controlled laboratory experiment: a case study of decision-making with a group support systemis

Allen S. Lee\* & Alan R. Dennis<sup>†</sup>

\*Department of Information Systems, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia, USA, email: allenslee@alum.mit.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Operations and Decision Technologies, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana, USA, email: ardennis@indiana.edu

Abstract. We conduct a case study of a laboratory experiment involving a group support system and explain how it went awry. We take the perspectives of the experiment’s human subjects and the researchers themselves as the basis on which to interpret what happened in the experiment. We interpret the researchers as imputing, to the human subjects, the ‘conduit model’ of communication and the ‘calculator model’ of human information processing, which together constitute an instance of Ricoeur’s hermeneutic ‘world behind the text’. We interpret the human subjects as importing, into the laboratory, their socially constructed world of personal friends, their histories and even their popular culture – a world that is an instance of Ricoeur’s hermeneutic ‘world in front of the text’. We explain the experiment’s going awry as following from the researchers’ not accounting for, much less being aware of, the disparity between the two worlds. In taking the human subjects and the researchers seriously as human beings, we make recommendations about how such experiments might be better conducted, particularly in information systems research.

Keywords: information systems, research methods, group support systems, experiments, experimental design, hermeneutics, interpretation

## INTRODUCTION

What happens in a controlled laboratory experiment that involves human subjects? What roles do the subjective meanings held by the human subjects, along with those held by the researchers themselves, play in shaping the experimental outcomes? What light can qualitative and interpretive research, focused on not only the human subjects but also the researchers conducting the experiment, shed on these questions about quantitative and positivist research in the form of controlled laboratory experiments? We consider these questions in the study of a single case – namely, an experiment yielding results that were altogether unexpected by the researchers who conducted it. In particular, we take a hermeneutic approach to investigate what happened in a controlled laboratory experiment conducted by Dennis, Hilmer & Taylor (1998), who sought to demonstrate the decision-making benefits of information technology in the form of a group support system (GSS). We henceforth refer to this as the DHT experiment.

Subjectivity in scientific inquiry is typically regarded with suspicion for the bias it can introduce, but subjective meanings held by researchers also play a necessary and constructive role in how they do their work. Argyris & Schön (1978) have explained how a person’s implici ‘theories-in-use’ about his everyday life, even he is not aware of them, ‘govern his actions’. We consider a researcher who is conducting an experiment also to be a person, whose own ‘theories-in-use’ about the human subjects she is observing can, if made explicit and revised as needed, support and improve the research that she carries out in the positivist framework of her experiment. We emphasise that a researcher’s theory-in-use does not refer to the scientific theory that she builds or tests, but to her taken-for-granted beliefs about human nature and social reality, where these beliefs are so ingrained that she and her colleagues are typically not even aware of them.

Our case study’s intended contribution consists of two related lessons. One lesson pertains to the fact that subjective meaning is an objective reality (Lee, 2004); failure to acknowledge this fact can lead to an experiment’s going awry. The other lesson is that researchers can strengthen their controlled laboratory experiments by turning their attention to their own subjective understanding – the otherwise taken-for-granted theories-in-use they have about people and people’s behaviours, which are distinct from their scientific theories – to seek out a cause of the anomalous results.

Our case study proceeds as follows. First, we present details of the case. This includes an explanation of why this particular case is worthy of examination and a description of the experiment and its unexpected results. We then proceed with a hermeneutic interpretation of the experiment to make sense of these results. The interpretation makes explicit the researchers theories-in-use. Our purpose in doing the interpretation is to surface the researchers’ underlying beliefs, rather than to assess the quality of their research as would be done by a peer in their scientific community. In effect, throughout the case study, we observe Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor as human subjects themselves. We interpret their underlying beliefs as the ‘conduit model of communication’, the ‘calculator model of human information processing’, and the ‘rational GSS man assumption'. We offer alternatives to these beliefs and, to illustrate the research value of the case study, we offer a reformulation of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory to account for the additional factors that the case study uncovers. The reformulation will not be the replacement ot Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s original positivist theory by a hermeneutic interpretation, but rather, it will be their positivist theory adjusted and strengthened by insights from our hermeneutic interpretation. The strengthening of their positivist theory may be considered an instance of how interpretive research can complement and reinforce positivist research (Lee, 1991). Finally, our discussion and conclusions offer some guidance to researchers in information systems (and other fields) who conduct experiments involving human subjects.

## DETAILS OF THE CASE

A major stream of information systems research has been devoted to the study of GSS. GSS research is significant for having had a lengthy history and having involved some of the best information systems researchers for over two decades. We will not, however, provide a review of the GSS literature. The reason is that we are not conducting GSS research. Instead we are engaging in research on the research process itself, where the case that we study instantiates the research process. The instantiation involves particular GSS researchers and their experimental subjects. But to the extent that our empirical subject matter is about information systems, our study will require some discussion of, and make contributions to information systems research involving experiments including GSS research.

There are at least two good reasons for us to consider the DHT experiment to be a suitable case for studying what happens in a controlled laboratory experiment involving human sub jects. First, the DHT experiment qualifies as a good example of a ‘breakdown’ in research, where breakdowns can serve the research purpose of laying bare otherwise unseen processes and components. Simon (1981, p. 17) explains the observational value of a breakdown in his classic book The Sciences of the Artificial: ‘A bridge, under its usual conditions of service, behaves simply as a relatively smooth level surface on which vehicles can move. Only when it has been overloaded do we learn the physical properties of the materials from which it is built.’ Similarly, when research has been ‘overloaded’, its breakdown can usefully call attention to the properties of the ‘materials’ from which the research is built. Under the weight of empirical testing, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s research experienced overload in two ways. One is that their experiment failed to secure convincing evidence for seven of the eight hypotheses that they were advancing. Second, contrary to much of the spirit underlying GSS research in general, their experiment actually provided evidence that under one set of conditions, the use of information technology served to hurt, rather than benefit, decision-making. Thus, there were contradictory findings within the same study. Because of the major research breakdown, the DHT experiment presents rich material for reflection on research itself (as distinct from reflection on the empirical referents that the research investigates).

Another reason for investigating the DHT experiment is that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylo provided additional materials (including meeting transcripts and interviews) that facilitated ou study of the case. Without the additional materials, the case study would not have been feasible.

A description of some details of the case – the DHT experiment – follows.

Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor tested a positivist theory of group decision-making enabled by information technology (IT). The theory consisted of two parts. The first part is captured in Figure 1, which reproduces Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s own Figure 2. The second part of the theory focuses on the process by which a group comes to a decision, in contrast to the decision itself. It theorises that IT promotes the adequacy of the information exchanged, the adequacy of the criteria used and the adequacy of the information processing. This means that IT is theorised to improve the quality of the decision made by a group.

In their experiment, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor applied this theory to 17 different groups. Some groups used a GSS; the other groups did not. Also, some groups were structured so that a minority of the group members held key information for the group to make the optima decision (to see if use of the GSS would help to bring out this information); in each of the other groups, all the group members received the same or ‘uniform’ information. Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor derived eight hypotheses (pp. 67–69) about what their theory claimed would happen in this experiment, depending on whether or not a group had technology to support its decisionmaking and whether or not the group had a majority/minority split. The experimental results provided convincing evidence for only one of the eight hypotheses. Three hypotheses received only partial support. The remaining four hypotheses received no support at all. Perhaps the most troublesome and hence intriguing experimental result was the harmful, rather than helpful, role of IT under certain conditions (pp. 75–76, emphasis added): ‘In the majority/ minority treatment, five of eight groups made the optimal decision when using the GSS, but only two of these same eight groups made the optimal decision without the GSS.’ The reverse was true for the uniform groups: Only one of nine groups made the optimal decision when using the GSS, while seven of these same nine groups made the optimal decision without the GSS. In short, GSS use significantly helped the majority/minority groups but significantly hurt the uniform groups. ‘In other words, for groups in the uniform treatment, there was contradictory evidence taking the form of information technology that actually harmed a group’s decision-making.’ Insofar as Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory posited technology as having only beneficial effects, the contradictory evidence constituted an anomaly that the theory could not explain.

With the foregoing details of the case, we now proceed with a hermeneutic interpretation of it.

## A HERMENEUTIC INTERPRETATION OF THE DHT EXPERIMENT

The outcome of the DHT experiment was an overwhelmed theory that we may compare to Simon’s (1981) bridge. Similar to how the overloading of the bridge causes it to collapse but then opens up for observation the component materials from which it was built, the overwhelm ingly unsupportive evidence in the DHT experiment opens up for observation the assumptions from which the theory was built. We commence our observations by interpreting the artefacts (the many research documents) that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor left behind.

A particularly suitable form of interpretive research appropriate for our investigation is hermeneutics, which pertains to the study of the interpretation of text. We now (1) provide a description of hermeneutics; (2) present Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s own reflections on their experiment’s failure; (3) take advantage of their reflections as material from which we surface the theories-in-use underlying their thinking (the ‘conduit model’ of human communication and the ‘calculator model’ of human information processing) and (4) apply the hermeneutic concepts of the ‘world behind the text’ and the ‘world in front of the text’ to explain the evidence refuting Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theories-in-use and to propose an alternative (the ‘enactment model’).

## A description of hermeneutics

Hermeneutics is sometimes described as having originated as the study of the interpretation of the Bible (as distinct from the study of the Bible). In addition, the Greek word hermeneutice appears in the work of Plato, and Aristotle named one of his own works Peri hermeneias (Ramberg & Gjesdal, 2005). Hermeneutics is salient to our study of the DHT experiment because it produced several revealing texts: a published journal article, a set of instructions given to the human subjects participating in the experiment, a transcript of all the comments made by the human subjects when using a GSS and coding sheets filled out by Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor.

Hermeneutics is hardly limited to any single form (Mueller-Vollmer, 1994; Inwood, 2005). Some forms of it proceed on the basis that a text contains a single meaning as intended by the author, and therefore requires literal interpretation. Other forms free themselves from the strictures of any authorial intention and entertain the interpretation of whatever the text can come to mean to its readers. Some forms of hermeneutics move beyond the interpretation of texts and see the overall world as a ‘text analogue’ suitable for interpretation and, within these forms, there are scholars who presume the existence of an objective social reality while other scholars presume that all social reality is subjective and relative.

The academic discipline of information systems has long been aware of and accepted hermeneutical research, but has produced only a modest number of papers in which hermeneutical approaches have played a prominent role. Using ProQuest/ABI-Inform and Academic Search Complete, a search of the Association for Information Systems’ ‘basket’ of top journals reveals just 12 articles for which the term ‘hermeneut\*’ appears in the title or abstract: Butler (1998); Butler & Murphy (2007); Chalmers (2004); Cole & Avison (2007); Dickey et al. (2007); Huang & Watson (1998); Klecun-Dabrowska & Cornford (2000); Lee (1994a); Linden & Cybulski (2009); Tingling & Parent (2004); Whitley (1999); and Zahedi et al. (2006). Of course, there are also instances of hermeneutical information systems research appearing in articles published in other equally respected academic journals, in articles where the term ‘hermeneut\* does not appear in the title or abstract, and in articles appearing in volumes not yet electronically indexed; they include Boland & Day (1989); Boland et al. (1994); Davis et al. (1992); Klein & Myers (1999); Myers (1995); and Sarker & Lee (2006). In addition, there have been instances of hermeneutical information systems research appearing in book chapters and conference papers, such as Boland (1985; 1987; 1991); Lee (1993; 1994b); and Myers (2004). In some cases, the object of interpretation is text; in others, text analogues. Just a sample o these papers will be sufficient to indicate the breadth of different hermeneutic approaches an the ways in which they have been applied in information systems research.

Butler (1998) and Myers (2004) both provide reviews of hermeneutical research published in the discipline of information systems and elucidate basic hermeneutical concepts. Butle favours the approaches of Martin Heidegger and Hans-Georg Gadamer and illustrates his ideas empirically with a case study of systems development. Myers favours the approach of critical hermeneutics and illustrates his ideas empirically with a case study of a failed implementation of a payroll system which he previously published in Information and Organization.

Boland (1991) relies on the phenomenological hermeneutics of Paul Ricoeur. Boland pre sents accounting data – excellent for typifying the sort of data at the core of many information systems – and lays bare the research subjects’ fascinating interpretations of the mostly numerical text that was presented to them. The research subjects’ rich interpretations reflect their organisational lives as experienced managers and disabuse any notion that accounting data can be summarily dismissed as ‘cold, dead numbers’ (p. 453). Boland (1993) refers again to the hermeneutic interpretations of these research subjects in another study. To the extent that all information systems eventually end up presenting text, including numbers, which appear on screens or paper and which people then interpret, Boland’s seminal study firmly establishes how all information systems, and all information systems research, are ultimately hermeneutic.

Lee (1994a) and Klecun-Dabrowska & Cornford (2000) take a hermeneutic approach to interpret text and extend the interpretation to the social context that the text refers to.

Zahedi et al. (2006) combine Gadamer’s hermeneutics with grounded theory and semiology in order to interpret text that takes the form of Web documents.

Dickey et al. (2007) subscribe to the approach of cultural hermeneutics, originated by Alton Becker and Clifford Geertz. The key hermeneutic concepts here are ‘contextual relations’, taking the form of what they call coherence, invention, intention and reference. They perform hermeneutic interpretations of actual text-based chats (problematic for their ‘conversational incoherence and disorderliness’) between customer service representatives and customers, showing ‘how each of the four contextual relations mitigated failures or guaranteed suc cess[es]’ in establishing a shared perspective in the chat (p. 48).

Finally, Butler & Murphy (2007) take the approach of philosophical hermeneutics, which they describe as the hermeneutics of Hans-Georg Gadamer and Paul Ricoeur, in a participative action-research project involving the design and development of a knowledge-management tool.

In this study, we adopt Lee’s (1993; 1994a; 1994b) manner of doing hermeneutic interpre tation, where Lee follows Boland’s lead in relying on Ricoeur’s hermeneutic concepts of distanciation and appropriation, adopts an additional concept from Ricoeur (autonomisation), and applies Berger & Luckmann’s (1966) idea of social construction as an objective reality. Lee (1993; 1994a) shows an actual case of email communication to be inconsistent with a core assertion of media richness theory, which is that written text, compared to a face-to-face meeting, is only a ‘thin’ communication medium. In conducting a hermeneutic interpretation of the email collected in this case, for which Lee credits M. Lynne Markus, Lee demonstrates that email enables rich communication even on matters politically sensitive to the email users. An examination of Ricoeur’s concepts of distanciation, autonomisation and appropriation wil prepare us to introduce two more of Ricoeur’s concepts, the world behind the text and the world in front of the text, which will lead us to significant insights in our hermeneutic interpretation of the DHT experiment.

Distanciation (Ricoeur, 1981d) refers to the distance that emerges between the person who writes and the inscribed words themselves. Distanciation can occur even with verbal dialogue, where the distance would emerge between the person who speaks and the spoken words themselves (which can then travel, by word of mouth, to listeners whom the speaker never intended to address). However, distanciation is dramatised in the situation of writing, where the author is no longer present to explain or re-explain what the words mean. The Bible and the US Constitution provide good examples: Extensive distances have emerged in time, space and culture between the human beings who authored these documents and the documents as they survive today.

Autonomisation refers to a text’s taking on a life of its own, with the result that the text can support different meanings, regardless of the intentions of its originating author. Distanciation makes autonomisation possible. Ricoeur (1981a, pp. 200–201) states: ‘With written discourse, the author’s intention and the meaning of the text cease to coincide . . . [T]he text’s career escapes the finite horizon lived by its author. What the text says now matters more than what the author meant to say, and every exegesis unfolds its procedures within the circumference of a meaning that has broken its moorings to the psychology of its author.’ Again, the Bible and the US Constitution provide interesting examples: these documents are meaningful for contemporary readers in ways that might not, and need not, have ever occurred to or were intended by the originating authors. In autonomisation, a text comes to speak for itself, regardless of whatever its author meant.

Appropriation refers not only to a reader’s making the text her own when forming an understanding of it, but also to the text’s making the reader its own and even the text’s transformation of the reader. With regard to the former, Ricoeur states that to appropriate ‘means “to make one’s own” what was initially “alien” ’ (Ricoeur, 1981b, p. 185), so that ‘interpretation brings together, equalises, renders contemporary and similar’ (Ricoeur, 1981c, p. 159). With regard to the latter, the concepts of a world behind the text and a world in front of the text are important.

Lee (1994a, p. 150) describes Ricoeur’s world behind the text as follows: ‘. . . examples of . . . worlds standing behind a text are a world of science standing behind an article in the MIS Quarterly, a world of American civilization standing behind an article in Newsweek, and a world of spirituality standing behind the Bible.’ A key feature of a world behind a text is that it was already in existence prior to the author’s writing the text. It is the world of which the text is a product or artefact. It is the world of which the author is a member, and in a sense, the author acts as an extension or agent of this world. In this sense, a world behind the text appropriates the reader, transforming the reader and thereby shaping the understanding that he or she develops.

In appropriation, there is another world that the reader can enact. It is a world that is separate from the already existing world behind the text. It is what Ricoeur calls a world in front of the text or simply the ‘world of the text’. For an example, Ricoeur (1981c, p. 149) cites the world of the ancient Greek classics – which we take to mean, for instance, the world of Odysseus in The Iliad and The Odyssey. It is a virtual world, ‘a creation of literature’ (p. 149), which has never existed empirically, but nonetheless maintains a true existence in the collective milieu of texts (‘literature’) which we know as the Greek classics. The point is that a text is more than an artefact containing clues about the ‘real world’ of its author (the world behind the text); a text also allows the enactment of an altogether separate world in front of the text. Of course, a text need not be fictional (like The Odyssey) in order to allow readers to create a new world in front of the text, separate from and independent of the old world behind the text. Examples of this involve, again, the Bible and the US Constitution; both have allowed their readers to see and enact new worlds altogether separate from, and independent of, the old worlds in which the authors of these texts lived. These readers come to understand the texts even if they have no knowledge about texts’ authors, much less the authors’ cultures and societies.

For experiments about decision-making that use GSS technology, the hermeneutic concepts of the world behind the text and the world in front of the text are particularly relevant. Consider the document containing the instructions that a GSS researcher might write for his or her human subjects to read at the beginning of the experiment. The instructions might contain the assumption that people make decisions by reasoning in a logical fashion, where this assumption stems from the world behind the text as found in the published research literature in the researcher’s discipline. On the other hand, when read by the human subjects, the instructions (being words fixed in writing) can undergo distanciation and then autonomisation, with the result that the human subjects appropriate the text (the instructions) by enacting, as their world in front of the text. the socially constructed reality in which they happen to live and of which they themselves are products, even if decision-making in a logical fashion is not a part of it.

## Two theories-in-use by the experimenters

With the help of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s reflections, which they included in their published article, we proceed to surface the theories-in-use (not the scientific theories) underlying thei thinking. Below, we identify one such theory-in-use as guiding their understanding of how people (including participants in an experiment) communicate, and another theory-in-use as guiding their understanding of how people process information.

## The conduit model of human communication

‘Information exchange’, a key term in Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s study, is itself a telling artefact about their view of communication. They treat communication as the flow of data from one person to another, much as if it was the flow of a physical fluid through a pipe from one point to another. Consistent with their Figure 2, including their usage of the related terms ‘complete information’, ‘information exchanged’, and ‘adequate information exchange’, is a portrayal that likens people to computers: here, communication consists of the uploading and downloading of data between computers, and the quality of communication refers to whether the information in one computer is uploaded or downloaded to (i.e. ‘exchanged’ with) another computer so that, after all such uploading and downloading in a GSS meeting have taken place, enough of the data (‘an adequate amount of information’, p. 77) is transferred. This is a portrayal that some scholars call the ‘conduit model’ of communication and to which they offer alternatives (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997, who credit Contractor & Eisenberg, 1990).

In interpreting the ‘conduit model’ as Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory-in-use of human communication, we characterise them as taking the perspective that ‘a communication medium operates like a conduit that transports meaning from one person to another, as if the meaning were something physical’ (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997, p. 149). Regarding the conduit metaphor, the terms ‘complete information’, ‘information exchanged’ and ‘adequate information exchange’ suggest the underlying question: Was there just a trickle coming from the pipe, or did everything come through, or just enough? Our interpretation that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory-in-use of human communication was the ‘conduit model’ is consistent with their own reflections, reported in their published article, as well as with the res of the article.

There is additional documentary evidence – coding sheets – consistent with the interpretation that the ‘conduit model’ serves as Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory-in-use of communication. Some groups were given the task of selecting the best applicant out of four for admission to a university. The other groups were given the task of selecting the best brand of personal computer out of four different brands for a university to purchase. For both tasks, the researchers filled out coding sheets. Each group in the DHT experiment generated a transcript of the remarks that the group members (the human subjects in the controlled laboratory experiment) exchanged with one other via their workstations. In looking at hardcopies of some of the transcripts that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor used as their coding sheets, one sees that, next to one or another individual remark, they wrote one or more codes, such as ‘C15’ or ‘X11’. These codes refer to what Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor interchangeably call ‘facts’ and ‘information’, which describe particular features of the different student applicants (in the university admissions task) or the different brands of computers (in the computer purchasing task). For instance, in a set of written instructions that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor distributed to some (not all) of the human subjects, there was the piece of information that, for the ‘Beta’ brand of computer, ‘the company will send a service technican [sic] to repair the problem at the owners house or apartment’. On a hardcopy of the transcript that one GSS meeting generated, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor wrote the code ‘C15’ next to a participant’s remark, ‘House calls to fix computer’ and next to all other remarks also referring to this same particular information. For Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor, an instance of their inscription of a code (such as ‘C15’) next to a remark indicates that, from their perspective, certain information was ‘exchanged’ between group members or (in the terminology of the conduit model) ‘uploaded’ from one person (who typed it into his or her workstation keyboard) and then ‘downloaded’ to other persons (who obtained it from their workstation monitors).

Further operationalising Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory-in-use of human communication as the transporting of bits of information through a conduit is their research method of counting the number of times they wrote each code on their transcripts. They used the resulting frequencies to quantify the communication in a GSS meeting (see the heading ‘information exchange’ in their Table 2, page 74) where these quantifications, in turn, allowed Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor to test their two hypotheses that pertained to communication (see the headings ‘Common information’ and ‘Unique information’ in their Table 3, page 75).

In observing Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor as human subjects themselves, we have interpreted their theory-in-use of human communication as the ‘conduit model’. This will later help us to explain how their experiment elicited contradictory evidence. But first, we turn to anothe theory-in-use that we also observe in their work.

## The calculator model of human information processing

Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor conceptualised information processing as mathematical computa tions. They operationalised this conceptualisation, as revealed by Dennis to the first author of this article, by using a management-science multi-criteria decision-making model to establish benchmarks against which they measured the quality of the information processing performed by the human subjects (or group members) in the GSS meetings (see their Table 4, which is our Table 1). Specifically, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor used these benchmarks to determine, first, whether the criteria that the members used for making their decision (for instance, to purchase a computer of the Beta brand) were adequate (see the ‘Adequate criteria’ column in their Table 4), and second, whether the information processing by the group members was adequate (see the ‘Adequate info. processing’ column in their Table 4).

By establishing mathematical logic as the ideal for human logic to emulate and by which the quality of human information processing is to be measured, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor were actually treating human logic as an instance of the formal logic of the syllogism. This is the logic of applying a major premise (such as the formal statements of a mathematical model) to a minor premise (data, facts, information describing the particular real-world setting to which the mathematical model is being applied) in order to reach a conclusion (the decision identifying the best student to admit or the best brand of computer to purchase). Because Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor likened human logic to the logic of a management-science multi-criteria decisionmaking model, a fitting label for their conception of human logic is the ‘calculator model’ of human information processing. The term ‘calculator model’ also captures Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s conception of human beings as purely cognitive creatures – creatures who, in Dennis Hilmer and Taylor’s own terminology, ‘must exchange information appropriately, must use appropriate criteria, must process and use the information and criteria and must not be overly swayed by normative influence’ (p. 77).

Argyris & Schön (1978, p. 7) state ‘the theory that actually governs [a person’s] actions is his theory-in-use’. In our observations of the researchers Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor as human subjects themselves, we interpret that both of their theories-in-use – the ‘conduit model’ of human communication and the ‘calculator model’ of human information processing – governed their actions in developing and testing scientific theory. We will refer again to both theoriesin-use in our later discussion.

## Rational GSS man

We emphasise that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s presumption of rationality, along with their accompanying theories-in-use of human communication (the ‘conduit model’) and human information processing (the ‘calculator model’), is an altogether reasonable and standard feature of positivist theories of human behaviour. Perhaps the classic example of this is what microeconomic theory calls ‘rational economic man’. It is a portrayal of a human being who is a purely cognitive creature, who carries utility functions and indifference curves in his head and who possesses the computational capability necessary for applying marginal utility analysis to determine the minima, maxima, and intersection points of these curves. In the same spirit, we coin the term, ‘rational GSS man’, to describe Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s presumption of the ‘conduit model’ and the ‘calculator model’ in how a human being communicates and processes information in a GSS meeting. Rational GSS man has a calculator for a mind and a set of wires and cables (‘conduits’) at his disposal for communicating.

In the ongoing development of a positivist theory, the appearance of extensive unfavourable evidence in controlled empirical testing (as Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor experienced) can follow from a theory’s underlying assumptions’ being no longer sufficiently robust and becoming problematic in themselves. In this situation, a researcher would need to explore the possibility that her theory’s assumptions no longer hold. In the case of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor, we will notice (in the immediately following discussion) a disparity between what they assume (i.e. a member of a group is a rational GSS man, who employs the ‘conduit model’ for communicating and the ‘calculator model’ for information processing) and what we observe (i.e. the actual behaviours of the group members in the DHT experiment). This will call for us (1) to reassess Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s initial rational-GSS-man assumption and its accompanying theories-in-use – the ‘conduit model’ and ‘calculator model’ – of how group members communicate and process information; and (2) to interpret the actual process by which group members behave in the way that they do.

How might we explain the disparity between the actual behaviour of the human subjects and the behaviour expected from Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s rational GSS man?

A good starting point for explaining the disparity is the two hermeneutic concepts, the ‘world behind the text’ and the ‘world in front of the text’ (described earlier). This allows us to frame the disparity as originating in the distinction between the two worlds.

Regarding the text of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s written instructions to the group members (not the text of the transcript), we recognise the rational and even mathematical world of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor to be an instance of what Ricoeur’s hermeneutics would identify as a world behind the text. It is not a world that is randomly invented by the text’s author(s); it is an objective, pre-existing world that, as explained earlier, the author appropriates and that appropriates the author. In the case of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s document containing their instructions to the human subjects, the world behind the text is the world of GSS research from which they write up instructions for the participants in their experiments. It is a world made up of ‘rational GSS men who have calculators for minds and conduits for carrying data back and forth from each other.

However, the world behind the text, as the world from which an author writes, is distinct from the world in front of the text, which is the world that a reader of the text enacts and sees. As the transcript from the DHT experiment reveals (of which excerpts appear in Figures 2 and 3), the group members also enacted a world in front of the text (in particular, the text of the written instructions that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor had authored), where the enacted world consisted of the already existing world of the students. Hardly something that the group members merely imagined or made up, this was the actual, objective world where some of them lived in apartment complexes (‘Everybody come to 322 Landsend Road in Chelsea after this’), where they knew each other by name and had relationships with each other (‘JKR. are you and heather breaking up tonight?), where they shared a popular culture (‘Alread a pledge for Phi Slamma Jamma!’, a reference to a movie), where they followed sports (‘Susan Simms is Florida material, she’s dumber than Spurrier’), and so forth. The human subjects literally imported their own world – the world in front of the text–to the group’s meeting room. This enacted world was a phenomenon that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor never anticipated with their own world behind the text and its presumption of rational GSS man.

Immediately below, we identify three specific ways, involving the human subjects’ enactment of their world in front of the text, by which they reacted to Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s written instructions.

## Enacted information or tabula rasa?

We ask the reader to return to Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s Figure 2 (reproduced as our Figure 1). We present it as evidence consistent with the interpretation that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor conceptualised rational GSS man as a tabula rasa or ‘blank slate’ – a person without any task-specific knowledge (such as information from a life prior to or outside of the IT-supported group decision-making meeting) when he walks into an IT-supported group decision-making meeting, but during which all the knowledge he comes to possess is ‘downloaded’ to him eithe from the task’s written instructions or from the wires and cables (‘conduits’) that transport ‘data to him from other ‘rational GSS men’ in the meeting. Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s Figure 2 (ou Figure 1) allows no sources of knowledge from outside the GSS meeting itself. Figure 2 shows a closed system. In Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s depiction, nothing outside the system can or does enter any group member’s thinking. A conclusion we draw from their depiction is that, given the closed system, each and every rational GSS man has no history prior to his being in the IT-supported group decision-making meeting or (equivalently) that any information from a prio part of one’s life, if acknowledged to exist, does not enter into the information processing and communicating in which rational GSS man engages. Significantly, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s own phrase, ‘complete information’, is itself a telling artefact. It refers only and entirely to information contained in their instructions to the human subjects; it excludes any and all information from an experimental participant’s life prior to and outside of the experiment.

Consider again the remarks in Figure 2. The ‘information’ or ‘facts’ in these 14 remarks appear nowhere in Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s written instructions to the participants; they could not have originated in Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s closed system. Where, then, did these ‘facts’ come from? In contrast to the closed-system/tabula rasa assumption of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor, these facts all came from the objective world in which the students (the group members) were living. It was a world that was already in existence prior to the GSS meeting, that the group members shared, that they imported with them to the GSS meeting, and that they enacted in their reaction to the written instructions. It is what we may call the ‘world of the students’, which is the socially constructed and objective world in which they lived. It is a vast and entire civilisation that the group members carried with them to the meeting room and that they enacted in reaction to the researchers’ written instructions to them. Far from being a tabula rasa, each group member was already shaped by this world and filled with knowledge about it.

Hence, the above remarks in the transcript are evidence that human beings in an IT-supported group decision-making meeting are not ‘blank slates’ relying on conduits to deliver information to them, but already possess extensive knowledge prior to the meeting and import it with them to the meeting, whereupon this information permeates the human communication and human information processing that unfold during the meeting. This information is rooted in the world of which the meeting participants are already members, and which they literally bring to mind or ‘enact’ in the course of a GSS meeting. In contrast to the ‘condui model’ and ‘calculator model’, we will call this the ‘enactment model’ of human communication and information processing because the information comes from the world in front of the tex that the group members enact.

## Enacted criteria or tabula rasa?

In addition to the enactment of information, we can observe (Figure 3) that there was also the enactment of criteria (i.e. considerations for judging the university applicants).

The ‘criteria’ in these 13 remarks appear nowhere in Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s written instructions to the group members. These ‘criteria’ were not delivered to the group members via any ‘conduits’ from other group members or from Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s written instructions. Where, then, did these ‘criteria’ come from? Again, in contrast to Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s closed-system/tabula rasa assumption, these criteria came from the world of the students – the world in front of the text. The point is not that most of these criteria for selecting one candidate over another for admission to the university lacked a rational basis. The salien point is that these criteria had their origins in the already existing, objective world of which the participants in the meeting were members and on which they drew when enacting these criteria.

It was an objective world, existing prior to and outside of their IT-supported group decisionmaking meeting, supplying its own ‘criteria’ for judging applicants (such as, ‘IF WE LET HER IN WE WILL CATCH WHATEVER MAKES HER ITCH’). These were criteria that the group members imported to their meeting. Not all such ‘criteria’ were disdainful (‘Better SAT score than Josh Reid’; ‘You shouldn’t vote for Peter just because others are. See the facts’; ‘knows what he wants out of life’; ‘VOTE PETER ‘BECAUSE I’M HUNGRY’; ‘LET’S ALL VOTE FOR SUSAN AND GET OUT OF HERE!’; ‘He’s our man!!!’; ‘let’s get on with this already’). But again, the point is that the source of these ‘criteria’ was the ‘world of the students’ and not anything internal to the written instructions or other aspects of the GSS meeting.

Drawing on the objective world of which they were already members, the participants in the IT-supported group decision-making meeting did not wait for any ‘conduits’ to deliver the criteria to them; rather, they enacted criteria (just as they enacted information) from outside the strict confines of the setting of their laboratory experiment.

## Enacted logic or calculator model?

The logic by which the group members processed information diverged considerably from the rationality of the ‘calculator model’ that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory-in-use considered being hardwired into their presumed rational GSS man. Consider that much of the group members’ ‘reasoning’ did not appear relevant, in any serious way, to the task of selecting the best university applicant (e.g. ‘JKR. are you and heather breaking up tonight?’ and ‘VOTE PETER BECAUSE I’M HUNGRY’), but did have personal relevance in the socially constructed world to which the individuals belonged. The ‘logic’ by which some group members reasoned with their facts and criteria was sometimes apparently intended to incite other group members in ways that were seemingly irrational (‘Susan is one of those communists!’; ‘Alread a pledge for Phi Slamma Jamma!’). Consistent with this ‘logic’, some of the enacted information was obviously false, apparently designed to persuade by allegory or merely to inflame (e.g. ‘communists': I HEARD SUSAN IS A YANKEE': 'SUSAN HAS COMMUNICABLE DISEASES’: ‘Sleeps with instructors’; ‘BROWN-NOSER!’). Furthermore, some of the enacted ‘facts’ had no basis either in the text or in the human subiects' world (e.g. PRATT DID NOT PASS THE AP IN HIS FUTURE FIELD OF STUDY!’), where such false ‘facts’ (seen in every single transcript in the DHT experiment) may have been simple misunderstandings or may have been deliberate lies aimed at swaying other group members to the author’s preference. The group members thus did not employ the mathematical logic of a management-science multi-criteria decision-making model or the formal logic of the syllogism. Rather, they used a ‘logic’ that was entirely their own, enacted in their own world in front of the text.

## SUMMARY: HOW THE DHT EXPERIMENT WENT AWRY

From a hermeneutic perspective, a major reason that the DHT experiment yielded anomalous results is that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor did not account for, much less were aware of, a particular phenomenon associated with the experiment’s human subjects – namely, the world in front of the text. To the human subjects, the world in front of the text is their objectively existing world. Furthermore, like any other objective reality that an experiment deals with, it needs to be accounted for by the researchers who are observing the human subjects in the experiment. It is unsurprising that, in ignoring or not even being aware of a major facet of the objective reality, an experiment would go awry.

Much of the information and criteria that the group members used was not delivered to them via conduits from other group members and did not originate in the experiment as intended. Instead, it originated in each human subject’s past in the already existing, objective and shared ‘world of the students’ of which he or she was already a member. It was a world in front of the text that existed prior to and outside of the experiment, and that was meaningful to the human subjects who enacted it. Each group member was already, in a sense, a repository of information and criteria and did not rely on conduits to transport this knowledge to him or her. The transcript is consistent with the interpretation that the pre-existing repository of knowledge in the world in front of the text was so substantial that it displaced much of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s information (as contained in their instructions to the human subjects) from the human subjects’ discussion.

The transcript is also consistent with the interpretation that this displacement had the effect of wreaking havoc in the particular conditions that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor hoped to set up in their experiment. We can observe that the time and effort that the group members expended in the considerable non-rational portion of their activities had the effect of displacing the group’s consideration of task-relevant matters. On the one hand, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theoretical propositions conceptualised group members as communicating and processing only task-relevant information and criteria. On the other hand, the group members’ enactment of information, criteria and logic from their own, pre-existing shared world served to crowd ou or displace the empirical conditions that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor intended to be present in their experiment.

We see no indication that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor had anticipated that their instructions, as a stimulus applied to the experimental subjects, would elicit from them a response involving a whole, different world separate from their own. Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor simply did not account for this other world. In general, not accounting for any of the major factors present in an experiment will lead to unexpected and unsuccessful results. In this regard, it is useful to take a second look at their eight hypotheses. Four of them presumed the ‘conduit model’ of communication. Given that this mode of communication was largely displaced by the group member’s non-rational enactment of extra-experimental knowledge, it is no surprise then that the experiment resulted in only partial support for one of these hypotheses and no support at all for the remaining three. The remaining four hypotheses presumed the ‘calculator model’ of information processing. Given that this mode of information processing, too, was largely displaced by the group member’s enactment of extra-experimenta criteria and logic, it is unsurprising that the experiment resulted in only partial support for two of these hypotheses and no support at all for one of them. Only one received convincing support.

For the researchers conducting the experiment, the world behind the text–consisting of the state of the art of their research discipline – and its variance with the world in front of the text can be things that they are not consciously aware of. In so far as researchers are also human beings, the subjective meanings that they themselves bring to an experiment can be a cause of the experiment’s going awry. In this event, they may fruitfully turn their attention to their own subjective understanding to search for a possible cause of the anomalous results. Furthermore, because Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s subjective understanding presumed a condui model, a calculator model and rational GSS man–none of which existed – it is no wonder that their experiment failed.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Subjective meanings are objective realities. The meanings held by a human subject in a controlled laboratory experiment may or may not be particularly rational, but they nonetheless exist as part of the empirical phenomenon that the researcher, conducting the experiment, is observing. Our case study has shown that subjective meanings, if unaccounted for, can lead a controlled laboratory experiment to go awry. It therefore behoves experimenters to observe (and, if they so wish, develop ways to measure) subjective meanings no less than any othe objective feature of the subject matter that they are investigating.

Furthermore, researchers who are conducting an experiment are no less ‘natives’ than are the human subjects in the experiment. Researchers hold and act on subjective meanings of their own no less than experimental subjects do. Taking the form of tacit and unexamined theories-in-use about human behaviour (e.g. the premise that a human being is a ‘rational economic man’), a researcher’s own subjective meanings can, if unaccounted for, also con tribute to the experiment’s going awry.

Beyond the foregoing general conclusions, we offer some additional points specific to the DHT experiment and also to all experiments in which human subjects are asked to perform a task. The first point is that we can revise Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s theory by adding to it as indicated in Figure 4, where the additional elements are indicated in dashed lines. In other words, we are using our hermeneutic interpretation not to replace, but actually to improve and strengthen Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s positivist theory. This is an instance of how interpretive research can complement and reinforce positivist research as described by Lee (1991).

The additional elements refer to the information and criteria that the participants in an IT-enabled group decision-making meeting can import from the already existing socially constructed world (such as a society, community or organisation) of which they have been members. By our interpretation, the ‘complete information’ and ‘complete criteria’ that Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor originally conceptualised (see Figure 1) were not complete at all. Instead, the information and criteria used in IT-supported group decision-making consist of not only task-relevant information and task-relevant criteria (provided in the instructions to the group members), but also information and criteria that group members import from their own socially constructed worlds. In the case of the DHT experiment’s human subjects, all of whom were students, the imported information and criteria were largely irrelevant to the task and happened to displace the relevant information and criteria. However, this need not be the general case. In a group different from the one examined above, Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor observed that a member, who happened to grow up on a farm, shed light on the qualifications of a college applicant who also grew up on a farm, thereby facilitating the decision-making in this instance. Therefore, according to our extended version of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s original theory, imported information and criteria are, in themselves, not inherently beneficial or detrimental; their impact can depend on their relevance to the task.

The extended theory also provides a way to account for both the explicit and the tacit knowledge that managers in a corporation would bring with them to an IT-supported group decision-making meeting. The additional knowledge would stem from the socially constructed world of the particular corporation – including its culture, social structure and history – of which the managers have been members. In Figure 4, this knowledge would appear in the top row of additional boxes, drawn in dashed lines. Hence, our extension of Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor’s original theory also bears potential significance for real-world decision-making outside of university research settings.

Following from the extended theory is a related point about the research value in observing natural groups. Some have argued that the use of students as human subjects can be problematic because, unlike managers, they lack experience with the task and thus would not approach the task in the same way that managers do (Gordon et al., 1987; Greenberg, 1987). Most of these arguments have been based on task-specific skills and knowledge. We agree that it would be preferable in general for experiments involving human subjects to draw their research participants from the population of people to whom their theories actually refer (i.e. managers in decision-making positions in corporations and other organisations), but there is at least one redeeming virtue in the use of student participants in the DHT experiment. We offer the perspective that these students did form a natural group: they possessed and shared a ‘world in front of the text’ that already naturally existed prior to and outside of the experiment and that they applied to the experimental task.

This perspective makes a difference to the often-heard criticism that laboratory research using ad hoc groups, including student groups, built especially for a specific study produces ‘unnatural groups of individuals who have no history before the study and no future expected after the study. Such ad hoc groups, it has been argued, may behave differently from groups that share a history and an expected future. Our interpretation of the transcript of such an ad hoc group in the DHT experiment suggests something quite different. Contrary to these arguments, we see a set of individuals with a shared past and expected future and the ability to use that shared history to communicate. So rather than describe student groups as ‘sterile’ and ‘ad hoc’ as mentioned in the research methodology literature, we instead see a living group quite reminiscent of the putative ‘natural’ groups in organisations. This point applies to all 17 groups in the DHT experiment, not just the one whose transcript we have examined.

Furthermore, members of a group will always be enacting one world or another whenever they gather for a meeting. Ad hoc groups are not and cannot be the ‘pure’ tabula rasa presumed in the research literature; no researcher can (or even should) banish such worlds from an experiment. The concept of adaptive structuration is well accepted in terms of work processes in that researchers understand that groups have existing structures and habitual routines. and when presented with a GSS. selectively choose what elements of the GSS to appropriate into those structures and routines (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Our case study suggests that, in the same way that groups appropriate a GSS and the GSS’ structures, they also appropriate the task and the task’s structures. The same task presented to different groups from different backgrounds would elicit the enactment of different worlds in front of the text – worlds with information and criteria that differ in meaningful ways from the worlds intended by the researchers.

In experiments where human subjects are asked to perform a task, the phenomenon of task appropriation is, in itself, sufficiently important that it warrants focused attention from the researcher. One reason for this is that unless one understands how the participants in an experiment appropriate the experimental task, one could only understand with difficulty, if at all, how the experimental treatment affects the processes and outcomes of the participants’ work on the task. The observations in our case study are consistent with the notion that a non-managerial way of approaching the task follows from how a particular world is, or is not, enacted in front of the text. University students, as human subjects, do not, cannot, and therefore should not be expected to enact the same world in front of the text in exactly the same way that managers from an actual company would; however, these subjects still enact a world in front of the text.

The second reason, following from the first, is that the researcher should consider designing the experimental task so as to induce the human subjects to enact an appropriate world in front of the text–that is, appropriate for the theory being tested. This does not necessarily require the same world in front of the text as a group of managers would enact; such a goal would be nearly impossible. Rather, the goal would be to induce the human subjects to enact a world in front of the text that is appropriate for the theory being tested, where observations of the experimental results would allow the researchers to generalise to theory (Yin, 1994; Lee &

Baskerville, 2003). For example, if a theory is designed to explain behaviour in organisationa settings familiar to the participants, then the task should be designed to invoke a world in fron of the text that is familiar to the human subjects. For the majority of experimental research whose goal is to understand and predict behaviour in familiar organisational settings, the tasks used in the experiments should be designed to induce participants to enact a familiar world in front of the text. Simply put, experimental tasks must live in the students’ worlds (cf. Gordon et al. 1986) if students are the human subjects.

Third, researchers should consider the additional research step of determining whether the task appropriated by the experimental participants is markedly different from the one that the researchers designed. A number of participants in the DHT experiment used ‘facts’ about a made-up character in the task to draw unwarranted conclusions (at least, unwarranted from the viewpoint of the researchers who designed the task) about that character’s ability to play for the university football team. which at the time was suffering from a lack of skilled running backs Also, participants in both the GSS and non-GSS treatments made misstatements of fact in about equal numbers (according to data analyses conducted by Dennis, Hilmer and Taylor that were not included in the published article); some probably did this accidentally and some probably intentionally. In controlled laboratory experiments involving human subjects, it therefore becomes important to conduct a manipulation check to ensure that the task presented to the subjects induces them to enact a world in front of the text that is what the experimenter intends – or at least one that does not conflict with the theory being tested.

The fourth and last point about task appropriation is that participants in GSS groups may have a greater ability to enact a task different from the one presented by the experimenter than do members of non-GSS groups. Compared to non-GSS group members, members of GSS groups have greater bandwidth available for communication and can therefore extend the task by sharing imported information and criteria. This would allow them to engage in non-task relevant personal conversations without directly limiting other participants’ ability to speak. For example, imagine the possible reaction of a group to someone discussing at length a potentially tangential issue (such as their upbringing on a farm) in a GSS setting where many people can ‘speak’ at once electronically vs. a non-GSS setting where only one person can hold the floor. GSS groups are more likely to tolerate some digression because it does not prevent others from speaking. Furthermore, members of GSS groups can take advantage of anonymity to shield themselves from the possible negative reactions or sanctions from other participants that may arise if they import their own world. Previous research has shown that the use of GSS creates a more participatory environment in which individual members of GSS groups are more likely to have greater influence on the processes and outcomes of group discussion than those in authority (Dennis & Garfield, 2003).

In our case study of a laboratory experiment, we have observed that members of decisionmaking groups import information and criteria from their already existing socially constructed world into the laboratory setting. However, this phenomenon is not necessarily limited just to groups. We speculate that participants in other forms of laboratory experiments (e.g. individua decision-making) also can import information and criteria from their world into the laboratory setting and that this may impact those studies as well.

Our case study has involved (1) investigating and surfacing a set of GSS researchers underlying and previously unarticulated assumptions, which we consider to fall under Argyris and Schön’s ‘theories-in-use’; (2) discarding the GSS researchers’ theories-in-use when observations refute it; and (3) proposing changes to methodology and theory that reflect these. We suggest that our perspective on the DHT experiment be tried in other controlled laboratory experiments involving human subjects, where the experiments (1) presume ‘rational economic man’ or a variant, along with its associated ‘conduit model’ of communication and ‘calculato model’ of information processing; or (2) use university students as human subjects who appropriate the experimental task in ways unanticipated by the experimenters. Our study can lead to insights and clues into inconsistent results incurred in such experimentation.
