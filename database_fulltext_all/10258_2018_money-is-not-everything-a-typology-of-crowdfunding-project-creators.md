# Money is not everything: A typology of crowdfunding project creators

Sunghan Ryu<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Young-Gul Kim<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> USC−SJTU Institute of Cultural and Creative Industry, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China

<sup>b</sup> Graduate School of Information and Media Management, KASIT, Republic of Korea

## ARTICLE INFO

Keywords: Crowdfunding Creator Entrepreneurship Typology Motivation Investor

## A B S T R A C T

While studies focus on how crowdfunding promotes the launch of innovative products or services through two-sided (creator-investor) platforms and digital tools, knowledge on the creator-side motivation is limited. To address this knowledge gap, our study identifies four types of crowdfunding project creators—social entrepreneur, fund seeker, indie producer, and daring drea mer—based on four motivations—achievement, monetary need, prosociality, and relationship building. We also examine how crowdfunding projects’ characteristics difer by the creator type and which characteristics are critical for enhancing projects’ performance. The study extends crowdfunding literature by deepening knowledge of creator-side motivation and contributes to crowdfunding practices by suggesting platform operators ways to attract and promote diferent types of project creators.

## 1. Introduction

While traditional sources of financing such as banks are confined to more established firms, amidst advances in enabling digital technologies, crowdfunding is emerging as a new source of financing for both business and social innovations (Allison et al., 2015; Burtch et al., 2014). It promotes the launch of innovative products or services by providing two-sided (creator-investor) platforms and flexible digital tools. For example, since its founding in 2009, Kickstarter, a leading global crowdfunding platform, has raised more than 2.9 billion USD for 130,000 projects from 13 million investors.<sup>1</sup> Moreover, various governments have promoted this new funding source for diverse entrepreneurial activities. With regards to regulations, the Jumpstart Our Business Startups (JOBS) Act was passed in the US in 2012 to boost economic growth by loosening regulations on the crowdfunding for small, startup firms. The South Korean government also passed a bill to legalize crowdfunding platforms in 2015

Crowdfunding has also received increasing attention from scholars of diferent disciplines including information systems (IS) and entrepreneurship. The main literature on crowdfunding has identified factors afecting the success of crowdfunding projects (Burtch et al., 2013; Colombo et al., 2015; Mollick, 2014). For example, project creators’ personal networks and expressed project quality are significantly associated with project success (Mollick, 2014). Colombo et al. (2015) examined the importance of project creators’ internal social capital in the crowdfunding platform and its investment in the early phases of the project to project success. Generally, studies highlight the importance of explicit drivers in determining project success

Since crowdfunding platforms introduce diverse projects, creators are likely to participate in crowdfunding under diferent motivations (Gerber et al., 2012; Gleasure, 2015). For example, creators may launch their projects for raising funds, connecting with potential customers, testing product concepts, or promoting product awareness. Creators’ motivations should influence their selection of behavioral actions for progressing with crowdfunding projects, as motivation is the psychological force that directs and explains individual behaviors (Gagné and Deci, 2005). Since creators can intentionally manipulate project components in early project stages, the selection of behaviors based on creators’ motivations is likely to be associated with project characteristics and thus success (Hsu, 2007; Rauch et al., 2009). However, the existing literature has paid little attention to whether and how creator motivations are related to project characteristics and crowdfunding success. Very few studies have empirically examined the role of creator motivations in crowdfunding (Calic and Mosakowski, 2016; Gleasure, 2015). To supplement this limited body of literature and extend our knowledge on the role of creator motivations in the crowdfunding context, this study aims to empirically validate the association between creator motivations, project characteristics, and crowdfunding project performance.

Since diferent creators should be motivated in diferent ways, this study will develop a typology of crowdfunding project creators based on the theories of motivation and the literature on typologies of entrepreneurs, assuming that a majority of the crowdfunding projects are entrepreneurial in nature. Formulating a typology of crowdfunding creators is expected to make a fundamental contribution to the exploration of whether and how creator motivations influence the characteristics and performance of the crowdfunding projects (Zahra et al., 2009). The categorization of project creators into diferent types can help us to examine how project characteristics difer across creator types. Finally, we check whether projects with diferent project characteristic values difer in performance to validate the essence of this study’s proposition: Do creator motivations matter?

Our study makes several contributions to research and practice. First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first study to empirically examine the existence of diverse creator motivations and diferent creator groups in crowdfunding platforms, and hence the study extends the crowdfunding literature. Second, we extend the literature on entrepreneurship and entrepreneur typologies by using the matched sample of creators and their investors. The study also has practical implications. Understanding the diverse needs of diferent creator types will enable crowdfunding platform operators to focus on proper targets and provide a customized service for each group.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Crowdfunding

The main literature on crowdfunding highlights factors crucial for determining the success of a crowdfunding project. First, a strand of research found that efective dissemination of information regarding creators and their project through the crowdfunding platform is critical to crowdfunding success (Mollick. 2014). For example, information on project quality and the creator's social information serve as efective cues for potential investors (Ciuchta et al., 2016). Additionally, providing detailed information about risks strongly impacts project success (Ahlers et al., 2015). Information on project progress in the crowdfunding platforms is another influential factor. Investor participation in early project stages is essential for signaling project quality and subsequently attracting more investors (Agrawal et al., 2015; Burtch et al., 2013; Colombo et al., 2015). For example, Kim and Viswanathan (2018) showed that information about early investors with expertise has a distinct influence on later investors. It is also established that peer efects drive demand for a crowdfunding project, pointing toward the efect of social influence on individual funding decisions (Liu et al., 2015).

Second, it has been found that diferent types of creators’ networks act as key influencers of crowdfunding success. For example, based on the social capital theory, Zheng et al. (2014) examined how project creators’ social network ties, experience in funding other projects, and the shared meaning between creators and investors impact crowdfunding success. Particularly, internal social capita developed inside a crowdfunding community contributes significantly toward triggering “a self-reinforcing mechanism” of a crowdfunding project, and the relationship between internal social capital and the success of a project is fully mediated by the investors and their funding amounts in the project’s early stage (Colombo et al., 2015). In the same vein, investors are more likely to contribute to “culturally similar and geographically proximate” creators’ projects (Burtch et al., 2014). The distance between a creator and potential investors is important in explaining social influence. Local investors are more likely to pledge at the initia stages of the funding period when compared to distant investors. They are also less sensitive to information about the cumulative amount of funding (Agrawal et al., 2015; Lin and Viswanathan, 2015).

In sum, the main research streams on crowdfunding have focused on diferent types of explicit factors related to creators and their projects afecting the success of a crowdfunding project, but little attention has been paid to the creator’s motivation. Creators launch crowdfunding projects under diferent motivations (Gerber et al., 2012). In addition to raising funds, the awareness generated through investors and project feedback are also valuable to creators, and such motivations work in diferent ways (Stanko and Henard, 2017). Despite the foreseeable benefits, some creators resist launching a project on crowdfunding platforms. Gleasure (2015) focused on “non-adopters"” of crowdfunding and found that such resistance is shaped by the creators' fear of disclosure, exposure to failure, and the appearance of desperation. As discussed, a few pioneering studies dealt with creators’ motivations in the crowdfunding context, but the literature has not established how those motivations interact with other factors, such as whether and how diferent creator motivations afect project characteristics and crowdfunding success. To fill this gap, we examine how diferent creator motivations interrelate with project characteristics and funding success by constructing a typology of crowdfunding creators based on their motivations. ## 2.2. Typologies of entrepreneurs

The use of typologies as means of explaining diferent types of entrepreneurs is common in entrepreneurship research (Jones-Evans, 1995; Miner, 2000; Routamaa and Vesalainen, 1987; Woo et al., 1991; Zahra et al., 2009). A typology of entrepreneurs has high theoretical and practical potential. An early example is Smith (1967), which distinguished between craftsperson and opportunistic entrepreneurs based on diferences in entrepreneurial characteristics such as social competence and time orientation. Following studies suggested typologies based on matching characteristics of entrepreneurs (Lafuente and Salas, 1989; Miner et al., 1992; Routamaa and Vesalainen, 1987). Another approach to classifying entrepreneurs is focusing on a distinct form of entrepreneurship. For example, a typology of technical entrepreneurs has been developed according to their background (Jones-Evans, 1995), including “researcher,” “producer,” “user,” and “opportunist” technical entrepreneurs. Emphasizing social entrepreneurship’s importance in tackling social problems and enriching communities and societies, a study ofered a typology of entrepreneur search processes that lead to the discovery of opportunities for starting a social enterprise (Zahra et al., 2009); the results disclose three types of socia entrepreneurs—“social bricoleurs,” “social constructionists,” and “social engineers.”

While those studies attempted to build typologies based on explicit factors, such as the background of entrepreneurs, other researchers have focused on diferentiating between entrepreneurs on the basis of internal characteristics such as motivations and intentions. For example, Dubini (1989) categorized the following three entrepreneur types according to their motivations for starting a new enterprise. “Self-actualizers” start their business driven by the achievement motivation, a sense of independence, and autonomy, whereas “discontented entrepreneurs” discover startups due to a lack of career alternatives; a strong sense of “famil tradition” motivates the last type of entrepreneur. Miner (2000) focused on four diferent personality types (i.e., personal achievers, real managers, expert idea generators, and empathetic super salespersons) and explained how these types are related to the business: founding activities of graduate business students. The results showed that those belonging to one or more of the types are more likely to be entrepreneurs after graduation: the results also showed that tolerance of risk, perceived feasibility, and net desirability sig nificantly predict the intention to start a business (Segal et al., 2005).

Based on approaches and findings from the literature of entrepreneur typologies, our study attempts to identify diferent types of creators in the crowdfunding context. Specifically, by referring to the studies on entrepreneurial motivations (e.g., Dubini, 1989), we focus on creator motivations as main variable for constructing a typology. Key findings of the prior literature on entrepreneurship typologies and our study’s expected contributions are summarized in Table A.2 of Appendix A.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation

## 3.1. Diverse motivations of crowdfunding project creators

Motivation has been an essential theme in various academic fields. Since it explains the process directing actions, understanding of motivation is important for predicting individual behavior (Gagné and Deci, 2005). Among diverse motivational frameworks, we select the following two dominant motivation dimensions to classify the motivations of crowdfunding creators: intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation and self-oriented vs. others-oriented motivation (Ryu and Kim, 2016). The division between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in the self-determination theory (SDT) is one of the most frequently referenced frameworks in the motivation literature (Dec and Rvan, 1985: Rvan and Deci, 2000a. 2000b). Earlier, researchers argued that external factors such as rewards and incentives were required to motivate behavior (Steers et al., 2004). This process, called “extrinsic motivation." represents the desire to make an effort to earn outcomes external to the behavior itself (Bock et al., 2005: Ryan and Deci, 2000a). However, as their view of human nature changed, researchers proposed that behaviors can be non-extrinsically motivated (Deci and Ryan, 1985; Ryan and Deci, 2000a, 2000b). The literature confirms that an individual is motivated by own interest in or enjoyment of a behavior or by internalizing the value of the behavior (Gagné and Deci, 2005). From a diferent perspective, depending on its orientations, motivation can be divided into self-oriented motivation and others-oriented motivation (Hemetsberger, 2002). Self-oriented motivation involves a direct link between an actor and an obiect such as a task or a reward. while others-oriented motivation is related to the actor's social and emotional connections around the object. People are either self-interested or social in nature with strong others-orientation (De Dreu, 2006). For example, personal initiative, job performance, and prosocial behavior are a function of individual-level characteristics when a person has a high self-orientated motivation, but they are a function of group-level attributes when a person has a high othersoriented motivation (De Dreu and Nauta, 2009). Others-oriented individuals tend to ensure that others are comfortable and happy and they sympathize with others, while self-oriented individuals tend to be less agreeable (Nettle, 2007). The literature suggests that the distinction between self-oriented and others-oriented motivation might be useful for understanding the factors influencing individuals’ decisions (Hemetsberger, 2002).

Combining these motivation dimensions, we derived a two (intrinsic ys. extrinsic) by two (self-oriented ys. others-oriented) motivation matrix (Brüggen et al., 2011; Holbrook, 2002; Ryu and Kim, 2016). Based on the literature review and interviews with selected creators and platform operators, we posited four creator motivations in the motivation matrix: achievement (intrinsic and self-oriented), monetary need (extrinsic and self-oriented), prosociality (intrinsic and others-oriented), and relationship building (extrinsic and others-oriented). Achievement motivation refers to a desire to accomplish a goal, and therefore to achieve the objective of a project (Vallerand et al., 1992). A creator with a high-level of achievement motivation engages in crowdfunding to experience satisfaction while attempting a challenge or creating something new. The number of investors and amount of money raised are often seen as indicators of the project achievement. Creators’ self-belief increases when they experience success.

The monetary need is a critical motivation for most creators. It operates when raising financial resources for a project assumes urgency (Gerber et al., 2012). This motivation is associated with various fundraising contexts (Davila et al., 2003). Creators are motivated to use crowdfunding because it provides an organized way to obtain financial support from the public in a network. Crowdfunding is particularly useful for creators who cannot receive funding from traditional financing sources. It allows creators to raise similar amounts of funds through many investors each contributing small amount.

Prosociality motivation is the will to benefit a society voluntarily. This motivation focuses on the goal of promoting the welfare of communities and the society. It is triggered by encounters with social issues that require member participation (Grant, 2008). While many creators are motivated to achieve for themselves, some are motivated to help others. This motivation resembles the philanthropic motivation. Such creators express a strong desire to help others and are willing to prioritize others. Finally, relationship building motivation refers to an association with other people, particularly investors in the crowdfunding context. This motivation is related to social aspects of human behavior, including emotional interaction and social bonding (Ko et al., 2006). In addition to raising funds, creators are motivated to participate in crowdfunding to connect with people. As most crowdfunding platforms provide online communication services, they allow creators to communicate with investors during and/or after the project period by answering questions or updating them on the project’s progress. ## 3.2. Crowdfunding project characteristics

On the basis of three generic criteria for venture capital (VC) investments (i.e., concept, management, and returns), as proposed by Fried and Hisrich (1994), we suggest the following three dimensions of criteria for evaluating the characteristics of a crowdfunding project: concept, creator, and, returns. We then select the five project characteristics based on the three dimensions: novelty and societal contribution (of concept), trustworthiness and activeness (of creators), and feasibility and reward value (of returns). Novelty refers to the degree to which a project’s ideas and objectives are new and original (Koslow et al., 2003). In the crowd participation context, novelty is very important for promoting the participation of competitive users (Leimeister et al., 2009). For VCs pursuing high-risk and high-return strategies, novelty plays an essential role in investment decisions (Hisrich and Jankowicz, 1990). For crowdfunding projects, novelty also plays a key role because it draws the curiosity and interest of potential investors. A crowdfunding project perceived as innovative or unprecedented can gain public attention, while tedious projects are ignored. Societal contribution is the degree to which a project has positive efects on the community or society (Reynolds, 1987). The charity-related nature of crowdfunding plays an important role when investors determine their pledge (Belleflamme et al., 2014). This characteristic is essential to charity projects and strongly influences the participants of crowd-driven projects. As the crowdfunding mechanism is mainly based on collective behavior, a project associated with social causes or movements can expect more fundraising momentum. Trustworthiness is a vital criterion determining investors' attitudes toward creators and their projects (Belanger et al., 2002). Similar to the criterion that VCs use to appraise startup founders (Fried and Hisrich, 1994), trustworthiness refers to the extent to which creators deserve to be viewed as reliable and worthy of investors’ confidence (Sheinin et al., 2011). Activeness indicates the level of a creator’s enterprising attitude (Koh and Kim, 2003). This characteristic is required of startup founders who need to show their diligence (Fried and Hisrich, 1994). Along with trustworthiness, activeness can play an important role in lowering the perceived risk for potential crowdfunding investors. This is because information on creators is less available, while information on funding progress is salient and easily available (Burtch et al., 2013). Feasibility. from the perspective of returns, reflects the possibility of a project being accomplished as promised, and it is a requisite for persuading down-to-earth investors (Krueger, 1993). Estimating the possibility of success is an indispensable process for investors (Macmillan et al.. 1985: Tversky and Kahneman. 1974). Reward value in return for funding is another standard used to assess the attractiveness of a project. An important obligation of creators is to design suitable rewards for investors and to deliver them promptly. Similar to feasibility, reward value is the dominant index (i.e., expected return on investment) that leads to a decision of whether to make an investment (Fried and Hisrich, 1994). But, crowdfunding investors do not care solely about financial returns, unlike traditional investors, and they may rely on diferent quality information when making decisions (Lin et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2015).

## 3.3. Creator motivations, project characteristics, and performance

Given the relationship between motivation and behavior (Arazy and Gellatly, 2012: Terborg and Miller, 1978), creator motivations should determine how creators design and prepare their projects. This includes selecting reward types or adjusting the level of investor participation. It is well-established that entrepreneurs influence the cultures and activities of their startups (Mullins. 1996). As a unique feature of each creator, diferent motivations may crystallize activities for a crowdfunding project and lead to the implementation of those purposeful and intended activities. Motivations can be regarded as a strong discriminator between the diferent characteristics of crowdfunding projects. Specifically, each motivation may impact the corresponding project characteristics by driving creators’ commitment to the associated aspects of projects.

While the association between creator motivations and project characteristics is complicated and not purely independent of external influencers, potential links can be proposed based on the related literature. For example, achievement motivation is expected to gain the trustworthiness of investors for creators and their projects. If projects are essential to creators, as revealed through the significance creators place on achievements, such as obtaining personal goals and visions, then the creators will undertake projects with greater authenticity, and potential investors may perceive the projects to be more trustworthy (Belanger et al., 2002; Strader and Ramaswami, 2002). The potential efect of the creators’ monetary need on the characteristics of their projects is clear and straightforward. Creators will commit to the projects to obtain desired outcomes, similar to the entrepreneurs with high-growth intention who invest more efforts in their businesses (Cassar. 2007: Davidsson. 1989). Such commitment includes designing attractive rewards for crowdfunders’ contribution and developing and maintaining efective communication channels with potential crowdfunders. Creators with a greater monetary need are expected to attract more crowdfunders by indicating how they can address potential risks attributed to their competency and experience (Yang and Hahn, 2015). Prosociality motivation may drive the creators to add more components to their projects that contribute to communities and society (Allison et al., 2015). This will enable creators to fulfill their motivation. A related association can also be observed between prosociality and trustworthiness. As prosociality motivation is a type of others-oriented motivation. investors are more likely to consider such projects as being more reliable than the others (Van den Bos and Lind, 2001). Finally, when creators’ relationship building motivation is formed, they tend to develop their projects in such a way that allows investors to develop a relationship with them or with other investors (Stanko and Henard, 2017). These projects are more likely to be characterized by a keen sense of community-building and social networking (Bateman et al., 2011). As a result, in this context, potential crowdfunders may regard the creators as being more active than the others in terms of delivering their projects and better understanding their needs.

Based on the arguments presented, the following proposition is proposed.

Proposition 1. Creators’ motivations are reflected in their respective crowdfunding projects’ characteristics because creators are likely to set plans and behave according to their own motivations. Creators can fulfill their motivations throughout the crowdfunding process by planning and conducting the projects according to their motivations.

Founder motivations are important indicators of the startup's performance (Birley and Westhead. 1994). Previous literature examined how these motivations are associated with growth intention and subsequent growth achievement (Kolvereid, 1992). Moreover, as motivations for initiating a startup are not homogeneous, this association between founder motivations and the startup’s performance varies across diferent types of motivations (Cassar, 2007). Creator motivations may play a similar role in the context of crowdfunding. First, creator motivations can be directly publicized to potential investors through diverse routes. Some creators clearly indicate their motivations for launching crowdfunding projects by describing their objectives and expected output (Gerber et al., 2012). Second, project characteristics can mediate the link between creator motivations and project performance. Potential investors decide whether to support a certain crowdfunding project by evaluating the characteristics of the project (Allison et al., 2015), and the success of a crowdfunding project is determined solely by how many investors participate in the project (in contrast to the pre-submitted goal amount). We propose that creator motivations are positively associated with project performance, but at diferent degrees according to diferences in motivation type.

Achievement motivation, related to actualizing personal visions or being able to conduct challenging tasks, may be fulfilled through a superior performance of the crowdfunding project. The opportunity to grow as an individual, in a crowdfunding setting should be a function of the challenges to which an individual is exposed. Given that success in the crowdfunding proiect is regarded as challenging, as it is dificult to manage uncommon situations and/or crises, creators who place greater importance on achieving their goals should be more likely to undertake successful crowdfunding projects. Monetary need certainly drives creators to conduct crowdfunding projects of superior performance; this is because economic incentives provide dominant motivation to creators with a stronger willingness to grow (Davidsson, 1989). A high-performing crowdfunding project ofers an opportunity to the creator for obtaining substantial funds to continue and develop the proiect. Others-oriented motivations are highly influential and may be more influential than expected economic incentives in determining higher performance (Wiklund et al., 2003). Prosociality motivation is another dominant driver for creators to aim for superior performance. If contributing to communities is important to the creator and the communities value the project, then the creator will undertake crowdfunding projects with a high potential because increased participation of crowdfunders creates a significant impact. This is strongly supported because crowdfunders actively contribute to crowdfunding not only by supporting projects but also by sharing them on their social networks. Alternatively, relationship building motivation may be associated with superior performance in two ways. On the one hand, generally, greater crowdfunding success should generate more connections. Creators who place importance on benefits, such as building broader networks, will launch crowdfunding projects with a greater performance potential. On the other hand, greater success of crowdfunding increases the resources required, resulting in limited eforts to build strong relationships. Particularly, highly successful crowdfunding projects are more likely to experience shortages in resources and require immediate attention and action for resolving unexpected crises (Mollick, 2014).

Given the above arguments, the following proposition is presented.

Proposition 2. Creator motivations are associated with the extent of crowdfunding project performance. Generally, higher motivations lead t superior performance: this is because highly motivated creators commit more to their crowdfunding projects when compared to the others However, according to the characteristics of motivations, the direction and extent of the association can be diferent.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Study context and sample

This study’s survey sample consists of both the creators and investors of two reward-based crowdfunding platforms in South Korea, in which most projects are in creative domains, such as film and music. In 2015, the South Korean government passed the Crowdfunding Act, 2 years after it was first proposed in 2013, which aims at providing an alternative funding channel for creators by allowing individuals to invest in an entrepreneurial project through an online platform. In the past, since South Korea had very few angel investors or local VC firms, initial funds for the projects were provided by founders or their friends and family. Though the concept of raising money from crowds or investing in a local business is not new, the crowdfunding market in South Korea has grown rapidly since the advent of the Internet. As the crowdfunding mechanism is associated with collective behavior designed to solve a problem, rather than individual efort (Belleflamme et al., 2014), its collectivist culture and emphasis on the importance of helping others and a sense of responsibility may expand crowdfunding in South Korea (Cho and Kim, 2017).

Our target platforms are similar to Kickstarter and Indiegogo of the US in several ways. Similar to them, these platforms support diverse creative sectors. Investors tend to receive tangible rewards, such as DVDs and CDs, for their investments; additionally, most projects allow investors to pledge small investments and receive recognition such as acknowledgment notes or inclusion in the investors’ list. The aforementioned platforms are qualitatively similar in terms of the types and scales of their projects and are hence considered competing alternatives for potential creators. Like most reward-based crowdfunding platforms, both platforms adopt an “all-or-nothing” policy, whereby project creators receive funding only when their projects achieve their funding goals. We designed separate online questionnaires for creators and investors. In the introduction of the survey, we included the questionnaire and asked the participants to indicate the name of the project that they launched (for creators) or invested in most recently (for investors). It was emphasized that all questionnaires should be completed based on those experiences. To secure higher portions of a matched sample (pairs of creators and their investors), a two-step approach was employed. In the first phase, two types of e-mail newsletter were sent separately to the creators of successful crowdfunding projects and to the investors with at least one funding experience. For two weeks, we listed the respondents of the two groups. In the second phase, we sent another e-mail to selected creators and investors whose counterparts participated in the survey during the first phase, emphasizing the necessity of the participation of both creator and investors from the same project. Both steps included an incentive in the form of mobile coupons upon return of the completed survey.

Through the two-step approach, we received 729 responses (617 investors of 144 projects and 112 creators). Considering that the total number of investors with funding experience was around 30,000 and the number of successful projects was around 500 in the two platforms, the response rates of the two surveys were about 2% and 28%, respectively. Of the total responses, 62 responses (58 investors and 4 creators) were eliminated from the sample due to incomplete responses such as missing values, resulting in 667 responses of 559 investors, from 131 projects, and 108 creators. Of these, 362 investors and 45 creators were matched, meaning that 45 projects had both creator and investor responses, while the others had responses from only one side. We used diferent sets of samples to validate our propositions. First, the sample of 108 creators was used to develop a typology of crowdfunding project creators and examine the association between creator motivations and project performance. Second, the sample of 362 investors was used to examine the association between creator motivations and investor-perceived project characteristics. The platform operators confirmed that the demographic composition of the respondents was similar to that of all registered members, eliminating the potential for non-response bias. Demographic profiles of respondents from the two samples are presented in Table 2.

## 4.2. Measurement

Measurement items were initially adopted based on relevant theories in the literature. The adopted constructs and questionnaire items from the extant literature were converted into a revised form suitable for the specific context of crowdfunding. For motivation, we primarily adopted the instrument from motivation studies (Brüggen et al., 2011; Grant, 2008; Hsu and Lin, 2008; Vallerand et al., 1992). For project characteristics, we used instruments from diverse research domains, including entrepreneurship, charity, crowdsourcing, and virtual communities (Davidsson, 1995; Koh et al., 2007; Kohler et al., 2011; Krueger, 1993; Sweeney and Soutar, 2001). A multiple-item method based on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree” was used to develop the measurement.

Achievement motivation was expressed as the degree to which a creator has a desire to accomplish own goal and objective through the project (Vallerand et al., 1992) and was measured using three items, including “I decided to launch the project using this service because of the pleasure that I experience when I surpass myself in accomplishing the goal of the project.” Prosociality motivation was defined as the degree of a creator’s willingness to benefit the society voluntarily through the project (Grant, 2008) and was measured using four items, including “I decided to launch the project using this service because I care about benefiting others through my work.” The monetary need was expressed as the degree of a creator’s need to raise financial resources for the project (Brüggen et al., 2011) and was measured using three items, including “I decided to launch the project using this service because of the monetary sponsors I can get for my efort.” Finally, relationship building motivation was defined as the extent of a creator’s willingness to be associated with potential investors and build new connections (Hsu and Lin, 2008). It was measured using three items, including “I decided to launch the project using this service because I want to be associated with investors in my project.”

For project characteristics, novelty was defined as the degree to which a project idea is new and original (Koslow et al., 2003) and was measured using four items, including “The project is novel.” Societal contribution was defined as the extent to which a project positively impacts the society (Davidsson, 1995) and was measured using four items, including “The value that the project creates would be very important for the society.” Trustworthiness reflected the degree to which the creator displayed reliability (Sheinin et al., 2011) and was measured using three items, including “The creator of the project is dependable.” Activeness reflected the degree of the creator’s enterprising attitude (Koh and Kim, 2003) and was measured using three items, including “The creator of the project actively updates investors on project progress.” Feasibility was expressed as the extent to which the project seems capable of being accomplished as promised (Krueger, 1993) and was measured using three items, including “The project seems easy to complete.” We defined reward value as the investors’ perception of the worth of the reward ofered for their support (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001); this was measured using five items, including “The reward is one that I will enjoy.”

To measure the project performance, we used the total funding amount, the total number of investors, and the average funding amount per investor. The total funding amount was expressed as the logarithm value of the actual funding amount of a project. The total number of investors was defined as the logarithm value of the number of investors who participated in the focal crowdfunding project. The average funding amount per investor was measured as the log-transformed value of the average funding amount provided by individual investors.

Finally, several descriptive variables were created. We collected demographic information such as age and gender. We added dummies for project categories, including publications, music, movies, games, arts, and charity. In addition to the project category, we considered other relevant project characteristics such as the funding goal amount and project duration. Table 3 presents the operational definitions and their sources for each variable.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement model

Since our sample consists of two groups (i.e., creators and investors) with diferent questionnaires (i.e., creator motivations and perceived project characteristics), we separately verified the validity of the measurement models for each group. First, we examined the internal consistency of the constructs using Cronbach’s α to assess the reliability of the measurements (Cronbach, 1951). For both creator motivations and project characteristics, the results (ranging from 0.785 to 0.937) indicated that all instruments met or exceeded the acceptable level. To validate the measurement model, two types of validity were assessed. Convergent validity was assessed by examining the item-to-total correlation, which indicates the correlation of each item to the average of the remaining items. Items with item-to-total correlation scores lower than 0.6 were dropped (Churchill, 1979). Discriminant validity was checked using the factor analysis. Since each variable was measured using the multi-item constructs, factor analysis with varimax was conducted to check the unidimensionality among the items. One item with a factor loading lower than 0.6 was dropped (Campbel and Fiske, 1959). Subsequently, we conducted a correlation test with revised constructs using the reliability and validity test. A summary of the test results, which included all the measures used in this research, is provided in Appendix B. Appendix C.1 presents a correlation matrix of the research constructs.

## 5.2. Result of cluster analysis and validation

Non-hierarchical clustering requires the number of clusters to be specified a priori (Milligan, 1980). The decision on the number of clusters is a key issue and relies mainly on the researcher because no formal statistical basis for the selection of one cluster solution over another is available. Given the exploratory nature of the study, we ran the analysis specifying from two to five clusters, considering the number of variables (i.e., four motivations) and samples (i.e.. 108 creators) used for the analysis. For each solution, the diferences in the mean scores of the clusters for the variables were examined to evaluate the magnitude of the diferences among clusters. Table 4 provides the F-values for each cluster solution. The four-cluster solution was preferred to the three-cluster partition because it allowed for a distinctly diferent fourth group to emerge. The four-cluster solution was also preferred to the five-cluster solution, which generated a cluster with few cases belonging to (N = 7), even though it showed significant diferences among the clusters. Optimal convergence for the four-cluster solution was achieved in four iterations. The F-values computed to identify the significance level of the between-cluster differences for four motivations showed that all of them had significant differentiating values in the four-cluster solution.

We labeled each cluster according to its motivational characteristics. Daring dreamer (Cluster 2) is the largest cluster, accounting for 40.7% (N = 44) of the total creators, which was followed by indie producer (Cluster 4, N = 26, 24.1%), social entrepreneur (Cluster 1, N = 19, 17.6%), and fund seeker (Cluster 3, N = 19, 17.6%). These clusters represent the four types of creators in crowdfunding platforms, and each cluster’s average motivation factor values imply the nature and the characteristic of each cluster. Social entrepreneur has the highest value of prosocial motivation and the lowest monetary need. While their monetary need cannot be ignored, they are driven mainly by the willingness to contribute to the society. Regarding crowdfunding as an alternative funding source for diverse startups and organization, this type of creator could be one of the major beneficiaries. Daring dreamer scores the highest value in achievement motivation, which is intrinsic and self-oriented. It also scores relatively high for relationship building and prosocia motivations. The primary objective of this type of creator might be to fulfill own lifelong plans. This type of creator is less professiona than other types but still plays an important role by representing a considerable portion of crowdfunding markets and generating opportunities for communities. Fund seeker scores a relatively low value for all motivations, but the monetary need in this type of creator is stronger when compared to the others. Such creators would leverage crowdfunding as a new source of financing due to their vulnerability to financial risks. It is assumed that they regard crowdfunding as a tool rather than a new business opportunity. Indie producer, the second-largest cluster, scores the highest value in both monetary need and relationship building. For this type, building a relationship with investors in the earlier stage is as critical as raising funds. In addition to financial support, investors assist in developing crowdfunding projects in diverse ways, including giving feedback for improvement and helping acquire new investors. The indie producer tries to utilize crowdfunding to leverage these aids provided by investors. The results of the cluster analysis are depicted in Table 5.

## 5.3. Creator motivations and investor-perceived project characteristics

Next, we examined how investors’ perceptions of project characteristics difer from cluster to cluster. We used data from responses of 362 investors of 45 projects. The 362 investors were assigned to each cluster according to their afiliation with the projects they supported. The sample consisted of 69, 67, 167, and 59 investors for social entrepreneurs, daring dreamers, fund seekers, and indie producers, respectively. The ANOVA results indicate that the five project characteristics, except novelty, exhibited diferences across clusters.

The projects of social entrepreneurs were perceived as the most significant contributions to society, while those of indie producers were perceived least significant. Social entrepreneurs launch crowdfunding projects from prosocial motivations; therefore, their projects are intended to contribute to society. Investors might support these projects with a wish to participate in that contribution. The result shows that crowdfunding can lead to a new avenue for social innovation by connecting a group of creators with prosocial motivation and their potential investors. This is possible because crowdfunding allows investors to contribute a small amount of money and support societally meaningful projects; crowdfunding has gained significance with the failure of the traditional charity model to attract suficient creative ideas and donors. The projects of indie producers tend to focus on the producers’ purposes and benefits; therefore, their contribution to society may be marginal. Thus, investors in those projects might be motivated by other characteristics such as expected rewards or relational features of the projects. Daring dreamers were recognized as the least trustworthy type and the least active among all creator groups. Their projects were also considered less feasible than the projects of sociaì entrepreneurs. The high achievement motivation of daring dreamers might condition investor perceptions toward their projects. As they are novice creators, potential investors might perceive their projects as less trustworthy and feasible. Interestingly, even with their higher relationship building motivation, they were considered the least active creator group. This suggests the perception that their motivation may not turn into executable plans because they lack experience and expertise. Finally, the investors of fund seeker projects perceived that the rewards they received were the most valuable. As fund seekers launch their projects solely due to monetary need, they might seriously consider the value exchange with potential investors. Furthermore, as they are the oldest group, they may be the most highly experienced. The existence of this type of creators confirms that crowdfunding plays a substantial role in financing diverse projects that have been neglected by institutional investors and thus had difficulties fundraising for survival or growth Overall, the results show that creators’ motivations play important roles in forming the characteristics of the crowdfunding projects they launch.

## 6. Further analysis: project characteristics and performance (aggregated project level)

We further examined the relationship between creator motivation and project performance using a different approach. As we verified in our main analysis, creator motivations significantly influence the characteristics of crowdfunding projects. Those characteristics should afect project performance, playing an important role in forming potential investors’ attitude to the projects. Thus, we located the characteristics of crowdfunding projects as a mediator connecting creator motivation and project performance. As we collected data on perceptions of project characteristics at the investor level, we aggregated the individual-level data to the project level to align the unit of analysis. We began with the original dataset of 559 investors from 131 projects. Since we were not required to match the responses of creators to the investors of their projects for the analysis. we could fully leverage the whole dataset from our original survey. We aggregated the investor-level data to project-level characteristic scales by calculating the average values of individual investor responses for a focal project.<sup>2</sup> We confirmed the validity of our aggregation by examining the inter-rater agreement (IRA) and the intraclass correlation coeficient (ICC) (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979). The IRA is used to determine the appropriateness of averaging individual-level ratings to the higher level (van Mierlo et al., 2009). It has been used to examine the aggregation of individual responses to the group level, such as organizational culture perception to the organizational level. The ICC is also a useful estimate of inter-rater reliability. As we used the same subjects for each construct (i.e., investors of a project rated al characteristics of the project), we used ICC(2) assuming that the variance of the subjects only added noise to the estimates of the constructs. The ICC(2) models the efects of subjects and constructs and assumes that both are chosen randomly from larger populations. We excluded 31 projects with 193 investors due to weak support for validating the aggregation. When any of the IRA or ICC(2) did not exceed the recommended value of 0.7 for the projects (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979; van Mierlo et al., 2009), the respondents of the projects were all excluded. This resulted in a validated project-level dataset of 100 projects from the 366 investor responses. As discussed, with the dataset, we formed project-level constructs indicating the characteristics of each project by calculating the average values of the investors’ perceptions of a project. Additionally, we examined the relationship between project characteristics and performance via the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis. To operationalize project performance, we used three variables predefined in the main analysis: the total amount of funding, the total number of investors, and the average amount of funding. We included several constructs to control for heterogeneity among the projects, including creator- and projectrelated information. Since our independent variables were an aggregated form of subjective data (i.e., from the investor survey) and the dependent variables were objective data (i.e., actual performance), there was no concern about the common method bias. ## 7. Discussion and conclusion

Our study empirically validated the existence of diverse types of crowdfunding project creators. Unlike the prior crowdfunding studies that have used transactional data at the project-level or surveyed two sides of crowdfunding participants (creator-investor) incoherently, we conducted a survey of crowdfunding memberships comprising creators and investors of the same project, thereby securing a higher granularity of data (project vs. individual) and validity (participants of the same project). The results of our study, as expected, reveal the relationships among creator motivations, project characteristics, and crowdfunding performance. Based on the motivations for launching projects, four types of crowdfunding creators were discovered, grouped into clusters, and labeled according to their characteristics. We also examined the diferences among these four types of creators, including investor-perceived project characteristics and project performance. Furthermore, we examined the association between crowdfunding project characteristics and project performance at the project level by aggregating individual investor responses.

Social entrepreneurs are found to be one of the most suitable types of creators in crowdfunding platforms (Calic and Mosakowski,

2016). These results show that projects with a higher reward value and societal contribution are more likely to have the potential to achieve superior project performance. Interestingly, their performance is competitive, even though they have the lowest monetary need of all the types of creators. They are younger than other groups, which confirms the encouraging role of crowdfunding in the community of young and adventurous social entrepreneurs (Zahra et al., 2009). It should be noted that such type of creators would be more successful in countries with a collectivist culture such as South Korea when compared to countries with an individualist culture (Cho and Kim, 2017). Fund seekers are considered entrepreneurial organizations that prefer quick and less formal business-to-consumer funding, such as crowdfunding, to more traditional business-to-business funding sources, such as banks or VCs. The results indicate that monetary need is their main motivation. They are also the oldest among all creators, which implies that they are more experienced than the others. Thus, it is not surprising that investors in their projects were the most satisfied with their reward. This is reconfirmed by the fact that they record the highest average funding amount. The existence of this creator group suggests that crowdfunding is a new digital platform for providing funding sources and other opportunities for creators who sufered as a result of the asymmetric capital market (Sorenson et al., 2016). Furthermore, crowdfunding can play a particularly constructive role in countries lacking stable financing systems for creators. Indie producer represents a group of creators who pursue the creation of new cultures and communities. They mainly propose artistic projects in crowdfunding platforms, such as projects involving music, films, and plays. They launch their projects to fulfill the monetary need and driven by relationship building motivations. They, generally, lack financial resources, and mainly aim to build an active fan base.<sup>3</sup> The results show that crowdfunding plays an important role as a platform for entrepreneurial subcultures and artistic communities, where new experiments can be tried, as is illustrated by the fact that crowdfunding platforms and investors in South Korea are largely found in the creative sectors. Finally, the daring dreamer represents a group of novices or less-known creators who hope to debut on the stage through crowdfunding. They record the highest value in achievement motivation, while their projects are recognized by the investors as the least feasible, trustworthy, and active The mismatch between their activated motivation and less-favored evaluations from the investors indicates that their projects would not fare well against the other creator groups’ projects.

As seen from the brief summary of the four creator groups’ motivations and investor perceptions of their projects, the results of our analysis suggest the multi-faceted role of the crowdfunding platforms in boosting and initiating entrepreneurial activities in diverse domains. ## 7.1. Contribution to the literature

We believe that our empirical study advances the understanding of crowdfunding and entrepreneurship in several aspects. First, our study extends the literature on crowdfunding, specifically the research on the creator-side motivations in crowdfunding platforms. Studies have largely ignored the motivations of crowdfunding creators while focusing on explicit factors afecting project success and individual investor behavior (Burtch et al., 2013; Kim and Viswanathan, 2018; Kuppuswamy and Bayus, 2017). There is little knowledge on the possible role of creator motivations in the crowdfunding context (Gerber et al., 2012; Gleasure, 2015). Our study complements this biased body of literature by showing that diferent creator motivations afect characteristics and the performance of crowdfunding projects. We proposed a theoretical framework elaborating how diferent creator motivations afect project characteristics and performance through platforms. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first studies to empirically examine the existence of diverse creator motivations and diferent creator groups in crowdfunding platforms. We combined objective (e.g., project attributes) and subjective (e.g., surveys) data to examine the role of psychological factors in project performance. We theoretically drew a set of dominant creator motivations and verified the important roles of creator motivations in forming project characteristics and enhancing the project performance. This initial examination of the efect of motivations on project characteristics and performance will contribute to generating more new constructs and causal relationships, thereby expanding our knowledge on crowdfunding. Moreover, in terms of methodology in crowdfunding research, we also contribute to the literature by proposing a matched sample approach, linking creators to their investors by surveving both parties involved in the same project

We also contribute to the literature on entrepreneurship, specifically in the domain of the entrepreneurial typologies, by incorporating the motivations of creators and the perceptions of their investors. Our study examines the role of creator motivations in forming project characteristics and driving project performance, by measuring the perceptions of investors in crowdfunding projects and the level of project success. While a few studies have focused on entrepreneurial motivations (Miner, 2000; Schlaegel and Koenig, 2014), they do not examine the role of those motivations in influencing characteristics and the performance of their projects. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to discover a typology of entrepreneurs in the context of crowdfunding. This typology is diferent from those identified in the previous literature on entrepreneurial typology in several ways. For example, when compared to the typologies of Dubini (1989) and Birley and Westhead (1994), including some types of discontented founders, the typology discovered in this study indicates that all types of crowdfunding creators are driven by dominant motivations to launch their projects proactively. It should also be emphasized that the typology of this study includes a wider range of entrepreneurial tryouts. from creative projects to social enterprises. Understanding the difference between these creators is important for explaining and predicting the growth of crowdfunding in the future.

In conclusion, our study confirms the importance of crowdfunding as a digital platform for financing innovative creator activities in different domains. The results show that the use of crowdfunding is not limited to fundraising. but can initiate new artistic or entrepreneurial causes and build active, supportive communities. This study identified the prominent role of crowdfunding in promoting new types of challenges, including social entrepreneurship (Calic and Mosakowski, 2016). Identifying these new creator groups may lead to a more in-depth provider-side research on future crowdfunding research.

## 7.2. Contribution to the practice

Our study also provides practical insights, especially into the management of crowdfunding platforms and the diverse stakeholders who serve or rely on them. Platform operators should not only chase potential creators but also ofer them the most suitable benefits based on their distinct motivations. Understanding creator motivations and project characteristics are important for satisfying all creator types. This study’s proposed typology of crowdfunding creators can provide practical aid in this regard. For example, crowdfunding platforms can provide an indie producer easier and more accessible communication channels to their investors to ensure that they would be able to build a community for their projects. Concerning social entrepreneurs, platform operators can provide platforms such as the social media that would extend their reach beyond their platforms; this is crucial because social entrepreneurs aim at the diffusion of causes and value it over fundraising on the platforms. Amidst the tremendous growth of crowdfunding platforms, this typology can enable platform operators to identify distinct customer segments, thereby helping them to provide customized oferings to those target segments. Furthermore, matching those diferent types of creators to each potentia investor groups would be a key success factor for platforms.

## 7.3. Limitations, future research, and conclusion

This study has several limitations, which direct us to new research questions. Most of all. even though we validated the associations between creator motivations, project characteristics, and project performance, due to its exploratory nature and data limitations, we were unable to address and examine the detailed theoretical mechanism of how a specific creator’s motivation influences a crowdfunding project. Future research can find more theoretical evidence for our propositions, based on theorizing the underlying mechanism of a specific creator’s motivations’ role in the crowdfunding context. This can be conducted by collecting featured data related to project characteristics or by collecting primary survey data that clarify the creators’ project development process. In this context, future research can also investigate how creators in diferent stages of conducting entrepreneurial activities are diferent from the ones in the earlier stage, concerning motivations for using crowdfunding and their efect on the projects.

We also acknowledge that our results may be endogenous concerning unobservable heterogeneity of the creators and their projects. For example, it may be the case that creator motivations, project characteristics, and project performance are spuriously correlated with some unobserved attributes simultaneously, thereby influencing the project characteristics and their performance. Regarding the exploratory nature and reporting associations between the variables, we should be cautious when interpreting the results as causality than correlation. Future research may adopt validated applications, such as experimental manipulation and longitudinal examination, for properly alleviating concerns on endogeneity and validating the causal relationships between creator motivations and project characteristics and performance.

The study’s results present the role of crowdfunding in supporting diferent types of creators and their projects. However, many questions remain about how creators leverage crowdfunding to develop their projects and how investors react to creators’ activities. The degree to which this new platform is utilized to help creators finance their projects and build new communities depends largely on an understanding of its core users—creators. We believe that this study can set milestones for future research to understand and support creators in crowdfunding platforms.
