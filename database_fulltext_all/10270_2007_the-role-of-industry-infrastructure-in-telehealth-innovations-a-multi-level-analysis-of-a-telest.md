# The role of industry infrastructure in telehealth innovations: a multi-level analysis of a telestroke program

Sunyoung Cho<sup>1</sup> and Lars Mathiassen<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Virginia State University, Petersburg, VA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Center for Process Innovation, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta GA, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Sunyoung Cho, Department of Computer Information Systems, Virginia State University, P.O. Box 9038, Petersburg, VA 23806, USA. Tel: þ 1 804 524 5372; Fax: þ 1 804 524 6849; E-mail: scho@vsu.edu

## Abstract

The information-intensive nature of the healthcare industry and the potential of information technology (IT) to reduce costs and improve quality of services have increased the focus on IT-based innovations. Yet, our ability to understand and manage how IT-based innovations unfold in the context of healthcare is still limited. In this paper, we apply Van de Ven’s industry infrastructure framework to investigate a telehealth innovation that provides remote medical assistance to stroke patients in a network of collaborating hospitals. The resulting multi-level analysis contributes to understanding the innovation by revealing a highly complex process of interactions between key stakeholders and healthcare industry infrastructure. Despite the innovation’s strong potential, the process is mainly push-driven with minimal pull from potential adopters. Moreover, the push is created by a small group of medical innovators with limited technological and financial resources and little infrastructural support. The study contributes with contextual insights into the telehealth innovation, suggesting complementary explanations of why the healthcare industry despite considerable investments continues to lag behind other industries in adoption of IT-based innovations. The study also adapts Van de Ven’s framework by applying it to a single case of innovation rather than as originally conceived to several instances of an innovation within an industry. The analysis shows how some of the components of the framework were adapted to and interpreted in the context of telehealth innovations. European Journal of Information Systems (2007) 16, 738–750.

## Introduction

As the adoption of information technology (IT)-based innovations vary across industries, economies, and organizations (Avgerou, 2001), contextual studies may reveal insights which were unnoticed or not explained in previous studies (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004, 2005). Identification of variations in adoption patterns across contexts can also help researchers and managers better understand and improve new innovation initiatives. As a consequence, several studies emphasize the importance of understanding IT-based innovations as integral parts of the context in which they are embedded (e.g., Avgerou, 2001; Lamb et al., 2003; Avgerou & Madon, 2004; Chiasson & Davidson, 2004, 2005; Crowston & Myers, 2004; Van De Ven, 2005).

The healthcare area, which accounts for an important part of the economy, provides particularly complex contexts for IT innovations. IT can enable potential breakthroughs in quality improvement and cost reduction of healthcare services. As a result, IT investments in healthcare are growing at a rapid pace. IT investments were expected to reach 23.6 billion USD in 2003, rising at a rate of 9.3% from \$21.6 billion expended in 2002 (Sheldon I. Dorenfest & Associates, Ltd., 2004). Still, adoption of IT in healthcare proceeds slowly, and difficulties in successful adoption of IT-based healthcare innovations are well-documented (Anderson, 1997; Lorenzi et al., 1997; Raghupathi, 1997; Aarts et al., 1998; Tanriverdi & Iacono, 1998; Davidson, 2000; Menon et al., 2000; Berg, 2001; Lorenzi & Riley, 2003; Brown et al., 2004). However, while previous studies attest to barriers and difficulties for IT adoption and diffusion in healthcare industy, they do not adequately explain why a technology innovation will succeed in one context and fail in another. There are, therefore, particularly good reasons to use contextual approaches to study IT-based innovations within healthcare (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004). Contextual insights might help us understand the particular characteristics that make it difficult to successfully adopt IT-based innovations in this industry.

On this backdrop, we apply a contextual lens to investigate a telehealth innovation. In particular, Van de Ven’s industry infrastructure framework is chosen here, since it provides a way to understand why technology innovations succeed or fail in specific industries. In this study we adapt this framework to explain organizational processes involving the adoption of a telestroke innovation that provides remote medical assistance to patients in a network of collaborating hospitals (Cho et al., 2007). Stroke is the third leading cause of death in the U.S. and the leading cause of disabilities with an expected annual cost of 58 billion U.S. dollars for stroke-related medical cost and disability in 2006 (American Stroke Association, 2006). Moreover, there is a critical lack of stroke specialists in rural underserved areas, which contributes higher mortality rates in the U.S. stroke belt, the south-eastern part of the country (Casper et al., 2003). The focal innovation was launched to make stroke specialist services available to underserved communities in these areas. Generally speaking, telehealth innovations are particularly challenging because they require collaboration and knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries (Paul, 2006), they pose particular challenges related to ensure the trust needed in medical practices (Brown et al., 2004; Paul, 2006), and they raise delicate issues related to how investments and reimbursements are distributed and shared (Chau & Hu, 2004).

With its mission critical features, the innovation under investigation has great potential to be lifesaving and become widely diffused. In fact, efforts to commercialize the innovation have been going on for a while. While the conception and implementation of innovation into the initial network of collaborating hospitals is a success, further diffusion of the innovation is slow and negotiations to commercialize the innovation are complicated. To understand this apparent paradox, we apply Van de Ven’s industry infrastructure framework (Van De Ven et al., 1999; Van De Ven, 2005) and engage in an investigation of how the innovation process was shaped through interactions between key stakeholders and the surrounding healthcare context. Specifically, we conduct a multi-level process analysis related to the following research questions:

1. Stakeholder-level analysis: How do key stakeholders address industrial infrastructure issues during adoption of the telehealth innovation under study?

2. Context-level analysis: How do industrial infrastructure factors shape the adoption of the telehealth innovation under study?

The study contributes to healthcare information systems (IS) research through a detailed analysis of the telestroke program. The analysis reveals a complex adoption process and provides contextual insights into the innovation, suggesting complementary explanations on why the healthcare industry lags behind other industries in effectively adopting and using IT (Raghupathi, 1997; Aarts et al., 1998; Tanriverdi & Iacono, 1998; Berg, 2001; Lorenzi & Riley, 2003). Also, the paper shows how Van de Ven’s framework (Van De Ven et al., 1999) can be used to study interaction between agency and industrial infrastructure in the real-life context of a telehealth innovation. The empirical analysis provides qualitatively evidence that Van de Ven’s framework applies to the organization-level processes that contribute to the success or failure of technology innovations, and it shows how the framework and its components were adapted to and interpreted in the context of the telestroke innovation. The case analysis demonstrates, in this way, how Van de Ven’s framework can help explain a single case of innovation adoption different from the framework’s original use to study several instances on an innovation across an industry. Last but not the least, practical implications are offered suggesting that healthcare entrepreneurs apply an industry infrastructure perspective to proactively manage innovations as integral parts of the healthcare network in which they are embedded.

The paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews healthcare IS research, in particular research on telehealth innovation, and it presents Van de Ven’s infrastructure framework (Van De Ven et al., 1999). Next, we outline the case study design and present our multilevel analysis of the telestroke innovation under investigation. Finally, we discuss contributions and implications for both research and practice.

## Theoretical foundation

Increasing investments in IT-based innovations in the healthcare industry are reflected in IS research. Chiasson & Davidson (2004) have classified this body of knowledge on design, development, implementation, and use of information-intensive technologies in healthcare settings into four groups based on how IS theory and the healthcare context was emphasized.

IS-only papers focus on generalizable theory without specific consideration of the healthcare context. The authors of these papers do not explore how the healthcare context influences theoretical constructs, assumptions, or analysis. IS-healthcare papers primarily focus on developing or testing IS theories with some consideration given to interactions with the healthcare context. These researchers consider how the healthcare context might influence application or interpretation of IS theory. Healthcare-IS papers more systematically consider the context by applying IS theory to analyze healthcare issues. The authors of these papers explicitly take into account unique aspects of healthcare contexts in order to develop, test, and extend theory through the application of a general IS topic. Finally, healthcare-only papers analyze the design, development, implementation, and use of information-intensive technologies in healthcare without significant use of IS theories.

Chiasson & Davidson (2004) argue that IS-healthcare and healthcare-IS approaches are more balanced between contextual specificity and general IS theory, and they consider healthcare-IS as the better approach to explore context specificities within the healthcare industry. In addition, they emphasize that the healthcare context provides a niche for IS researchers to extend, refine, and develop IS theories (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004). This research is in line with these insights and as a consequence it is framed as a healthcare-IS study focused specifically on adoption of telehealth innovations.

## Telehealth innovation research

Increased experiments and innovations based on advanced information technologies in the 1990s produced a number of conceptually overlapping terms such as telemedicine, telehealth, and e-health (Bashshur, 2000; Maheu et al., 2001). Telemedicine involves provision of healthcare services, clinical information, and education over distance using telecommunication technology (Maheu et al., 2001). Telehealth is a broader term involving distant delivery of health services including clinical, educational, and administrative services, through transfer of various forms of information (e.g. audio, video and graphics) via telecommunication (Bali & Naguib, 2001).

Our extended literature review of healthcare IS research revealed rather few studies of telehealth innovations (e.g., Adewale, 2004; Brown et al., 2004; Chau & Hu, 2004; Mbarika, 2004; Paul & Mcdaniel Jr., 2004; Constantinides & Barrett, 2006; Liang et al., 2006; Paul, 2006). Some of these studies approach telehealth innovation from an individual adoption perspective (Hu et al., 1999; Brown et al., 2004; Chau & Hu, 2004; Paul & Mcdaniel Jr., 2004; Paul, 2006). For example, Hu et al. (1999) and Chau & Hu (2004) investigate individual-level adoption of telehealth innovations using the Technology Acceptance Model and Brown et al. (2004)discuss relationships between individual adoption behaviors and trust. Paul (2006) and Paul and McDaniel (2004) provide organization-level adoption analyses using the theoretical framing of trust to understand adoption patterns (Brown et al., 2004; Paul & Mcdaniel Jr., 2004; Paul, 2006). Common for these studies are that they are factor-oriented rather than process-focused.

Other studies investigate telehealth innovation processes at the societal level. Adewale (2004) discusses challenges and implementation issues related to telemedicine in Nigeria and Mbarika (2004) discusses important management issues for implementing telemedicine projects in sub-Saharan Africa. We also found a number of process studies providing organization-level analyses of telehealth innovations (Adewale, 2004; Chau & Hu, 2004; Constantinides & Barrett, 2006). Chau & Hu (2004) investigate the implementation process of a neurosurgical program in Hong Kong based on the technology diffusion model by Kwon & Zmud (1987) and Cooper & Zmud (1990). Their analysis focuses on the process from a hub hospital perspective. Constantinides & Barrett (2006) address the interaction between a telehealth innovation and its context with particular emphasis on the relationships between structure and culture and power and politics. More specifically, they investigate the development and implementation of a telecardiology innovation as an ongoing process of negotiations between multiple stakeholders and technological choices. IS theories (i.e., practice lens, boundary objects, and Actor Network Theory) are applied to frame the analysis.

There are, however, only a limited number of studies that provide contextual insights into telehealth innovation processes at the organizational level. More empirical studies are therefore needed to address questions like: How are telehealth innovations adopted and diffused? In what contexts are those innovations embedded? How do contexts influence the adoption and diffusion of telehealth innovations? The review of existing literature justifies, in this way, more empirical research to better understand the contextual challenges related to adoption of telehealth innovations and, if possible, to further extend, refine, and develop IS theory in the healthcare context (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004).

## Industry infrastructure framework

As our interest lies in investigating the interactions between agency and context in relation to a specific telehealth innovation, we adopt an institutional lens in line with a number of other IS studies (Covi & Kling, 1996; Avgerou, 2001; Lamb et al., 2003; Avgerou & Madon, 2004; Crowston & Myers, 2004; Chiasson & Davidson, 2005; Van De Ven, 2005). With some variation, institutional studies implicitly or explicitly relate to what DiMaggio and Powell calls an ‘organizational field’ (Dimaggio & Powell, 1983). An organizational field is identified when ‘organizations, in the aggregate, constitute a recognized area of institutional life: key suppliers, resource and product consumers, regulatory agencies, and other organizations that produce similar services and products.’ A focus on specific organizational fields direct researchers’ attention to the totality of individuals, groups, organizations, and networks that within that particular context interact to shape the innovation under consideration (Dimaggio & Powell, 1983). Hence, these studies recognize the embeddedness of IT-based innovations beyond organizational boundaries; they assume that process characteristics vary; and, they see context as emergent and the interaction between context and innovation as difficult to trace.

Building on this tradition, we apply a particular institutional approach, Van de Ven’s infrastructure framework (Van De Ven et al., 1999; Van De Ven, 2005), to analyze the telehealth innovation under investigation. The framework was developed through studies of cochlear implant technology (Van De Ven & Garud, 1989) and later extended and refined. The framework is also referred to as the social-systems perspective. The framework emphasizes ‘community infrastructure’ or ‘industry infrastructure’ incorporating three dominant perspectives on innovations, namely the technological imperative, the institutional determinism, and the resource endowment perspectives. Van De Ven et al. (1999) argue that these three perspectives are highly interdependent and therefore must be analyzed within a context of continuing interaction. As a result, the framework incorporates a variety of industrial infrastructures for technological innovations (see Figure 1): (1) institutional arrangements to legitimize, regulate, and standardize a new technology; (2) resource endowments of basic scientific knowledge, financing mechanisms, and a pool of competent labor; (3) market mechanisms to educate consumers and stimulate demand for a new technology; and (4) proprietary research and development, manufacturing, marketing, and distribution functions by entrepreneurial firms to commercialize the innovation for profit. The main proposition of the industry infrastructure framework is that success of technological innovations depends on the extent to which the necessary components of the industrial infrastructure in which the innovation is embedded are available and mature (Van De Ven et al., 1999).

The industry infrastructure framework belongs to the ‘collective action’ perspective on institutional change and it was conceived to illuminate the social, economic, and political infrastructure that can sustain members of a technological community (Van De Ven & Hargrave, 2004). The framework provides an appropriate theoretical lens to examine telehealth innovations; first, because of its emphasis on explaining technological innovation within a specific industrial context (Van De Ven et al., 1999); second, because of its focus on networked technologies in increasingly knowledge-intensive industries (Van De Ven, 2005).

While the framework is designed for industry-level analyses of innovations across organizations, we adapt the framework to the level of an individual, situated innovation to analyze its conception, implementation, and further diffusion within a network of collaborating organizations. The intention behind Van de Ven’s framework was to provide an augmented view of an industry to help us ‘understand how industrial infrastructure emerges to support and constrain innovation’ (2005, p. 369); but the focus of this paper is not on the healthcare industry and telehealth innovations in general. Instead, we adapt Van De Ven’s et al. (1999) methodological suggestion to analyze a single telestroke program on two different levels: a stakeholder-level focusing on how entrepreneurs and organizations interacted with industrial infrastructure components, and a context-level focusing on how industrial infrastructure components shaped the development and adoption of the innovation. Specifically, we are interested in macro-level concerns: (1) how and when different components in the industrial infrastructure emerged and were organized over time; (2) how stakeholders created, performed, or related to these components; and (3) what impact the industrial infrastructure components had on the adoption of the telehealth innovation under investigation.

## Research method

## Context of study

There is a critical lack of stroke specialist expertise in most rural areas and in many urban areas as well. This contributes to a higher rate of stroke deaths in rural and underserved communities (Casper et al., 2003). For the case of non-bleeding stroke, or ischemic stroke, tPA (Tissue Plasminogen Activator), a blood-clot dissolving agent, greatly reduces chances of severe disabilities if it is administered within three hours from the first show of stroke symptoms. It should, however, be avoided for bleeding patients, because it is fatal to them. Despite the potential benefits of the drug for ischemic stoke patients, it is estimated only 2% of stroke patients receive its benefits, due to lack of on-site expertise of stroke specialists who can discern ischemic and non-ischemic strokes.

In March 2003, the department of neurology at a large university hospital (referred to as the hub hospital) in the state of Georgia in the U.S. launched a telehealth innovation, called REACH (the Remote Evaluation for Acute Ischemic Stroke Program). REACH provides roundthe-clock neurological service to rural hospitals in nearby areas through a telemedicine system. It makes centrally located neurologists available to ER staff at rural hospitals enabling the central neurologists to hear, see, and receive data about patients over distance in real time. CT scan and other vital information about the patient at the rural hospital are transferred to the central expert over REACH and the neurologist makes a decision on the administration of tPA and follow-up treatments. The initial conception and implementation of REACH into the network of collaborating hospitals was largely a success; the involved neurologist at the hub hospital and the ER staff at the rural hospital found the system easy to use; and, more than 100 patients were successfully examined and subsequently treated during initial usage of REACH.

By the time of writing this paper, nine rural hospitals had joined the program. The innovators were at this point actively seeking ways to further diffuse REACH and the hub hospital was preparing to establish another hub by joining forces with another large hospital in the state. In January 2005, two entrepreneurs sponsored by a state R&D funding agency joined and formed a company (referred to as the Firm in the following) to commercialize REACH. The hub hospital and the two entrepreneurs went through several rounds of negotiations, but failed to reach an agreement on licensing and operation terms and conditions. As a result, the sponsorship of the state to the Firm discontinued and the two entrepreneurs instead sought partnership with another company that possessed a similar technology.

## Research design

A single case study (Yin, 2003) was planned to study this telehealth innovation involving multiple organizations. Case study research is well suited to understand IT-based innovations in organizational contexts (Darke et al., 1998) and single cases allow researchers to investigate phenomena in depth to provide rich description and understanding (Walsham, 1995).

There were three main data sources for the research: documents, interviews, and participant observation. All available documents were analyzed such as those related to system development and project management, financial documents, and publicly available articles from news media and academic journals. A total of 25 individuals in five hospitals including the hub and four rural hospitals were interviewed. Seven were doctors, five administrative staff, three technical staff, nine nurses, and one radiology technician. All interviews were semi-structured and lasted typically between 30 and 60 min. The majority of interviews were individual and a few were group interviews of 2–4. Interview notes were prepared right after each interview. The researchers also held nine advisory workshops with the two entrepreneurs of the Firm and these were documented in field notes. All interviews and workshops were recorded and transcribed. The second author had continued interaction with the state research funding agency having opportunities to learn about its position and support of the entrepreneurial launch for REACH.

Interview guidelines were prepared before interviews tailored to different groups of interviewees such as users, IT staff, and hospital administrators. Interview notes were made during and after each interview. The two researchers held debriefing sessions after most of the interviews and workshops to exchange their understanding and interpretation. Once the theory was chosen for analysis, themes and coding schemes were developed based on the chosen theory. The data were then analyzed based on the theory to develop an institutional analysis of REACH. First, individual, key stakeholders were identified and data were analyzed according to the four main components of the social-system framework (see Figure 1) based on research question 1. Second, major events were identified to anchor the context-level analysis of the infrastructure components and the interactions between the components following research question 2. The analysis process was highly iterative and differences among the researchers were resolved through discussions. The overall analysis was refined by going back and forth between theory and data, that is, interview notes and transcripts (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Mason, 2002). The results of the multi-level analysis presented in the following helped us understand how the innovation was shaped in this particular context.

## Results

## Stakeholder-level analysis

The core project group consisting of a group of neurologists and a system developer (referred to as the innovators in the following), the hub hospital, the rural hospitals, the Firm, and the state R&D funding agency sponsoring the entrepreneurial initiative of the Firm were the main stakeholders driving the telehealth innovation. The innovators were treated as a separate stakeholder, as their interest in and perspective of REACH were distinct from those of the hub hospital as an institution, even though they were part of the organization. Table 1 summarizes how these stakeholder groups addressed the components of the industry infrastructure framework. The underlying analysis is detailed in the following, considering for each stakeholder group (e.g. the innovators) and each high-level component (e.g. resource endowments, see Figure 1) those low-level components (e.g. science and technology, financing and insurance, competence training and accreditation, see Figure 1) that apply to that particular relationship.

The innovators with the most enthusiasm and interest in REACH were active in relation to all four components. They were proactive in terms of proprietary activities by developing the web-based, cost-effective innovation. In developing REACH, they were able to leverage resource endowments by successfully appropriating scientific and technological knowledge and available competent labor and mobilizing those resources into their project. Scientific and technological research provided the knowledge needed to develop the telehealth innovation. The following remark by the main system developer illustrates how the innovators made use of the available levels of scientific knowledge and technologies:

Even as early as 2001, we wouldn’t have been able to do thisyWe needed a wireless component and we needed flat screensy In 2001, they didn’t have the wireless bridges that we used to connect the REACH carts to the wires. None of those existed. Those came out literally when I got here.

Overall, the innovators were effective in leveraging resources that were easily available. They did not, however, manage to promote resource endowments to create a stronger and more sustainable platform for development. Also, in the early stages of the process they did not effectively take the institutional arrangements and market consumption components of the project into account. For example, REACH was designed as one-way system and therefore misaligned with the current insurance reimbursement scheme which required two-way video stream for reimbursement; only later in the process did the innovators engage in negotiations and explorations with insurance institutions and government agencies to resolve this reimbursement issue. When first conceived, the innovators did not vision REACH as a for-profit program and no formal business transactions were put in place to cover REACH installation and maintenance in the rural hospitals. Yet, driven by the medical interest in providing remote medical services to stroke patients, the innovators wanted to enroll as many rural hospitals as possible into the REACH program. In this sense, the innovators did proactively address the market component of the framework.

The hub hospital supported the innovators’ proprietary activities as they sponsored and hosted the development of the telehealth innovation. Also, the hospital financed provision of equipment and installation and maintenance of REACH in the participating rural hospitals. In doing so, the hospital leveraged available technological knowledge and resources and its activities related to resource endowments were at a similar level as the innovators’. The hub hospital gave little consideration to governance and legitimization issues and there were, as a result, no effective alignment of the innovation with institutional arrangements regarding reimbursement. The hub hospital and the innovators engaged in filing a patent, but no serious efforts were made to develop technology standards that could facilitate further diffusion of the program. In terms of the market consumption component, there were at this point no serious efforts to create a commercial market for REACH. The expansion of REACH was, however, supported by the hub hospital’s outreach unit, promoting increased collaboration between the hub and nearby rural hospitals. This is illustrated by a hub hospital high-ranking manager who is in charge of the unit:

We [the hub hospital] have provided support and strategic planning and development for those organizations and by doing that, the intent is to make them as successful as they can be in their local environments and that care is provided well at a high quality level locally. But then, because of that relationship and because of the trust level, we receive referrals for the things that can’t be done locally. And that’s really where REACH fits into this.

The rural hospitals were the most passive of the involved stakeholders. They did not engage in any proprietary activities as the innovation was entirely developed and financed by the hub hospital. They were operating under similar conditions as the hub hospital and the innovators in terms of resource endowments, but they faced tougher conditions. For example, they had difficulties tapping into available competent labor market. Several rural hospitals faced problems maintaining IT staff; they had little IT staff (none or part-time IT staff member in many a case); and, they experienced very high turnover ratios of IT staff. Most of the rural hospitals had financial problems and they would most likely not have invested in equipment purchase and maintenance for the innovation, had these not been provided for free by the hub hospital. Such unfavorable operation conditions at many rural sites are reflected in the following remark by a CFO in one of the REACH hospitals:

We have to put aside things for future growth and development, improved technology, infrastructure, etc. With just a break even point, you’re not able to do those things. All you’re doing is covering costs.

As the rural hospitals started to treat more patients through REACH, they also became aware that the U.S. government-sponsored insurance programs like Medicare and Medicaid did not sufficiently reimburse the services provided. However, the rural hospitals did not take individual or collective actions to rectify these problems. Finally, other than participating in the new hospital network, they did not actively seek to further develop a market for the new innovation.

The Firm was established by two entrepreneurs in January 2005 to commercialize REACH. This commercialization initiative was sponsored by the state R&D funding agency. The two entrepreneurs were highly active and engaged like the innovators, though their activities were short-lived as the negotiation with the hub hospital and the innovators ended without results at the end of 2005. Their initial activities concentrated on developing a viable business and marketing plan and carving out a contract with the hub hospital about licensing and operational terms. The entrepreneurs were proactive in terms of proprietary activities, as they tried to engage in the business functions of marketing and sales on behalf of the hub hospital as illustrated by the following:

[the hub hospital] has set up these beta-sites, but basically they give it to them so, of course, you’d want it if somebody gave it to you. But that’s not a survivable long-term business model to give away everything you have. So my charge is to figure out, well, how we are going to use this technology to develop a business and our strategy.

They were also proactive in creating resource endowments, as they tried to mobilize resources to commercialize REACH by negotiating with potential investors including venture capitalists and angel investors. The ultimate goal of the entrepreneurs was to create a commercial market demand for REACH and to that end they explored all the components of the industry infrastructure framework.

There were not many other stakeholders involved at this point of the process. Since the innovation was new to the world it had not yet created serious attention and response from potential stakeholders such as regulatory agencies and insurance institutions within the healthcare industry. Only the state R&D funding agency emerged as a stakeholder by sponsoring the two entrepreneurs to commercialize the innovation. By financing the firm, the agency clearly affected the resource endowments component and indirectly supported marketing and distribution activities in the proprietary component. The agency played a more conspicuous role in terms of the other two components of the industry infrastructure. The state funding agency’s involvement in commercializing REACH lent some legitimacy and increased visibility to the innovation. Later, the funding agency was engaged in mediating the negotiations between the entrepreneurs and the hub hospital. Such activities are critical for newto-the-world innovations because they help attract a critical mass of researchers to the field and build consumers’ trust in the market (Van De Ven et al., 1999). The funding agency indirectly supported the activities to create the market consumption component.

The telestroke innovation was conceived by the neurologists at the hub hospital in 2001 and the development of the system began in 2002 financed by the neurology department. The core project team consisting of four stroke specialists and one system developer was formed around the investment. The development project was driven mostly by pure scientific and humanitarian motivations among the core project team members. During the period from the conception to the first rollout of the innovation, the main activities were concentrated on system development, hence in the two industry infrastructure components of proprietary activities and resource endowments. The innovators and the hub hospital were the main stakeholders involved. These two stakeholders funded the development and equipment purchase to build the system and the hub hospital later financially supported most of the installation and the equipment for rural hospital rollout. However, other than the funding, the development activities were solely carried out by the innovators and no other business functions at the hub hospital like R&D or IT were engaged at this point. The following comment by one neurologist indicates clear separation of roles between the innovators and the hub hospital regarding REACH development and operation:

The hospital has funded some of the equipment, but we [the neurology department] funded all the development cost. We paid all of the people who worked on it. The hospital paid part of the developer’s salary but not the development part just the operational part. Research and development has always been neurologyy The health system [the hub hospital] isn’t allowed to pay for research and development.

The innovators did consider business process integration with practices in the adopting network of hospitals, but little attention was paid to develop a sustainable, longterm business model for investments, expenses, and rewards across the participating hospitals. The development was successful due to mature resource endowments, in particular in science and technology such as relevant neurological expertise and affordable hardware, software, and fast-access communication network services. In that sense, the two stakeholders successfully appropriated resource endowments, but their activities were quite isolated without any collaboration with other stakeholders, and also without active consideration of other components of the industry infrastructure. At this stage, no other stakeholders emerged as significant except the hub hospital. Activities were concentrated in the proprietary components with resource endowments through the hub hospital.

The system rollout began in March 2003 with the first installation in a rural hospital and it continued to include nine rural hospitals by summer 2005. The innovators had the ambition to expand the innovation to the entire state and even to other states. New stakeholders, namely the adopting rural hospitals, joined and interaction between the hub and the rural hospitals increased to handle complicated issues like contracts, legal issues, installations, and training of medical staff at the rural hospitals. During the initial adoption and expansion of the innovation, the involved stakeholders began to show some activities in all the four components, including institutional arrangements and market components.

With the growing use of REACH, both the hub and the rural hospitals began to realize the misalignment between the innovation and the institutional arrangements such as insurance reimbursement issues. The innovators engaged to remedy the situation by filing petitions to the insurance agencies like Medicaid and government agencies. However, the actions on the part of the other stakeholders, the hub and the rural hospitals, remained minimal. No regulatory agencies and other institutions such as state regulatory or insurance-related agencies were involved to resolve the problems as the innovation failed to generate much attention. Misalignment with institutional arrangements such as these emerged as potential barriers to further adoption of the innovation. A vice president of the hub hospital comments on these issues of REACH adoption as follows:

The current implicit barrier is that they aren’t being reimbursed today for their on-call work or their consultation.

In terms of the market component, the group of participating rural hospitals could be considered as an initial or potential market segment for the innovation, but transactions were implicit rather than explicit, indicating before-market stage of development. Market mechanism with critical mass and demand could potentially have large impact on institutional arrangements by directing influential stakeholders to pay attention and actively seek resolutions. For now, the four components began to witness activities to some degree, but underdevelopment and non-alignment of these components delayed further adoption and diffusion of the innovation.

In early 2005, the two entrepreneurs sponsored by the state R&D funding agency created the Firm with an ambition to commercialize the innovation. The arrival of the two new stakeholders greatly increased the level of activities in all four components. However, their activities were short-lived. The negotiations between the Firm and the hub hospital were difficult due to a gap in understanding between the two parties on issues like licensing and operating terms. The R&D funding agency tried to mediate the negotiation of a license agreement, a critical and principal resource to launch the Firm’s activities. These attempts failed to reach agreement and the agency ceased to provide funding for the firm, leading to a halt of all the activities by the Firm. The following remarks by one of the innovators summarize the process and its results:

So the [state funding agency] did pressure [the hub hospital representative] to try to develop a contract with the two entrepreneursy[The representative] facilitated a lot of meetingsy.In November or December of 2005, we pretty much broke up with the two entrepreneurs.

As a consequence, the hub, the Firm, and the agency lost this opportunity for continued diffusion of the innovation. The lack of collaboration and coordination among major stakeholders (or the absence of mindset for collaboration) contributed to this lost opportunity.

## Discussion

We have presented a case study of a telehealth innovation that makes real-time, remote neurological expertise accessible to multiple rural hospitals. The innovation has been successfully developed and implemented into a network of hospitals. However, despite its affordable and easy-to-use technical features and potential lifesaving medical benefits, the innovation’s further diffusion rate is slow and a first attempt to commercialize the innovation failed. To understand why, we used the industry infrastructure framework (Van De Ven & Garud, 1989; Van De Ven et al., 1999; Van De Ven, 2005) in an attempt to broaden our knowledge about telehealth innovations.

## Understanding telehealth innovations

The first contribution of this research is the insights it provides into telehealth innovations. Our analysis adds to the understanding of how a telehealth innovation is adopted over time in interaction with its context. The study systematically explore the telehealth innovation context focusing on the components of the industry infrastructure framework suggested by Van de Ven and his colleagues (Van De Ven et al., 1999; Van De Ven, 2005). The stakeholder-level analysis answers the first research question of how key stakeholders addressed industrial infrastructure issues during adoption of the telehealth innovation showing how different stakeholders (the innovators, the hub hospital, the rural hospitals, the firm by the two entrepreneurs, and the state R&D funding agency) emerged over time and what they did or did not do to promote and develop individual industry infrastructure components. The innovators were identified as the main drivers of the telestroke program. Their activities in the early design and development stages were concentrated on the proprietary and resource endowment components, but the innovators were largely isolated without input from and interaction with other stakeholders. The rural hospitals were the most passive as in many other telehealth innovation cases with the innovation being pushed to them. The later emergence of the firm and the state funding agency significantly boosted the activities in all four components sharpening the different interests and perspectives among stakeholders.

The insights from the stakeholder-level analysis were complemented at the context-level addressing the second research question of how industrial infrastructure components in turn shaped the adoption of the telehealth innovation. The context-level analysis illustrated how contextual issues (the components of the industry infrastructure framework) were not only being developed and promoted by the stakeholders, but also shaped the process of adoption. The misalignment of the innovation with the existing insurance arrangement turned out to be a barrier of further adoption. The almost non-existent market mechanisms to inform and educate potential customer sectors also delayed the pace of adoption. The limited access of many rural hospitals to available competent labor and technologies was considered a constraining factor for wider adoption.

These insights contribute to the existing body of knowledge on telehealth innovations. Earlier studies have emphasized how telehealth innovations can help provide medical services to rural communities (Adewale, 2004; Chau & Hu, 2004; Mbarika, 2004), the need to effectively support knowledge sharing across the boundaries of the adopting hospitals (Paul & Mcdaniel, 2004), and the importance of effectively establishing trust between the participating medical experts and patients (Brown et al., 2004; Paul, 2006). In contrast, the insights from our analysis of REACH adds to our understanding of how telehealth innovations unfold and are shaped over time through interactions with the healthcare context, thereby adding to healthcare-IS research (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004).

There exist a few related context-oriented studies on telehealth innovations. For example, Chau & Hu (2004) examined the implementation process of a neurology telemedicine program in Hong Kong using Cooper & Zmud’s (1990) process model of technology diffusion. Whereas their analysis mainly focuses on adoption at the hub hospital, our study covers perspectives on both the hub and rural hospitals. Constantinides et al. (2006) investigated the implementation of a telemedicine system into the region of Crete, Greece, emphasizing the role played by power, by resistance to new medical practices enabled by IT, and by different forms of IT artifacts. The key contribution of their study is the insights it provides into how the innovation process and its outcomes were shaped through negotiations between actors and artifacts, between development and use, and between traditional and new medical practices. In contrast, our study is more explicit in its emphasis on context and adopts a systematic and comprehensive framework to analyzing it.

Our study of REACH adds in this way to the emerging contextual insights into telehealth innovation processes by providing new insights that can help us further explain the challenges involved. Most importantly, the study has provided a broader perspective on telehealth innovations as compared to earlier studies. First, in addition to the initial conception and implementation of the telestroke program our study has also emphasized continued attempts to further diffuse the innovation to make it readily available in other healthcare contexts. Second, we adopted a two-level analysis of the four components of industry infrastructure: proprietary activities, resource endowments, institutional arrangements, and market consumption (Van De Ven & Garud, 1989; Van de Ven, 2005). In summary, the resulting analysis shows that efforts related to proprietary activities and resource endowments were sufficiently effective to explain the successful development and initial implementation of the innovation. However, the other components of institutional arrangements and market consumption were not sufficiently mature, nor were influential stakeholders engaged to effectively develop these components to facilitate further diffusion of the innovation. Some stakeholders emerged in attempts to address these components, but their initial efforts failed without tangible results. We suggest on that basis that the unbalance between the components of the industrial infrastructure created major barriers for further and wider adoption of the telestroke program.

## The industry infrastructure perspective

This research also contributes to our understanding of how institutional theory, the industry infrastructure framework in particular, applies to and is adapted to studying IT-based innovations within healthcare. The industry infrastructure framework was initially proposed for industry-level analyses of emerging market segments and knowledge-intensive innovations (Van De Ven & Garud, 1989; Van de Ven, 2005). Our study shows how the framework also can be used to support a multi-level analysis of a single IT-based innovation as an integral part of the network of hospitals and institutions in which it is embedded. The components of the theory had to be interpreted appropriately in the context of a telehealth innovation. For REACH, proprietary activities were limited to the development of the innovation because of the non-profit nature of the academic pilot program. The innovators and the hub hospital were rarely engaged in other sub components of proprietary activities such as marketing and building resource channels. This points out differences in the investigated telehealth innovation context from that of other for-profit innovations. Also, the group of neurologists invented the innovation and pushed for its adoption into many rural hospitals. Put in a different way, the stakeholders promoting the proprietary and resource endowment components were the same. In terms of the market component, it was difficult to analyze vendor–supplier–distributor channels and consumer information and education. Given the limited time span of the study, it is difficult to tell whether these non-existent aspects of the market component were caused by specific contextual characteristics or by the early stage of the telestroke innovation.

This study also leads to new and useful interpretations of Van de Ven’s basic propositions. Van de Ven proposes that ‘actors who run in packs will be more successful than those who do it alone’ (2005, p. 370). In the context of industry-level analyses this means that entrepreneurs should simultaneously cooperate and compete with each other as they seek to develop and commercialize particular types of technological innovations. Such arrangements are analogous to the way competing bicycle racers cue their pace to one another and share the burden of breaking the wind until they reach the final stage of a race (2005, p. 371). In contexts like REACH, focusing on a single IT-based innovation and its interaction with the context, running in packs means that innovators should proactively address all industrial components, they should as early as possible engage in collaboration or negotiation with relevant stakeholders, and they should seek to align interests to facilitate subsequent further diffusion of their innovation. This insight is emphasized by Van de Ven’s second proposition: ‘actors with political savvy – an ability to recognize the interests of key actors and enroll them to one’s viewpoint – will be more successful in effecting institutional change and realizing their goals than actors without political savvy’ (2005, p. 371). Further diffusing and commercializing innovations like REACH typically require resources that are beyond the capabilities of one hospital or a small group of innovators. It requires collective action across organizational boundaries and established configurations of aligned interest to successfully build an infrastructure to facilitate continued innovation.

Overall, the industry infrastructure perspective can provide plausible and alternative explanations of why the healthcare industry is lagging behind in adopting and using IT. Healthcare entrepreneurs are therefore advised to apply the perspective to proactively manage IT-based innovations as integral parts of the organizational networks in which they are embedded. Doing so can expand their understanding of the complex and demanding conditions for IT-based innovations within the healthcare industry and it can help them identify and address institutional arrangements that represent important barriers to innovation.

## Limitations and further research

As all other research, this study has limitations. Most importantly, the argument relies on a single case of a telehealth innovation that unfolded in a specific healthcare environment in the US. While we found Van de Van’s industry infrastructure framework useful in this case as a lens to understand how agency and context interacted, some of the components and factors included in the framework might not apply equally well to other healthcare environments. Also, we have limited ourselves to analyze the case from the point of view of the components and factors explicated in the industry infrastructure framework. We have not, at this point, attempted to systematically explore whether there are different components or factors that can help us understand how healthcare industry infrastructure shapes ITbased innovation. Research is needed to further develop the framework as a useful approach to understand healthcare innovations. Such efforts should study other cases of IT-based healthcare innovations based on the framework, explore the usefulness of the framework in different healthcare environments (like for example Europe (Constantinides & Barrett, 2006), Asia (Chau & Hu, 2004), and Africa (Adewale, 2004; Mbarika, 2004), and, investigate whether the components and factors of the framework could be tailored to better represent the specifics of the healthcare industry.
