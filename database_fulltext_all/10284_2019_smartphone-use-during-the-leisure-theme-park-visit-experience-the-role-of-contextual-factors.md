## Abstract

Recent studies indicate the increasingly important role of smartphones in the customer experience. This research proposes and empirically validates a conceptual framework capturing the influence of five contextual factors on the types and intensity of smartphone use and therefore on the leisure experience during a theme park visit. A mixed research protocol combining an ethnographic approach, leisure experience narratives, and phenomenological interviews is constructed. Because of the secluded nature of theme parks, smartphones play a key role during downtime periods and thus allow visitors to enhance their leisure experience

Keywords: leisure experience; smartphone use; contextual factors; theme park.

## 1. Introduction

Smartphone use is a relevant research area that is receiving increasing attention in the tourism and information systems literature [1,2]. The ubiquitous and continuous access to information provided by smartphones allows users to communicate, collaborate, and manage and perform everyday tasks when traveling [3,4]. Unlike traditional mobile phones, smartphones offer a greater number of functions such as a camera, Internet access, and

multimedia players. One innovative aspect of smartphones in particular allows users to download numerous kinds of mobile applications. Users can thus have very individualized smartphones and services by acquiring the various smartphones functions and applications they desire [5].

The capacity of smartphones to go beyond space and time can allow users to be in two such as shopping, travelling, banking, and working. This blurring of leisure and work space and time is referred to as “fragmented time” [9] and “digital elasticity” [10]. MacKay and Vogt [11] draw on the spillover theory to explore how technology use at home can be transferred to tourism. Smartphones are therefore considered an important tool that can influence tourists’ behavior [12]. However, the singular role of smartphones in the tourism industry compared with other sectors is accentuated by the distinctiveness of the touristic experience from everyday life or work activities. Wang, Xiang, and Fesenmaier [2] suggest that the use of smartphones in the context of travel has led to a new articulation of tourism, as it challenges a number of its underlying principles used to characterize the travel experience, including home/away, authentic/inauthentic, leisure/work, host/guest, extraordinary/mundane, and present/absent. Consequently, it is important to perform additional research in the tourism context to fully characterize the role of smartphones with

Recent studies show that travel experiences are not only co-created but also influenced by technology. Indeed, the use of smartphones enables tourists to create more meaningful experiences. It allows them to learn about travel opportunities and to share experiences at any time during a trip [13]. Mobile technology such as smartphone has a specific and beneficiary role in tourism, as tourists are traversing unfamiliar environments, are in need of information to resolve practical travel problems and enrich the tourist

experience, and are in search of ways to entertain themselves and maintain relationships with

friends/family at home [6]. More importantly, smartphone use changes the traditional way of

understanding the chronology of tourism experiences. Such experiences are usually

composed of a sequence of three time-based phases: anticipatory (before the trip),

experiential (on-site), and reflective (after the trip) [4]. Because of smartphones, tourists tend

to make more decisions on-site. For example, planning tasks are moving from the

anticipatory phase to the experiential phase, and sharing tasks are moving from the reflective

to the experiential phase [1,4]. Consequently, with the use of smartphones, on-site experience

represents the critical stage of the tourism experience. From this perspective, there is a huge

opportunity for tourist destinations to shape tourists’ behavior by influencing their decisions

through relevant context-based push marketing strategies [4]. The identification of tourists

internal and external contexts could be used strategically by tourism professionals to address

tourists’ changing travel contexts with alternative and highly personalized recommendations.

To this end, a combination of social media (So) and context-aware marketing strategy (Co)

on mobile devices (Mo), abbreviated as SoCoMo marketing, can dynamically add value to

the tourists’ experience [14].

Previous studies on smartphone use for travel purposes tend to focus on particular aspects such as the development of mobile applications [15,16] or the relationship between smartphone use in everyday life and travel [11,2]. In a more recent study, Tussyadiah [4] considers the relationships of tourism with technology innovativeness and various types of smartphone use during the experiential stage of travel.

In the information systems field, Mallat et al. [17] analyze the impact of use context on mobile services acceptance (mobile ticketing acceptance). They show that mobile services acceptance depends on both users’ perception of their value and contextual factors. Jung [5] also adopts a value-oriented approach by investigating what values users pursue in using

smartphones. Salo and Frank [18] recognize the relationship of situational context with postexperience behaviors when users have critical incidents with information systems.

Despite the significant contribution of these studies, some important aspects remain insufficiently explored. First, little research examines the different types of smartphone use while traveling and their influence on tourists’ experiences beyond pretrip planning and [19,4]. Second, according to Mang et al. [20], while previous studies identify the likeliness of mobile phone use when travelling, the intensity of use, defined as the aggregate of time (duration) and frequency of smartphone use, remains unexplored. Third, smartphone use for travel can be conditioned by complex interactions between previous experiences, everyday use, cognitive beliefs, and contextual factors [1,21]. Indeed, among the predictors of consumer behavior, contextual factors are considered by a certain number of marketing and information systems studies. In particular, researchers analyze the influence of contextual factors on in-store buying behavior, postexperience behaviors, mobile services acceptance, and the consumer decision-making process [22,17,23,18]. However, with the advent of mobile technology, purchasing acts are no longer limited to physical stores. Some researchers [24] extend this perspective by studying the influence of contextual factors on the continuous use of mobile services and its effects on the consumption experience. In the tourism context, Tanti and Buhalis [1] identify the factors that boost and/or restrain travelers from obtaining a digitally enhanced tourism experience.

While tourism and information systems-related studies offer interesting insights into the contextual factors or types of smartphone use, they are missing the link between the situational context, the types and intensity of on-site smartphone use, and the leisure experience. To address this gap, the current research aims to understand in depth (1) the different types of smartphone use and the contextual factors operating during the leisure

experience and (2) the influence of these contextual factors on the types and intensity of smartphone use and therefore on the leisure experience.

## 2. Different types of smartphone use

The topic of smartphone use is examined in both the information systems and tourism information, information search, and choice of apps wanted. The study demonstrates that functions most frequently used on a smartphone include communication (e.g., mobile messengers and social networking service apps) and information search (e.g., map and dictionary). Van Deursen et al. [25] investigate the role of process and social smartphone usage in relation to habitual and addictive smartphone behavior. Process usage of smartphone comprises escaping from real life, relaxing, entertaining, staying up to date of the latest news, and so forth. Social usage refers to interacting with people, maintaining relationships by calling, texting messages, and contacting people through social media. According to Jeong et al. [26], individual use of smartphones for various purposes includes information, entertainment, and social relations. Researchers distinguish four types of smartphone use: (1) study-related use, (2) entertainment-related use (e.g., listening to music and watching videos), (3) SNS use (e.g., conversation and social interaction), and (4) game use. Liu and Yu [27] consider that usage behavior of smartphones can be categorized into four dimensions: taskbased activities (e.g., shopping, e-commerce, and learning), information-seeking activities (e.g., health, travel, and weather), communication activities (e.g., social networking, dialing, and text messaging), and recreational activities (e.g., camera, music, video, and games).

Although these studies focus on different concerns, they all reveal that the social purpose of smartphone use is predominant.

In tourism, according to Tussyadiah [4], in terms of smartphone use for travel, prior research can be classified into two groups: (1) work on the analysis of travel-related applications (apps), i.e., potential smartphone use: what smartphones can be used for, and (2) really used for.

With reference to potential smartphone use, through an analysis of travel-related apps, Wang et al. [28] note that the various types of smartphone use while traveling (before, during and after a trip) can be related to navigation and itinerary management (i.e., the use of tour guide apps), the search for information and entertainment (i.e., for passing the time), and the facilitation of travel (e.g., weather checking, translation, and currency converters). Additionally, Dickinson et al. [29], using a content analysis of visitor attraction apps in the UK, suggest that main on-site types of smartphone use are information searches (e.g., for descriptions of attractions), information sharing, itinerary management, and tagging (e.g.,

Concerning actual smartphone use, the results of the experience-based survey and ethnographic study piloted by Tussyadiah et al. [30] highlight several on-site types of smartphone use: searching for information, navigation, and information sharing. In a more recent study, Wang et al. [2] conduct a phenomenological study to examine how American travelers use smartphones while traveling in comparison to their regular everyday use. This study’s results show that the different types of smartphone use can be categorized as communication, social activity, information acquisition, information search, entertainment, and facilitation. Moreover, from her research on the impact of tourism and technology innovativeness on the on-site smartphone use of American travelers, Tussyadiah [4]

establishes a list of independent variables related to different types of smartphone use. These include trip management, navigation, push recommendation checking, online review checking, search deals, and social networking. Some smartphone applications are of more value to tourists than others. Connecting to a social network, accessing maps for navigation, and finding transportation information are found to be key uses [20].

Overall, the results of the above-mentioned studies are more or less convergent. Based on these works, the different types of smartphone use while traveling can be grouped into three main dimensions: hedonic (e.g., listening to music, playing games), relational (social networking, phone, email), and utilitarian (e.g., navigation, checking user reviews, weather, bus schedules, and translation).

## 3. Contextual factors

Context is an important research topic among academics. Context is described as “any relevant information that characterizes the situation of the user” [31, p. 609] and influences, to some extent, the pattern of user behavior [14,32,18]. Belk [33, p. 158] defines contextual personal knowledge (intra-individual) or reactions to a stimulus and has a systematic and demonstrable effect on the normal behavior of the consumer.” Any situation can be described using the following five dimensions:

The physical environment refers to the apparent characteristics of the situation, such as decoration, smells, colors, temperature, climate, and brightness.

The social environment is the presence or absence of other persons whom share the consumer experience. In the context of a theme park visit, fellow visitors may be family members, colleagues, friends, other visitors, etc.

\- The temporal perspective is relative to the time available to purchase goods or services or the time elapsed since the last purchase.

\- The antecedent state of the consumer is relative to his/her state of mind or mood such as anxiety, excitation, and tiredness.

The task definition addresses the objectives of the individual in the situation analyzed and is relative to the reasons that generate the need for consumers to buy or consume a product or service.

The conceptual framework proposed by Belk [33] has already been employed in a number of previous studies. Park et al. [23] examine the influence of the available time and store environment on consumer purchasing behavior in retail stores. Machleit and Eroglu [22] discuss the effects of store atmosphere and purchase situations. Mallat et al. [17] study the impact of use context on mobile services acceptance by adopting Belk’s [33] conceptualization. The authors demonstrate that the use context mediates the effect of perceived benefits on user intention to adopt technology. In analyzing the use of mobile services, Liang et al. [24] note that the environmental situation in which a service is used is a key variable that should be considered. Collier et al. [34] explore situational influences on customers’ self-service technology decisions. The results indicate that four situational variables (order size, wait-time tolerance, location convenience, and employee presence) all have a significant effect on customers’ self-service technology decisions.

In tourism, Vogt and Fesenmaier [35] consider background factors (e.g., social, cultural, and situational) that influence the need for information search to plan and take trips, and they conclude that information is largely collected and used for functional reasons. In particular, the notion of context is emerging in travel and tourism, and it has become essential with the advent of mobile technologies including smartphones and their apps. In line with this, Gretzel [36] and Tussyadiah [4] state that mobile devices such as smartphones change

the decision environment for en route and on-site decisions. From a managerial perspective, service providers must better capture information on contexts in which travel decisions are made to identify travelers’ needs and to design products to satisfy their needs [37]. Gretzel [12] considers that the context is extremely important when studying the potential and actual impacts of using mobile technologies. Indeed, in tourism- and technology-related studies, researchers tend to overuse the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which focuses on intention to use. In addition, the traditional technology adoption framework provides little explanation of actual use, use patterns, and the diverse user values of smartphones [5]. Beyond assessing consumers’ need for website information and intention to use devices (e.g., TAM), there remains a paucity of research that addresses tourists’ experiences with actual smartphone use when travelling and its effect on vacation behavior and experience [11]. Consequently, research on actual use, use patterns, and, most importantly, nonuse had not been sufficiently investigated. Moreover, according to Lamsfus et al. [38], the change in the travel context, such as various on-site stimuli, favors unplanned behavior. They call for interdisciplinary research to bolster the understanding of the notion of context in tourism. With regard to this, Tanti and Buhalis’s [1] study states that the tourists’ connectivity while travelling is influenced by both technological and nontechnological reasons, which are classified into four main factors: (1) hardware and software, (2) needs and contexts, (3) openness to usage, and (4) supply and provision of connectivity. According to these authors, each of these four main factors has underlying features that can enable or discourage Internetenhanced experiences. Furthermore, Buhalis and Foerste [14] propose a new concept, named SoCoMo marketing, that incorporates information emerging from the user, product suppliers, and smart destination sensors. The authors consider that innovative technologies can respond more accurately to the context within and around their users and enable them to co-create positive tourism experiences.

## 4. Methodology

4.1. Choice of study site

Theme parks are major actors in the tourism industry at both national and international levels. The theme park industry is of high economic significance [39] (Box 1). The leisure experience related to a theme park visit is chosen because of the specific nature of this tourist attraction and the lack of previous studies on the influence of contextual factors on the types and intensity of smartphone use in a theme park context. In a theme park, there is a heightens sensations and emotions. Compared with a conventional destination, a theme park is a secluded physical space, where the servicescape is of critical importance because of the hedonic nature of the service setting [40]. Indeed, theme parks are rather pleasant to be in, with no apparent negative aspects of everyday life [41]. On the other hand, theme parks are generally outdoor venues with rides as the primary attraction [39]. Theme parks are highly orderly, and they generally offer unusual worlds where the contextual changes are limited and highly predictable. The behavior of visitors follows an unchanging set of internally consistent rules, and it is highly predictable within this context. The limited set of tasks and situations makes the parks pleasurable. Indeed, the parks provide visitors with an escape from everyday life and a safe environment [41]. Nevertheless, the leisure experience in a theme park may be affected by smartphones with useful applications. As claimed by Tanti and Buhalis [1], different contexts can either increase or discourage the need for users to be connected during a tourism experience. Therefore, compared with other tourism contexts, living an experience in a secluded physical environment could lead to a different smartphone use.

These three categories of data collection methods were implemented to better reproduce the real context of a leisure park visit. Before the park visit, the research topic is first introduced to the 12 ethnologists to give them instructions relative to the ethnological observation protocol. The ethnologists agree not to disclose the study objectives to their partners. Then, a briefing session is held with the 12 visitor-participants to inform them of the safety rules during the trip. In the contract signed by all three parties (researchers, ethnologists, and visitor-participants), all informants agreed to take part in a 1-hour semi-structured interview with the researchers after visiting the park. In addition, each of the 12 ethnologists provided an observation report and photos of the visit, whereas the 12 visitor-participants agreed to write a visit experience report to be returned no later than 24 hours after the park visit. To ensure that the report focused on the smartphone use of each of the two partners, the research topic was communicated to each visitor-participant immediately after the theme park visit. To reproduce the real leisure experience, each of the 12 pairs went to Futuroscope at a different time during November/December 2015.

A few days later, the researchers conduct the content analysis of the 24 observation and experience reports to develop an initial understanding of the phenomenon. The study of the initially emerging results in the light of the theoretical background allows the identification of key themes and the construction of the in-depth interview protocol after the first phase of data collection and analysis. The main interview questions are designed to encourage the informants to interpret their behavior in light of the specific context of the leisure experience.

In January 2016, a 1-hour semi-structured interview was conducted with each sense and to gain an in-depth understanding of the visitor-participants’ and ethnologists experiences based on the results of the analysis of their reports. This approach is a comprehensive research method that seeks to unravel the structures of the lived experience. The interaction between researcher and informant makes explicit the lived experience and encourages the meaning-making activity. In this research, triple interpretation is applied. On the one hand, visitor-participants provide stories of how they understand their experiences with smartphones. Ethnologists interpret the visitor-participants’ smartphone use and are engaged in making sense of their own experiences. On the other hand, the researchers aim to interpret the two categories of informants’ experiences.

The two-step field approach is particularly suited to the analysis of the leisure experience because after a period of immersion, visitors need to detach themselves emotionally to provide a more in-depth interpretation of their behavior. This method is also appropriate when the intention is to capture informants’ responses at different time-based phases during and after the trip. The final qualitative data corpus contains 12 observation reports, 12 experience reports, and 24 phenomenological semi-structured interviews.

## 4.3. Data analysis

To perform the analysis of the collected data corpus, a code list incorporating all the themes present in the literature review is first developed. Then, the data corpus is analyzed with QSR NVivo 10. This qualitative data processing software allows a more precise thematic categorization using both predefined and emergent themes. Given that a range of contextual factors and three types of smartphone use are already identified in the literature, a mixed coding method (deductive-inductive approach) is more suitable, particularly regarding how to allow one to ascertain the novel sub-themes. Indeed, these sub-themes are extracted generated by an iterative process combining the data collected, the two researchers’ interpretations based on the existing literature, and the individual and dyadic encoding sessions. QSR NVivo 10 software allows the researchers to work in copies of a standalone project and merge them into a master project at appropriate intervals, thus making use of user profiles to track and discuss changes. The overall rate of agreement is the comparison of coding realized by two researchers, thus indicating the coding consistency. To pursue this purpose, the researchers use the function of Coding Comparison Query provided in QSR NVivo 10 software. The overall rate of agreement between the two researchers is 89%. The encoding differences are discussed to reach unanimity. To confirm the quality of the

methodology and the reliability of the research technique, the double encoding procedure is strictly respected [43].

## 5.1. Types of smartphone use during the leisure experience

The data analysis allows the identification of three dimensions in relation to the various types of smartphone use during the leisure experience: relational, utilitarian, and hedonic. Among all, 60.65% of the encoded references are related to the relational dimension, which is the one most often mentioned by the informants (Table 3). Informants regularly use social networks and consult emails, have telephone calls, and exchange messages. Sometimes, they share on social networks in real time the delightful moments experienced within the park. Communication with the outside world is continuous, mainly with friends and family members:

“I sent messages to my boyfriend at about 10 a.m. Because last June we went to the Little Prince attraction and he thought it was great, but well... it’s not... it’s more for the kids...” (Marie)

The percentages of references relative to the utilitarian and hedonic dimensions are almost equal with, respectively, 20.83% and 18.52% of the encoded references. During the trip, utilitarian smartphone use is essentially related to checking train or bus schedules, as well as consulting maps and information about on-site tourism providers. Taking pictures, playing games, reading online news, listening to music, and watching videos are all activities that reveal the hedonic use of mobile devices. In some situations, several types of use occur simultaneously:

“I looked quickly at Facebook, and I played a little. I set the alarm so as not to miss the arrival of the train.” (Thomas)

Additionally, the verbatim analysis reveals no difference between first-time and repeat visitors (to Futuroscope) in terms of both the types and intensity of smartphone use.

## 5.2. Contextual factors during the leisure experience

Five contextual factors proposed by Belk [33] are all identified to describe a situation during the leisure experience. The first of these factors is the temporal perspective. This contextual factor relates to 49.82% of the encoded references and is particularly linked to the chronology of the visit. Dividing the visit into separate chronological phases reveals three smartphone use contexts, which each have equivalent weight in terms of references: the round-trip travel time, time spent on attractions, and downtime (standing in line, walking between attractions, etc.). The second contextual factor addresses the task definition with 25.03% of the references. The need for visitors to use their smartphone is generated either by travel-related purposes (13.00%) or by nontravel-related purposes (12.03%). The third contextual factor, with 10.81% of references, is the physical environment. This factor includes technology-related elements (smartphone characteristics, network availability, mobile applications, etc.) and other information media present during the visit experience (video screens, paper maps, service staff, etc.). With 7.78% of the references, the fourth contextual factor highlights the social environment and, most specifically, the role of the visit companion in smartphone use. The fifth factor includes references related to the visitor’s mood and the perceived attractiveness of the park. This factor constitutes 6.56% of the references. These two sub-dimensions have been grouped into a general dimension proposed by Belk [33] and labeled as the antecedent state of the consumer.

## 5.3. Influence of contextual factors on the intensity of smartphone use

During the visit of a theme park, each of the contextual factors influences the intensity of smartphone use differently.

With 60.09% of the references, the contextual factor that has the most impact on the intensity of smartphone use is the temporal perspective. There is more intensive smartphone visitors abandon their smartphones to get away from their everyday lives and immerse themselves in the leisure experience:

“Obviously, nobody uses their phone during the attraction. People enjoy the moment. They know that the attraction is right now and that they can’t experience it later.” (Julien)

The desire to fully enjoy the attractions and time constraints led visitors not to use their smartphones as frequently as usual. Moreover, visitors are conscious of living an extraordinary and unique experience at the attractions, whereas smartphones are considered to be a tool related to everyday life:

“Being part of a rare or exceptional event breaks with routine…; with this experience, you take a step back. Suddenly, when you enter the attraction, the smartphone is put to one side because it represents everyday reality.” (Tanguy)

On the other hand, in the time before and after the visit, as well as during the downtime, more intensive smartphone use reinforces the overall experience and shows that visitors seek to escape monotony because of smartphone use:

“She used her smartphone very frequently while walking.... I mean to go from one attraction to

another.” (Bérengère)

In particular, the downtime periods enable visitors to stay connected to the outside world and to interact more intensively with their families and friends:

“We had to wait 5 minutes before the movie started, so she sent a message to her boyfriend asking what he was doing at Christmas. At the same time, her brother was asking if she had any ideas for their parents’ Christmas gifts.” (Camille)

The second contextual factor influencing the intensity of smartphone use is the physical environment. With only 16.47% of the references, this contextual factor is less present in the verbatim. Within the physical dimension, a second set of contextual elements influencing the intensity of smartphone use is that of information sources other than the smartphone. The presence of these information tools within the park can lead visitors to use their smartphones less. In other words, the park paper maps, signage, and service staff negatively influence the intensity of smartphone use:

“He realized that he had forgotten to download the park map onto his smartphone. So we took a paper map, and we managed well on our own with this. It was not as complicated as you might imagine. It’s the good old method.” (Marine)

These other information media are not only a barrier to smartphone use within the park but are often favored by visitors during the experience. Indeed, conventional information supports may be accorded greater value:

“Of course, there was a paper map at the main entrance, and suddenly, it was more fun to look at the map, to look for a particular location. I downloaded the app, but finally, the paper map is bigger, and it was easier to follow a path.” (Marine)

The social environment also has an impact on the intensity of smartphone use. A small proportion of verbatim (13.23%) focuses on the specific influence of visit companions on the intensity of smartphone use. The presence of companions negatively influences the intensity of smartphone use. Informants confirmed that they preferred human contact:

“Because in fact when I was with A, I shared my feelings with him, so I didn’t need to share with someone else during that time. I found it a bit disrespectful to send a message while I was with him in the park.” (Clément)

Two exceptions to this finding should be highlighted: the imitation effect and shared recreational activities. In a group, the intensity of smartphone use may increase simply by reproducing the behavior of others:

“And when I was on the Internet, he was also connected to the Internet and sent text messages.” (Paul)

The intensity of smartphone use also increases when visitors use their smartphones to share recreational activities with their visit companions, such as playing games or listening to music:

“At one point, he used an app on his smartphone because we listened to music together, and we didn’t know this music. So, he used a smartphone app to find out more about it.” (Gladys)

The fourth contextual factor influencing the intensity of smartphone use is the antecedent state of the consumer. This relates to 10.21% of the references. The intensity of smartphone use primarily fluctuates with the perceived attractiveness of the theme park. Indeed, when their perceived attractiveness of a park is strong, visitors use their smartphones less often:

“No, because in my opinion, Futuroscope isn’t interesting. If there had been more attractions, this would have pleased me, and I would really have turned off my smartphone. Yes, it’s a matter of interest.” (Augustin)

In addition, the intensity of use depends on the visitors’ state of mind. The pleasure and sense of well-being experienced within the park lead informants to either share their experiences with the outside world or renounce their smartphone use, while a sense of unhappiness or family emergency issues lead to a more intensive use:

“In any case, I mainly sent messages to my mother, because she was sick; I was worried about her, and I wanted to know how she was.” (Thomas)

## 5.4. Influence of contextual factors on the types of smartphone use

The temporal perspective has an impact on the types of smartphone use. During the round-trip, visitors use their smartphones for relational, utilitarian, and hedonic activities, whereas they give priority to relational smartphone use during downtime periods. Thus, the time between two activities is considered a priority devoted to sharing the leisure experience and to having real-time interaction with not only companions but also nonvisitors. While communicating with the outside world, visitors seek to enhance their personal leisure experience. From this perspective, real-time smartphone use leads them to give additional value to their experience by sharing it with other people:

“Yes, I sent some messages to my mother to tell her about my experience in the park. She wanted to know how things were going. I also sent messages and photos to my sister to annoy her—in fact, to say that I was experiencing the park and that she was not.” (Thomas)

The task definition has a positive influence on the three smartphone use types and thus on leisure experience outcomes. Nonetheless, travel-related task or nontravel-related task informants mainly use their smartphones for relational purposes. Hedonic and utilitarian uses also positively affect the leisure experience but to a lesser extent than relational use.

“With my boyfriend, we had already seen the Little Prince attraction before, but we did not appreciate it. So, I sent him a message to remind him of this unpleasant experience.” (Camille) “Well with her friend, they had to see each other in the evening to go to a dance party, but Marie was tired, so she called her friend to cancel the appointment.” (Bérengère)

The physical environment also affects the types of smartphone use. Informants take better advantage of the different types of smartphone use (relational, hedonic, and utilitarian) when they experience favorable material and technical conditions. As an illustration, a low level of ergonomic factors and other smartphone performance issues discourage visitors from searching real-time information on their smartphones:

“No, I did not use it (smartphone) precisely because the major problem of a BlackBerry is that it is not very practical for going on the Internet.” (Charlotte)

However, having a good, powerful smartphone increases hedonic use:

“Well, my digital camera is not really very good. It’s a Samsung, but pictures are often unclear. I have to adjust the picture every time. But the smartphone, I know it takes great pictures, and I had it on me.” (Pauline)

The presence of information support materials other than smartphones in the park only affects the utilitarian dimension. Making available these support materials (paper maps, signage, and park staff) reduces the utilitarian use. Informants unanimously state that they prefer to use conventional information media rather than the digital ones available within the park:

“We downloaded the app, but ultimately, the paper map was bigger and better, so it was more convenient for us.” (Marine)

The impact of the social environment on the types of smartphone use also deserves to be emphasized. The smartphone can be a tool for exchange and interaction between visitors. The desire to interact more with visit companions leads visitors to use their smartphones for shared recreational activities such as playing games, exchanging photos, or listening to music:

“At this time, we were sitting on a bench, looking at some pictures while we were talking. He showed me a funny picture of a mutual friend.” (Clara)

The social context also influences the relational dimension. The imitation effect essentially seems to lead visitors to more intensive smartphone uses for relational purposes. Discussion on social networks or message exchanges with people outside may encourage a partner to do the same. Moreover, these relational activities can open common topics for discussion and improve interaction:

“We both used our smartphones to send messages and discussed our messages each time. It is true that we are close enough to discuss this kind of thing.” (Claire)

Consequently, the verbatim clearly showed an influence of the social context on the hedonic and relational dimensions, but there is no verbatim that showed its impact on the utilitarian dimension.

Verbatim did not illustrate the influence of the visitor’s antecedent state on the hedonic and utilitarian use of smartphones. The antecedent state of the visitor, however, does have an impact on the relational use of smartphone. Through the smartphone, visitors are not entirely cut off from their daily lives. For instance, they can continue to maintain a poor state of mind, which can weaken the pleasure felt by the leisure experience. Indeed, in a difficult personal context, relational exchanges by text messages or phone calls do not allow visitors to escape from daily reality and can hinder the benefits usually provided by leisure activities:

“Indeed, I found T very thoughtful, skeptical, and even willing to be alone. It seems that T had a conversation with someone who had made him uncomfortable during that visit day. He often looked at his smartphone, as if he was expecting an answer.” (Paul)

## 6. Discussion

This study helps to underline the importance of contextual factors regarding smartphone use during the leisure experience. The identification of five contextual factors related to a visit to a theme park illustrates the hindrances and motivations with regard to smartphone use.

The main hindrances to smartphone use in a theme park setting arise from the desire to enjoy the attractions, the value given to traditional information support materials, and the material and technical conditions. Compared with a conventional long-term tourist holiday, during a theme park visit, visitors want to gain the most of their time on attractions and give up using their smartphones during this time.

Moreover, the existence of conventional information support materials in the park leads visitors to reduce the utilitarian use of smartphones. Presently, despite the importance

#

given to digital information supports in the strategies designed by tourist destinations and companies, the value of traditional information media has not decreased. A number of material and technical hindrances including poor network performance and ineffective mobile applications also hamper smartphone use, which further explains the commitment and importance given to traditional information media. This result is in line with Tanti and a barrier to connectivity. With regard to this, Gretzel [12] calls for more in-depth research on the nonuse of mobile technologies in the travel and tourism context.

By highlighting three main types of smartphone use, the results of this research confirm those of the existing literature in other tourism contexts [4,2]. In concrete terms, the types of smartphone use tend to remain the same regardless of the nature of the tourism experience. However, the results of previous studies conducted in a conventional tourist visit context differ regarding the predominance of the specific type of smartphone use. For example, Dickinson et al. [29] and Tussyadiah et al. [30] show that smartphones are mainly used for utilitarian purposes, whereas Gretzel et al. [44] and Mang et al. [20] stress the in the use of this technology, as well as the importance of sharing the travel experience with others. This study is consistent with the previous studies and suggests that smartphone use in the theme park context is predominantly relational. The high proportion of verbatim related to the relational dimension reveals that during a theme park visit, the smartphone is used by visitors mainly to interact with the outside world. Smartphone use enhances the leisure experience mainly as a result of relational use linked to either travel or nontravel tasks. Because of the secluded nature of theme parks, smartphones play a key role during downtime periods and thus allow visitors to enhance their leisure experience through quasi-permanent social interaction with nonvisitors. Visitors amplify the value that they obtain from such

#

experience by sharing real-time feelings with outside people. Moreover, interaction with the outside world engenders a positive self-image because of the extraordinary and emotional nature of the leisure experience. Visitors’ feelings of self-esteem appear to increase when they communicate the satisfaction resulting from their real-time experience to outside people. Relational use of smartphones can also generate discussion topics and enrich relationships with visit companions. Because of smartphones, this dynamic interaction between visitors SoCoMo concept [14].

Smartphone use for utilitarian and hedonic purposes is relatively less frequent. Compared with other tourism contexts, the attractions or guidance services (e.g., service staff, maps, and signage) within a theme park are often easy to locate or readily available, which might minimize the utilitarian use of smartphones. Furthermore, according to Sternberg and Grigorenko [41], the behavior of theme park visitors follows a fixed itinerary and is highly predictable. The restrictions of tasks in a safe environment make such parks pleasant for visitors and may reduce the utilitarian and hedonic uses of smartphones.

Although the hedonic use of smartphones is less frequent than their relational use, it offers visitors an undeniable benefit. Beyond the ability to play games or listen to music during downtime, smartphones provide an interaction interface between visit companions. They can thus encourage reciprocity and bring visitors to further exchanges. Consistent with prior studies [13], this result also helps explain the growing success of mobile applications and social networks in tourism. These online services provide new ways for visitors to share their experiences and interact with visit companions.

The findings of this research are to certain extent consistent with the study conducted by Rivera et al. [16] on the use of mobile services, in the small island destination of Aruba.

Indeed, the results presented here indicate that there is no difference in terms of the types and intensity of smartphone use between first-time and repeat visitors (to Futuroscope).

Furthermore, this study shows that smartphone use is less intense in the theme park visit context because a theme park visit is often limited to one day and is carried out by several persons. Visitors tend to prefer to fully experience a park with their companions. This others affects the level of dependency on the Internet. Our results also confirm the findings of previous studies on the role of visitor companions during leisure experiences. On the one hand, social contact within a group is considered to be not only a full leisure activity (e.g., having conversations) but also an important reason to actively take part in all shared leisure activities [45]. Most recreational activities take place in the presence of others, such as going to a concert, playing tennis, and visiting a park. Thus, leisure activities shared with others would appear to partly fulfill some major needs related to social interaction, affiliation, or harmony with others. On the other hand, the absence of a companion is often considered to be a major barrier to participating in shared leisure activities [46]. For example, Searle and Jackson [47] show that difficulty in finding a visit companion is classified fourth among 15 potential hindrances to leisure activities.

To synthetize the results, the present research constitutes an empirical illustration of the SoCoMo model. In fact, contextual factors are highly dynamic, and some of them can be objectively characterized and controlled by park managers, while others are subjective and dependent on visitors. Thus, factors such as the chronology of the visit, technology-related issues in the park, and conventional information supports are automatically derived from the environment, whereas other factors such as visitors’ state of mind, visit companions, and perceived attractiveness of the park are specific to the visitor. Hence, the contextual

information is increasingly relevant, and it can help tourism organizations dynamically construct their context-aware services and offerings [14].

## 7. Managerial recommendations

The clarification of barriers and motivations for smartphone use in the theme park park managers. This study demonstrates the influence of contextual factors on smartphone use. It is therefore necessary for theme park managers to accurately identify and give more attention to these contextual factors. Beyond the three-dimension categorization, these contextual factors can be classified according to the degree of control that theme park managers can have over them.

Park managers have no control over the round-trip stage, the type of smartphone owned, and the antecedent states of individuals. However, given the importance of contextaware marketing strategy, in accordance with the SoCoMo framework, they can apply some push marketing strategies during the round-trip phase. For instance, managers can send to visitors through their smartphones practical information when going to the park or messages of thanks, satisfaction surveys, and promotional offers during the return from the park. Nevertheless, managers can to some extent control the social environment. They can have a significant influence on smartphone use during the time dedicated to attractions and the downtime periods, as well as on the provision of powerful Internet access and mobile applications and the set of conventional information support materials within the park.

To be more efficient, it is necessary to principally act on controllable contextual factors. The present results show that the relational use of smartphones during downtime enriches the leisure experience, circumventing the negative perception due to a long waiting time. Park managers should make available to their visitors good Wi-Fi access and interactive

#

virtual collaboration spaces, as smartphones can be used any place and time while connected through Wi-Fi or 4G mobile networks. The utilization of social networks generally relies on mobile devices with Internet access. As a result, users can stay socially connected while traveling [48,14]. In addition, through interactive virtual collaboration spaces, as suggested by the SoCoMo marketing approach, information can be shared not only with visitors by the park but also by visitors themselves. Concretely, in terms of value co-creation for all stakeholders, one can develop a mobile platform that allows the park to amalgamate all interactions of staff and visitors on one level throughout the whole visitor’s journey—before, during, and after the visit. Visitors provide personal information and preferences such as preferred attractions, desired access time to selected attractions, and favorite foods and beverages, and the staff can collect this specific information. By accessing this platform, all stakeholders co-create through exchanging information in real time, which leads to more personalized interactions, more valuable service encounters, and an overall enhanced experience for visitors.

In addition, in queues, the park can install photo booths related to the attraction themes and/or connected terminals to create mini-videos so that visitors can take pictures, make videos, and then share them on social networks and virtual collaboration spaces. This would encourage visitors to share content and to interact with their communities and would thus allow them to avoid having negative feelings caused by waiting times. The relational use of smartphones could therefore create a better image and a good reputation for theme parks. Thus, theme parks could effectively expand their market.

Likewise, it is also important to make efficient digital information supports available to visitors. Within the context of extensive digitization of tourism environments, the cost and difficulty of accessing the theme park application can negatively affect the perceived quality of the experience and therefore visitors’ satisfaction. The results indicate that the essential

#

characteristics of an efficient theme park mobile application are information reliability, interactivity (interactive map indicating the attractions already attended and allowing one to evaluate these attractions, interactive thematic games), regular updating, schedule access, waiting times of attractions in real time, distance and routes to attractions, and free access. To this end, theme park managers should develop an efficient mobile application to not only accompany visitors in their discovery of the park but also collect external and internal Otherwise, it is observed that gamification remains little valued in light of the mobile applications proposed to date by the majority of theme parks, while this research shows that the hedonic and relational value of smartphone use allows visitors, often accompanied, to enhance their visit experience. Consequently, gamification can be added to existing applications to encourage visitors to interact with their communities and create buzz. For example, one can check the attractions that one has made. The more attractions that one tests, the more points one earns, and the more steps one goes through. One can thus compare oneself to the community. One can also earn points by answering questions and then be rewarded. In addition, one can order meals via the park’s application to avoid waiting times. This has not yet been considered in theme parks.

This research identifies the importance that visitors attach to conventional information media, which are indispensable within the park and make the visitor experience more enjoyable. Although the positive impact of smartphones on the tourist experience has been demonstrated in previous studies, it is also essential to continue to develop and improve conventional information supports. Instead of exclusively focusing on digital support, theme park managers must seek to create complementarities between conventional and digital media.

The final set of recommendations focuses on the characteristics and role of visit companions. Theme park managers can have an influence on the social context of theme park visit by offering family, student, or group visit packages. The smartphone can be integrated in these visits in a profitable way if the park offers interactive activities and online games adapted to the profile of members within a visit group. The smartphone can thereby be experience within the park. This kind of hedonic and collective offer may lead visitors to spontaneously use their smartphones in order to be fully immersed in the experience of various attractions.

## 8. Contributions and further research

This research yields theoretical, methodological, and managerial contributions.

Theoretically, this research has five main contributions. The first contribution lies in the identification of five contextual factors as proposed by Belk [33]. The content of these five factors specifically is applicable to smartphone use during theme park visits. Although previous marketing and information systems studies [34,22,18] address the role of contextual factors in various consumption experiences, contextual factors are often overlooked in tourism studies. Consequently, the present results show the transferability of the theoretical concept of contextual factors from the general management field to leisure management and therefore confirm the relevance of the conceptual framework developed by Belk [33]. Moreover, for each contextual factor, this research identifies a range of variables particularly specific to the theme park visit context. These variables differently influence the types and intensity of smartphone use. In this way, the authors propose a conceptual framework capturing a specific nuance of smartphone use in the leisure experience context (Fig. 2). Second, the current empirical research allows to conceptualize and validate three types of

smartphone use (relational, hedonic, and utilitarian) in the theme park visit context. The few recent previous works [30,4,2] related to the types of smartphone use that have been conducted in other tourism contexts do not explicitly propose this tripartite categorization. Third, the present research highlights the influence either positive or negative of contextual factors related to the intensity of smartphone use. Fourth, this research provides evidence that contextual factors influence the types of smartphone use. Consequently, the sub-dimensions identified within each contextual factor affect the types and intensity of smartphone use and, by extension, the leisure experience in the particular theme park setting. This theoretical contribution captures the dynamic interplay between theme park-specific contextual factors, smartphone use, and the leisure experience. Finally, this research empirically validates the theoretical framework proposed and therefore the SoCoMo concept.

The methodological originality lies in the research protocol implemented and the nature of the data collected. To analyze the influence of contextual factors on the actual use of smartphones during the leisure experience, the research protocol combines on-site observation of visitor behaviors, experience reports after visiting theme parks, and phenomenological interviews. The combination of these three sources is particularly important because they relate the same experience but are reported at three different chronological times.

From a managerial point of view, it is necessary to emphasize the importance given to the relational use of smartphones in a theme park visit context, which reduces the negative perception of long waiting times and increases the interactivity between visitors. Although the digitalization of tourist information supports is favored by destination managers today, this study asserts that conventional information supports remain appreciated by visitors in the theme park visit context. Park managers must therefore create complementarities between digital and physical supports.

#

The main limitation of this qualitative study resides in the homogeneous characteristics of the sample, which consists of young French people who are familiar with the use of smartphones (all informants have at least 2 years of experience in terms of smartphone use). Nevertheless, the work by Tussyadiah [4] demonstrates that the technology innovativeness of travelers has a significant and positive effect on the on-site use of smartphones for trip management, navigation, push recommendations, online reviews, search deals, and social networking and that older travelers (aged 35 years and above) have a higher extent of smartphone use for push recommendations. Thus, the results of this study offer new perspectives for future research on different profile samples (older travelers, travelers with less experience in smartphone use, travelers of other nationalities). A quantitative study helping to confirm the present results would also be useful.

Valentina Kirova holds a PhD degree in marketing from the University of Bordeaux, France. She is currently associate professor at La Rochelle Business School, France. Her current research focuses on strategic marketing and tourism issues and has been published in peerreviewed journals such as Journal of Business Research, Journal of Marketing Trends, Revue Française du Marketing, and Teoros.

Tan Vo Thanh graduated with a PhD degree in tourism science at the University of Perpignan Via Domitia, France. He is currently associate professor at La Rochelle Business School, France. His recent research has been published in peer-reviewed journals such as Journal of Business Research, Tourism Analysis, European Journal of Tourism Research, International Journal of Culture, Tourism and Hospitality Research, Management International, etc.
