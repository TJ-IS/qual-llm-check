# A doxa-informed practice analysis: reflexivity and representations, technology and action

Benjamin P. W. Ellway\* & Geoff Walsham<sup>†</sup>

\*School of Management, Faculty of Business, Government & Law, University of Canberra, Australia, email: benjamin.ellway@canberra.edu.au, and <sup>†</sup>Cambridge Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, UK, email: g.walsham@jbs.cam.ac.uk

Abstract. The information systems (IS) work practice literature recognises that the use of information technology in organisations and broader social structuring is composed of both human action and the properties of technology. Social representations of work are important as re<sup>fl</sup>exive agents may see contradictions between these and their use of technology in practice, thus providing a trigger for change. However, agents do not always see contradictions, which allows them to endure by favouring practice reproduction. To explain this inconsistency, this paper introduces the concept of doxa as a sensitising device for a practice analysis of call centre work and examines the narratives developed by advisors to represent their call-handling practices. The contributions of our doxa-informed practice analysis to the literature on IS and work practices include the following: <sup>fi</sup>rstly, to indicate how a simultaneous presence and absence of agents’ re<sup>fl</sup>exivity occurs; secondly, to demonstrate how the sensemaking function provided by social representations of work and technology can be undermined; and <sup>fi</sup>nally to show how material conditions of work and organisation may remain hidden, despite being directly implicated in the logic of practice.

Keywords: practice theory, human agency, re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, social representations Bourdieu, doxa, narratives, call centres

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS WORK PRACTICES: REFLEXIVITY, REPRESENTATIONS, AGENCY AND MATERIALITY

Practice theory (Bourdieu, 1977, 1990) and structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) emphasise the mutual constitution of recurrent human action and broader social structures and as a resul have become in<sup>fl</sup>uential theoretical lenses in the information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld. As information technology (IT) is not explicitly discussed by either Bourdieu or Giddens. there have beer various attempts at conceptualising technology (Table 1, <sup>fi</sup>rst section). Firstly, Orlikowski (1992) introduced the notion of the duality of technology, which highlighted that IT systems are used by humans but at the same time are ‘interpretively <sup>fl</sup>exible’ and can be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed through use. DeSanctis & Poole (1994) argued that social structures could become embedded within technologies and that individuals then appropriate them through use. However, this was rejected by Orlikowski (2000), whose revised conceptualisation of technology instead focused upon ‘technology-in-practice’, maintaining that it is only through interaction with speci<sup>fi</sup>c properties of a technology that structures emerge as they are enacted through recurrent practice by humans.

Each of these approaches provides a central role for human agency. We know that some IS, such as enterprise resource planning systems, are often in<sup>fl</sup>exible following their con<sup>fi</sup>guration and implementation (Robey et al., 2002; Kallinikos, 2004), which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the observation that certain technologies may represent more rigid constraints on our actions (Markus. 2004). By recognising that agents can always “choose to do otherwise” (Giddens, 1993), researchers arguing for the importance of human agency emphasise that we can resist, ignore or reinvent as pects of technology (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). This helps to explain why organisations use iden tical software applications in different ways (Robey & Sahay, 1996) and how users appropriate features that differ from the purposes for which IT systems were originally designed (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004). However, despite being valuable in guarding against deterministic accounts of technology, research has increasingly attempted to address the role of materiality and non-human agents alongside human agency (Leonardi, 2007, 2011; Leonardi & Barley, 2008). For example, ‘sociomaterial’ approaches to studying practice argue that the social and the material are inextricably linked (Orlikowski, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). This implies that identifying precisely how the two aspects are mutually implicated in practice is potentially com plex. Certain properties of IS may become materialised during practice through ‘agential cuts’, which create boundaries between human and non-human actors (e.g. technology) (Barad, 2003).

One particularly important aspect of human agency is the ‘re<sup>fl</sup>exive monitoring’ of everyday life (Giddens, 1993). Re<sup>fl</sup>exivity can be understood as the mental capability of agents to consider themselves in relation to the immediate social context(s) in which they operate, which includes the material properties of technology they use, and to consider how these are implicated in their own attitudes, actions and personal circumstances. IS research has acknowledged the important role of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity processes related to IT use (Table 1, second section). Re<sup>fl</sup>exive processes help explain agents’ reactions to and recognition of how newly introduced IT may affect their work and context (Barrett et al., 2001). Re<sup>fl</sup>exivity can also assis in the analysis of cross-cultural working associated with developing and using technology (Walsham, 2002). Re<sup>fl</sup>exive processes are important to examine because of their subsequent impact on broader social structures (Barrett & Walsham, 1999). Even where re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a concept or process is not explicitly examined in practice-based studies of IS use, it is often an implicit assumption (see, for example, Pawlowski & Robey, 2004; Leonardi, 2007). de Vaujany (2008) demonstrated the existence of different re<sup>fl</sup>exivity modes outlined by Archer (2003) in relation to how workers represent and use IT. However, he noted that “strangely, re<sup>fl</sup>exivity processes seemed absent in many presentations where instincts and habits dominated the mental activity” (de Vaujany, 2008: 65). de Vaujany (2008) therefore acknowledged that re<sup>fl</sup>exive processes may be more or less active and accepted that in some instances, routines or instincts will dominate an actor’s agency. He concluded with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c call for further empirica work to understand sociotechnical re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

IS research has further explored human agency and re<sup>fl</sup>exivity by drawing upon the theory of social representations, which is a social-cognitive approach to understanding how common sense knowledge is produced within a given social context (Moscovici, 1988). Socia representations are socially constructed by (re<sup>fl</sup>exive) agents to constitute and make sense of their world (Moscovici, 1988). They help individuals to orient a given social context and communicate with its members, including classifying various phenomena that constitute it (Moscovici, 2001). Social representations are thus implicated in ‘sensemaking’ processes (Weick, 1995), which can occur within groups as members develop shared understanding, or between communities through re<sup>fl</sup>ection and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of existing perspectives (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). IS research has drawn upon social representations as a way to explain how technology is perceived and used, and how this affects how work is performed (Table 1, <sup>fi</sup>na section). For example, the notion of ‘technological frames’, which involve shared beliefs, values and understandings concerning a technology that are held by a group (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), is re<sup>fl</sup>ective of a social representations approach. Incongruence or shifts in technologica frames can affect how technology is interpreted, whether it is adopted and how it is used (Barrett, 1999; Davidson, 2002; Olesen, 2012). A social representations perspective enables a broader examination of technology along with the wider social and organisational context, emphasising the role of social processes (Gal & Berente, 2008). Through the development o social representations, IT users make sense of the consequences of work and its surrounding context (Pawlowski et al., 2007), while the way IT is represented can in<sup>fl</sup>uence attitudes and work practices (Vaast, 2007) and organisational policies concerning technology that are re<sup>fl</sup>ective of social representations (Kaganer & Vaast, 2010). Social representations may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence how technology is tailored by users during use (Germonprez & Gal, 2009).

A key theoretical insight concerning social representations is provided by Vaast & Walsham (2005), who examined the micro-level of practices as a basis for explaining why people continue or change how they work and use IT. Vaast & Walsham (2005) argued that social representations are central to the process of IS use and reproduction or change in broader social structures, based upon whether agents see contradiction or congruence between their use of IT, their actions and the representations of their own and others’ work. This is because this prompts agents, respectively, to experience either dissonance or consonance, which is a trigger for changes in practice or for the reproduction of existing ways of working. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects the fact that social representations play a key role in helping groups to develop a shared understanding about an unfamiliar event, situation or development (Voelklein & Howarth, 2005). However, there is contradictory evidence to Vaast & Walsham’s (2005) argument. Although not adopting a social representations approach, Schultze & Boland’s (2000) practice analysis of a newly introduced knowledge management technology is particularly interesting. In contrast to Vaast & Walsham’s (2005) position, agents failed to see contradictions between the capabilities of a newly introduced system and their role, work and status within the organisation until after the pilot implementation, and then apparently only following discussion with the researcher. This was despite the rather ironic fact that they themselves were the group who were championing the introduction of the new technology within the company.

This prompts intriguing questions. How can contradictions between work practices, IS use and representations of work endure? Why, in some instances, do agents not identify contradictions between their use of technology in practice within a given social context and their associated representations of this? Does this indicate an absence of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, and if so, why does it persist? This question is particularly interesting in light of de Vaujany’s (2008) call for IS researcher to further study sociotechnical re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. Furthermore, a speci<sup>fi</sup>c emphasis upon sociotechnical re<sup>fl</sup>exivity clearly resonates with the recent focus on the social and material aspects of practice highlighted in the <sup>fi</sup>rst theme within Table 1. This prompts an additional question – are the material properties of technologies always necessarily revealed to/by human agents through so-called cuts in practice? Moscovici (2001) speci<sup>fi</sup>cally referred to the role of social representations in relation to peoples’ social and material world. But is the materia always represented in and through the social?

This paper addresses these questions through a practice-based analysis of an interpretive study of work practices in a call centre, particularly focusing on advisors’ reactions to a newly introduced IS and their opinions regarding challenges associated with call handling and providing effective customer service. The theoretical background for our investigation is Bourdieu’s theory of practice (Bourdieu, 1977, 1990), and we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally extend the application of his theory within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld by introducing the concept of doxa (to be explained in the following section) as a sensitising device for analysing the dynamics of practice. To furthe strengthen our analysis, we draw upon Pentland’s (1999) levels of narrative structure to enable a more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained examination of the stories and accounts produced about work and technology in the call centre.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows. The following section introduces Bourdieu’s theory of practice and doxa. Thereafter, the methodology is explained with particular reference to how Klein & Myers’s (1999) principles for conducting interpretive research guided and were upheld during the development of the research. Following a brief introduction to the case organisation, the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the case analysis reveals inconsistencies between stories constructed in relation to three different call-handling scenarios and associated work practices. The second part of the analysis utilises the doxa concept to help explain why these contradictions endure and how technological and organisational conditions of work are reproduced through advisors’ attitudes towards IS, information and customers. The paper concludes by discussing the insights arising from the analysis and identifying contributions to IS research that examines re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and its consequences, the function of social representations, and the mutual in<sup>fl</sup>uence of human agency and material properties of technology in practice.

## BOURDIEU’S THEORY OF PRACTICE AND THE CONCEPT OF DOXA

The IS literature has demonstrated the insightfulness of Bourdieu’s ideas for analysing IS in organisations and contemporary society (Kvasny & Truex, 2000; Schultze & Boland, 2000; Richardson & Howcroft, 2006; Richardson, 2009) but has not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally utilised the concept of doxa. Bourdieu introduced the concept of doxa in his original theorisation of practice (Bourdieu, 1977) and has since used it in one of his most widely recognised works – Distinction (Bourdieu, 1984) – and has discussed it in relation to organisations and work (Bourdieu, 1981). The following description provides a summary of Bourdieu’s key concepts of practice, habitus and <sup>fi</sup>eld and then highlights the relevance of doxa as a sensitising device for examining practice reproduction and IS use.

Bourdieu characterises society as being made up of different <sup>fi</sup>elds, which can relate to broad <sup>fi</sup>elds such as education or politics, and also includes smaller and more speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>elds, such as call handling and service provision in a call centre. A <sup>fi</sup>eld is composed of ‘objective structures’, which, for example, re<sup>fl</sup>ect particular ways of organising and performing work, often based upon speci<sup>fi</sup>c values or systems of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Agents are guided by their habitus, which is composed of a set of dispositions, both durable and transposable in nature, that acts as a mental template for action based upon particular attitudes and interests. Habitus equips agents with practical sense to understand, navigate and act within a given <sup>fi</sup>eld. As represented in Figure 1, habitus, <sup>fi</sup>eld and practice are linked through a reciprocal relationship. Thus, it is only through agents recurrent practice that the obiective structures' of field become reflected in the ‘incorporated structures’ of habitus, which serves to reproduce the very objective structures of the <sup>fi</sup>eld of which the habitus itself is the product. Bourdieu refers to this as ‘the circuit of reproduction’. Thus, if one is to understand the logic of practice that underpins a given <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is imperative to carefully examine the nature of practices and how they serve to constitute particular ways of organising work based upon speci<sup>fi</sup>c values or classi<sup>fi</sup>catory systems, and how these are incorporated into individuals’ dispositions and attitudes.

Bourdieu de<sup>fi</sup>nes doxa through its oppositional relation to opinion, the latter representing the existence of beliefs and values emerging through discourse from a given <sup>fi</sup>eld, for example, in relation to systems of classifying appropriate or favoured ways of performing work activities. As shown in Figure 2, opinion emerges from ‘the universe of discourse’, which can involve orthodox and heterodox positions, the latter representing the potential to challenge the more dominant and widely accepted status of the former. In contrast, doxa represents ‘the universe of the undiscussed’, which is characterised by what Bourdieu refers to as ‘misrecognised’ and unquestioned aspects of practice. Opinion emerging from discourse therefore re<sup>fl</sup>ects “awareness and recognition of the possibility of different or antagonistic beliefs” (Bourdieu, 1977: 164) within any given <sup>fi</sup>eld of practice. Put in simple terms, management may maintain that a newly introduced technology will make work easier (orthodox position), while some employees in the organisation may begin to challenge this position by highlighting technica limitations of the technology (heterodoxy). However, doxa highlights that further de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the social world may remain beyond question and therefore undisputed, as something “each agent tacitly accords to by the mere fact of acting in accord with social convention” (Bourdieu, 1977: 169). With reference to the simple example mentioned earlier, doxa could involve questions such as whether technology is needed at all, or how it is more broadly implicated in organising and performing work in the organisation.

A key implication of doxa is that it favours practice reproduction rather than change. Doxa enables a relationship of ‘ontological complicity’ between the objective structures of <sup>fi</sup>eld and incorporated structures of habitus. If some aspects of practical action and its immediate context become doxa, they become taken-for-granted, self-evident and part of agents’ ‘natural attitude’ towards their <sup>fi</sup>eld of practice, which exists outside of discourse. Thus, while one role of habitus is to equip agents with practical sense of how to act in a given social <sup>fi</sup>eld, a doxic relation between habitus and <sup>fi</sup>eld may limit the process o sensemaking by producing what Bourdieu calls a ‘sense of limits’. Agents may fail to see how certain aspects of a <sup>fi</sup>eld have become in<sup>fl</sup>uential in their ways of seeing and performing work.

A practice-based analysis of IS and work that incorporates the sensitising concept of doxa has important implications for three themes outlined in the introduction. Firstly, doxa highlights the possibility that sociotechnical re<sup>fl</sup>exivity may arise from opinion formed within the universe of discourse but that further issues or aspects of our work and the technology we use may become taken for granted and misrecognised. Rather than denying re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, doxa encourages a careful reading of which aspects or conditions of a <sup>fi</sup>eld are represented in discourse, and which have potentially become taken for granted. Secondly, doxa illustrates that while social representations of work, technology and organisation will involve opinion formed in the universe of discourse, they may not necessarily include or engage all aspects of how work is performed, how IT is used or the broader organisational environment. Thus, the sense of limits emerging from doxa may also limit the sensemaking role of social representations. Thirdly, in relation to technology and human agency, or the material and social, doxa sensitises us to the possibility that certain ways of using technology in practice may become taken for granted. This is an important observation because it suggests that the particular entanglement of the social and material in practice may not always be revealed. Therefore, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technological properties upon our attitudes, practices and organising dynamics of a <sup>fi</sup>eld may become misrecognised. Thus, if we are to understand precisely how IS are implicated in the logic of practice within any given <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is crucial to identify how technological properties are explicitly recognised and whether any have become misrecognised and undiscussed.

## METHODS

The methodological assumptions and concerns of interpretivism in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993, 1995a, 1995b) underpinned the research. The in-depth interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld study was informed by ethnographic principles as the observation of work practices formed the central data construction technique used, thus re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the point that “there is no hard and fast distinction between the two” (Klein & Myers, 1999: 69). The case site was Telecentre (a pseudonym), where the <sup>fi</sup>rst author was present from February to June 2008, spanning a period of 5 months. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author had previously worked at the company for a brief period in 1999 and had also conducted research at the site in 2003 for a master’s dissertation.

‘Sit-bys’ were conducted with 47 different ‘advisors’ (the name used in Telecentre for front-line service employees dealing directly with customers), sometimes on more than one occasion, within 13 different teams across all four functions of Telecentre (Table 2). Sit-bys involved sitting next to an advisor, listening to their conversation with the customer, observing their use of the IS and discussing their work and opinions of it. This combination of observation and interaction with research participants ensured that insights were derived into “what people are doing as well as what they say they are doing” (Myers, 1999: 2). Experiencing live call handling also provided a window into real problems and issues that emerged in responding to customer enquiries. In addition to sit-bys, a total of 31 interviews were conducted with team managers, coaches and the centre managers responsible for the four functions (Table 2). The interviews were semi-structured and relatively informal in nature, for example, only two of them took place behind closed doors. Ten further interviews were conducted with individuals not operating directly on the call centre <sup>fl</sup>oors, responsible for various issues such as process, IT, change, business improvement and customer experience. A week was also spent with the training team, which included participant observation of classroom-based training. completion of some computer-based training to gain familiarity with the IS and interviews with the training manager, training designer and three trainers.

Klein & Myers’s (1999) principles for conducting and evaluating interpretive research were adopted for the study. Klein & Myers’s (1999) principles have been widely used in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Davidson, 2002; Pawlowski & Robey, 2004; Backhouse et al., 2006; Levina & Vaast, 2006; Sarker & Sarker, 2009; Vaast & Walsham, 2009). We found the principles to be especially appropriate and useful for guiding this research because of the nature of the phenomenon under investigation and the characteristics of the <sup>fi</sup>eld site. The reproduction of practice involving the use of IT systems and accompanying narratives concerning action and its immediate context, including re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, represent complex and multifaceted phenomenon to examine. In this respect, the fundamental hermeneutic principle guided the research as it highlighted that understanding is achieved through an ongoing process of moving from the parts (for example, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c use of various systems, attitudes towards handling calls and customers, and opinions and representation of work and its immediate context) to an emergent whole (why work is performed in particular ways and how reflexivity, narratives and properties of IT are implicated in the reproduction of practice). The principle of contextualisation was also important because a central tenet of practice-based approaches to theorising is the requirement for a dual focus upon examining the micro-level of human action and the more macro-level socia and technical context within which work is situated. Similarly, the principle of suspicion was a necessary precondition for an investigation of potential inconsistencies between how work is performed and represented, and for an examination of the extent of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and its consequences. Finally, as the <sup>fi</sup>eld site was divided into what were, during the course of the research, identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as relatively self-contained collectives, it was imperative that the principle of multiple interpretations was upheld to seek agents’ opinions and accounts from multiple teams across all functions of the call centre operation. The analysis of the <sup>fi</sup>eld data was supported by Pentland’s (1999) structures of narrative, which supported the examination of the socially produced representations of work and its immediate context in the research site. Pentland (1999) argued that a narrative analysis of interpretive research is useful because

we need to be aware of the fact that stories may be their own best explanation. That is, agents may be actively attempting to reproduce particular templates of action that arise from their culture, their education, or their own experience. [Pentland, 1999, p. 721]

Stories therefore support the processes of ‘sensemaking’ (Weick, 1995) because of the meaning systems and values they encode. Pentland (1999) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four levels of narrative structure: text, stories, fabula and generating mechanisms (Table 4). Text is the raw speech of a person’s story, the latter being offered as just one of many possible versions of a particular fabula. Fabula refers to a generic description of how a particular course of events unfolded. Pentland (1999) noted that narrative analysis is usually restricted to these three levels but that it can enriched by the addition of a fourth level of ‘deep structure’, which aims at identifying generating mechanisms that underpin the various fabula. Thus, an analysis of narrative moves away from simple description towards an explanation of how and why narratives develop in certain ways. Table 4 provides a description of the four levels of narrative, and an example of each in relation to the topic of this paper, the handling of customer calls by call centre advisors. Work practices involving IS use constitute the fabula, while text and stories re<sup>fl</sup>ect their representation by advisors. In relation to Bourdieu’s theory of practice, each fabula will involve practices that draw from and re<sup>fl</sup>ect aspects of habitus (attitudes and opinions towards calls, customers and IS use) and <sup>fi</sup>eld (the broader contextual conditions within which call centre work is performed). The generating mechanisms represent the logic of practice, or how calls in general are handled by advisors.

With regard to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and composition of fabula within Telecentre, the qualitative text was primarily examined in relation to the concepts of <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus. This helped to identify attitudes and dispositions held by employees (habitus) and broader aspects of the working context of Telecentre (<sup>fi</sup>eld). Because a practice-based analysis was adopted, in identifying common themes during re-readings of the <sup>fi</sup>eld data from individual stories or comments, a central emphasis was placed upon fabula that concerned the central focus of cal centre advisors’ work – the handling of customer calls. Other issues and themes included the opinion of the training team that the introduction of the SingleView system had been too fast, comments on the use of a peer-review initiative, concerns of agency employees about securing a permanent position and discussion between Telecentre advisors regarding the changing nature of employment contracts in the company. However, although these were constitutive of alternative fabula, they were either less frequently discussed or only discussed in individua functions. This is in contrast to the three fabula that form the basis of the following analysis, which were central to IS-enabled call-handling practices.

## CASE SUMMARY: CALL CENTRE WORK IN TELECENTRE

Following the privatisation of British Telecom, the UK telecommunications sector was opened up to new entrants and subseguently became an increasingly competitive marketplace. Telecentre was a key player in the market and, like its competitors, supported its expanding service and product range through its call centre operations. Technological developments in the industry resulted in <sup>fi</sup>xed-line telephone service becoming supplemented with an evolving range of services such as dial-up internet, broadband, mobile broadband and digital TV packages. Telecentre’s call centre operation was large (multiple sites in the UK) and involved a highly specialised division of labour as the four functions of Customer Services, Provision, Repair, and Billing were each subdivided into specialist call-handling teams. In the research site, each function had five teams, and each team was composed of a team manager, a coach (responsible for supporting call resolution, dealing with ‘escalated’ calls and providing support for newly introduced procedures or changing product/service lines) and between 12 and 16 advisors.

The company was rolling out a new information system called SingleView to replace the existing two systems, called Customer Service System and FrontClever, thus removing the need for multiple systems and enabling the consolidation of customer accounts and technical processes. At the time of the research, approximately half of the teams were using SingleView, some having just <sup>fi</sup>nished training and gone live, while others had been using it for a number of months. Each team had a different access pro<sup>fi</sup>le for the IS, which restricted the information that advisors could view and retrieve, and the processes and transactions they could perform or complete. Each team worked on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c call stream (often referred to in the call centre literature as a ‘work<sup>fl</sup>ow’ (Frenkel et al., 1999; Bain et al., 2002), which determined their particular access pro<sup>fi</sup>le for the systems. Additional supporting software included a knowledge database containing information about internal procedures and information on products and services, and the contact matrix, a tool for searching and retrieving information about the contact numbers of every individual team within Telecentre.

Despite the formal division of labour being designed to achieve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by having specialised call-handling teams work on a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned range of customer enquiries, in practice, the dynamics of work organisation and service provision differed dramatically. Firstly, problems with the interactive voice response system included customer confusion with the menu options, intentionally choosing incorrect options in an attempt to reduce their wait time or using direct numbers of particular teams. These problems resulted in incorrectly routed calls arriving in the wrong team or service area, so advisors regularly faced a much wider variety of call types than prescribed by their of<sup>fi</sup>cial call streams. Because customers were often incorrectly transferred or misdirected between teams, they experienced “going around the houses”, or as one customer nicely put it, “being passed from pillar to post”. The following analysis investigates the narratives that advisors constructed about their own work and the work of other teams, the reasons offered for customers’ problematic journey through the service process and observed work practices, including the use of IS that they enacted to handle calls.

Thus, advisors used events that were revealed during live interactions with customers, either through an examination of the customer's account in the IS or by customers' own recollection of preceding events, to produce a narrative that blamed other teams and functions for the problems with customer service in the company, and to highlight how it impacted upon their own work. Following this exchange, Narinder “took ownership” of the call, a practice encouraged by Telecentre management, which referred to not ending the call until the customer enquiry was effectively resolved. The practice of taking ownership of a call often entailed two related aspects of practice, a prolonged interaction with the customer and extensive use of the IS. The latter helped to identify relevant events from the account history, clarify the current status of the enquiry and determine which procedures or actions were required. As shown in Figure 3, the practice of taking ownership re<sup>fl</sup>ected a habitus that felt responsibility and concern for the customer, viewed the call as legitimate work that required attention and was disposed to actively searching for information and utilising the main IS and supporting software. As also shown in Figure 3, these dispositions of habitus enacted through practice re<sup>fl</sup>ected and reproduced objective structuring principles of the Telecentre <sup>fi</sup>eld – a highly specialised division of labour, within which advisors are responsible for resolving a specialist range of enquiries, and customer orientation and service. Finally, advisors’ practices also reproduced the principle of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, because despite the possibility of a prolonged interaction with the customer and extensive use of the IS, they always attempted to effectively resolve the call in the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient manner possible.

The narrative of blaming other teams was most commonly constructed in relation to call transfers. The Customer Services function was particularly singled out for criticism, but the negative representation of others’ work practices extended across functions, and advisors in Customer Services were themselves critical of other areas of the company. For example, Nick (broadband integrated services team, Customer Services) believed that advisors (in other functions) lacked the requisite knowledge to correctly transfer calls:

A lot of calls are transferred through because people don’t know who to put the customer through to.

Greg, an advisor in the Repair centre, corroborated Nick’s view that other teams were to blame for the transfers he received and made the rather audacious claim that his function was perhaps more knowledgeable than others, even in relation to the latter’s own expertise:

A customer coming through with a genuine hard fault seems very rare. They (other teams) are all ‘passing the parcel’. Then we (Repair) are having to tell other departments their own processes or how to deal with things.

Although Greg’s comment was a particularly strong criticism of other departments, the narrative of blame and negative representation of advisors’ work in other functions, especially when referring to transferred calls, was common. Furthermore, employees from three of the four functions all believed that their speci<sup>fi</sup>c area of the company in particular received a disproportionate share of incorrectly transferred calls, leading them to label their own function as “a dumping ground” for calls.

The narrative of blame was recounted by advisors in multiple teams across all four functions as a particular version of the fabula of handling calls within their work<sup>fl</sup>ow, who each blamed one another for the problems, a situation that in practice, along with claims about being a dumping ground, was logically impossible. This inconsistency in the narrative can be partially explained by the division of labour, which resulted in multiple, relatively self-contained social collectivities, with opinions formed within them to some extent being sealed off from scrutiny from other areas of the call centre operations.

## Blaming SingleView

The second fabula also involved the handling of calls that fell within advisors’ call streams but concerns complaints about how SingleView undermined their ability to effectively serve customers (Table 5). Clive, a coach in Customer Services who had been working at Telecentre for 9 years, provided the following comparison of the three systems used in the company and commented upon one of the problems with SingleView:

If you want to get customer satisfaction you need to know how to use the systems. FrontClever is idiot friendly, you can’t do much wrong. But SingleView and CustomerServiceSystem are both similar because you don’t have to do transactions and processes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c order. But doing a ‘re-number’ in CustomerServiceSystem took <sup>fi</sup>ve minutes, now it can take up to three weeks on SingleView!

While problems with some processes were being resolved by ongoing system updates, there were wider concerns about the functionality of SingleView. Lucy, who had started as an apprentice at Telecentre in a line testing unit before moving to a combined provision team, was concerned about the number of clicks required to complete processes and the tendency for SingleView to freeze between screens:

It’s not a particularly nice system, it’s not very user friendly. There are certain processes where you have to do four clicks from one screen to another, it’s so slow. It just gets frustrating, at the end of the day you are just trying to help the customer.

During Lucy’s comments about the system, Rachel, another advisor sitting on an adjacent desk, interjected:

I think Telecentre are going to start getting RSI (repetitive strain injury) claims because SingleView is just click, click, click!

The comments of Lucy and Rachel re<sup>fl</sup>ected the concerns of many advisors. Geoff, a coach in the Repair function who was highly respected for his technical knowledge of the IS, clari<sup>fi</sup>ed that system speed had been improved but that updates (currently version 12) were ongoing and frequently affected a wide range of existing processes. Adam, an advisor in Repair, summed up his view of updates and the impact of convoluted processes upon the customer:

They’ve been doing patches on patches, it’s like a virtual quilt. To do non-standard procedures for customers it takes days and days of messing about.

With reference to the mutual constitution of habitus and <sup>fi</sup>eld through the reproduction of practice, the stories narrated about the problems with SingleView re<sup>fl</sup>ected a habitus that felt responsibility and concern for the customer, which incorporated the objective principle of customer orientation and service, and re<sup>fl</sup>ected the principle of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, because perceived limitations of the system were often based upon the additional or unnecessary time involved in using it, which delayed the resolution of customer enquiries (Figure 3).

## Absolving responsibility

The third fabula concerned a common event in Telecentre, which were situations where advisors faced calls that did not fall within their formal call stream, and their subsequent opinions, attitudes and actions associated with handling them (Table 5). Given the nature of the <sup>fi</sup>rst two narratives, narratives of the third fabula were rather surprising. Despite lamenting how the actions of advisors in other teams and SingleView were implicated in undermining the customer experience, advisors produced a narrative that absolved themselves of responsibility for some of the calls they faced. For example, during a call in Provision, a customer wanted additional information about their account clarifying, to which the advisor, Aruna, responded, “You’ll need to speak to our Customer Services about that”. The customer questioned this and attempted to continue explaining about the problem on his line, to which Aruna responded, “Well if you’ve got a fault then you have to speak to faults (Repair)”. After <sup>fi</sup>nishing the call, Aruna commented, “Its tunnel vision – they (customers) think ‘you’ are Telecentre, … ‘I’ve got my problem here and I want it done’”. Although Aruna was ultimately correct as she could not resolve the customer’s enquiry, it was advisors broader attitudes towards such calls that were surprising given their emphasis upon customer service in narratives 1 and 2. Advisors absolved themselves of responsibility because they did not see some calls as representing legitimate work. For example, Karl from one of the line testing units commented upon his work:

A lot of calls come through that are not our business, usually transfers, but also sometimes from customers who keep the number and then use it again in the future. (emphasis added)

Similarly, Ranjit, from the reluctant payers broadband team in the Billing function, made a clear distinction between his team’s formal call stream and the wider variety of other calls he received and, in reference to the latter, had the following comment:

That’s not my issue, I don’t deal with that…(pause)…but I will do some things, I will try to deal with it if I can. (emphasis added)

Ranjit appeared to realise that he was revealing a rather negative representation of his attitude towards certain calls and subsequently attempted to adjust his position to one that was more attuned to customer service. However, attitudes towards such calls were common and were succinctly summed up by an advisor in Customer Services, who pulled the contact matrix up on her screen and pointed to her department – “this is our (call) queue” and then pointed to another team – “we don’t touch that”.

With regard to the reciprocal triad of practice, <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus, attitudes associated with absolving responsibility obviously differed dramatically from those linked to the <sup>fi</sup>rst two fabula. Rather than taking ownership, practices often appeared to represent what Greg had referred to as “passing the parcel”. With reference to Figure 3, because advisors did not see some calls as legitimate work, they felt neither responsibility for them nor concern for customers, which was re<sup>fl</sup>ected in their attempts to actively limit interaction with the customer and limit their use of the IS. These dispositions towards such calls also reproduced the highly specialised division of labour in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, but obviously not customer orientation. It was not uncommon for advisors to interrupt the customer’s explanation of their experience or problems by using statements such as “We are help desk one – you have to call help desk two for that”, to justify their position that they were unable to deal with the call. While it was true that advisors were ultimately unable to resolve some calls themselves. they often chose to redirect them using either a referral, which involved providing the customer with the contact number of the relevant department, or a cold transfer, which involved transferring the call directly to the relevant department, but not waiting with the customer until the other advisor had answered to explain the call to the latter. Both of these actions were more likely to result in customers being redirected to the wrong team, the very events that advisors criticised other teams for. This was because decisions about where to redirect the call were preceded by a reluctance to interact with the customer or utilise systems. After con<sup>fi</sup>rming the customer account details for security purposes, it was extremely common for advisors to make no further use of the IS. Advisors might take a cursory look at the IS by looking into one or two screens of the account to respond to customer questions. In some cases, advisors pulled up the contract matrix, but this was usually to retrieve a forgotten number for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c team, rather than to support the process of <sup>fi</sup>nding the relevant team for a given enquiry. Similarly, it was rare for advisors to use the knowledge database.

## Summary

The preceding analysis indicated how advisors’ call-handling practices served to re<sup>fl</sup>ect and reproduce broader structures of call centre work (<sup>fi</sup>eld) through their incorporation within the attitudes and dispositions of advisors (habitus). This was enabled by observation of their actual work practices and also through an analysis of the structures of narrative that represented their work in Telecentre. In the latter instance, a striking contradiction becomes apparent: between advisors blaming of other teams and SingleView for problems with customer service provision (stories constructed about fabula 1 and 2); and their own attempts to absolve themselves of responsibility, and their reluctance to fully utilise or refer to systems or to interact with the customer (fabula 3). Therefore, despite advisors blaming other areas of the company or the new system, their own actions also directly caused misdirected calls and undermined the customer journey. Important questions therefore remain. How is it that advisors failed to see how their own handling of some calls was directly implicated in problems with effective service provision that they themselves were so eager to highlight in blaming other teams and functions? In terms of IS use, why did advisors fail to see the irony between their concerns and complaints about SingleView and their own reluctance to utilise available systems and software to support the redirecting of customers’ calls, or the constraints that access pro<sup>fi</sup>les and the division of labour placed created for them? With reference to a key question highlighted in the introduction to this paper, how can the inconsistency between how work is performed (practices linked to different fabula) and how work is represented (stories constructed as versions of each fabula) be accounted for? To address these questions, the second part of the subsequent analysis utilises the concept of doxa to investigate advisors’ practices and narratives in more detail. This enables a consideration of the level of deep structure at which generating mechanisms give rise to the various fabula in Telecentre, and to the divergent attitudes and practices associated with handling different calls.

## Narratives: discourse vs. the undiscussed in Telecentre

Stories about the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second fabula, namely opinions about problems with customer service in the company, can be seen as existing within the universe of discourse (Figure 4). Furthermore, there was some evidence to support the managerial orthodoxy. Firstly, Jez, a manager in Repair, recalled that similar concerns had followed the previous implementation of FrontClever, which over time had reduced after advisors became familiar with using the system. He believed a similar process was now being played out with SingleView. Another coach and centre manager also expressed a similar view. Secondly, two teams of advisors in credit referrals in the Billing function, who had been the <sup>fi</sup>rst group in Telecentre to use the new system, did not comment upon any problems with it.

But in terms of the mutual constitution of <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus through recurrent practice, problems with SingleView were less in<sup>fl</sup>uential than access pro<sup>fi</sup>les – a technological property of both existing and new systems – and the specialised division of labour. Both the highly circumscribed permission for accessing information and processes, and the organisation of work into highly specialised teams linked to individual call streams were fundamenta organising principles of work and service in Telecentre. However, they were absent in advisors representations of their work and were certainly not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being implicated in the frequency of transferred calls or blamed for the problematic customer journey. As illustrated in Figure 4, they had become doxa, existing within the universe of the undiscussed. Although the examination of doxa arose from advisors’ call-handling practices that were in turn situated within the unique organisational setting of Telecentre, it provides a broader theoretical insigh for understanding work practices and IS use (Table 6). Doxa reveals that even in acknowledging re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, agents can be re<sup>fl</sup>exive with respect to some issues while remaining largely unre<sup>fl</sup>exive with respect to others, which shows that certain technological and organisationa conditions of practical action will be recognised while others may not be re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon or even visible to agents.

## Access pro<sup>fi</sup>les and division of labour as a sense of limits

Doxa enables ontological complicity between <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus, through which existing ways of organising and performing work become naturalised, and ultimately from which a sense of limits may emerge. The sense of limits arising from doxa applies to practices and also to narratives and representations of work. Because access pro<sup>fi</sup>les and division of labour in Telecentre existed as doxa in the universe of the undiscussed, they did not emerge as issues from which different opinions were formed within the <sup>fi</sup>eld of practice. As a result, they were not present in the stories that advisors constructed about their work, and so the sensemaking function of narratives was therefore also restricted by the sense of limits. Doxa thus helps to explain precisely why incongruences endure between how work is performed and how it is represented, because aspects of a <sup>fi</sup>eld become re<sup>fl</sup>ected in attitudes, reproduced through practice and constitute the different fabula but are not explicitly represented in the stories constructed about those fabula. Advisors thus failed to see how their practices enacted in the third fabula undermined the customer experience, or therefore how their own actions directly contradicted a key value that underpinned the stories they produced about the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second fabula – concern for the customer. Similarly, the inconsistency between advisors highlighting that SingleView undermined the provision of customer service, but themselves refraining from using operational systems containing information resources, is never revealed. Because SingleView is in the universe of discourse, it is explicitly discussed, whereas access pro<sup>fi</sup>les, despite being more fundamental to the logic of call handling, are not present in their stories of blame regarding problems with the customer journey in the company, despite always being implicated in how fabula are in practice played out. As outlined in Table 6, the broader theoretical insight that can be gleaned from this is that because stories may not be complete or accurate accounts of practice, the sensemaking role of narratives may be undermined and may limit the possibility of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity by hiding inconsistencies between the performance and the representation of work.

With regard to practice, a sense of limits is revealed in relation to the third fabula. Not utilising the IS, attempting to limit interactions with customers, and ‘passing the parcel’, re<sup>fl</sup>ects highly restricted access associated with the IS and the highly circumscribed role within the broader call centre service process. It is important to qualify that a sense of limits is dynamically constituted through and by recurrent practices, so advisors essentially ‘self-limit’ themselves (their attitudes towards customers during some calls/their consideration of information) from what they are limited from (their position within the <sup>fi</sup>eld – in a specialised call-handling team/access to information).

What is particularly interesting, and also important. is that advisors self-limited themselves beyond the original material limits of access pro<sup>fi</sup>les and the division of labour. This is because, regardless of which specialised access pro<sup>fi</sup>le advisors in a given team held, advisors in all teams and functions had access to a range of basic screens and processes in the main IS. However, they tended not to refer to them after realising that the customer they faced was not within their formal call stream. Furthermore, the contact matrix and knowledge database were available as additional informational resources for advisors to use but were rarely utilised for calls outside the call stream. These two practices re<sup>fl</sup>ect a self-limiting habitus arising from doxa, one not interested in searching for, considering or using information to suppor call-handling practices even where, despite ‘rigid’ access pro<sup>fi</sup>les, information is available. These attitudes and associated practices are therefore an ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of the original functional constraints of access pro<sup>fi</sup>les, and so advisors self-limited themselves beyond the origina material limits of the technology and organisational environment within which they work. Thus, doxa may limit agents representations of action and also practice itself, such that materiality in the form of properties of technology or other features of organisational contexts may be actively enacted in use but remains largely hidden and moreover through recurrent practices becomes either extended or ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed (Table 6).

## An implicit classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of calls as a generating mechanism for the logic of practice

A <sup>fi</sup>nal theme concerns the deep structure that acts as a generating mechanism for the fabula in Telecentre. That is, what is the underlying logic of practice? How an advisor approaches and handles a given customer enquiry is based upon a system of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This is implied through advisors’ narratives and evident in their practices but remains implicit. In Telecentre, the system of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation distinguishes between what can be best described as ‘in-team calls and ‘other calls types’. The former represents any given team’s formal call stream, while the latter refers to all other calls that advisors face. This simple but clear distinction between calls is a system of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that mutually constitutes the objective conditions of call centre work and their incorporation within the subjective dispositions of habitus, the latter of which provides an interpretive template that discriminates between customers based upon immediately evaluating whether their call is ‘in-team’ or not.

Systems of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation exist in all <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice and are fundamental to understanding the logic that guides human action and constitutes broader social structures. However, because the classi<sup>fi</sup>catory scheme is represented by a doxic relation between <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus, advisors failed to see precisely how the interpretive template of habitus re<sup>fl</sup>ects and reproduces the technological and organisational conditions of their work. The interpretive template helps advisors to distinguish between in-team calls and other call types and subsequently evaluate whether they represent legitimate work or not. This has immediate implications for whether advisors feel responsibility for the call, feel concern for the customer and have an interest in using IS to search for information to support the resolution of the call, and ultimately whether advisors “take ownership” or alternatively “pass the parcel”. This represents the logic of how calls, in general, are handled and provides a generating mechanism for divergent attitudes towards customers, technology use and information.

Because the classificatory system is associated with doxa, it is a generating mechanism for the various fabula that are possible but remains misrecognised and undiscussed and therefore is not explicitly present in advisors’ narratives. Thus, the material conditions of organising work through multiple teams with limited access to a shared IS are deeply implicated in advisors attitudes and practices but remain absent in the stories they construct to make sense of their work, most strikingly in relation to their espoused value of customer service. The <sup>fi</sup>nal theoretical insight that can be abstracted from this part of the application of doxa to a practice-based analysis of work and IS use is that the logic of practice that guides human action and reproduces broader structures of work and technology use may be based upon implicit and unquestioned systems of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that are directly linked to material features of organisations (Table 6). In such instances, generating mechanisms associated with technological and organisational aspects may dictate what fabula are possible and drive the logic of human action, while remaining largely invisible.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

In the previous section, we showed the relevance of Bourdieu’s practice theory and especially the concept of doxa for analysing IS-enabled work practices in a call centre. However, as shown in the second column of Table 6, the analysis provides broader theoretical insights. This concluding section discusses their relevance to the literature on IS use and work practices, which was outlined in the introduction and formed the motivation for this paper. From the perspective of Bourdieu, practice exists in a recursive triad with habitus and <sup>fi</sup>eld. The value of introducing the concept of doxa as a sensitising device for analysing practice and IS use is that it describes a particular relation of ontological complicity between <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus, which favours the reproduction of practice. We identify contributions to three literatures in this <sup>fi</sup>nal section: on re<sup>fl</sup>exivity; on social representation; and on technology use and broader social structuring.

Firstly, in relation to the literature on re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, this paper has demonstrated the simultaneous presence and absence of agents’ re<sup>fl</sup>exivity towards technology and their work, and its consequences for practice reproduction. de Vaujany (2008) speci<sup>fi</sup>cally suggested Bourdieu’s concept of habitus as it could enable insights into the ‘biographical realm’ (Thompson, 2004) of agents’ deeply personal histories, to provide a more sophisticated understanding of sociotechnical re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. We adopted Bourdieu’s practice theory, which incorporated habitus but centred on the concept of doxa in our analysis. The contribution of our paper to studies that examine re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as an aspect of human agency associated with IS use and work practices has been to demonstrate how certain material or technical conditions of work and organisations may be discussed and actively challenged, while other aspects may be neither re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon nor even recognised. The strength of the doxa concept as a sensitising device for analysing practice and IS use is that it shows how a particular relation of ontological complicity between certain aspects of <sup>fi</sup>eld and habitus arises from an absence of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, which favours the reproduction of practice.

The contribution of this paper to the literature on social representations has been to reveal the limits of representations and narratives about work and the use of technology, Vaast 8 Walsham (2005) argued that agents’ experience of consonance or dissonance between thei work practices, use of ITand accompanying representations of their own and others’ work could explain triggers for modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in practice or support and favour the continuation of existing practices. This is consistent with Pentland’s (1999) position that analysis of narratives is insightful for examining human action and practice because stories act as sensemaking devices for agents’ understanding and performance of their work. Our analysis has revealed that doxa creates a sense of limits to practice and also a sense of limits to narratives. Doxa essentially limits or neutralises the sensemaking function usually provided by narratives as stories are potentially incomplete, inconsistent or inaccurate representations or accounts of practice. This is what enables the inconsistency between representations and action, or between stories and fabula, to endure: agents do not perceive dissonance because they fail to see how some aspects of the material and social context of their work actually in<sup>fl</sup>uence their own attitudes and recurrent practices, as these are not necessarily present in the narratives they produce. Therefore, with no trigger for practice adjustment or modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, existing ways of working and using IS endure even in the context of contradictions, and despite re<sup>fl</sup>exive discussion about work and its surrounding context. It is important to note that one advisor, Greg, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the division of labour as implicated in problems with delivering effective service –

When you have got so many thumbs in the pie, you can’t take ownership…things are not being done right, and because of separating work into little pieces, so people don’t know the whole picture.

However, it is noteworthy that the advisor in question did not identify access pro<sup>fi</sup>les as implicated in problems. This shows that doxa is permeable, with this advisor’s view being an exception to the more commonly shared views and opinions highlighted in the analysis.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal contribution of this paper is to literature on technology use and broader social structuring that attempts to incorporate a dual emphasis upon the social and material as they are con<sup>fi</sup>gured through practice. The application of doxa in our case draws attention to the fact that social and material entanglements do not necessarily become explicitly produced by agential cuts in practice. Nyberg (2009) noted that while technology in call centres has been classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as ‘coercive vs caring’, or ‘controlling’ vs. ‘supportive’ (Sewell & Barker, 2006), cuts in practice provide opportunities for sensemaking (about technology and organisation) that is contingent and open to many possibilities. We agree. The materiality of the new SingleView system emerged during practice as frozen screens and slowness. New and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed procedures built into the system were the basis for advisors developing narratives that criticised how they perceived SingleView to restrict, undermine or interrupt their call-handling practices and the central objective of providing effective customer service. However, in contrast, because access pro<sup>fi</sup>les existed as doxa, they were not explicitly materialised through any speci<sup>fi</sup>c cuts in practice, and therefore, neither was their mutual in<sup>fl</sup>uence with advisors’ attitudes towards information or use of IS revealed.

Social interactions may in<sup>fl</sup>uence agents’ perceptions of a newly introduced technology but can be subsequently reversed by ‘material interactions’, which refer to agents’ direct engagement with properties of the technology (Leonardi, 2009). This paper has illustrated the limits of both social representations and material interactions or ‘cuts in practice’, as technological or organisational properties may to an extent become ‘lost’ in the entanglement of the social and material within practice. Systems of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation can, for example, categorise work activities as legitimate and attitudes and actions as appropriate and desirable, but their role in constituting the overriding logic of practice may remain implicit and unquestioned. The doxa-informed practice analysis introduced in this paper offers a way of understanding this lack of questioning and its implications.
