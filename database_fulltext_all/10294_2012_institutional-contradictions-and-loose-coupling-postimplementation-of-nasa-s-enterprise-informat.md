# Institutional Contradictions and Loose Coupling: Postimplementation of NASA’s Enterprise Information System

Nicholas Berente

Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602, berente@uga.edu

Youngjin Yoo

Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19122, youngjin.yoo@temple.edu

hrough a grounded analysis of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA’s) enterprise information system (IS) implementation in the months immediately following the go-live, we show how NASA can be characterized as an institutionally plural organization, rife with diverse institutional logics, some consistent and some contradictory to each other. The enterprise system is introduced in accordance with the logic of managerial rationalism, but some of the institutional logics that organizational actors draw upon and reproduce contradict the logic of managerial rationalism in certain situations. In these situations, organizational actors loosely couple elements of their practices from the practices implied by the enterprise system, thus satisfying the demands associated with both institutional fields. We identify four generalizable forms of loose coupling that result from these institutional contradictions: temporal, material, procedural, and interpretive, and discuss their effects on both the system implementation and local practices. Further, we show how, through the use of institutional logics, researchers can identify fundamental institutional contradictions that explain regularities in the situated responses to enterprise system implementations—regularities that are consistently identified in the literature across a variety of organizational contexts.

Key words: institutional pluralism; institutional contradiction; institutional logic; institutional theory; loose coupling; loosely coupled; enterprise systems; ERP; NASA

## 1. Introduction

If we are to take decades of social studies of enterprise system-related practices seriously, we might conclude that enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems cannot possibly be successfully employed across diverse organizational contexts (Pollock et al. 2007). Such systems are premised on notions of standardization, integration, and inscribing stable, generic best practices across entire organizations; but no two organizations are identical, their environments are constantly changing, and they are comprised of dissimilar groups with an array of potentially conflicting goals and interests. These diverse communities are notorious for idiosyncratically appropriating enterprise systems—partially, improvisationally, inconsistently, or unfaithfully (e.g., Ciborra 2000, Scott and Wagner 2003, Wagner and Newell 2004, Pollock and Cornford 2004, Boudreau and Robey 2005)— inevitably undermining the very premise of standardization and integration on which the implementation of enterprise systems are based. Thus we see a number of failures in the literature (e.g., Lee and Myers 2004, Barker and Frolick 2003, Al-Mashari and Al-Mudimigh 2003). However, organizational victories with ERP are also evident in much of the literature. ERP systems have been widely adopted across a variety of organizations, and ERP-related expenditures represent perhaps the single most significant portion of modern corporate information technology (IT) expenses (e.g., Davenport 1998, Weill et al. 2002, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Volkoff et al. 2005, Gattiker and Goodhue 2005, Srivardhana and Pawlowski 2007). How is it, then, that ERP systems can often be successful in practice, while their promise is so obviously unachievable?

Institutional theory provides a lens for dealing with this sort of apparent paradox (Robey and Boudreau 1999) by bridging local circumstances and related situated practices with broader organizational, cultural, and societal contexts (King et al. 1994, Orlikowski and Barley 2001). While much of the prevailing institutional analysis focused on the level of broad cross-organizational fields, Friedland and Alford (1991) introduce the notion of “institutional logics” to link individual practices with broad social institutions (Friedland and Alford 1991, p. 242). Institutional logics are the symbolically grounded organizing principles or “rationalities” that underpin individual practices in a manner consistent with a given institution (Friedland and Alford 1991). Contemporary institutional analyses are increasingly characterizing organizations as institutionally plural (Kraatz and Block 2008, Dunn and Jones 2010). They are subject to multiple, often contradictory institutional logics simultaneously. Organizational actors reconcile these competing logics by loosely coupling the practices that are guided by conflicting logics (Meyer and Rowan 1977, Brunsson 1989, Boxenbaum and Jonsson 2008). Loose coupling refers to patterns of action that are distinct, or separate from each other, yet are still responsive to each other in some fashion (Orton and Weick 1990). Although there are many examples of local practices that are loosely coupled with information systems (IS) in the literature, these activities are not explicitly described in terms of loose coupling. As such, there is little attention to generalizable forms of loose coupling, with no direct ties to broad social institutions. Enterprise systems are IS used to integrate an entire organization (Davenport 1998, Markus and Tanis 2000) and are often associated with a specific institutional logic (Gosain 2004). The logic that underpins enterprise systems may contradict other logics in a pluralistic organization, and we argue that it is precisely through loose coupling that idiosyncratic situated practices can coexist with a tightly integrated and standardized enterprise system.

IS research has long identified the tension between social structures made durable through the materiality of software and hardware artifacts, and local practices in unique particular contexts with diverging group interests (see Leonardi and Barley 2008 for a review). While many indicate that enterprise systems act as structures that materially constrain human agency (Gosain 2004, Volkoff et al. 2007), others highlight how, in practice, individuals exercise their agency as they accommodate this imposed structure in idiosyncratic improvisational ways (Boudreau and Robey 2005). These idiosyncratic accommodations have been broadly researched in the IS discourse, often under the rubric of “resistance” (e.g., Keen 1981, LaPointe and Rivard 2005), but more descriptively as reinvention, improvisation, and bricolage (e.g., Rice and Rogers 1980, Orlikowski 1996, Ciborra 2002), using terms such as “chauffeurs,” “shadow systems,” or “workarounds” (e.g., Culnan 1983, Boudreau and Robey 2005, Azad and King 2008). Although these accommodations can seem quite unpredictable, we argue that regularities do exist across contexts. Understanding such regularities of seemingly unpredictable and idiosyncratic responses to IT (without succumbing to determinism) remains a key theoretical challenge for IS research (Robey and Boudreau 1999, Leonardi and Barley 2008).

We found that institutional theory offers a lens for addressing this key challenge. In this study, we conducted a grounded analysis of the ERP implementation at the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the months immediately after the system went live across 2 of its 10 field centers. The institutional lens was not employed ex ante in the motivation or design of the study, during the data collection, or in the early rounds of coding. Instead, institutional theory “earned its way” (Strauss and Corbin 1988, p. 292) into explicit attention during data analysis through empirical relevance. Institutional theory is specifically concerned with regularities across social contexts, and offers a potentially fruitful lens for theorizing about the appropriation of information technologies, while avoiding technologically deterministic arguments (King et al. 1994, Orlikowski and Barley 2001, Robey and Boudreau 1999). The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Next, as scaffolding for the reader (not to be confused with a chronological depiction of our analysis), we briefly introduce our institutional approach and the idea of loose coupling. This is followed by a case description and our analysis and a discussion of our findings. We conclude with implications for research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. Organizational Institutionalism and Institutional Logics

Organizational institutionalism is a broad sweeping set of perspectives that traverses multiple traditions (Scott 2008). The commonality among the various institutional traditions is the understanding that human actions are not ahistorical and strictly “rational,” according to some universal standard. Rather, human activity is historically dependent, culturally conditioned, politically charged, and contextually embedded. Contemporary organizational institutionalism is broadly rooted in the perspectives of Giddens, Bourdieu, and Berger and Luckman, among others, and generally seeks to extend this sociological cannon to the organizational sphere (see Powell and DiMaggio 1991 and Greenwood et al. 2008). The fundamental construct of institutional theory is that of an “institution,” which can be defined simply as “an organized, established, procedure” (Jepperson 1991, p. 143). The term organized implies structure, and established implies historical roots, persistence, and objectification of the procedure over time. This procedure, when reproduced consistently, describes a specific social order that guides the actions of individual actors through their rule-like qualities, and from which those actors establish and maintain their identities (Jepperson 1991). Actors enact the institutionalized scripts associated with a procedure and, in turn, reinforce that institution. Thus, contemporary (new) institutional literature takes an explicitly structurational view of human activity. In their seminal work, Powell and DiMaggio (1991, p. 23) argue that the structurational view of theorists such as Giddens (1984) “provides a cognitive theory of commitment to scripted behaviors” that offers an alternative to the assumptions of Parsonian (old) institutionalism.

The bulk of institutional literature focuses on macrolevel phenomena concerning industries, sectors, and fields—with organizations as the smallest level of institutional actor (e.g., DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Powell and DiMaggio (1991), however, cautioned institutional researchers that any macrolevel phenomena can find their roots in microlevel human action. Drawing upon Bourdieu’s (1977) notion of habitus, Powell and DiMaggio (1991) describe internalized rules that are chronically reproduced by individuals through everyday action, yet generative in nature, as the foundation for conceptualizing institutions. Thus, institutional theory offers a lens for analyzing goals, values, and prescriptions that underlie and legitimize behaviors of groups and individuals (Powell and Colyvas 2008). A set of goals, values, and prescriptions associated with a specific institution form a rationale, or institutional logic.

An institutional logic can be described as “a set of material practices and symbolic constructions— which constitutes its organizing principles and which is available to organizations and individuals to elaborate” (Friedland and Alford 1991, pp. 248–249). Contrary to the notion that actors are somehow passive, nonrational recipients of institutional scripts, the notion of an institutional logic emphasizes that actors do act rationally, but that this rationality is embedded in a context of goals and taken-for-granted assumptions that are a situated within a particular institutional context—a concept described as embedded agency (Thornton and Ocasio 2008). The theoretical device of institutional logics allows researchers to span multiple levels of analysis (fields, organizations, individuals, see Friedland and Alford 1991), and thus relate individual activity to any of society’s broader institutions. Also, in highlighting individual rationality with the term logic, this notion of institutional logic clearly emphasizes the (embedded) agency of individual actors. Knowledgeable actors do not simply apply institutional rules, but they navigate and engage institutional orders in their everyday practices in ways that are consistent with particular logics. Individual dispositions are critical to this navigation, and it is this dispositional element, which is rooted in a given field of practice, that is so important to Bourdieu’s (1977) habitus and the form of human agency that is fundamental to contemporary institutionalism (see Powell and DiMaggio 1991).

We focus on four key dimensions of institutional logics, primarily based on Thornton and Ocasio (2008). First, institutional logics act as organizing principles that guide activities, and thus embody the goals and values of the institution (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, Friedland and Alford 1991, Townley 1997). Second, institutional logics are founded on assumptions associated with specific causal means-end relationships (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, Friedland and Alford 1991). Third, social actors draw upon institutional logics to form individual identities (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, Friedland and Alford 1991, Jepperson 1991, Townley 1997). Fourth, institutions are more and less salient to specific domains and their practices (Powell and DiMaggio 1991, Jepperson 1991).

## 2.2. Institutional Pluralism and Loose Coupling 2.2. Institutional Pluralism and Loose Coupling

Because contemporary organizations typically span multiple institutional fields, they are subject to multiple, often contradictory, institutional logics simultaneously—a situation known as “institutional pluralism” (Kraatz and Block 2008, Dunn and Jones 2010). Some logics are consistent with each other and can be readily reconciled, but many cannot. Institutionally pluralistic organizations navigate and maintain contradictory logics by insulating practices guided by these contradictory logics from each other—essentially “loosely coupling” them (Boxenbaum and Jonsson 2008). Loose coupling refers to patterns of action that are distinct, or separate from each other, yet are still responsive to each other in some fashion (Orton and Weick 1990). The concept of loose coupling is intended to offer an alternative to both tightly coupled and decoupled system concepts. Tightly coupled systems are highly integrated and responsive to each other, and decoupled systems are completely separate and unresponsive (Orton and Weick 1990). While the notion of loose coupling has been appropriated by organizational literature in a variety of different ways (e.g., Weick 1976, Hannan and Freeman 1977, Daft 1978, Pfeffer and Salancik 1978, Perrow 1984, Scott 1987), institutional theory has a particular way of conceptualizing loose coupling— by distinguishing between the performance of a given practice (which is consistent with one institutional logic) and the presentation of that practice (in a way that is consistent with a contradictory institutional logic, see Boxenbaum and Jonsson 2008).

In their seminal institutional analysis of loose coupling, Meyer and Rowan (1977) showed how educational organizations satisfy multiple incompatible expectations from a variety of institutionalized sources by decoupling the performance of certain activities from the monitoring of these activities. They achieved loose coupling through a variety of mechanisms, including informal coordination, the avoidance of detailed inspection, and the performance of activities “beyond the purview of managers” (Meyer and Rowan 1977, pp. 57–58). Such organizations ceremonially conform to certain expectations to give the appearance of compliance and maintain legitimacy. This notion of ceremonial conformity has been used to describe a loosely coupled form of organizational resistance to institutional forces for change (Oliver 1991, Goodstein 1994). Loose coupling, then, provides a “working space” for individuals navigating multiple conflicting institutional forces (Seo Creed et al. 2002). Through loose coupling, the messy reality of organizational activity needs not interfere with the “assumption that formal structures are really working 0 0 0 [loose coupling] enables organizations to maintain standardized, legitimating, formal structures while their activities vary in response to practical considerations” (Meyer and Rowan 1977, p. 58).

The implementation of enterprise systems in contemporary organizations can be understood through the backdrop of institutional pluralism and the loose coupling of practices associated with contradictory institutional logics. In our analysis of NASA’s ERP implementation, we identified a number of institutional contradictions and loosely coupled practices.

## 3. Research Method

This research is based on a nine-month, in-depth qualitative field study of NASA’s agencywide implementation of an ERP system immediately after the agency went live with the system in 2003. We followed the basic principles of grounded theory building (Strauss and Corbin 1998), iterating between data and literature throughout the data collection and analysis. We take an interpretive approach, guided by a commitment to a process that involves constant cross-checking among different data sources, and assessing and interpreting theoretical constructs against empirical data.

We conducted a total of 68 interviews primarily at NASA’s Glenn Research Center (Glenn) in Cleveland, Ohio, and also at agency headquarters (HQ) in Washington, D.C. and at the Marshall Space Flight Center (Marshall) in Huntsville, Alabama. The bulk of the data collection occurred at Glenn, where the second author had a nine-month research fellowship. The interviews within Glenn were primarily conducted following purposeful sampling (Morse 2007, Strauss and Corbin 1998). Sampling focused on Glenn employees in research and project organizations, functional managers, and users drawn randomly from the list of active users. We conducted open-ended interviews designed to reveal the informants’ everyday work practice, and the use of the ERP system. Interviews were conducted in a manner consistent with the “dramaturgical model” for semistructured interviews (Myers and Newman 2007). First, the interviewer situated himself within the context by introducing himself and the research project. Second, to minimize social dissonance in the interview, the interviewer explained how each interview will be anonymized and secured, and then he presented his university’s informed consent documentation, which aided in building trust. Further, to build rapport, each informant told about their background and relationship to the ERP implementation in a way that both mirrored the verbal pos ture and vocabulary of the informant and in a way that flexibly allowed the informant to go in a direction that he or she found interesting, using terms that are comfortable for that informant. Further, we paid special attention to capturing as many relevant “voices” as possible (see Myers and Newman 2007). The interviews can be grouped as follows (from Glenn unless otherwise indicated): 22 finance and administration personnel and managers (1 Marshall; 2 HQ); 15 ERP implementation team leaders and members (1 Marshall; 5 HQ); 23 center managers, research managers, and researchers (1 Marshall); and 8 project managers. We ended data collection when we felt we had achieved saturation, and additional interviews no longer reveal new insight. We complemented purposeful sampling with interviews with senior executives and ERP project team members. Through these additional interviews, we explored the organizational, technological, historical, and political factors that influenced the design and implementation of the ERP system.

The project’s research database (Yin 2003) included every transcribed interview and more than 200 project-related documents, including internal NASA reports about the implementation, news coverage related to the project, and audit reports concerning the implementation from the U.S. General Accounting Office (GAO), the NASA Advisory Council, and the Inspector General. To triangulate our interpretation of the events (Yin 2003), we produced a full case write-up for NASA management and all participants, and we presented the case write-up at all three sites. Although at the time they agreed that our depiction of events was valid, it is important to note that a number of the situations have changed significantly since the time of the research.

The first round of coding took place during data collection and involved note taking, memo writing, and open coding (Strauss and Corbin 1998), through which we became immersed in the data and began to explore for recurring themes. The second round of coding occurred after data collection was completed and involved axial coding (Strauss and Corbin 1998), where we constructed categories and subcategories, grouped codes together and looked for relationships and patterns. As is common with the practice of grounded theory (Urquhart 2007), this coding did not take the form of neat linear rounds of coding. Rather, we were continuously moving between open and axial coding and constantly moving back to the interviews and to the literature. After identifying a “central” code (Strauss and Corbin 1998, i.e., “institutional incongruence was related to loosely coupled activity”), we selectively re-coded the data focusing on institutional logics and loose coupling.

## 4. Case Study

## 4.1. NASA’s Structure and Culture

NASA was established in 1958 with the passage of National Aeronautics and Space Act. At the time of this research, NASA consisted of an HQ and 10 field centers around the United States. Each center was established for specific technical or political reasons and has become a well-established fixture in its local community. NASA has traditionally encouraged competition among centers to produce the best-possible outcomes. Such intercenter competition was particularly intensified during the 1990s under Dan Goldin’s leadership who advocated the “lead center” concept, resulting in rivalry among centers, lack of communication, and competing and redundant investments— including information technologies (Starbuck and Farjourn 2005). This has resulted in stove-piped IS, organizational processes, accounting standards, and cultures at each center. Each center built systems to meet its peculiar needs, without regard for compatibility with other locations.

Beyond the 10 field centers, NASA had seven Strategic Enterprises (at the time of data collection)— Aeronautics, Biological and Physical Research, Earth Science, Education, Exploration Systems, Space Flight, and Space Science—that cut across individual centers and directly support NASA’s space and science missions. Finally, NASA also had functional offices that cover a variety of management and advisory roles, including financial, human resource, procurement, engineering, safety, and IT. Each center has a matrix structure with functional units and performing organizations under center management (vertical), and project offices aligned with the agencywide strategic enterprise programs (horizontal). Performing organizations provide engineering and scientific capabilities within centers, and program and project managers at local centers receive their funding from the strategic enterprises to hire engineers and scientists from performing organizations. Examples of performing organizations at Glenn included the Space Flight, Safety and Mission Assurance, and Research and Technology directorates.

In addition to this complex organizational structure, NASA faces a unique challenge in dealing with its budget. NASA’s budget is a part of the U.S. government’s annual budget. As a result, the projects and missions are funded on an annual basis, constantly under congressional oversight and its budgetary political influence. Because the agency director is a political appointee who serves at the pleasure of the president, a new administration typically selects a new director who often re-prioritizes the agency’s goals and activities. Such re-prioritization forces program and project managers change their current and projected budgets. At the same time, most scientific and engineering research projects require long-term planning and stable funding. As a result, program and project managers work on several years of budget at the same time, constantly revising its future plan and current activity levels.

## 4.2. Context of NASA’s ERP Implementation

NASA first started working on an agencywide integrated financial system in 1987 when the GAO found that NASA’s accounting and financial IS “constitute a material weakness of the agency.” Since then, the executive branch of the U.S. government has been focusing on greater accountability and efficiency from its agencies with initiatives such as Clinton-Gore’s Reinventing Government and Bush’s Presidential Management Agenda. In addition, Congress passed a series of regulatory and statutory requirements that agencies need to meet in financial management. These include the Chief Financial Officer’s Act of 1990, the Federal Financial Management Improvement Act of 1996, and OMB Circular A-127 that requires a single integrated system for agency financial management.

Recognizing these growing regulatory needs, NASA unsuccessfully attempted to implement integrated financial management systems on two different occasions: 1989 and 1995, costing the agency more than \$300 million over 10 years. These two failed attempts were internally recognized as “business” projects, as opposed to space or science mission projects, and never received agencywide support beyond the financial management community. After the second failure, a series of events created momentum for renewed efforts to implement agencywide ERP. Perhaps the highest profile problem involved more than \$5 billion of budget overruns on the International Space Station project, where NASA could not account for the project’s total costs because of its complex internal accounting structures. With intense criticism from Congress and the GAO for a lack of financial accountability, there was a growing internal recognition within NASA that something must be done to fix its financial management system to regain public trust. Further, the Bush administration’s Presidential Management Agenda emphasized five strategic areas: human capital, competitive sourcing, financial management, e-Government, and integrated budget and performance. Federal agencies needed to get green ratings on these five areas. NASA received red in all five areas, and NASA administrators saw the ERP system as the key to earning green in financial management and integrated budget and performance.

Furthermore, there was a growing consensus among managers at NASA that the intercenter competition that was actively promoted during the 1990s was unproductive. This recognition led to the One NASA initiative, which grew out of a management development program. One NASA involved implementing structures, policies, and procedures that encourage NASA employees to operate as members of a single agency, rather than 10 different centers. As a result of the Columbia Accident Investigation Board report that emphasized the necessity of intercenter communication and collaborations, the One NASA initiative gained significant momentum.

## 4.3. Implementing Core Financial at NASA

In early 2000, NASA initiated its third attempt to implement Integrated Financial Management. A new program director decided to manage the system implementation project (IFMP) in the same manner that engineering projects are managed, following NASA’s project management guideline (NPG 7120.5). The “IFMP Principles” as articulated by the program director recognized building a coalition with the core NASA constituents as a critical success factor. As a result, the project team promoted IFMP as management tools designed to support NASA’s main mission by providing “timely, consistent, and reliable information for management decisions 0 0 0 0 IFMP will also provide analysis and reporting tools to get the right information to the right people at the right level, so that they can make timely informed decisions” (NASA Press Release 2000). Integrated financial management program (IFMP) was seen as a tool that would “improve NASA’s accountability and enable full cost management 0 0 0 [IFMP] will allow the agency to manage programs using full cost management techniques and enhance the ability to manage institutional capabilities” (NASA Press Release 2000). In addition, IFMP managers consciously linked IFMP to the One NASA initiative that was gaining agencywide currency—they promoted IFMP as a tool to enable One NASA. The program director wrote in his newsletter:

[IFMP] should expand its focus to provide program and project management tools that facilitate collaboration across the NASA Centers 0 0 0 0 Without IFMP, agency processes would be severely constrained in the ability to transform our new [One NASA] vision into results. Information stovepipes exist at the Centers and Enterprises, inconsistent functional processes hampered our business capabilities and inflexible and obsolete technology created significant gaps in our ability to integrate budget and performance.

IFMP received some important momentum in November 2000, when President Bush appointed Sean O’Keefe—a self-described “bean counter”—as the agency’s administrator. O’Keefe was the first nontechnical administrator since the agency’s first, James Webb. Upon arrival, Sean O’Keefe made it clear that successfully implementing the ERP system is one of his highest priorities and he adopted One NASA as a signature initiative. One project manager from Marshall recalled:

When Sean O’Keefe arrived and understood what we had put in place and where we were going, and he raised the flag and said, “This is the most important thing that we have going on the administrative side of this agency,” we couldn’t stop 0 0 0 0 There’s always this swell of resistance once people start to realize, “They may really do it this time.” You can tell that was coming and it was like, “We’ve got to start raising flags and we’ve got to throw up roadblocks.” Well, he showed up and said, “Don’t talk to me about that. I know there’s going to be problem. There always are in systems like this. I come from a place where I’ve seen this happen before. Yes, it’s going to be hard; and yes it’s going to be ugly, but you know what? It’s what I want done.” And that just made all the difference in the world. (Implementation Manager C, 4-1)

O’Keefe provided more than moral support to the project. In February 2002, he created a new senior executive position to oversee the IFMP project and to report directly to him. He elevated IFMP out from under the CFO’s office to “the ninth floor” where the senior executives were located, and the associate deputy administrator chaired the IFMP council that included senior executive representatives of all major groups. In October 2001, the technical implementation of Core Financial at Glenn and Marshall began. Marshall was originally going to be the pilot site, followed quickly by HQ and Glenn. However, HQ opted to implement it later with other field centers because of technical challenges, and Marshall ended up implementing together with Glenn. The Core Financial implementation began with the agency design phase that included training and user procedure templates, security and internal controls, a data conversion strategy, and a detailed technical architecture. The outcome of the agency design phase served as a baseline template for individual centers. At Glenn, the implementation team identified eight major gaps that were unique to Glenn. The implementation team at Glenn decided to address these gaps through training and process change, rather than by changing the systems. This decision was, in part, because of the time pressure to meet the goal of October 2002 go-live and, in part, because of the cost consideration associated with code modifications to the ERP software. The Glenn team conducted the data conversation by September 2002 and Glenn and Marshall both went live on October 2002, with the rest of the agency going live by June 2003. Core Financial replaced 32 legacy systems within Glenn alone and more than 145 systems throughout the agency. In September 2003, NASA implemented the next version of SAP Core Financial and this study takes place in the immediate wake of that implementation.

## 4.4. Consequences of NASA’s ERP Implementation

NASA experienced significant organizational and technical challenges immediately after the “successful” implementation of Core Financial. Although in many areas, the implementation went generally as expected, we focused our investigation on four areas where there appeared to be a discrepancy between the expectations of the implementation team and the resulting activity.<sup>1</sup> These four areas were: budget reporting, funds control, purchasing, and time reporting.

4.4.1. Budget Reporting. Many managers who were supportive of the project during the design phase quickly became disappointed by the new system’s inability to generate reports that they needed. Program and project managers reported that they were not able to see the variance between actual and planned budget and could not easily see how much funding was still left for the project. The ERP system was based on the assumption of fixed budgets, fixed calendar-based timeframes, and tracking expenses when recognized rather than accrued. Progress of projects, however, are difficult to predict and rife with variability. A project manager was adamant about the inappropriateness of ERP for her work:

That means absolutely nothing to a project manager like myself who is tracking plans versus actuals. What are my variances? Who blew my budget? Who’s overrunning? Who’s underrunning? To me a ledger was not going to help me very much with that. (Project Manager A, transcript 2-9)

To address this concern, the IFMP team implemented a reporting tool known as Business Warehouse in March 2003, which offered better reporting but still did not resolve the problem associated with tracking variance. Many project managers indicated that they managed projects outside of the system— on shadow systems—only to periodically reconcile the data in these outside systems with ERP.

We’re still 0 0 0 using offline systems, planning spreadsheets 0 0 0 we use the Business Warehouse [reporting tool] as a data source 0 0 0 extract the data off and still put it in our front end that we give the project managers in their monthly status report. We build a phasing plan at the project level offline from the center-level system. Okay. We extract the actuals off the center-level system and marry it up with our plan offline. And that’s the report the project managers use 0 0 0 to status variance. (Project Manager E, 2-26)

Research managers in the performing organizations also had issues with IFM budget management because they could not see the current budget numbers at any point in time. One manager commented:

It’s not the actual funding amount 0 0 0 0 We need to know what we are budgeted and not what we spent 0 0 0 0 So you can’t find out and so you send in a purchase and you say, “I want to spend this fund out,” and if you’re \$2.00 too high, it bounces and then you’ve got to start over again. So I have to keep my [spreadsheet]. (Research Manager C, 2-11)

Because the various research programs and associated expenditures occur at an unpredictable pace, yet programs are managed in well-defined milestones, there were center managers who kept a separate set of books to manage their budgets, only to reconcile them periodically with the ERP system. For example, one executive allowed a staff member to shuffle funds between budgets:

If you’re the head of an office and you’re running low on money, you can come to the Budget Meeting and say “Look, I need this money” and I can say “Well, I know so and so has it and he’s looking for travel money 0 0 0 0 Each month I reconcile it back. So you can cut deals among each other or I can reallocate money, and my boss 0 0 0 allows me to go ahead and work the budget so that everybody’s satisfied. “You know, [informant’s name] I’ve got a problem I need \$4,000 to do XYZ.”0 0 0 “Let me see where I can dig it out” 0 0 0 0 “I noticed that the contract’s running behind plan, do you mind if I give so and so \$4,000?” “Sure, go ahead.” (Center Administrative Support A, 2-9)

Thus a number of the center’s managers reconciled budget activity outside of the system with the numbers reported in the ERP system.

4.4.2. Funds Control. Another issue was that with the ERP system, project managers were not able to control the funds allocated to their projects. This problem arose from the combination of two design choices that were made. First, each project had its unique funding code called a “work breakdown structure” (WBS) number (based on the project’s work breakdown structure). Because each project had a number of subprojects and tasks, the WBS had a hierarchical structure. HQ funded projects at three-digit level, which were subdivided into five-digit subprojects and seven or nine-digit tasks. During the design phase, the IFMP team decided that Core Financial would recognize the projects only at three-digit level because that was how HQ sent its funds. As such, there were no walls between project levels, which were the levels where the majority of the projects were managed. Second, the new system completely changed the fund approval process. In the old system, each purchasing request had to be approved by the project manager before the fund was committed. Under the new process, a project manager was only notified after the money was committed. The logic was that individual researchers could correctly initiate a requisition process with all of the appropriate information (project, fund, general ledger account), then it must be a legitimate request, thus approved immediately as long as there were funds available. From an accounting standpoint, this was seen as an improvement because the system shows the real-time balance without any noise. This was, however, a major problem from the perspective of many project managers because they lost control over their funds:

My personal perspective is we may be giving ourselves a false sense of management. In other words, we’re watching the data and thinking we’re in control. But we’re seeing the data change and being reactive instead of proactive. We’ve probably decreased the amount of active project management because we’re watching data instead of managing the project.

(Project Manager E, 2-26)

Combined with the fact that IFMP did not provide any protection at seven- or nine-digit project level, many project managers were nervous about spending control. Once fund is committed, it was nearly impossible to reverse it even it was later found to be erroneous. One project manager commented:

In our old system we used to, when we wanted to buy something or fund a contract, we would initiate a purchase request—a PR—and that PR had a routing it would have to go through for approval, and nobody could spend my money without my approval ahead of time. With IFM, it’s different. I don’t know until after the fact that somebody has spent my money by looking at a report. Anybody with knowledge of these accounting codes could initiate a requisition 0 0 0 and have it charged against my project without my knowledge or approval and I would have no way of knowing that because there is no routing and approval process.

(Project Manager F, 2-17)

To readdress this concern, IFMP team at Glenn implemented research and program management (RPM), a separate Web-based system that allows project managers to manually approve individual purchasing requests. Researchers and engineers were required to get approval from the project manager using RPM first and must enter the RPM approval number when they initiate the requisition process in SAP.

4.4.3. Purchasing. Another issue involved procurement. Researchers often need to purchase items to support their research—from supplies to computer hardware and software to external contracts for research activities. In the past, researchers submitted paper purchase request forms to clerical staff who managed purchasing activity. As part of the ERP system implementation, researchers were expected to conduct all of their purchasing activity directly with the ERP system. Such a “single point of data capture” put the burden of the paperwork on the researchers, who were expected to find data associated with purchases such as vendor codes, which needed to be looked up in different menus of the enterprise system. Furthermore, as a part of the U.S. government, NASA had a set of regulatory requirements that overwhelmed the SAP menus, and required that a good deal of detail be captured in the unstructured header field:

There’s a lot you have to put in any government requisition or a PO. There’s a lot that the buyer needs and a lot that has to be reported somewhere or captured somewhere, so we have our own tab—the NASA data tab—and that’s maxed out 0 0 0 the number of fields that are used in it with SAP’s functionality now.

(Service Checker B, 2-23)

Many researchers developed their own tactics of dealing with this unnecessary complexity. For example, one researcher indicated that he addressed this through “cheat sheets” and related practices. Other engineers and researchers simply filled out the input screen in one way that they know the system took, knowing that it was not the right information. They relied on quality control people in the procurement department to call them and correct their order entry. Yet, still others circumvented the system entirely— using credit cards to purchase items, when possible, and thus following a completely different procedure. In certain areas, credit card use was either inappropriate or limited by management, and many researchers worked through a single administrative person for purchases rather than interface directly with the system. In one case, a researcher who initially signed up as a “superuser” and was first to be trained, indicated that he later fully mediated his system use:

We have an executive assistant to the division chief who is primarily responsible for inputting procurement requests 0 0 0 researchers can do it and some of them do 0 0 0 it takes more [of a researcher’s] time to work that system and to work issues associated with that system. It takes away from their ability to actually do research. (Researcher B, 2-11)

4.4.4. Time Reporting. Center administration and the managers of performing organizations at Glenn became increasingly concerned about fully funding all of their human resources and capital-intensive capabilities under the new system. Particular concerns were raised for certain research groups that were not funded by any current programs and shared services (such as labs, wind tunnels, and a zero gravity tower) that were not frequently used. Because these cost a fixed amount of money to maintain and they must be funded by projects under the new system, their average costs become more expensive as they become less popular. If they could not be funded by projects, the center had to absorb the cost under its overhead account. For example, in 2004, for each dollar for a person who is assigned to a project, Glenn had to charge another \$0.47 as overhead, which included the salaries of unassigned engineers, researchers, and other infrastructure. As such, the center management needed to focus on remaining competitive and cost effective. According to a senior executive at Glenn:

Now everything will be put into one pot of money so you get to see how much you pay for your salary, for the work force that you have, how much you pay for the office space, the lab space, how much you pay for utility and all that will be wrote up and then will be then translated into how expensive you are, how inexpensive you are in doing business. Now I talk about competition, when we actually write a proposal 0 0 0 which taking into account all of this work force and all this overhead benefit would determine how much you’ll be charged in house in order to do business with you. So the more cost effective we are maybe the better we are in position and competition.

(Research Manager E, 1-13)

To center administration and managers of research groups, it was critically important that their center and research group appears to be competitive and efficient, and to put forth the best appearance possible. To do so, managers often tried to minimize the time billed to general and administrative (G&A) overhead. One high-level research manager justified billing overhead researcher time to projects as follows:

You ask employees to charge all their time to a project and they push back saying, well the time we spent at a branch meeting, we can’t charge that to a particular project. I say, spread it across 0 0 0 may not directly be in support of a project, but indirectly they support, they make you a more knowledgeable person about the things that are happening at the center. So 0 0 0 some benefits to a project. (Research Manager B, 2-19)

Reflecting this rationale in time-reporting activities, many researchers looked to categorize as much activity as possible in line with the programs:

Well, if you’re going to charge to overhead tasks, it looks like you spent a lot of time on something and didn’t get much done. Don’t charge to overhead because it makes us look bad. It’s better off to have the people look like they cost less and that they’re inefficient than it is to have the people cost more but when they are working a task, they’re efficient.

(Researcher D, 3-2)

This activity did have ramifications that were quite visible in the research community:

Generally people are told to charge their time to either the project they just finished or the one that’s coming up because they want to keep their overhead rates down. Supervisors who never had anything to do with the test—don’t even know what the test is about—are even charging their time to the projects, too and so you end up with [a skewed] number of what it really costs to run a facility or to do a test.

(Research Manager D, 2-17).

## 5. Discussion

Consistent with previous interpretive studies of enterprise systems, NASA’s implementation of ERP reveals pluralistic and inconsistent situated responses by different individuals (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Soh and Sia 2004; Sia and Soh 2007; Wagner and Newell 2004, 2006). Instead of seeing these irregularities as a conflict between local individuals and standardized logic of the system, we propose an institutional analysis that explores the four examples we present in the NASA case as particular forms of loose coupling that arise to deal with competing institutional logics (Meyer and Rowan 1977, Brunsson 1989, Boxenbaum and Jonson 2008). Individuals at NASA manage competing pressures from multiple institutional logics simultaneously through loose coupling. These incidents of loose coupling did not arise because of conflicts among individuals or groups. While many researchers and managers of all sorts did loosely couple their activity, others did not. Instead, this loose coupling resulted from “structural contradictions,” which involve a more fundamental disjunction between principles, rules, and resources that guide human agency in historically dependent, contextually bound situations (Giddens 1984). It is these structural, or institutional, contradictions that fundamentally drive social change (Giddens 1984, p. 194). Organizational actors navigate the plurality of institutional structures and reconcile them a variety of ways (Dunn and Jones 2010). In the following section, we interpret NASA’s ERP implementation using a theoretical lens of institutional logics to show how local practices are embedded in broader social structures. Our analysis of the case suggests that the existence of multiple institutional logics within NASA is the source of ongoing institutional contradictions and that organizational actors reconcile these contradictions through different forms of loose coupling, which have varying effects.

## 5.1. Contradictions Among Institutional Logics

NASA can be characterized as a nexus of varyingly diverse institutional logics that are often in contradiction. As an agency, the explicitly stated goal of NASA involves space exploration and science. To support this goal, the agency must also engage in administrative activity, as well as advocate for funding. At the same time, the overall goal of an individual center such as Glenn involves maintaining the center’s health and preserving work forces, and this could involve efficient and effective operations as well as the maintenance and development of certain competencies that improve the position of Glenn relative to other centers. Finally, like most government agencies, NASA is under increased pressure for accountability in line with rational management principles. Within the government business language such as “bottom line,” “competition,” and “efficiency” has become a part of everyday language. Even though NASA does not have profit motive and any competition in one sense, much of their management reform actions were influenced by this logic.

Specifically, we identified four institutional logics that were at play in aspects of the enterprise system implementation (Table 1). Although we identified more institutional logics than are presented here, these were selected because they were specifically relevant to the ERP implementation and its appropriation. When individuals espoused the logic of managerial rationalism, we found that they appropriated the system in a manner that was consistent with the expected appropriation of the system. When individuals indicated that the logic they drew upon to guide a specific practice was inconsistent with the logic of managerial rationalism, however, they satisfied the requirements of both logics by loosely coupling their practices from those implied by the ERP system. Next, we will address each of these logics and attend to the way institutional contradictions resulted in loosely coupled practices.

5.1.1. The Logic of Managerial Rationalism. Our data shows that NASA is under the growing influence of the institutional logic of “managerial rationalism” (Danziger et al. 1982, Townley 2002) that emphasizes accountability, control, and agencywide standardization. In the context of the ERP implementation, this control takes the form of adherence to approved program budgets. This institutional logic emphasizes business-like efficiency and is associated with a specific set of assumptions about financial management and ERP (see Table 2).

The institutional logic of managerial rationalism draws from a set of external coercive forces that are increasingly bearing on government agencies— essentially encouraging them to act more like businesses. As perhaps stands to reason, we found that accounting and financial personnel generally spoke in terms that were consistent with the logic of managerial rationalism. We also found a number of project managers, researchers, and research managers who spoke in terms that were quite consistent with this logic as well. 5.1.2. The Logic of Project Management Professionalism. Another institutional logic relevant to the ERP implementation was that of the project management profession (see Table 3). Under this logic, projects are evaluated based on their ability to meet planned deliverable expectations for various longterm space and aeronautics projects. Therefore the institutional logic of project management professionalism at NASA finds its principles in the explicitly stated goal of NASA as a space agency. Because specific scientific and engineering objectives associated with a space or science mission are hard to measure and evaluate, the profession often relies on variance between the plan and actual to assess the performance. Following this logic, project managers attempt to manage and track scientific and engineering projects by accurately gauging and reporting the variance of time and budget.

The ERP system was initially introduced as simply a new way of managing projects and making decisions. It was presented and aggressively promoted as a project management tool that delivers timely information for the program and project community. It was managed by a former space mission project manager and treated as one of the engineering projects. Yet, the system as implemented was designed more as an accounting project. The project management module was not even considered to be part of IFMP until almost one year after the system went live. The original report design was that of an accounting report, and Business Warehouse was introduced only as a reaction to the clamor of the project managers. Informants consistently indicated that the ERP system was not implemented to aid the project groups or centers, but to aid NASA overall with respect to consistency and accountability. A lead consultant who worked at Glenn also expressed a similar sentiment when he said, “SAP, like it or not, is an accounting system first and foremost.”

A contradiction between the logic of project management and the logic of managerial rationalism was evident in our data. One source of the contradiction involved the differences in the temporal assumptions between the two logics. Whereas the logic of project management assumed the presence of a great deal of variance in scientific projects and the need for real-time tracking, the logic of managerial rationalism implied evenly parsed predictable allocation of resources. Because of this difference in assumptions, many project managers felt that they simply could not get the reporting they needed out of ERP. As a result, the implementation team customized a reporting tool to accommodate project manager requests. Using this tool, data in the ERP system was periodically extracted and reconciled with the shadow systems that many project managers used as the primary tools. Another source of contradiction was related to identity. The logic of project management professionalism ties the project manager’s identity to the activity of control over activities that are billed to their projects. The implementation of the ERP system, however, broke this tie and replaced it with a standardized requisition process. In an effort to maximize integration and efficiency, the implementation team avoided configuring proactive controls for project-related costs at the appropriate level. As a result, project managers were able to control the budget only retroactively after the ERP implementation. Therefore the project management organization implemented RPM outside of the ERP to reinstitute proactive approvals.

5.1.3. The Logic of Scientific Professionalism. The third relevant institutional logic that we identified from our data was scientific professionalism (see Table 4). Scientific personnel of the performing organizations are highly educated and narrowly focused in their fields of expertise. Many note that they joined NASA because of the glamour of space and their fascination with research that “pushes the envelope.” Ph.D.s among researchers are typical, and when asked what they do, they often gave highly technical replies. Researchers at NASA Glenn focus largely on technical concerns associated with ground-breaking discoveries in areas of propulsion, communication, and materials science. Their research cannot be controlled procedurally, but activity is dictated by the norms of the relevant profession, scientific method, and the practices of design and experimentation. This logic emphasizes scientific accomplishments such as patents, scientific papers, and conference presentations. Researchers see their professional identify in the field of their own expertise as highly important and see often programmatic goals pursued by the strategic enterprises as a way of getting funding to pursue their own research agenda.

The sentiment associated with the logic of science professionalism is that the work of the researcher is the important, real work of NASA, and that any distraction from this is wasteful. There is a general notion among researchers that their activity is more valuable than administrators or project managers, “because there’s those who control and those who want to get stuff done” (Research Manager C, 2-11). Researchers consider themselves to be those who “get stuff done.”

One contradiction that came about between the logic of the scientific profession and managerial rationalism involved researcher procurement activity. Typically, researchers had no problem with accountability with respect to their purchasing, and generally understood the assumption of system implementers relating the full integration of financial data and capturing data at its source. The conflict, however, involved the identity of the researchers. Where the system implementers expected all NASA employees to directly interface with the system for relevant practices, many researchers identified with a higher level of task. It was not that these researchers were incapable of using the system (many were literal rocket scientists, after all!), but that they chose not to because it was somehow a type of task that they should not be doing, in their estimation. Whether they used an “official” human intermediary, an informal intermediary in the form of the checker, or the credit card method (where others would have to later go back and reconcile purchases with researcher budgets), researchers often removed themselves from the activity directly. They placed a go-between in the fabric, or structure, of the organization between their activity and the enterprise system data.

5.1.4. The Logic of Organizational Persistence. The fourth relevant institutional logic that we identified is the logic of organizational persistence (see Table 5). This logic is rooted in a local center’s relationship with its local constituents and their important position in the local economy. With this institutional logic, the performance evaluation is based on the preservation of the work force and its capabilities and infrastructure. Drawing on this logic, centers maintain unique yet infrequently demanded resources (such as wind tunnels), and face a particular challenge in preserving their expertise and unique resources by aligning those resources with programmatic demands from the seven enterprises. Although these areas may prove vital to NASA’s mission on some later date, many of them may not directly impact NASA’s current mission. One Glenn manager points out the indeterminacy associated with NASA’s competencies, offering the following tribology research example to justify this logic:

The goals of managerial rationalism involve accountability and control across the entire organization, which, in this case, involves optimizing the entire agency to focus on strategic enterprises. In situations where centers or research groups within those centers adopt the logic of organizational persistence, this could be in direct opposition to the stated purpose of the ERP system. If a center manager sees tribology as a core competency of Glenn, he or she may be loathe to relinquish this capability simply because it does not merit short-term funding. As managers ask researchers to creatively allocate their time to bolster the image of certain groups, the data in the system may bear little resemblance to the actual time allocation.

## 5.2. Loose Coupling and Institutional Contradiction

The loose coupling we observed results from structural contradictions between the logic of managerial rationalism, which was associated with the enterprise system during the implementation, and the logic that certain individuals draw upon to guide their practices. In our analysis, we traced these contradictions and found deep structural regularity behind what may appear to be idiosyncratic local responses to the ERP implementation on the surface.

Specifically, we identified four general forms of loose coupling between local practices as they were enacted and the way those practices were represented in the ERP system (Table 6). In these situations, the formal, planned, and documented expectations associated with the relevant practices and associated data did not involve a direct, one-to-one mapping to the enacted practice. Of course, the aspects of the practice associated with the enterprise system are limited to the codified aspects of the practice (Vaast and Levina 2006), and therefore can never fully reflect actual practice. However, the system is expected to embed aspects of that practice to an extent, and thus materially constrain that practice (Volkoff et al. 2007). The local practices and their representation within the system were never entirely independent, and this loose coupling was maintained by a variety of mechanisms that reconciled the two, including shadow systems, intermediation, and creative interpretation. In each of these cases, some goal for the ERP implementation was undermined or compromised.

First, we identified temporal loose coupling. In our data, project managers simultaneously satisfied both the logic of project management professionalism and the logic of management rationalism through temporal loose coupling. The contradiction between the two arises at the point of assumptions—while tracking and control is important, actors who espouse the logic of the project management professionalism indicate that variance is inevitable and must be managed, whereas the assumptions associated with the logic of managerial rationalism involve standardized evenly segmented distributions of work. Therefore, project managers attempted to reconcile these two logics by leveraging the time lag between events and the reporting of those events. The two are reconciled frequently and accurately, but at any given time, the information on the spreadsheet (shadow system) may be out of sync with the enterprise system. Our analysis clearly indicates that this temporal loose coupling compromises the goal of real-time data, which was touted as an important benefit of the ERP system.

We refer to the second form of loose coupling as procedural loose coupling0 Our researcher purchasing example shows a structural contradiction between the logic of scientific professionalism and logic of managerial rationalism. While many researchers understood the need to account for their spending and relevance of this spending to overall budgets and such, they often felt that they should not be engaged in the administrative work of navigating system standards in what they deemed to be a byzantine requisition process. Their identities were quite solidly rooted in the scientific community and that involved focusing on the real work of NASA. Thus purchasing was off-loaded to administrative personnel whose identities were more in line with procurement activity or researchers circumvented the standard process entirely with credit cards. In this case, procedural loose coupling resulted where there were standardized procedures—the sequence of activities and roles associated with each activity—represented by proper channels in the organization are circumvented. If people do not think that they should engage with the system for a task, but the task must be performed through the system, then they will use an intermediary or circumvent the system. The procedural loose coupling can be distinguished from the temporal form because procedural refers to the roles and sequences of activities within a process, whereas temporal refers to the timing of activities within that process. The procedural form of loose coupling may benefit NASA because it frees up valuable researcher time but undermines the goal of having every person directly use the system (“universal system use”).

Third, we identified material loose coupling. In the example of the new RPM system, the contradiction again involved the logic of the project management profession. While the process implied by the ERP implementation empowered performing organizations to bill their time and make purchases with WBS numbers, the process preferred by many of the project managers involved the proactive approval and management of the funds that would be allocated to their projects. The solution of a separate RPM approval system represented a material departure from the original process. In using the term “material,” we highlight the aspect of organizational practices that is embedded in IT artifacts (Volkoff et al. 2007). The approval process implied by the original system implementation involved a retroactive tracking of items billed to a project through the ERP system. The process associated with the new RPM system involved a proactive method of approving funds. The RPM number was included in a field of the ERP system, so in that sense, they were coupled, but the two were separate systems, so were loosely coupled through the activities of those doing the purchasing. While this situation did provide the control required by project managers, it also compromised the benefit of a single integrated system by requiring a parallel system for this approval, and again allowed for human errors and inefficiencies associated with multiple systems that were among the key touted advantages of this integrated system. Although material loose coupling may involve procedural and temporal elements, the key distinction is that material loose coupling involves the use of additional material artifacts to perform an activity.

Finally, we identified interpretive loose coupling. In our data, an example of interpretive loose coupling was enacted to resolve the contradiction between the logic of managerial rationalism and the logic of organizational persistence. While control and accountability were central to the logic of managerial rationalism associated with the ERP system, this was often directly contradictory to the goal of local organizations in many cases—be they centers, performing organizations, or smaller units—which earnestly believed that their competencies should be preserved for the good of the organization. In these situations, individuals creatively interpreted their activities and actively adjusted and manipulated the data to demonstrate compliance, maintain legitimacy, and generally look good, yet performed activities that were occasionally quite inconsistent with the data in the system. They rationalized their activity by representing it in such a way that allowed for greater legitimacy. Over time these adjustments will likely balance out—as organizations swap budget dollars and researchers eventually do bill time to projects that have allocated budgets—but at any given point, the data in the system simply does not match the reality of ongoing practice. If one were to take a longterm perspective and see the ERP system as primarily a tool for bolstering legitimacy, then this is not necessarily a problem and only poses issues relating to material and temporal loose coupling—some redundancy but over time, it is in balance. However, if one were to take a functional literal view of the enterprise system and expect a one-to-one mapping of data to practice, such principle-oriented loose coupling that involves some spinning and occasional outright misrepresentation, one might question whether shortterm actionable decisions can or should be made based on the data in the system. For any given period, data reported in the system may, in fact, vary widely from the activity it is intended to represent. Thus the control enabled by tight coupling of organizational activity to an enterprise system (Ciborra 2000, El Amrani et al. 2006) is effectively weakened by interpretive loose coupling.

These four forms of loose coupling are not mutually exclusive. For example, the temporal and material forms are both evident in the project tracking process. However, each identifies specific elements of loosely coupled practice. Together they enable us to be more precise in articulating the ways in which loose coupling can be maintained when an enterprise IS is introduced into an institutionally plural organizational environment.

5.3. Institutional Pluralism and Embedded Agency Our analysis of NASA as an institutionally plural organization where contradictory institutions coexist through various forms of loose coupling is consistent with a variety of traditions in organizational research (Scott 1987). For example, Strauss et al. (1963) describe how the various professions coexist harmoniously through a “negotiated order” that manifests through a division of labor, where groups fall into a stable equilibrium through direct conflict and intergroup jockeying for position. A result of this conflict may be some sort of compromise between the formal, or legitimate, group and the informal but necessary group (Blau and Scott 1962). However, we contend that what appears to be a negotiated order among groups may actually be a single point in an ongoing dynamic order between contradictory institutions reflecting the ebb and flow of logics and ideologies as they gain and lose salience in given contexts (Dunn and Jones 2010). Clegg (1975), for example, pointed out that this stability may have less to do with the groups themselves, and more to do with the modes of rationality that they draw upon. He more recently described this in terms of a “stabilization of an episodic field” at a moment in time, where individuals reconcile rationalities to guide action in a dynamic continually transforming arrangement (Clegg et al. 2006, p. 241). Through the device of an institutional logic, a researcher can characterize modern organizing as having ongoing contradictions in a “field of forces” (Bourdieu and Wacquant 1992), rather than a semi-stable negotiated order among social groups.

Through an institutional analysis, particularly through the concept of an institutional logic, our study shows that IS researchers can contextualize local situated practices with respect to the broader social structures that individuals draw upon to guide action, maintain identities, and reconstitute through their actions (King et al. 1994, Orlikowski and Barley 2001, Jones and Karsten 2008). Each of the institutions we addressed represents ideologies, professions, and institutionalized organizations, and the activities illustrate how practices relating to an enterprise system implementation are deeply embedded in this nexus of institutions. Through this institutional lens, we take a step toward identifying regularities and consistencies that exist behind situated, what appears to be idiosyncratic behaviors (Scott 1990), and simultaneously avoiding the deterministic theorizing that often accompanies efforts to identify consistent patterns across IT adaptation (Robey and Boudreau 1999, Leonardi and Barley 2008).

The focus on institutional logics puts the locus of contestation at the intersection of one broad social structure versus another. Often enterprise systemrelated research pits top-down managerial control against bottom-up situated organizing (e.g., Ciborra 2000). In many cases, these researchers invoke the notion of Bentham’s panopticon as an artifact of this top-down control, and liken ERP systems to this technique of total managerial control (albeit as an imperfect example, see Zuboff 1988, Sia et al. 2002 and Elmes et al. 2005). However, power relations in modern organizations are not so straightforward and unidirectional. Although many organizations operate under the legitimizing myth of hierarchical bureaucratic organizational forms, in reality, these firms act more like hybrids, or “polyarchies” (Clegg et al. 2006). Particularly in organizations where knowledge work is fundamental, individuals draw upon a variety of cultural resources, such as expertise, for example, and can exercise more de facto control than the organizational form would typically allow (Clegg et al. 2006). Knowledge work increasingly takes the form of interorganizational projects, and in these contexts, organizational actors must resolve ambiguous power arrangements in the absence of institutionalized authority structures (Levina and Orlikowski 2009). Thus the emerging view of contemporary organizing might be described as postpanoptical, implying that the binary management-worker opposition is no longer ubiquitously relevant (Bauman 2000). In this more “liquid” state, “the prime technique of power is now escape, slippage, elision, and avoidance” (Bauman 2000, p. 11). In the postpanoptical era, one can no longer assume unidirectional, top-to-bottom power struggles, nor can homogenous communities be taken for granted. Rather, one broad institutionalized social structure is often pitted against another, and individuals navigate a variety of competing institutions (Dunn and Jones 2010).

Thus our operationalization of institutional logics, combined with the framework of different forms of loose coupling, allows us to take an institutional perspective that accommodates—even highlights— human agency. A common criticism of using an institutional lens is that it downplays the role of human agency (c.f. Volkoff et al. 2007). However, embedded agency is fundamental to contemporary institutionalist thought (Powell and DiMaggio 1991, Greenwood et al. 2008), and the concept of institutional logic allows a direct way of capturing the rules and resources in a dynamic fashion that organizational actors reproduce without making claims about the primacy of either structure or agency (Thornton and Ocasio 2008). Institutions are not physical realities that are “out there” driving human action from some supraorganizational level. Rather, institutions are enacted through reproduced patterns of activity and discursively reproduced across individuals. With a focus on institutional logics, we accommodate the ability of humans to set their own course and act in an unpredictable idiosyncratic manner, yet we do so acknowledging that this agency is entrenched in eons of human history and the objective social realities into which humans are born (Berger and Luckman 1967, Giddens 1984).

Previous interpretive and qualitative studies of enterprise system implementations recognize the situatedness of individuation actions (Suchman 1987) and the plurality of local groups, whose norms and shared beliefs may not be consistent with that of financial outcomes and enterprisewide efficiency and integrations that are often assumed in ERP implementations (Allen 2005; Ciborra 2000; Pollock and Cornford 2004; Soh and Sia 2004; Sia and Soh 2007; Wagner and Newell 2004, 2006). These studies are consistent with a “practice” perspective common in the broader IS discourse, and characterize individuals as members of a particular social group or communities of practice (Østerlund and Carlile 2005) that draw upon fields such as professions for rules and resources (e.g., Levina and Vaast 2006, Howard-Grenville and Carlile 2006), and interpretive frames (e.g., Barrett 1999, Vaast and Walsham 2005) to guide action. Such research has a long tradition of describing organizations as pluralistic (Danziger et al. 1982), where the locus of conflict involves power struggles between individuals and local collectives and the standardizing logic of the system—often resulting in some form of resistance, rejection, or accommodation to the enterprise systems. By adopting an institutionally plural view of organizations (Kraatz and Block 2008, Dunn and Jones 2010), we extend this practice view by contextualizing practices in broader institutional fields and characterizing inconsistencies, not only in terms of overt conflicts, but also in terms of more fundamental, often unspoken, structural contradictions.

Organizational actors manage institutional contradictions by loosely coupling elements of their activities, thus enabling the standardized presentation of nonstandardized practice. Organizational researchers have long understood that enacted practices are not always consistent with their presentation and have contrasted these two elements of practice using a variety of distinctions, including formal versus informal organization (Blau and Scott 1962), canonical versus noncanonical practice (Brown and Duguid 1991), ostensive versus performative aspects of routines (Feldman and Pentland 2003), and espoused versus enacted genres (Levina and Orlikowski 2009). Our analysis indicates that inconsistencies between ostensive or performative aspects of routines, for example, might be fruitfully characterized as the result of situated actors reconciling diverse institutional logics. The notion of contradictory institutional logics may help explain why there are these inconsistencies. This idea is particularly important to the study of enterprise-wide IS, because such systems are based on a notion of standardization that is counter to such practice views of organizing. Therefore it is no surprise that the detailed study of the implementation of such systems reports a variety of workarounds, shadow systems, bolt-ons, and a long list of improvisational accommodations (e.g., Ciborra 2000, Wagner and Newell 2004, Boudreau and Robey

2005). However, there is little work distinguishing between these different forms of accommodation (with some exceptions, c.f. Azad and King 2008), nor tracing the organizational implications of different forms of accommodation. This work represents an initial attempt to leverage the particular form of loose coupling that is emphasized by the contemporary institutionalist tradition (Meyer and Rowan 1977, Oliver 1991, Brunsson 1989, Boxenbaum and Jonnson 2008) to theorize about different general forms of accommodation to institutional contradictions and their organizational implications.

A common explanation for these widespread accommodation tactics points to the “fit” between enterprise systems and organizational contexts (Davenport 1998, Soh et al. 2003, Soh and Sia 2004, Sia and Soh 2007). Recent work has indicated that the notion of fit can refer to two different concepts: (1) the degree to which enterprise systems “enable” more effective work practices and (2) how enterprise systems can be more or less comprehensive in their “coverage” of organizational practices (Strong and Volkoff 2010). Our research builds on this work in two ways. First, our pluralistic perspective emphasizes the way in which a system that enables more effective work practices relative to one institutional logic, might be enforcing less effective practices according to another logic. Second, our formulation of loose coupling highlights the way in which coverage can be simultaneously complete in one sense (i.e., system is used for organizational process) and incomplete in another (i.e., situated practices loosely coupled from the system).

## 5.4. Implications for Practice

Our findings have several implications for practice. First, while the data in an enterprise system is intended to represent certain organizational activity, we found that it is fairly commonplace for the data to bear little resemblance to the activity it is intended to make visible and control at any moment in time. This suggests that managers who implement ERP to bring about changes in certain activities in the firm must be cautious. Our findings show that even when there is system use, the enacted practice may be loosely coupled from the aspects of practice represented by the data in the system. That is, just because the system is used and data is populated does not mean that the process prescriptions associated with the ERP implementation are necessarily complied with. Process improvements such as those associated with an ERP implementation require more than just system use; they require process compliance, or adherence to the process scripts outside of system use to gain many of the benefits of the system (Berente et al. 2010). As we showed, particularly with the example of the interpretive loose coupling, all situations where contradictory logics are simultaneously accommodated are not outcome neutral.

Furthermore, it is important to understand that contemporary organizations are institutionally plural. An organization is a nexus of multiple, nested, and contesting institutional logics. Organizational actors draw upon specific institutional logics to guide their actions at the moment, form their identities, and make meaning in their daily activity. When an IS is implemented into an organization, it is often aligned with a specific institutional order. However, managers and designers must be aware that the IS is inserted into an ecology of multiple existing logics of action. The IS will be consistent with certain logics—offering the potential for eventual alignment through mutual adaptation (Leonard-Barton 1988)—or it can be in conflict with an incumbent logic, which would likely bring about loose coupling, fundamental change to the practice, or failure.

Finally, any contemporary organization is subject to simultaneous often contradictory pressures such as concerns for efficiency, short-term profits, long-term innovation, regulatory compliance, etc. It is important too for managers to understand that the development and implementation of new IS, as well as the appropriation of these systems in practice, are fundamentally linked with issues of legitimacy. Legitimating logics shift across all phases of a system design and use, and these logics have very real consequences on organizational practices over time. It is important for practitioners to understand that loosely coupled practice, while not value neutral, is neither always negative nor always positive. While certain forms of loosely coupled practice undermine elements of the implementation—such as the manipulative rationalization of time tracking—these same practices also work to bolster other elements of an organization— such as the preservation of potentially important competencies. It is up to managers to assess each situation of loose coupling with the full appreciation of such trade-offs, and our analysis of the temporal, sequential, material and interpretive loose coupling and their direct effects on the ERP implementation offers managers a lens for analyzing such situations.

## 5.5. Limitations

This study has a number of limitations. Although one of the strengths of the study is that institutional pluralism is dramatically evident in the case of NASA, an organization that is subject to extreme and dynamic pressures, this may also be a key weakness of our findings. While we anticipate that many less extreme, nongovernment organizations face similar dynamics (c.f. Farjoun and Starbuck 2007), whether our theorized relationships hold up across contexts is an empirical question. Another limitation involves the observation that individuals in the same group do not respond uniformly to the enterprise system, because we did not discuss why some informants responded differently. Perhaps there was something about the work of these informants that made it possible for them to be different. Perhaps they had managerial aspirations or other unique interests or incentives. Maybe it was something about their personal experience. Such explanations do not necessarily contradict the applicability of institutional logics as a lens (quite the contrary—they show how adaptable the lens is), but certainly point to the causal limitations of applying this lens ex post. Finally, our research method departed from canonical grounded theory methodology with respect to the theoretical sampling (Morse 2007). First, although purposeful sampling did guide our data collection, it was not theoretically driven in the sense that the theoretical devices we eventually settled upon (i.e., institutional logics and loose coupling) were not brought to bear during data collection. Instead, the theoretical model was developed by analyzing data after collection was terminated. Thus we did not terminate data collection upon saturation with respect to our theoretical model, but rather based on a saturation of new insight in a general sense.

This study offers only a beginning to understanding the loose coupling that arises because of institutional contradictions in the wake of a large-scale IS implementation. Future research should do more to both systematically analyze the relationship between various forms of loose coupling and the relative magnitude of loose coupling associated with different practices and their effects.

## 6. Conclusion

In the beginning of this paper, we posed two contradictory statements. On the one hand, we indicated that much of the research on enterprise system implementations suggests that such monolithic standardized systems can never sufficiently accommodate the diversity of complex organizations, and are therefore doomed. On the other hand, while challenges with ERP implementations are certainly widespread, there are plentiful examples of organizational successes with ERP. The way we reconcile the two is by noting that human actors loosely couple local practices with the representation of those practices in the ERP system. This view puts a vocabulary around phenomena that other researchers have reported where stability of the system (i.e., “success”) is accompanied by practices that undermine the integration and control that the system is premised upon (e.g., Cordella and Simon 2000, Sia et al. 2002, Wagner and Newell 2006). However, in this study, we go beyond the existing literature to characterize contemporary organizations as institutionally plural—subject to shifting, dynamic legitimating institutional pressures that are often contradictory. The tensions and conflicts associated with an enterprise system implementation are not simply drawn from the idiosyncratic local differences, but reflect broader societal struggles. Individual actors are not confined by a single logic that guides their actions. Instead, actors skillfully draw upon multiple and often contradictory logics, while they simultaneously preserve the integrity of the logics across domains. Integration and coherence in organizations, therefore, are not enabled by the rigid enforcement of standardized best practices—they are enabled by the individuals who skillfully enact and reenact multiple forms of loose coupling (Weick 2001).
