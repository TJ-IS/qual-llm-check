# THE FAULT IN OUR STARS: MOLECULAR GENETICS AND INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY USE<sup>1</sup>

Susan A. Brown Management Information Systems Department, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, U.S.A. {suebrown@arizona.edu}

Richard W. Sias

Department of Finance, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, U.S.A. {sias@arizona.edu}

There is a growing interest in understanding the role of genetics in explaining heterogeneity in behaviors, including those related to information systems (IS). The majority of the recent genetics research focuses on searching the entire genome in genome-wide association studies (GWASs) to link DNA to human traits. The results of GWASs can be used on datasets to compute a measure of genetic propensity known as a polygenic score, or PGS. PGSs are widely viewed as the future of genetics research. We conducted an exploratory study, in the context of information technology (IT) use, to examine if the PGS approach can be used to better understand the role of genetics in IS research. Consistent with our hypotheses, genetic endowments associated with Educational Attainment and General Cognition positively predict technology use, and genetic endowments associated with Neuroticism, Depressive Symptoms, Myocardial Infarction, and Coronary Artery Disease negatively predict technology use more than half a century later (genetic endowments are established at conception and our sample consists of individuals aged 50 to 80). Many of the characteristics known to be associated with heterogeneity in IT use (e.g., trust, education) appear to be mediators linking PGSs to IT use. Nonetheless, a number of PGSs maintain meaningful direct effects.

Keywords: DNA, genetics, individual differences, phenotypes, technology use, NeuroIS, older adults

## Introduction

Consistent with Shakespeare’s claim, a fast-growing literature examines the important role of genetics in understanding social science phenomena, including those of interest to information systems (IS) researchers. Historically, scientists have inferred the importance of genetics via differences in correlations between monozygotic (i.e., identical) and dizygotic (i.e., fraternal) twins. The mapping of the human genome in 2003, however, transformed genetics research by allowing investigators to use molecular variation in DNA to better understand how and why genetics impact outcomes. For example, in what geneticists denote a candidate gene study, Browne and Walden (2020) found that variation in a single nucleotide (i.e., a single rung of the 3.2 billion steps on the human DNA ladder) can help explain internet search behaviors.

Genetics research in the last two decades, however, has revealed that almost all human traits of interest to social scientists are a function of hundreds, thousands, or potentially millions of locations across the entire genome rather than a single gene. The realization that most phenotypes (i.e., realized traits or characteristics that result from the interaction of both genetics and environment) are highly polygenic (i.e., the function of many genes across multiple chromosomes) has fundamentally altered molecular genetics research. Specifically, the vast majority of genetics research in the last decade has focused on searching the entire genome in genome-wide association studies (GWASs) to link genotypes (i.e., the DNA-based genetic makeup of an individual established at conception) to phenotypes. The results of GWASs can then be used on any genotyped dataset to compute, for the first time, an estimate of genetic propensity (known as a polygenic score, typically denoted PGS) for an individual for a given phenotype. PGSs are widely viewed as the future of much of genetics research. For example, MIT Technology Review named PGSs as one of the “Top 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2018.”

A growing number of large databases (e.g., the Health and Retirement Study dataset, the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study dataset, and the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health dataset) collect primary genetic data and compute respondent PGSs even when IRB restrictions prohibit the release of the underlying genetic data (see Okbay et al., 2018). Thus, public databases currently provide, and other databases are likely to soon add, measures of genetic propensities (i.e., PGSs) for a number of respondent phenotypes.

In this study, we offer an exploratory analysis of how PGSs from large, standardized databases can be used to investigate IS research questions without the need for the researcher to gather or analyze primary genetic data. That is, we investigate the possibility that PGSs offer a promising and exciting new dimension of NeuroIS research (e.g., Riedl et al., 2020). Thus, our overall research question is (RQ-O): Can the PGS approach be used to better understand the role of genetics in IS research?

To formally evaluate this question, we estimated a mediated model that examines the ability of genetic endowments to predict information technology (IT) use. We examined genetic endowments associated with cognitive ability (Educational Attainment, General Cognition), health (Myocardial Infarction, Coronary Artery Disease), and personality (Neuroticism, Depressive Symptoms) based on extant IS research evidence suggesting that the associated realized phenotypes influence IT use.<sup>2,3</sup> Importantly, we do not suggest that genetics fully account for variations in IT use. Clearly, experience and culture play a role (e.g., Straub et al., 1997). Our study, however, is the first to examine whether PGSs can be used to (1) predict IT use, (2) examine phenotypes as mediators linking these genetic endowments to IT use, and (3) examine if PGSs can explain IT use beyond the associated phenotype.

This points to three research questions that require examination. First, as shown in the top portion of Figure 1, we begin by leveraging extant evidence of relationships between phenotypes and IT use to develop hypotheses for the relationships between six PGSs and IT use. Thus, our specific Research Question 1 (RQ1) is: Can PGSs predict IT use? For example, because prior work has revealed that realized education is positively related to IT use (e.g., Carpenter & Buday, 2007), we hypothesize that a higher genetic endowment (i.e., the PGS) for Educational Attainment will predict greater IT use. Consistent with our hypotheses and RQ1, we demonstrate that genetic endowments established at least a half century prior (i.e., genetic endowments are established at conception and our sample consists of individuals aged 50 to 80) and prior to any heterogeneity in life experience, can predict whether an individual uses the internet, uses a computer, how often they use a computer, whether they use social media, and the extent to which they use social media. Moreover, the effect sizes are meaningful. For instance, a one-standard-deviation higher genetic endowment associated with Educational Attainment is associated with a 6.1% increase in the probability of using the internet. The relation between genetic endowments and social media use is weaker; however, we still found that genetic endowments associated with Educational Attainment and General Cognition are positively related to, and genetic endowments associated with Depressive Symptoms are negatively related to, social media use.

As shown in the bottom portion of Figure 1, we then focus on a mediation model to better understand how and why PGSs predict IT use. Specifically, Research Question 2 (RQ2) is: Are there meaningful indirect effects between PGSs, phenotypes, and IT use? That is, is there evidence that the associated phenotype mediates the relation between the PGSs and IT use? Research Question 3 (RQ3) asks: Are there meaningful direct effects between PGSs and IT use? That is, do PGSs continue to predict IT use even when controlling for the associated phenotype? In short, as shown in the equation at the bottom of Figure 1, we partition the total effect from RQ1 (denoted c; e.g., Educational Attainment PGS predicting internet use) into an indirect effect (RQ2)—the product of a and b—(e.g., Educational Attainment PGS predicting internet use via realized education levels) and a direct effect (RQ3) (denoted c′; e.g., Educational Attainment PGS predicting internet use even after accounting for realized educational levels).

For all cases where we found a meaningful relation between the genetic endowment and IT use, we found evidence that the associated phenotype(s) mediates the relation between the PGS and IT use, i.e., empirical support for RQ2. For example, as predicted, realized educational attainment mediates the relation between the Educational Attainment PGS and internet use. In most cases, however, we continued to find substantial direct (c′) effects, i.e., empirical support for RQ3. For instance, the PGS associated with Educational Attainment continues to predict internet use even after controlling for realized educational attainment. In sum, our results demonstrate that the PGS approach can be used to both explain additional variance in IS outcomes and understand the importance of genetics in linking phenotypes to IT use.

## Background

## IT Use

The technology adoption and use literature is vast. For example, the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) holds that four constructs— performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions—primarily determine the intention to use technology and subsequent technology use behavior (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Venkatesh et al. (2012) extended

UTAUT by adding constructs associated with hedonic motivation, price value, and habit. Each of the constructs, however, is related to underlying causes. For instance, performance expectancy is theorized to arise from perceived usefulness, extrinsic motivation, job fit, relative advantage, and outcome expectations (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Research has also identified several characteristics that impact IT adoption and use including IT identity (Carter et al., 2020; Carter & Grover, 2015), trust in technology (McKnight et al., 2011), personal innovativeness in IT (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998), IT overload (Ahuja et al., 2007), and computer selfefficacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). Holding situational constructs constant, however, individuals exhibit substantial heterogeneity in technology adoption and use (e.g., Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002; Zmud, 1979).

Although there are exceptions, most IS research does not consider the role of genetics. Yet, at a fundamental level, every choice is a function of genes, environment, or their interaction. As a result, understanding the role of genetics, and how genetics interact with the environment, helps one understand the mechanisms driving IS-related choices. In this study, we hypothesize that heterogeneity in genetic endowments influences heterogeneity in these underlying factors that influence IT use as many of these factors are subjective to the user. For instance, as detailed below, evidence suggests optimism is a function of both one’s genome and one’s environment. As a result, we predict that genetic endowments associated with Depressive Symptoms and Neuroticism will predict variation in optimism. Optimism also likely influences both the expected outcome and the perceived usefulness. Thus, genetic endowments associated with Depressive Symptoms or Neuroticism may influence technology use, in part, by influencing optimism, as one example. In addition, genetic endowments associated with Depressive Symptoms or Neuroticism may predict IT use beyond their impact on optimism for two reasons. First, as pointed out by Harlow and Brown (1990), biological data can improve our measurement of realizations, e.g., the Neuroticism PGS may capture variation in optimism beyond that captured by traditional (survey-based) measures. Second, as discussed below, there is strong evidence that most genes are pleiotropic—impacting multiple, often seemingly unrelated, traits. As a result, a PGS may predict both the target trait (e.g., Myocardial Infarction PGS predicts heart attacks) and non-target traits (e.g., Myocardial Infarction PGS predicts risk aversion (Sias et al., 2021).

## The Evolution of Genetics Research—A Brief Overview

Freese (2018) describes the history of social science genomics research as a “three-act play.” The first act was based on examining similarity between relatives to estimate the role of genetics in explaining phenotypes (including behavioral characteristics). The most common method evaluates differences between monozygotic (i.e., identical) twins, who share approximately 100% of their DNA, and dizygotic (i.e., fraternal) twins, who like any other siblings, share approximately 50% of the DNA that varies across humans. Assuming that twins have identical environments, one can estimate the relative heritability (i.e., an estimate of the importance of genetics in explaining variation in a phenotype) by comparing the correlation for monozygotic twins to dizygotic twins. For instance, Polderman et al. (2015) report results from a meta-analysis of more than 2,700 twin studies over the last 50 years that examine 17,800 phenotypes. In IS literature, the twin-study approach has been used to examine the heritability of online media use (Ayorech et al., 2017) and behavioral security (Chung & Galletta, 2013).

The mapping of the human genome in 2003 fundamentally changed genetics research by allowing investigators to directly evaluate genetic variation across individuals. The human genome, essentially the blueprint for building and maintaining a human, consists of 23 pairs of chromosomes within the nucleus of every cell (except red blood cells and germline cells). Each chromosome is comprised of a single tightly wound DNA molecule. Genes, which make up less than 1% of the chromosome, are the parts of the chromosome that code for proteins. The DNA molecule has the familiar double-helix shape. If one imagines the DNA molecule as a twisted ladder, the rungs of the ladder are comprised of pairs of four nitrogenous bases—adenine, thymine, cytosine, and guanine, typically referred to A, T, C, and G. These rungs exhibit complementary base pairing, which means that A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G. If one focuses on a single side of the ladder, this yields four possible combinations (AT, TA, CG, GC). A nucleotide is the nitrogenous base on one side of the ladder and the associated (attached) sugar molecule and phosphate group that comprise the structure of the ladder’s rail. That is, a nucleotide simply represents a single rung on the DNA ladder.

The human genome (within every cell) consists of approximately 3.2 billion nucleotides in the 23 chromosomes. Genomic sequencing is the process of determining the order of these nucleotides. The location of each nucleotide in the genome is known as a locus. The vast majority (more than 99%) of nucleotides are identical for humans. A locus where humans exhibit differences is known as a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP, pronounced “snip,” or plural SNPs, pronounced “snips”). The human genome has approximately 10 million SNPs that account for most of the genetic variation across humans.<sup>4</sup> The more common variation at a SNP (e.g., C) is denoted the major allele, while the less common variation (e.g., T) is denoted the minor allele.<sup>5</sup> Because individuals have pairs of chromosomes (paternal and maternal), an individual has zero, one, or two minor alleles for each SNP. Browne and Walden’s (2020) Appendix A provides an excellent discussion of the structure of DNA and how genes code for proteins.

In a candidate gene study—Act II (Freese, 2018) of genomics research—scientists hypothesized a relation between a phenotype and a particular SNP (or a small set of SNPs) based on the associated gene’s function or previous evidence of the SNP’s association with a phenotype. For example, most individuals have two C alleles at SNP rs1044396, but some individuals have one or two T alleles at that locus (i.e., T is the minor allele). Candidate gene studies have found that the C:C genotype (relative to C:T or T:T genotypes) at rs1044396 is associated with higher internet addiction (Jeong et al., 2017, Montag et al., 2012) and internet search behavior in the form of more time gathering information but less time with each piece of information (Browne & Walden, 2020). There are two primary limitations of candidate gene studies. First, although some rare traits can be attributed to a single (or few) SNP, the impact of genetics on most phenotypes arises from very small effects from a very large number—hundreds, thousands, or even millions—of SNPs spread throughout the genome. That is, most phenotypes exhibit (Gibson, 2018), “extraordinarily high polygenicity.” Second, given the high number of potential explanatory variables (i.e., 10 million SNPs) and small sample sizes, many candidate gene studies suffer from “non-replicability” (Benjamin et al., 2012, p. 629).

The GWAS/PGS approach—Act III (Freese, 2018) of genomics research—is aptly named: GWASs examine the relation between the phenotype and all SNPs, i.e., a genomewide search for associations between a phenotype and all SNPs. The approach has proven very successful. For example, a review in the American Journal of Human Genetics (Visscher et al., 2017) concludes, “the experimental design of GWASs has led to a remarkable range of discoveries in human genetics over the last decade.” MacArthur et al. (2017) cataloged over 2,518 GWASs published in 337 different journals. Two fundamental changes led to the development of the GWAS approach. First, as noted above, scientists discovered that most phenotypes are highly polygenic. The second important factor was the rapid decline in genotyping costs allowing for large samples that, as discussed below, are required for GWAS.

The GWAS approach faces unique challenges relative to traditional methods. First, because the number of predictors (i.e., \~10 million SNPs for each individual in the sample) is much greater than the number of observations (i.e., the number of genotyped individuals), multiple regression is not possible. As a result, GWASs examine the relation between a phenotype and each SNP individually. Second, to adjust for false positives with the very large number of predictor variables, most GWASs use a Bonferroni correction and set the statistical significance level at $p \ < \ 5 \ \times \ 1 0 ^ { - 8 } .$ . Third, because most phenotypes are genetically influenced by many small effects, GWASs require very large sample sizes (genotyped individuals) to generate enough power to identify and estimate the multitude of small effects. As a result, most GWASs are based on consortia of many universities and research studies.<sup>6</sup> Fourth, due to SNP frequency differences across ancestry groups, most GWASs are primarily limited to European ancestry samples (see Bentley et al., 2017). Fifth, even within a given ancestry group, “population stratification”— individuals in the same ancestry group exhibiting systematic differences in SNP frequency due to nonrandom mating—can contaminate results. For example, Hamer and Sirota (2000) point out that if a GWAS was estimated on a sample of Europeans and Asians, any SNP with allele frequency differences across these ancestry groups would be associated with the likelihood of chopstick use. Most GWASs control for population stratification within an ancestry group by using a set of (usually 10) principal components computed from the sample (see Appendix A for additional detail). Sixth, because whole genome sequencing (i.e., directly measuring the genotype of all approximately 10M SNPs) is too expensive, researchers usually measure a subset of SNPs and infer (known as SNP imputation) other SNPs that are highly correlated.

Although GWASs were originally developed to identify SNPs associated with a given phenotype, Wray et al. (2007) first proposed using the results of a GWAS to generate a genetic prediction for a phenotype for an individual in what has become known as a polygenic score. As pointed out by Ware et al. (2017, p. 1), the use of PGSs “has grown rapidly in the social, behavioral, and health sciences” as PGSs, “provide a single summary of measured genetic contribution of that trait that is easily integrated into more mainstream analyses of health and behaviors.”

PGSs are computed by applying the coefficients estimated from a GWAS (the base data) to the SNPs of a different sample (the target data).<sup>7</sup> Specifically, the PGS for individual i and phenotype k is computed as:

$$
P G S _ {i, k} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} W _ {j, k} G _ {i, j},\tag{1}
$$

where $P G S _ { i , k }$ is individual i’s polygenic score for phenotype k, $W _ { j , k }$ is the GWAS weight for phenotype k and SNP j, and $G _ { i , j }$ is the number of minor alleles (0, 1, or 2) for individual i and $\mathrm { { S N P } } j .$ Intuitively, the PGS provides a single genetic propensity estimate for a phenotype from the literally millions of regressions estimated by the GWAS.

Although PGSs are intuitively straightforward, a researcher must make a number of decisions in computing PGSs including (1) whether to include all SNPs or only statistically significant SNPs, and if the latter, at what level of significance; (2) whether to use SNP imputation; and (3) whether, and if so, how to adjust for correlation between SNPs. Directly analogous to a GWAS, a PGS sample can also suffer from population stratification. As a result, databases that include PGSs also include SNP principal components from the PGS sample (rather than the GWAS sample) that should be included in any analysis that includes the PGS.<sup>8</sup> Finally, because PGSs are not perfect estimates of genetic propensities, they suffer from an attenuation bias (see Frost & Thompson, 2000) implying that the estimated relation between a PGS and a phenotype will be biased toward zero (i.e., of finding no relation). Appendix A provides additional details on molecular genetics, GWAS, and PGSs. The present study uses the PGS approach (Act III) due to its advantages over previous approaches.

## Data

We used Health Retirement Study (HRS) data to examine the relationships among genetic endowments, phenotypes, and IT use. HRS is a biennial longitudinal survey of Americans age 50 and older.<sup>9</sup> HRS adds cohorts to the sample over time as individuals enter the age 50+ population. Starting in 2006, HRS selected half the sample for enhanced face-to-face interviews that include a request for a saliva sample (which serves as the raw genetic data). The enhanced face-to-face interviews also include a leave-behind questionnaire that, as detailed below, serves as the data source for both some measures of IT use and some measures of the mediators (e.g., trust). The enhanced faceto-face interview sample is rotated with each wave, e.g., individuals not selected in 2006 are selected in 2008 and 2006 participants are selected again in 2010. Moreover, the enhanced face-to-face sample is updated when new cohorts (e.g., midbaby boomers) are added to the HRS sample. Our sample consists of genetic data for over 11,000 individuals and IT use measures between 2002 and 2016. Details regarding HRS response rates, survey design, and sample design are available at https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/documentation.

## Measures of IT Use

We considered five measures of IT use. First, beginning in 2002, HRS asked individuals, “Do you regularly use the Internet (or World Wide Web) for sending and receiving email or for any other purpose, such as making purchases, searching for information, or making travel reservations?” We gathered this information for all participants who answer this question in any of the eight waves between 2002 and 2016.

Second, beginning in 2008, the leave-behind questionnaires ask “Please tell us HOW OFTEN YOU DO EACH ACTIVITY. (Mark (X) one box for each line.): Use a computer for e-mail, Internet, or other tasks?” Response choices include (1) “daily,” (2) “several times a week,” (3) “once a week,” (4) “several times a month,” (5) “at least once a month,” (6) “not in the last month,” and (7) “never/not relevant.” We coded individuals who answered with one of the first five responses as using a computer and coded those who responded “not in the last month” or “never/not relevant” as not using a computer, i.e., we grouped the last two categories.<sup>10</sup> Our third measure captures how often an individual uses a computer as 7 less the response (e.g., 6 represents daily use of a computer and 1 represents either not in the last month or never/not relevant).

HRS asked about social media usage in the 2014 and 2016 leave-behind questionnaires when inquiring about communicating with (1) children, (2) family, and (3) friends. Specifically, this section asks the respondent if they have children and, if they respond affirmatively, follows up with questions regarding how often they (1) meet up, (2) speak on the phone, (3) write or email, and (4) communicate by Skype, Facebook, or other social media with their children. The potential responses to each set of questions are (1) “three or more times a week,” (2) “once or twice a week,” (3) “once or twice a month,” (4) “every few months,” (5) “once or twice a year,” and (6) “less than once a year or never.” These same sets of questions are repeated for other family members (i.e., “other immediate family, for example, brothers or sisters, parents, cousins, or grandchildren.”) and friends. We coded respondents who answered any of the three (children, other family, friends) social media questions with one of the first five responses (i.e., “communicate by Skype, Facebook, or other social media at least once or twice a year”) as a social media user. Our final measure captures social media intensity. Specifically, for each of the three categories (children, family, and friends), we computed the extent of social media use as 7 less the response score. We then computed the average social media intensity across the three categories (for respondents who provided an answer for at least one of the three categories).

Consistent with the approach used by Venkatesh et al. (2008) to measure use, we treat internet use, computer use, computer frequency, social media use, and social media frequency as separate dependent variables. Appendix B provides additional details for the IT use measures.

## PGSs, Phenotypes, and Predictions

The HRS genetic data (Release 2, April 2018) contains 29 PGSs. We selected six PGSs from three broad categories (Cognition, Health, and Personality) based on evidence that associated phenotypes are related to IT use in older adults (the hypothesized relationships are shown in Figure 2 and Table 1). Specifically, given evidence that greater education and higher cognition are associated with greater IT use (e.g., Barnett et al., 2015; Carpenter & Buday, 2007; Chen & Persson, 2002; Choi & DiNitto, 2013a; Freese et al., 2006; Hamer & Stamatakis, 2014; Konig et al., 2018; Tams, 2022; Werner et al., 2011), we selected the Educational Attainment PGS and the General Cognition PGS and hypothesized that these PGSs positively predict IT use. Given evidence that personality traits such as optimism (e.g., Choi & DiNitto, 2013a; Walczuch et al., 2007), trust (e.g., Gabner-Krauter & Faullant, 2008; Gefen et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2007), and sociability (e.g., Barnett et al., 2015; Carpenter & Buday, 2007; Choi & DiNitto, 2013b; Correa et al., 2010; Devaraj et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2016) are positively associated with IT use, we selected the Neuroticism PGS and the Depressive Symptoms PGS and hypothesized that these PGSs negatively predict IT use. Finally, given evidence that better health is associated with older adults’ IT use (e.g., Carpenter & Buday, 2007; Chen & Persson, 2002; Konig et al., 2018; Seifert et al., 2017; Werner et al., 2011) and the fact that heart disease is the leading cause of death for older Americans, we selected the Myocardial Infarction PGS and the Coronary Artery Disease PGS and hypothesized that these PGSs negatively predict IT use. Although we selected these six PGSs (based on the evidence cited above) for our exploratory analysis, we provide total effect estimates for all 29 PGSs in Appendix C.

As discussed above, the calculation of a PGS requires several decisions, including selecting the specific GWAS upon which to build the PGS. Appendix B reports each GWAS selected by HRS to compute the associated PGSs for HRS participants. Based on Ware et al.’s (2017) investigation of PGS construction choices, HRS computes their PGSs (based on Equation 1) and includes all available SNPs that overlap between HRS (target) genetic data and the GWAS (base data) weights. Finally, as detailed above, HRS provides the first 10 SNP principal components from the HRS sample. Following the practice in genetics research (e.g., Braudt & Harris, 2020; Okbay et al., 2018; Ware et al., 2018), we limited our sample to individuals of European ancestry and included all 10 principal components to control for any remaining population stratification in the HRS data for any regression that includes a PGS. We provide additional details regarding how HRS constructs their PGSs (as well as comparison to PGSs in other large public databases) and principal components in Appendix A.

Given RQ2 and RQ3, we next selected mediators for each of our PGSs. We used realized years of education as the mediator for the Educational Attainment PGS and the HRS measure of cognitive ability (based on a series of 35 questions) for the Cognitive Ability PGS. We use self-rated health to mediate the Myocardial Infarction PGS and the Coronary Artery Disease PGS. We used HRS measures of respondents’ trust, optimism, and sociability (as parallel mediators) to mediate the personality PGSs (Neuroticism PGS and Depressive Symptoms PGS).<sup>11</sup> Appendix B provides construction details for each of these phenotypes.

As noted earlier, these PGSs may predict IT use beyond that captured by the associated phenotype (i.e., have nonzero direct effects) for two non-mutually exclusive reasons. First, the biological data may capture variation in the construct beyond that captured by traditional survey-based measures. For instance, we expected the Coronary Artery Disease PGS to predict IT use because it predicts variation in self-assessed health, and health is positively associated with greater IT use. Nonetheless, the Coronary Artery Disease PGS may capture variation in actual health beyond that captured by the HRS survey-based self-assessed measure of health—leading to a direct effect. Second, although each PGS is constructed for a target trait, the vast majority of genes are pleiotropic— impacting multiple, often seemingly unrelated, phenotypes.<sup>12</sup> Watanabe et al. (2019), for example, reported that 81% of the 9,374 genes they investigated impact more than one trait and the vast majority of these pleiotropic genes (83%) impact traits across domains (e.g., neurological traits and psychiatric traits). As a result, a PGS may predict a target trait (e.g., the Educational Attainment PGS predicts realized educational attainment) as well as nontarget traits (e.g., the Educational Attainment PGS predicts realized height).<sup>13</sup>

The pleiotropic effects may be especially important in driving direct effects for health-related PGSs. For example, the Coronary Artery Disease PGS may predict IT use because it predicts, to some degree, realized health and, as noted above, better health is associated with greater IT use. But the Coronary Artery Disease PGS may also predict IT use because it predicts other factors that influence IT use. For example, Strike and Steptoe (2004) point out that coronary artery disease is strongly related to (p. 337), “chronic stress, depression, socioeconomic status (SES), personality traits, and social support” and posit that (p. 340), “several biological pathways” may underlie these relations. The authors note, for example, that C-reactive protein (a biological trait) is associated with higher coronary artery disease, lower socioeconomic status, sleep problems, malaise, and hostility. Thus, for instance, Coronary Artery Disease PGS may predict IT use, even when controlling for self-assessed health, because it also predicts lower socioeconomic status, sleep problems, malaise, and hostility.

Panel A in Table 1 presents each PGS with the proposed phenotypic mediator(s) and expected sign for IT use. The expected signs for the relation between each PGS and IT use (i.e., the predicted c coefficient) are based on the phenotypic evidence, e.g., given that education is positively associated with IT use, a higher genetic propensity for education should predict greater IT use. The predicted signs of the relation between genetic propensity and the phenotypes (i.e., the predicted a coefficient) are based on the selection of the phenotype, e.g., a higher Educational Attainment PGS should predict higher realized education by construction. The predicted sign for the relation between the phenotype (i.e., the predicted b coefficient) and IT use is based on the evidence linking these variables discussed above. If the phenotype fully mediates the relation between the genetic propensity and IT use, the direct effect (c′) should be zero.<sup>14</sup> Alternatively, as discussed above, if genetic propensity captures either additional variation in the phenotypic mediator (e.g., the Coronary Artery Disease PGS predicts variation in realized health beyond variation in self-reported health) or other phenotypes (pleiotropic effects) that influence IT use, then the direct effect (c′) will have the same sign as the total effect (c).<sup>15</sup>

Specifically, for each wave (i.e., every other year between 2002 and 2016 to account for the increase in internet use over time) and for sex, we sorted all individuals into ventiles (20 groups) by the Educational Attainment PGS. We reaggregated over waves and sex to form a time- and sex-stratified sample for each PGS group (each group contains approximately 3,100 observations). Figure 3 reveals a strong positive relation between the Educational Attainment PGS (literally, variation in the structure of respondents’ DNA) and regular internet use. For instance, only 46% of those in the lowest Educational Attainment PGS group reported regularly using the internet versus 70% of those in the highest Educational Attainment PGS group.

We began our formal analysis by estimating regressions of each IT use measure on the first 10 principal components of the genetic data (to control for any population stratification), control variables that are established at the time of conception— respondent age, gender, and interview wave indicators—and each of the six genetic endowments individually. That is, we examined if the PGSs predict IT use (RQ1):

$$
I T U s e _ {i, n} = \gamma_ {0} + c _ {k, n} P G S _ {i, k} + \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \gamma_ {l} X _ {i, l} + \varepsilon_ {i, n},\tag{2}
$$

where IT $U s e _ { i , n }$ is the measure of IT use for individual i and IT use measure n (e.g., internet use), $P G S _ { i , k }$ is individual $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ PGS for trait k (e.g., Educational Attainment PGS), and $X _ { i , l }$ is the $l ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ control variable (the ten HRS SNP principal components, age, gender, and wave indicators). The coefficient $c _ { k , n }$ is the total effect (see Figure 1) of PGS<sub>k</sub> on IT use measure n.

We then focused on testing how the related phenotypes mediate the relation between the PGSs and IT use (RQ2: Are there meaningful indirect effects?) and if the PGSs predict IT use beyond the associated phenotypes (RQ3: Are there meaningful direct effects?). Specifically, to examine the mediation model detailed in Figure 1, we estimated two additional sets of regressions for each PGS-IT use pair:

$$
M _ {i, 1} = \gamma_ {0} + a _ {k, 1} P G S _ {i, k} + \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \gamma_ {l} X _ {i, l} + \varepsilon_ {i, 1}\tag{3a}
$$

$$
M _ {i, 2} = \gamma_ {0} + a _ {k, 2} P G S _ {i, k} + \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \gamma_ {l} X _ {i, l} + \varepsilon_ {i, 2}\tag{3b}
$$

$$
M _ {i, 3} = \gamma_ {0} + a _ {k, 3} P G S _ {i, k} + \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \gamma_ {l} X _ {i, l} + \varepsilon_ {i, 3}\tag{3c}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} I T U s e _ {i, n} = \gamma_ {0} + c _ {k, n} ^ {\prime} P G S _ {i, k} + b _ {1, n} M _ {i, 1} \\ + b _ {2, n} M _ {i, 2} + b _ {3, n} M _ {i, 3} + \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \gamma_ {l} X _ {i, l} + \varepsilon_ {i, n} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Equation (3) examines whether the PGS predicts each phenotypic mediator M, i.e., estimates the a coefficients in Figure 1. As in Equation (2), $X _ { i , l }$ is the $l ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ control variable. As shown in Table 1, the mediation models for PGSs associated with Educational Attainment, General Cognition, Myocardial Infarction, and Coronary Artery Disease each have a single mediator, while the PGSs associated with Neuroticism and Depressive Symptoms each have three parallel mediators, i.e., the first four PGSs have an Equation (3a) but no Equation (3b) or (3c). Equation (4) estimates the relation between IT use and the PGS when controlling for the phenotypes. Equation (4) is written for the two personality PGSs with three mediators. The other four PGSs have a single mediator (and therefore do not include $M _ { i , 2 }$ or $M _ { i , 3 } )$ . Because Equations (2), (3), and (4) are all linear models, mediation analysis mechanically yields the relation that the total effect (i.e., c from Equation 2) is the sum of the indirect (ab) and direct (c′) effects estimated in Equations (3) and (4).

$$
c = a _ {1} b _ {1} + a _ {2} b _ {2} + a _ {3} b _ {3} + c ^ {\prime}.\tag{5}
$$

Analogous to Equation (4), Equation (5) is written for the two personality PGSs that have three mediators. The cognition and health PGSs are single mediator models and therefore do not include the a<sub>2</sub>b<sub>2</sub> or a<sub>3</sub>b<sub>3</sub> indirect effects (i.e., $c = a _ { I } b _ { I } + c ^ { \prime }$ for these PGSs). Table 3 reports the empirical results for the cognition and health PGSs (i.e., the PGSs where the models include a single mediator).<sup>19</sup> Analogously, Table 4 reports the empirical results for the personality PGSs. Because individuals appear multiple times in the sample for the first three IT use measures (internet use, computer use, and computer frequency) standard errors are clustered at the respondent level for these three metrics when estimating the coefficients in Equations (2)-(5). Because the indirect effects (ab) are not estimated directly (i.e., are the product of the estimated coefficient a in Equation (3) and the estimated coefficient b in Equation (4), we follow Hayes (2018) and compute statistical significance for the indirect effects from bootstrapped confidence intervals based on 10,000 replications.<sup>20</sup>

## Empirical Results

We began by testing whether the PGSs can predict IT use (RQ1). Column 2 in Tables 3 and 4 reports the coefficient associated with the PGS from a regression of IT use on the control variables and each PGS, i.e., the c coefficient from Equation (2). Given the six PGSs and five technology outcome variables, Column 2 in Tables 3 and 4 reports the total effect (c) from a total of 30 regressions. The tests yield strong support for our hypotheses as 28 of the 30 coefficients have the predicted sign (i.e., see Table 1) and 22 of the coefficients are statistically significant (at the 5% level or better) in the hypothesized direction (based on one-tail tests).

For internet use, computer use, and computer frequency, the coefficients associated with every PGS differ meaningfully from zero in the hypothesized direction. That is, the results reveal that every genetic endowment we examine meaningfully predicts internet use, computer use, and the frequency of computer use. Moreover, the effect sizes are meaningful. For example, the coefficient in the top cell of Table 3 reveals that a one-standard-deviation larger Educational Attainment PGS predicts a 6.1% higher likelihood of regular internet use. Analogously, as shown in the first cell of Table 4, a one-standard-deviation higher Neuroticism PGS is associated with a 2.2% lower likelihood of regular internet use.

Although the signs of the coefficients tend to be consistent with our hypotheses, the results for the two social media measures are weaker. Nonetheless, we found that a higher Educational Attainment PGS predicts (statistically significant at the 1% level) that a respondent will be more likely to use social media and a higher General Cognition PGS (statistically significant at the 5% level) predicts that a respondent will use social media more often. Moreover, a genetic predisposition for Depressive Symptoms predicts both that an individual will be less likely to use social media (statistically significant at the 1% level) and that the individual will use social media less often (statistically significant at the 5% level). The remaining columns in each row of Table 3 report the results of the mediation analysis for the cognition and health PGSs (i.e., the PGSs where the models include a single mediator). For example, the first row of Panel A examines how realized years of education mediates the relation between the Educational Attainment PGS and internet use while the bottom row of Panel D examines how respondent’s health mediates the relation between the Coronary Artery Disease PGS and social media frequency. The results in Table 3 provide strong support for our hypotheses. First, consistent with the associated phenotypes mediating the relation between the genotypes and IT use (RQ2), all 20 relations between the PGSs and phenotypes (the a coefficients), all 20 relations between the phenotypes and IT use (i.e., the b coefficients), and all 20 indirect effects (i.e., the product of the a and b coefficients) have the predicted signs (see Table 1) and are statistically significant at the 1% level.<sup>21</sup>

Investigating RQ3, the results in the last column in Table 3 reveal that all four PGSs retain meaningful direct effects (c′) for the first three IT use measures (internet use, computer use, computer frequency). For example, the first row of Panel A reveals that the PGS for Educational Attainment predicts regular internet use even after controlling for actual (i.e., realized) educational attainment. As discussed earlier, this likely occurs for two non-mutually exclusive reasons. First, the biological data (i.e., the PGSs) may capture variation in the construct beyond that captured by traditional survey-based measures. For instance, a person growing up in a poor neighborhood will typically attend a school with fewer resources (i.e., an environmental effect) than a person growing up in a wealthy neighborhood (e.g., Owens, 2018). As a result, one may expect the Educational Attainment PGS, which is not impacted by environmental factors (as it is determined at conception), to maintain substantial explanatory power. Second, because most genes impact multiple traits (i.e., are pleiotropic), a PGS may predict both the target trait as well as nontarget traits. For instance, as discussed above, common biological pathways may drive both coronary artery disease and hostility (Strike & Steptoe, 2004).

In addition, most of the effect sizes are material. Consider the first model in Panel A—a one-standard-deviation higher Educational Attainment PGS predicts a 25%-standarddeviation higher realized education (i.e., the a coefficient divided by the standard deviation of years of education; 0.616/2.480). Years of education is strongly related to internet use: an additional year of education is associated with a 6.7% higher likelihood of regular internet use (i.e., the b coefficient). Thus, years of education mediates about two thirds of the relation between the Educational Attainment PGS and internet use (i.e., ab/c=0.67). Even after controlling for realized years of education, however, a one-standarddeviation higher Educational Attainment PGS predicts a 2% higher likelihood of regular internet use (i.e., the direct effect, c′) accounting for one third of the total effect (i.e., c′/c=0.33).

Columns 3-12 in Table 4 present the mediation analysis for the personality PGSs. Specifically, Panel A presents a mediation analysis for the relation between the Neuroticism PGS and each of the five IT use metrics. As noted in Table 1, we employed three parallel mediators—trust, sociability, and optimism—for both personality PGSs. Once again, the results are consistent with our hypothesis that these phenotypes mediate the relation between the personality PGSs and IT use, as all 30 a coefficients (Columns 3-5), 26 of 30 b coefficients (Columns 6-8), and 26 of 30 indirect effects (i.e., ab coefficients reported in Columns 9-11) have the predicted sign (see Table 1) and differ materially from zero. We found little evidence of meaningful direct effects for the Neuroticism PGS and the Depressive Symptoms PGS.

Once again, the effect sizes are material. For example, the results in Columns 3-5 in the first row reveal that a onestandard-deviation higher Neuroticism PGS predicts a 9.8%- standard-deviation lower level of trust (i.e., -0.098/0.998), a 4.8%-standard-deviation lower level of sociability (i.e., - 0.045/0.939), and an 11.3%-standard-deviation lower level of optimism (i.e., -0.101/0.894). Similarly, the results in Columns 6-8 in the first row reveal that a one-standarddeviation higher level of trust, sociability, or optimism is associated with a 3.0%, 12.5%, and 6.2% higher likelihood of regular internet use.<sup>22</sup> As shown in Column 2, a one-standarddeviation higher Neuroticism PGS is associated with 2.2% lower likelihood of regular internet use—partitioned into 1.6% from the indirect effects (i.e., the sum of the three ab values) and 0.6% from direct effects (i.e., the c′ value reported in the final column).

In sum, the results in Tables 3 and 4 provide strong support for all three research questions. Specifically, supporting RQ1, Column 2 in Tables 3 and 4 demonstrate that polygenic scores associated with cognition, health, and personality predict heterogeneity (in the hypothesized direction) in IT use more than half a century later. The results in the remaining columns also provide substantial support for RQ2—realized education mediates the relation between the Educational Attainment PGS and IT use, measured cognitive ability mediates the relation between the General Cognition PGS and IT use, respondents’ health mediates the relation between IT use and both the Myocardial Infraction and Coronary Artery Disease PGSs, and respondent’s trust, sociability, and optimism mediate the relation between IT use and the Neuroticism and Depressive Symptoms PGSs. Finally, the results provide substantial support for RQ3, as we found strong evidence of direct effects for the PGSs associated with Educational Attainment, General Cognition, Myocardial Infarction, and Coronary Artery Disease.

## Discussion

Although research suggests that heterogeneity in phenotypes related to education, cognition, health, and personality characteristics contribute to heterogeneity in IT use, most prior IS research does not directly consider the role of genetics. At a fundamental level, variation in any human phenotype results from heterogeneity in genetics, heterogeneity in life experience, or the interaction of genetics and environment. Although scientists have long been able to estimate phenotype heritability via twin studies, advances in molecular genetics over the last decade now allow scientists to use “a single summary of measured genetic contribution of a trait that is easily integrated into more mainstream analyses of health and behaviors” (Ware et al., 2017)—the polygenic score.

Our exploratory study has several important implications. PGSs associated with education, cognition, health, and personality predict IT use more than half a century later. This demonstrates both (1) the potential to use the PGS approach to investigate topics of interest in IS, and (2) the important role of genetics in explaining variation in IT use (RQ1).

We also demonstrate substantial indirect effects for all the PGSs we examine (RQ2). These results suggest that genetics play a substantial role in driving the relation between each of the phenotypes we examine (e.g., realized educational attainment, realized health, realized personality characteristics) and IT use. In short, these indirect effects help explain why variation in these PGSs explains variation in IT use. Thus, a researcher with access to a respondent’s PGS or underlying genetic data can use a PGS to proxy for a phenotype. For example, one could proxy for an individual’s general cognition with their General Cognition PGS without the need to directly estimate the individual’s general cognition. In addition, the PGS could be used when phenotypic data is yet unknown. For instance, if a researcher is using college students for experimental work, final educational attainment is, by definition, unknown. Thus, the Educational Attainment PGS may proxy for the educational attainment phenotype. More broadly, a researcher with access to a sample’s underlying genetics (i.e., SNPs) could create a

PGS for any trait for which a GWAS has been estimated (Harden & Koellinger, 2020). For example, a researcher could estimate personality dimensions such as Neuroticism or Depressive Symptoms from the respondents’ genetic data.

We also found substantial direct effects for the Educational Attainment PGS, the General Cognition PGS, and both health PGSs (i.e., Myocardial Infarction and Coronary Artery Disease) (RQ3), demonstrating that the genetic endowments capture qualities beyond the associated phenotype. For example, the Educational Attainment PGS predicts IT use even when controlling for realized educational attainment. The results imply that genetics can help explain additional variation in IT use beyond that captured by the associated phenotype. As noted above, this likely occurs for two nonmutually exclusive reasons. First, adding biological data can improve the measure of the underlying construct. For example, the Educational Attainment PGS may capture, to some extent, differences in IT use related to educational aptitude rather than differences in realized educational opportunities (that is a function of both environment and genetics). Regardless, the results suggest the relation between IT use and realized educational attainment is not fully due to the effects of education.

Second, the PGS may predict IT use, in part, because the gene variants they capture impact other phenotypes. For example, the ability of the Coronary Artery Disease PGS to predict IT use even after accounting for realized health may arise because some of the gene variants that increase the risk of coronary artery disease also predict socioeconomic status, hostility, isolation, risk aversion, or other factors that influence IT use. The fact that we found meaningful direct effects suggests that the link between health and IT use may be more complicated than traditionally interpreted. The indirect effects suggest, consistent with traditional interpretation, that poorer health leads to lower IT use. The direct effect, however, is consistent with the premise that other factors influence both health and IT use. That is, the direct effect adds to our understanding beyond the phenotypic evidence. As a practical implication, therefore, our results suggest that it may be harder to use IT to help solve health issues (versus other issues) as the direct effect suggests unhealthier respondents are already less likely to use IT. Regardless, consistent with our overall research question, our analysis suggests that the PGS approach adds an exciting new dimension to NeuroIS (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012; Riedl et al., 2010; Riedl et al., 2020) toolbox.

Our results also provide important insights into computer usage for older adults (e.g., Tams, 2022). For example, Cotten et al. (2012) conclude that the positive relation between mental well-being and internet use for older adults “indicate[s] a positive contribution of internet use to mental well-being of retired older adults (50 years), reducing depression categorization by approximately 20-28%” (p. 496). Our results reveal, however, that a genetic endowment associated with Depressive Symptoms predicts lower internet use. That is, part of the positive relationship between mental well-being and internet use for older adults likely results from depression causing lower internet use rather than lower internet use contributing to depression.

## Implications for Research

Our results have several implications for research. Most important, supporting our overall research question (RQ-O), we demonstrate that the PGS approach can be applied to investigate the role of genetics in IS research. Specifically, a growing number of large databases (e.g., the Health and Retirement Study dataset, the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study dataset, the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health) now collect primary genetic data and distribute respondent PGSs. Moreover, this practice is likely to increase over time with other datasets as the PGS approach has come to dominate genetics research in the social sciences (e.g., Duncan et al., 2019) and genotyping costs continue to fall.

In addition, as pointed out by Ware et al. (2017), tools are already in use that allow researchers to work with primary genetic data (i.e., combine SNPs for their sample with extant GWAS base data weights) to generate PGSs. As noted above, Harden and Koellinger (2020) point out that a researcher with access to primary genetic data (i.e., SNPs) could create a PGS for any trait for which a GWAS has been estimated.<sup>23</sup> Currently, the primary limitation is the cost—using the genotyping employed by HRS in their data costs approximately \$250 per person although prices are expected to fall dramatically over time.<sup>24</sup>

PGSs have the potential to enhance understanding of a multitude of IS issues including questions of causation. Specifically, because genetic endowments are established at conception, technological outcomes (e.g., internet use) cannot cause changes in an individual’s DNA. For instance, as noted above, our analysis suggests that at least part of the positive relationship between mental well-being and internet use for older adults results from the fact that a higher genetic endowment for Depressive Symptoms predicts lower internet use.

Our analysis also provides support for models that suggest heterogeneity in behaviors (such as IT use) should have a genetic component. For instance, a series of papers (Brennan & Lo, 2011, 2012; Zhang et al., 2014a, 2014b) suggests that genetic heterogeneity in behavior can be optimal for species survival while suboptimal for an individual as behavioral heterogeneity can help ensure species survival during rare events. That is, a given individual may act objectively irrationally (e.g., failing to use a technology when the person would objectively be better off by using it) because such genetic-induced behavioral heterogeneity helps ensure species survival. This is consistent with, for example, research examining how people cope with the introduction of technology (e.g., Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005, 2010), which highlights that different individuals can respond to the same technology in very different ways. Our work lays the biological foundation for understanding why some people actively engage with technology and others actively resist it (e.g., Bhattacherjee et al., 2017; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005).

Although our focus is on IT use in older adults, almost all issues of interest to IS researchers that involve decisions and choices are likely related to genetics. Thus, we outline three broad research questions where genetics may provide helpful insights. First, the relation between genetics and outcomes has the potential to generate customized training programs leading to greater success for IS-related outcomes. That is, directly analogous to using an individual’s unique genome to guide medical treatment (known as “personalized” or “precision” medicine), IS training and education could be customized based on one’s genome. For example, recent research (Siponen et al., 2020) suggests that training based on cognitive dissonance theory can improve workers’ adherence to password security policy. It is likely, however, that the specific training needed for an employee whose resistance to adhering to password security policy is associated with a low Educational Attainment PGS will likely differ from that needed for an employee whose resistance is associated with a high Neuroticism PGS. Thus, we offer the following as a potential research question: How can genetics be leveraged to design meaningful training programs?

Along these lines, PGSs can provide important information regarding variation in response to an intervention aimed at generating behavioral change. For example, Li et al. (2021) investigate peer-information intervention executed through a learning management system (LMS) to reduce student procrastination. The authors found that the intervention (e.g., a reminder that “Assignment 3 is due in 4 days. X% of students in your group have started working on this assignment”) results in a material decrease in procrastination for males (but not for females). More than 90% of the procrastination, however, remains unexplained (see their Table 6, Model 2). Given that differences in response to interventions have only three possible sources—differences in environments, differences in genes, or differences in the interaction between environment and genes—adding genetic data to the analysis is likely to explain why some individuals respond to the intervention and others do not. Thus, we offer the following as a potential research question: How can genetics be leveraged to design interventions that will influence behavior change?

Third, with online misinformation spreading rapidly, there is an increased need to understand what leads people to believe in and share this information. Dennis and colleagues (e.g., Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Moravec et al., 2019) have demonstrated the significance of confirmation bias in this phenomenon. However, recent research has demonstrated that confirmation bias alone is insufficient to explain the spread of misinformation (Suntwal et al., 2020). Further, Kitchens et al. (2020) found that differences in individuals’ choices of news consumption (e.g., highly partisan or moderate) are associated with the social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, and Reddit) they used. Both choices, viewed as information search and sourcing, could be influenced by genetics (e.g., Browne & Walden, 2020). Thus, we offer the following as a potential research question: How can genetics be leveraged to better understand the propensity for people to believe in and share online misinformation?

More broadly, it will also be important for future IS research to examine gene-environment interactions (typically denoted G×E effects). That is, as pointed out by Freese (2018 p. 527), “Evidence is clear that similar experiences affect different people differently, and this heterogeneity far exceeds anything that conventional social science can explain.” For example, Papageorge and Thom (2020) found that the Educational Attainment PGS better predicts college graduation for individuals from high socioeconomic status backgrounds than for those from low socioeconomic status backgrounds. Thus, the results reflect the fact that the impact of genetics is a function of the environment. Simply, in the words of Harden (2021, p. 50), “Studying the genetic lottery reveals the importance of the social lottery.”

## Implications for Practice

The value of genetic data in practice is immense. One need look no further than the recent (August 2020) acquisition of Ancestry.com for \$4.7 billion or the current (2021) proposed buyout/SPAC of 23andMe for \$3.5 billion. For example, a number of companies are using customers’ DNA to provide customized recommendations for goods such as food or music.<sup>25</sup> In addition, several companies have begun commercializing PGSs. For example, customers purchasing a 23andMe health report receive their PGS for type 2 diabetes (Molteni, 2019). Similarly, Karamgenes.co uses “a polygenic approach” to create a custom “psychometric assessment.” Lifeview.com offers preimplantation PGSs for embryos including, for example, PGSs for diabetes, coronary artery disease, and schizophrenia, “to help you and your physician prioritize embryos for transfer.”

Browne and Walden (2020) also point out that genetic data has value to IS designers by linking genetic data to geographical areas. Simply knowing that individuals in a given geographical area are more likely to exhibit a genetic predisposition is sufficient to adjust information and interface in much the same manner that user interface and experience design currently use geographical information. Similarly, Daviet et al. (2022) point out that genetic data can be used to segment and target consumers and give the example that a brand manager for a product that reduces hair loss could be marketed to men who exhibit a genetic predisposition for male pattern baldness. The authors further point out that a firm with access to underlying SNPs could create PGSs to target consumers when the associated phenotype may be hard to observe at scale (e.g., Depressive Symptoms).

In addition, identifying a link between genetic endowments and IS outcomes implies that a one-size-fits-all intervention program is likely suboptimal. As noted above, for example, recognizing that differences in genetic endowments may influence the differences in the reason why employees fail to adhere to password security protocols suggests that password security intervention should either be customized (based on the individual’s reason for nonadherence) or multidimensional (e.g., addressing both those whose nonadherence arises, at least in part, from genetic endowments associated with Educational Attainment and those whose nonadherence arises, at least in part, from genetic endowments associated with Neuroticism).

Similarly, evidence of gene-environment interactions can provide important insights for IS in practice. For instance, Bapna and Funk (2021) found that a simple intervention to reduce search costs (i.e., an email providing contact information for recommended connections at a professional conference) greatly increased the number of new contacts for women by 57% and the time spent talking with new contacts by 90%. Yet, it is possible that genetics and environment play a role in the outcome. That is, analogous to the evidence discussed above (Papageorge & Thom, 2020) regarding the relation between the Educational Attainment PGS, socioeconomic status, and college graduation, it is possible, for example, that the intervention is limited to those with both outgoing personalities (e.g., low Neuroticism PGS) and high socioeconomic status background, i.e., those who won both the genetic lottery and the social lottery. Evidence of such relations should lead to the development of tools to improve networking opportunities for women more broadly.

Finally, although beyond the scope of this study, there are important ethical issues associated with the use of genetic data (see, for example, the discussion in Daviet et al., 2022) that will need to be addressed going forward. A recent Washington Post article (Conley, 2021) focuses on the “Pandora’s Box of ethical issues” associated with PGSs.

## Limitations

Our study has a few limitations. First, because we rely on an established secondary dataset (the HRS data), our analysis is limited to PGSs, phenotypic mediators, and IT use variables that are included in the HRS data. There are, of course, other relevant genetic, phenotypic, and IT use measures that we could not investigate because we did not collect primary data. An interesting direction for future work will be to combine genetic data with primary survey data to develop a deeper understanding of the genetic determinants of IT use and other IS constructs. Second, as noted above, our sample is limited to European ancestry individuals, as the genetics literature suggests that PGSs not be used across different ancestry groups. In fact, the focus on European ancestry in genetics research (largely attributed to the fact that European ancestry groups have been genotyped to a much greater degree than other groups) is a major concern for genetics research (e.g., Mills & Rahal, 2019). Based on this, we expect that future genetics research will be focused on other ancestries, which may shed additional light on IS questions. Third, because some of the IT use measures and phenotypes were collected at the same point in time with the same instrument, there is the potential for common methods bias to influence our results. However, concerns are mitigated because the genetic data are biological and many of the variables are objective (e.g., years of education). Fourth, much of the IS adoption and use literature that helps frame our research questions arises from IT use in organizations. In contrast, our IT use measures focus on personal use. Note, however, that some prior work (e.g., Brown & Venkatesh, 2005; Venkatesh & Brown, 2001) has found results consistent with workplace models, even in the household setting. Fifth, although most of our results are statistically meaningful, the effect sizes associated with genotypes tend to be smaller than the effect sizes associated with phenotypes, as phenotypes capture both genetic and environmental factors.

An important question regarding PGSs is causation. Although it is true that an individual’s molecular genetics is determined at conception (and therefore are not impacted by environment), phenotypes are the result of both genetics and environment. As a result, the implications of a person’s genetics will depend on the environment. As Meyer et al. (2020) point out, “In other words, genetic association studies like ours turn up variants that we can only be sure are relevant in the studied environment” (emphasis in original). Consider, for example, that evidence suggests that taller individuals have social advantages in the U.S. leading to better employment opportunities and wealth (Persico et al., 2004). Thus, a PGS for height may predict wealth because it reflects the environment (i.e., social advantages associated with height in the U.S.) rather than immutable biological differences. An important direction for future work will be to tease out the various alternative explanations to better understand the contributions of genetics, the environment, and the interaction between genetics and the environment.

## Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that the six genetic endowments we investigate predict IT use more than half a century later. Moreover, these endowments play an important role in driving previously identified relationships between phenotypes and IT use. For four of the six genetic propensities, we found that the PGS explains variation beyond the associated phenotype (i.e., direct effects in our mediation analysis). In addition to understanding the underlying origins of IT use, our work suggests a promising line of future inquiry into a multitude of IS topics as PGSs become widely available on large public use datasets and as genotyping costs continue to fall (allowing for PGSs on primary genetic data). As Freese (2018, p. 525) points out, “the most important fact about polygenic scores is that they work” (emphasis in original).

## Genome-Wide Association Studies

Given the high polygenicity (i.e., many SNPs from all over the genome contribute to most phenotypes) and low penetrance (i.e., the contribution from each individual SNP is very small), GWASs take an atheoretical approach to examining the relation between the phenotype and all SNPs for which they have data. Moreover, because effect sizes are small, GWASs need very large samples to have enough power to identify meaningful relations. As a result, most GWASs are consortia of universities and institutes. Because privacy laws and/or IRB rules often prevent sharing of genetic data, consortia members estimate coefficients for their own data and then submit the resulting coefficient estimates to the consortium for a metaanalysis that determines the consortia GWAS weights and results.

To ensure the analysis is identical across consortia participants, GWASs preregister a plan. For example, the preregistration plan for the Okbay et al. (2016) Educational Attainment GWAS which serves as the basis for the HRS Educational Attainment PGS is available here: https://osf.io/paj9m/. Note that the plan defines the sample inclusion (e.g., “assessed for educational attainment at or after 30 years of age”), trait definitions, and a model for each sample. For example, each consortium member in the Educational Attainment GWAS estimates the following regression for each SNP j for which they have data (see Okbay et al., 2016 supplemental material):

$$
\text { Years   of   Education } _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S N P _ {j} + P C \gamma + B \alpha + X \theta + \varepsilon_ {i},\tag{A.1}
$$

where PC is the vector of the first 10 SNP principal components from the consortium member’s sample, B is a vector of controls (a third-order polynomial in age, gender indicator, and interactions between the two), and X is a vector of study-specific controls including wars or policy change that many have affected educational access. The Educational Attainment GWAS consortium consists of 64 members.

Note that Equation (A.1) is estimated for each SNP individually. GWASs must take this approach because the number of SNPs (explanatory variables) is much greater than the number of observations (i.e., human genome samples). For example, the Educational Attainment GWAS referenced above is based on a sample of just under 400,000 individuals (number of observations of years of education) and 9.3 million SNPs (explanatory variables per person).

There are a number of additional decisions regarding the execution of a GWAS. First, mapping each individual’s entire genome is prohibitively expensive. As a result, the GWAS genotypes a portion of the participant’s DNA via a commercially provided beadchip array that genotypes a subsample of SNPs, e.g., the Illumina Infinium Omni2.5-8 has genotyped approximately 2.4 million SNPs. The number of and which specific SNPs are genotypes varies across beadchips and manufacturers. GWASs use the correlation between SNPs (known as linkage disequilibrium) to infer (known as imputing) closely related SNPs to (1) generate a larger number of SNPs for evaluation and (2) harmonize SNPs across consortium members using different commercial beadchips. In addition, most GWASs eliminate rare variants although the specific criteria can vary. A common is exclusion is SNPs with a MAF (minor allele frequency) of less than 5%.

Because SNP frequencies vary across populations, most GWASs are ancestry specific.<sup>27</sup> Even within a given ancestry group, however, population stratification can lead to false-positive identification of SNPs (known as confounding). Specifically, population stratification arises from nonrandom mating (usually due to geography) within the population being examined. For example, if one were to estimate a GWAS for the amount of pasta consumed in a European ancestry GWAS, any SNP more common in Italian genomes would be associated with pasta consumption (Statista reports that per capita pasta consumption for Italy is more than twice that of any other country in Europe). GWASs typically handle this issue by including the first 10 SNP principa components for the sample (as in Equation B.1 above). Because samples can differ and because the GWAS consortium setup does not allow for the sharing of raw genetic data, each GWAS contributor computes the principal components for their sample and includes their PCs in Equation (A.1). Bush and Moore (2012) describe the process as, “When used as covariates, these [principal component] scores adjust for minute ancestry effects in the data.”

## Polygenic Scores

Polygenic scores (typically denoted PGSs; also known as genetic risk scores or polygenic risk scores—PRSs) are effectively a point estimate of all the information from a GWAS, i.e., a single estimate of genetic propensity for a phenotype. PGSs are the only method in genetics that provide a measure of genetic proclivity for a given individual (see Choi et al., 2020). Computing a PGS is theoretically straightforward—the GWAS-estimated SNP size effects are used in a different sample to generate a single genetic predictive score for a phenotype that can be easily integrated into standard methods. Thus, a PGS needs both the associated GWAS effect sizes (known as base data) and genetic data (i.e., SNPs) for the PGS sample (known as target data). Specifically, as detailed in Equation (1), the PGS is estimated (Braudt & Harris, 2020; Okbay et al., 2018; Ware et al., 2018) as the sum of the products of the GWAS weight for each SNP and the respondent’s number of minor alleles at that SNP.

To compute a PGS, a researcher first needs the target sample’s SNPs. Thus, the process begins by genotyping the sample. As noted above, however, beadchips vary both across manufacturers and within manufacturers (e.g., a beadchip that has typed more SNPs is, naturally, more expensive). HRS has used Illumina HumanOmni2.5 BeadChips (versions 4v1, 8v1, and 8v1.1), which have genotyped approximately 2.4 million SNPs, of which approximately 1.9 million have passed quality control restrictions. The National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health dataset (Braudt & Harris, 2020) has used the Illumina Omni1-Quad BeadChip for 80% of their sample (approximately 1.1 million SNPs) and the Illumina Omni2.5-Quad BeadChip for 20% of their sample. The Wisconsin Longitudinal Study has used the Illumina Human Omni Express BeadChip, which has typed approximately 710,000 SNPs.

As noted in our study, once the target sample is genotyped, researchers must make a number of decisions regarding the calculation of the PGS. First, researchers much decide whether to include all SNPs or only SNPs identified as statistically significant by the GWAS, and if the latter, at what level of significance. Recent research suggests that including all SNPs yields better measures (e.g., Choi et al., 2020; Ware, et al., 2017). As a result, HRS, the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health and the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study use all SNPs for which they have data when computing the PGSs for their datasets. Second, as detailed in the GWAS discussion above, nearby SNPs on the same chromosome can exhibit strong correlation allowing for researchers to impute target sample SNPs to increase the number of SNPs used to calculate the PGS. HRS and the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health both compute PGSs from genotyped SNPs only; the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study computes PGSs from both imputed and genotyped SNPs.<sup>28</sup> Third, the researcher must decide whether to account for the correlation (linkage disequilibrium) between SNPs, and if so, how. For example, clumping and thresholding (see Privé et al., 2019), where one examines the correlation and p-values between physically nearby SNPs to determine which are included, or a Bayesian approach that includes all measured SNPs but weighted by an adjusted effect size. Based on evidence from Ware et al. (2017), HRS computes PGSs without adjusting for linkage disequilibrium. The National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health also computes PGSs without linkage disequilibrium adjustments. In contrast, the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study adjusts for linkage disequilibrium using a Bayesian approach.

As with a GWAS, even when limited to a given ancestry (e.g., European), the target sample may exhibit population stratification. As a result, it is standard practice to include the first 10 principal components estimated from the target dataset SNPs when using PGSs. For example, the HRS documentation notes (Ware et al., 2018), “To control for confounding from population stratification, or to account for any ancestry differences in genetic structures within populations that could bias estimates, we highly recommend that users perform analyses separately by ancestral group and, at the very least, adjust for PCs 1-5.” The HRS, the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health, and Wisconsin Longitudinal Study PGS datasets all include the first 10 SNP principal components computed from their respective samples. As an example of a specific PGS, HRS computes the Educational Attainment PGS based on 1,309,267 SNPs that overlap with the Okbay et al. (2016) Educational Attainment GWAS. As noted above, the HRS target sample uses a beadchip that has genotyped approximately 2.4 million SNPs, of which approximately 1.9 million have passed quality control procedures. After eliminating SNPs excluded by the GWAS due to low minor allele frequency (< 5%) and ambiguous SNPs, the target (HRS) dataset overlaps with the base (GWAS) dataset by 1.3 million SNPs.<sup>29</sup>

## Other HRS PGS

As detailed in our study, we focus on six PGSs based on evidence relating associated phenotypes to IT use. The HRS polygenic score dataset (Version 2), however, contains 29 PGSs. The two Alzheimer’s Disease PGSs differ in inclusion or exclusion of a specific gene variant (APOE). Empirically, however, the two Alzheimer’s disease PGSs are essentially identical, with correlation greater than 99.9%. In addition, two of the PGSs (Age of Menarche and Age of Menopause) are for females only (and therefore we do not include a gender indicator for the associated regressions). Last, the PGS documentation (Ware et al., 2018) does not include the Antisocial Behavior PGS. Per conversations with HRS, this was simply an oversight in putting together the documentation.
