## Abstract

Studies claiming to use the Grounded theory methodology (GTM) have been quite prevalent in information systems (IS) literature. A cursory review of this literature reveals conflict in the understanding of GTM, with a variety of grounded theory approaches apparent. The purpose of this investigation was to establish what alternative grounded theory approaches have been employed in IS, and to what extent each has been used. In order to accomplish this goal, a comprehensive set of IS articles that claimed to have followed a grounded theory approach were reviewed. The articles chosen were those published in the widely acknowledged top eight IS-centric journals, since these journals most closely represent exemplar IS research. Articles for the period 1985-2008 were examined. The analysis revealed four main grounded theory approaches in use, namely (1) the classic grounded theory approach, (2) the evolved grounded theory approach, (3) the use of the grounded theory approach as part of a mixed methodology, and (4) the application of grounded theory techniques, typically for data analysis purposes. The latter has been the most common approach in IS research. The classic approach was the least often employed, with many studies opting for an evolved or mixed method approach. These and other findings are discussed and implications drawn. European Journal of Information Systems (2013) 22, 119–129. doi:10.1057/ejis.2011.35; published online 30 August 2011

Keywords: grounded theory methodology; IS research methodology

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) as a discipline has mainly relied on adapting theory from its cognate disciplines, such as social science and computer science (Gregor, 2006; Truex et al, 2006). This reliance on theory from reference disciplines has partly contributed to the characterization of IS as a fragmented adhocracy (Banville & Landry, 1989; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Furthermore, the problems that arise from the IS domain sometimes deviate sharply from known theorized areas in the cognate disciplines. Borrowing theory also risks ‘repeating mistakes made, debated, and dealt [with] within the original disciplines’ discourse; misinterpreting the underlying notions about the nature of reality and how knowledge is acquired that are implicit in the theory and the methodological implications those assumptions imply; and wasting time and effort by not adding value to the cumulative tradition in our own field’ (Truex et al, 2006).

Grounded theory methodology (GTM) provides IS researchers with a means to build theory relevant to the discipline. Lyytinen & King (2004) note that building theory for the field would help strengthen it. In the IS discipline opportunities exist to build theory in both emerging fields and well-researched ones. As Glaser (1992) states, in well-researched domains, new concepts and categories may not emerge, but GTM will still contribute to a better understanding of the basic social processes at play. The rise in IS studies claiming to use GTM shows that concerns about the development of IS theory are being considered. However, there is still limited understanding of the methodology and its application, hence the need for a study to clarify these issues.

A grounded theory is defined as theory which has been ‘systematically obtained through social research and is grounded in data’ (Goulding, 1998). Bryant (2002) draws a clear distinction between grounded theory – the outcome of research, and the grounded theory method, methodology, or approach – the process by which a grounded theory is produced through research. Many authors refer to having used ‘grounded theory’ in their studies, when in fact what they mean is that they have employed a grounded theory approach (Bryant, 2002). In some cases, the label ‘grounded theory’ is ‘often used as rhetorical sleight of hand by authors who are unfamiliar with qualitative research and who wish to avoid close description or illumination of their methods’ (Suddaby, 2006). The aim of this study is to shed light on grounded theory approaches used in IS research.

In the next section, the key general principles and procedures of GTM are broadly outlined. Four common grounded theory approaches are then delineated. Following that, some pertinent issues and misconceptions around the epistemological basis of GTM research are clarified. After this clarification, the research method employed in this study is explained before the results are reported and analysed. Following a discussion of the results, the paper is concluded.

## GTM, principles and coding procedures

GTM was first proposed by Glaser & Strauss (1967). A methodology encompasses a set of principles, together with methods and techniques to be employed in accordance with these principles (Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Checkland, 2002). The key principles, procedures and techniques to be applied in deriving grounded theory are articulated in Glaser & Strauss (1967), Glaser (1992) and other monographs by Glaser. Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) offer an alternative rendition of GTM. A broad overview of GTM drawing from all of these sources will initially be presented before a distinction is made between alternative grounded theory approaches. Three key principles that distinguish GTM are the principle of emergence, constant comparative analysis and theoretical sampling (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## The principle of emergence

The emergence principle of GTM holds that both the outcome (grounded theory) and the research design process should be emergent (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Researchers should avoid imposing any pre-conceived ideas and notions about the phenomenon under investigation on the data (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Glaser,

1992). Granted, it is not possible to ignore or pretend not to know what is already ‘known’ (Butler & O’Reilly, 2010). However, in order to allow for the emergence of theory from data, any pre-conceptions should be held at bay (Locke, 2001), or at the very least declared in advance. The emerging theory should be faithful to the persons being researched, faithful to context and be used to discover enduring frameworks (Duchscher & Morgan, 2004). Concerning the research design process a predefined, detailed research design and sample would inhibit the emergent nature of the grounded theory process (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

## Constant comparative analysis

Constant comparison is the major strategy used in discovering grounded theory. In this process, data are broken down into incidents, and these compared for similarities and differences (Glaser, 1992). The aim is to assign a common meaning to multiple data incidents (Locke, 2001). As concepts emerge and are named they are compared to other incidents in data, leading to clearer definition of properties of the concepts (Glaser, 1992). Finally concepts are compared to other concepts (Glaser & Holton, 2004). As such, there is a constant iteration between naming and comparing data incident to data incident, data incident to concept, and concept to concept (Locke, 2001; Glaser & Holton, 2004).

## Theoretical sampling

Theoretical sampling has been succinctly described as sampling directed by theory, as compared to purposeful sampling, which is typically pre-determined before the start of a study (Goulding, 1998). With theoretical sampling, the decision as to where and when to sample, and the size of the sample should be determined by the emerging theory, and should continue until each category identified in the theory is saturated (Glaser, 1992).

## Coding procedures

Coding represents ‘the analytic processes through which data are fractured, conceptualized, and integrated to form theory’ (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Glaser & Strauss (1967) and Glaser (1992) make mention of open, selective and theoretical coding, while Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) suggest three types of coding activities – open, axial and selective respectively. Coding for process has been mentioned as an additional phase (Urquhart et al, 2010). The coding phases are in practice performed iteratively, where the researcher moves between stages as demanded by the investigation.

Open coding Open coding is ‘the analytic process through which concepts and categories are identified and their properties and dimensions are discovered in data’ (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Naming, comparing and the writing of memos are the key activities that take place in this phase (Locke, 2001). Concepts are the basic building blocks of theory, and so the first task in developing them is to open up the data to reveal and name these concepts (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The constant comparative method is the means used to achieve this, whereby comparing similarities and differences between data incidents yields discrete concepts, which are then given names (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Where the names given are sourced directly from the data, they are referred to as ‘in vivo’ codes (Strauss & Corbin, 1990, 1998). Glaser (1992) suggests that naming of concepts be done using either ‘in vivo’ words, or by using sociological constructs derived from an awareness and familiarity with the sociological literature.

Axial coding Axial coding is defined as ‘the process of relating categories to their sub-categories, termed “axial” because coding occurs around the axis of a category, linking categories at the level of properties and dimensions’ (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). A sub-category, in this instance, is subordinate to a main category or phenomenon, in that it answers questions such as who, where, when, why and how about that category (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

In axial coding, Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) suggest the use of a paradigm model to identify how a category relates to its sub-categories. Such a model helps to integrate structure (the conditional context in which a phenomenon is situated), with process (sequences of action/interaction pertaining to a phenomenon) (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The paradigm model advocated by Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) has the following structure – concerning any one phenomenon (main category), routine or strategic actions/interactions give rise to certain consequences. Causal conditions are those categories that have an influence on the phenomenon, and thus on the actions/interaction of subjects, while intervening conditions limit the impact of causal conditions on the phenomenon. Glaser (1992) defines a contextual condition as ‘a condition of overriding scope, under which a set of related categories and properties occur’.

Usually coding proceeds until theoretical saturation is achieved, whereby in gathering additional data, nothing substantially new emerges. The theory that is formed represents the aggregate of data collected, rather than the view and perspective of only one data source (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

Selective coding Glaser (1992) elucidates the meaning of selective coding by noting that ‘to selectively code is to cease open coding and to delimit coding to only those variables that relate to the core variable, in sufficiently significant ways to be used in a parsimonious theory’. Part of the process of selective coding is the identification of a core category (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Such a category must be central, in that all other major categories relate to it, and that with almost all cases there are indicators pointing to it. It should also be able to explain variation, and even contradicting evidence (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The final theory may be represented as a set of propositions or as a running theoretical discussion (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

Theoretical coding Theoretical coding is a more complex process involving the conceptualizing of codes from selective coding and exploring how they can possibly be integrated into sound theory using constant comparative analysis (Glaser & Holton, 2004). The arising substantive codes form the basis for theoretical coding. Glaser (1978) identified 18 theoretical coding families into which emerging concepts could fit. The theoretical codes are not absolute and the researcher, where necessary, may generate additional code families as encouraged by Glaser (1992).

## GTM and types of data

GTM is perceived by some researchers to be a qualitative research approach (Myers, 1997). This has led to its data analysis techniques being closely linked to qualitative inquiry – in particular the techniques of open, axial and selective coding. The widespread use of these techniques demonstrates their utility for qualitative analysis. An undesirable consequence is that inappropriate criteria for judging the credibility of GTM studies have been applied (Skodol-Wilson & Ambler-Hutchinson, 1996; Glaser & Holton, 2004). Glaser differentiates between qualitative research and qualitative analysis, states Van Niekerk & Roode (2009). Qualitative analysis, it is asserted, is any type of analysis, as in GTM, that arrives at findings, concepts or theory without the use of statistical methods (Van Niekerk & Roode, 2009). Such qualitative analysis techniques may be applied to any type of data whether qualitative or quantitative. In GTM studies, as Glaser & Holton (2004) point out, ‘all is data’, implying the methodology can use any data whether qualitative or quantitative. Glaser (2008) outlines how grounded theory can be developed from quantitative data.

## Alternative grounded theory approaches

GTM has been the subject of great controversy since its creation by Glaser and Strauss (Glaser, 1992; Duchscher & Morgan, 2004; Kelle, 2005). Numerous authors have written about GTM, with renewed interest spawned by the intense debate between its originators, Glaser and Strauss, about its canons (Glaser, 1992; Duchscher & Morgan, 2004). Lehman et al (2006) as cited by Urquhart & Fernandez (2006) identify four alternative approaches to GTM, these being (1) full use of the method, (2) using the method to generate concepts, (3) mixing grounded theory with other research methods, and (4) studies mislabelled as GTM, that is not following any known procedures. Within the category labelled ‘full use of the method’ two alternatives are often referred to – the ‘Glaserian’ and ‘Straussian’ approaches respectively. Glaser indicates a preference for the term Classic GTM, rather than ‘Glaserian’ (Glaser & Holton, 2004). Mills et al (2006) refer to the work of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) as an evolved form of GTM rather than referring to the oft-used term ‘Straussian’ GTM. In this paper we will refer to Classic GTM and not ‘Glaserian’. Similarly we will refer to Evolved GTM, not ‘Straussian’, which allows for recognition of further developments in GTM, such as the work of Charmaz (2006, 2008) on constructivist GTM (Mills et al, 2006). Van Niekerk & Roode (2009) argue that ‘Glaserian’ [classic] and ‘Straussian’ [evolved] GTM are so different that of necessity they need to be separately classified. Some of the key differences between classic GTM and the evolved GTM proposed by Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) will be highlighted next.

## Classic vs evolved grounded theory approaches

Glaser (1992) objected strongly to the rendition of GTM as described in Strauss & Corbin (1990) which led to the development of the aforementioned ‘Glaserian [Classic] and ‘Straussian’ [Evolved] camps among grounded theorists (Goulding, 1998). Classic GTM is accepted as being faithful to the original formulation by Glaser & Strauss (1967). Some of the key differences between evolved and classic GTM are as follows.

Use of the paradigm model The extensive use of the paradigm model in Strauss & Corbin’s (1990, 1998) evolved GTM is an important point of divergence from classic GTM (Kelle, 2005). The paradigm model suggests that a grounded theory will at its core consist of action/interaction strategies, influenced by various conditions – namely contextual, causal and intervening respectively. The action/interaction strategies in turn give rise to consequences. Strauss & Corbin (1990) argue that without the use of such a coding paradigm the ‘grounded theory analysis will lack density and precision’. Glaser (1992) asserts that the use of this paradigm model produces forced ‘conceptual description’ rather than emergent categories and grounded theory, as in classic GTM. Taking the notion of contextual conditions as an example, Glaser & Holton (2004) assert that ‘context must emerge as a relevant category or as a theoretical code like all other categories in a GT. It cannot be assumed as relevant in advance’. According to Glaser (1992), these paradigm model codes form part of a wider family of theoretical codes to which no prescription to any research setting should be advocated beforehand. The rigid use of the paradigm model promotes deductive as compared to inductive reasoning (Glaser, 1992). Strauss & Corbin (1998) contend that the purpose of a paradigm model is to gain a better understanding of how a phenomenon relates to sub-categories. As such a category may serve as one or more of a causal condition, intervening condition, action/interaction or consequence, depending on the main category being investigated (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). It is therefore not the intent that as a category emerges during open coding it be assigned the label of causal, intervening, consequence, etc. Furthermore, in accordance with the principle of emergence, any relationships identified should arise from the data, although actual linking takes place conceptually at the level of properties and dimensions of categories (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

Role of a priori theory and literature review GTM is an inductive research methodology in which theory should be allowed to emerge from data, rather than be predefined at the start of a study (Glaser, 2002; Van Niekerk & Roode, 2009). Both classic and evolved versions of GTM encourage review of literature in unrelated fields as a means of, for example, enhancing theoretical sensitivity (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Glaser & Holton (2004) describe the essence of theoretical sensitivity as being ‘the ability to generate concepts from data and to relate them according to normal models of theory in general, and theory devel opment in sociology in particular’. Literature review concerning the core variable should only be carried out once that variable has emerged from the data (Van Niekerk & Roode, 2009). That literature in essence becomes another source of data for theory-building (Glaser & Holton, 2004). Nevertheless, there have been arguments for conducting an a priori literature review in the substantive area of study on the basis that it: (1) provides a context and justification for the study, (2) provides a basis for demonstrating the appropriate ness of a grounded theory approach, and (3) satisfies institutional or other such requirements for a literature review to precede data collection and analysis (McGhee et al, 2007). Arguments against an a priori literature review are that: (1) it compromises the researcher’s ability to keep to the tenets of a classic grounded theory approach; (2) the researcher may waste valuable time reviewing literature which is not significant to the core variable that eventually emerges through data analysis (Glaser & Holton, 2004); (3) there is the danger of the researcher being so immersed in literature that data are used to deductively verify reviewed theoretical concepts, rather than concepts inductively emerging from data (McGhee et al, 2007). Researchers who claim to follow the evolved approach of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) tend to be more flexible on the use of a priori theory and initial literature review in the substantive area of study. For example, Boudreau & Robey (2005) justify their use of evolved GTM by stating that ‘this version allows for the potential of prior theory, literature, and personal and professional experiences to guide researchers’ data analysis (Strauss and Corbin, 1994)’. Researchers who claim adherence to classic GTM, on the other hand, tend to avoid the use of a priori theory and initial literature review in the domain of interest (Van Niekerk & Roode, 2009). Suddaby (2006) surmises that ‘the real danger of prior knowledge in grounded theory is not that it will contaminate a researcher’s perspective, but rather that it will force the researcher into testing hypotheses, eithe overtly or unconsciously, rather than directly observing’.

The research question/problem Controversy surrounds the role of a pre-defined research question or problem in GTM. Van Niekerk & Roode (2009) assert that in classic GTM the ‘researcher does not start with a research question, but investigates an area’ while for evolved (‘Straussian’) GTM ‘the researcher starts with a research question, which is a statement about the phenomenon’. Suddaby (2006) declares that ‘the idea that reasonable research can be conducted without a clear research question and absent theory simply defies logic’. This position resonates with the typical practices of those who use evolved GTM. In classic GTM, in contrast, the stance is that ‘a good GT analysis starts right off with regular daily data collecting, coding and analysis. The start is not blocked by a preconceived problem, a methods chapter or a literature review. The focus and flow is immediately into conceptualization using the constant comparative method’ (Glaser & Holton, 2004).

Coding procedures Locke (2001) notes that the essential difference between the classic and evolved (‘Straussian’) approaches is that the classic tends towards ‘more openness, flexibility, and more parsimony in the elaboration of necessary analytical steps’. The evolved (‘Straussian’), on the other hand, tends towards ‘increased prescription and formal elaboration of operational procedures’. This is evident in the suggested use of detailed line-bv-line microanalysis of data as one of the techniques prescribed by Strauss & Corbin (1998). As Glaser (1992) points out, going through such a procedure will result in over-conceptualization of data incidents.

Evolved (‘Straussian’) GTM furthermore mentions three major coding phases – open, axial and selective coding respectively (Strauss & Corbin, 1990, 1998). Some authors include as a fourth phase coding-for-process (Urquhart et al, 2010). Classic GTM on the other hand mentions open, selective and theoretical coding respectively (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1992; Urquhart et al, 2010).

In evolved GTM, during open coding, categories are developed by grouping similar concepts together into more abstract explanatory terms (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Discrete concepts may then become properties or characteristics of a category, or may provide suggestions for such characteristics. The rationale for this higher order grouping is that categories are easier to remember, think about, and develop in terms of properties and dimensions, and can be further differentiated by breaking them down into sub-categories if necessary (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Glaser (1992) argues that firstly identifying discrete concepts and then grouping them into higher order categories is unnecessarily tedious and laborious. In classic GTM, through constant comparative analysis, broad-based categories emerge. Further comparative analysis yields properties and dimensions of these categories (Glaser, 1992).

Use of grounded theory techniques for data analysis While the overshadowing debate concerning alternative grounded theory approaches has been between the classic and evolved GTM camps, a third approach is discernible, where researchers do not necessarily follow either. Rather, they choose to use only the GTM techniques such as open, and/or axial and/or selective coding. Strict adherence to the principles of GTM is not evident. For instance, researchers may start with pre-conceived a priori theory, then go on to collect empirical data, and analyse it using grounded theory coding techniques. The intent may be to use the initial theoretical framework as a guide or sensitizing device, before applying grounded theory techniques to analyse data. Alternatively the intent may be to use GTM techniques as a means to develop concepts, but not theory (Lehman et al, 2006, cited by Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006). These types of studies are classified in this paper as being analytical, that is ‘the use of grounded theory techniques for analysis’. Lehman et al (2006) and Suddaby (2006) note the quite prevalent misuse of the term GTM in some of these cases. The reasons may vary from misunderstandings of GTM by researchers, to attempts at legitimizing poor qualitative research, by labelling it as following GTM techniques (Glaser & Holton, 2004).

## Mixed method approach

Consistent with Lehman et al (2006) as cited in Urquhart & Fernandez (2006), this paper identifies a fourth grounded theory approach labelled the mixed method approach (Mingers, 2001). Two particular formulations are grounded action research (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999; DeLuca et al, 2008) and the grounded case study approach (Eisenhardt, 1989). Glaser & Holton (2004) argue against combining classic GTM with other research methods, as it may erode the ability to generate conceptual theory.

## Epistemological basis of GTM

There is no shortage of literature alleging the epistemological position embedded within GTM (Goulding, 1998; Hughes & Jones, 2003; Duchscher & Morgan, 2004; Butler & O’Reilly, 2010). Some researchers have alleged a positivist inclination in GTM (Duchscher & Morgan, 2004), while others have associated it with interpretivist thinking (Hughes & Jones, 2003). Glaser’s (1992) assertion that theory should emerge from data, without influence from the researcher, who must suspend all pre-conceived notions may be interpreted as a positivist investigative stance. The detailed descriptive and prescriptive nature of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) has a hard and positivist feel (Charmaz, 2008). Indeed, Bryant (2002) argues that GTM. whether classic or evolved. is rooted in positivism. Hughes & Jones (2003) argue that despite there being no explicit epistemological basis within GTM, some of its principles and techniques resonate with the interpretivist paradigm, for example recognition of context, abstraction and generalization, and theoretical sampling (Klein & Myers, 1999). Urquhart (2002) argues that although many IS researchers who have applied GTM have adopted an interpretivist stance, the grounded theory approach is essentially a methodology, whose philosophical base is not specifically articulated. Fitzgerald & Howcroft (1998) illustrate the separation between the epistemological, ontological, axiological and methodological dimensions of any research. This distinction is particularly relevant to GTM as it demonstrates that choice of methodology may not automatically imply adoption of an epistemological stance. With any application of GTM, the researcher could make known their epistemological stance, and then apply the GTM in accordance with those tenets (Urquhart, 2002; Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006; Urquhart et al, 2010). Charmaz (2006, 2008) for instance outlines a constructivist approach to GTM, and contrasts it with what they describe as the objectivist grounded theory approach advocated by Glaser (1992). Glaser (2002) asserts that constructivist data are but one type of data used in developing grounded theory, and in so doing implies that classic GTM is paradigmatically neutral. Given that with GTM both the research design and the resultant theory should be allowed to emerge, it may be reasonable to assume also that the epistemological basis for a study may in fact emerge, and need not be defined a priori.

## Research method

To conduct the study, IS articles which claimed to have employed some form or aspect of GTM were collected and reviewed. Given the wide diversity of IS research outlets, it was beyond the scope of this study to identify all possible IS studies. It was decided to restrict the survey to journals, since most high-quality research is published in such outlets. The IS discipline is known to have more than 600 possible journal publication outlets (Lamp, 2004). Searching through such a large list of journals was beyond the scope of this study. Lewis et al (2007) conducted a scientometric study to establish validity of metrics used to ascertain journal quality in IS research. A list of the top 50 IS-centric journals was used. This list has been used to investigate other IS research trends, but was too large in scope for the purposes of this paper. Senior scholars within the Association for Information Systems have highlighted eight of these journals as being the most prestigious in the field – MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, European Journal of Information Systems and Journal of Strategic Information Systems. These eight journals were employed as the basket of journals to assess how top IS research has employed grounded theory approaches.

Articles reporting on use of GTM were identified by using ‘grounded theory’ as the search string. Only empirical studies were selected, rather than studies such as this which report conceptually on GTM, or studies proposing research methods. The time frame for articles was from 1985 to 2008. The year 1985 was chosen as it signifies the first relevant article found using GTM within the selected publications. The article was by Huff & Munro (1985) on ‘Information Technology Assessment and Adoption’. The search yielded a total of 76 articles. Every effort was made to ensure that all relevant articles were identified. However, it is quite possible that a few articles might have been missed.

Analytical approach Forty-four per cent of publications used the analytical techniques of GTM without adopting a particular methodological stance, whether classic or evolved. The prevalence of the analytical approach is perhaps not surprising, given the controversies associated with using either classic or evolved GTM. It may also reflect the desire for flexibility when conducting IS research, with methods and techniques used where perceived as appropriate, and not necessarily in order to produce a grounded theory. It is also a reflection of the difficulty of following classic GTM in traditional research contexts. Tradition often dictates that there be a priori conceptualizations of the research problem through extensive literature review, and well-structured research designs before data gathering (Glaser & Holton, 2004). These traditions are at odds with the emergent nature of GTM. Many researchers wanting to employ GTM are therefore obliged to adopt only the grounded theory techniques in order to fit within these prevailing research traditions. Researchers who adopt the analytical approach should be cautioned against claiming the use of GTM for their studies, as analytical approaches typically do not employ all the principles and procedures of GTM, and do not develop grounded theory.

A large number of articles in this category made no explicit reference to the coding techniques (e.g., open, axial, selective, etc.), although some did provide evidence of constant comparative analysis having been performed. Where coding techniques were mentioned it was most often open coding, which gave rise to concepts and themes, but not theory. Urquhart et al (2010) bemoan the prevalence of the analytical approach in IS research, since the main purpose of the grounded theory method is theory building, not data analysis.

Mixed method approach Twenty-four per cent of articles made use of mixed methods. Several of these studies drew from the work of Eisenhardt (1989), who popularized the use of case studies to build theory by employing grounded theory procedures and techniques. Others combined case study research and grounded theory techniques, but without reference to the work of Eisenhardt (1989). The latter studies, it may be argued, missed the opportunity to build a cumulative tradition by drawing from previous formulations of grounded case study research. A smaller number of studies combined action research with grounded theory techniques. Not many of these made reference to the work of Baskerville & Pries-Heje (1999), which again presents a missed opportunity for building a cumulative tradition concerning the grounded action research approach.

Evolved approach The evolved GTM, particularly that of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998), was as widely used as the mixed method approach (24% of articles). The guidelines and prescriptions provided by Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) have been noted as useful, especially for novice researchers (Hughes & Jones, 2003). In several papers, the use of the paradigm model was clearly evident, with contextual conditions, causal conditions, action/ interaction strategies and consequences explicit in the developed theory. Several studies applied the paradigm model in a rigid fashion, affirming the view of Glaser (1992) that such an approach may lead to categories and concepts being forced to fit into this paradigm, rather than the theory being allowed to emerge from data.

In almost all papers that adopted the evolved GTM, an a priori literature review was included. The literature review was used for purposes such as identifying a research gap, justifying the methodological approach, and informing data collection strategies. In several papers, only open and axial coding were employed, with no explicit reference to selective coding, or codingfor-process. The lack of selective coding, and the coding-for-process phases as highlighted by Urquhart et al (2010) indicate that evolved GTM is not always being applied in its entirety. The omission of selective coding may lead to theory that is not fully developed and lacking in parsimony.

Classic approach Classic GTM was not widely used as reflected by the 8% incidence of articles (six in all). Monographs on the classic approach have in the past offered limited guidelines and prescriptions. Rather adherence to principles has been emphasized. Classic GTM is often perceived as difficult to execute, especially for novice researchers (Hughes & Jones, 2003). The less frequent use in IS research may also be attributed to the propensity for mainstream IS research to require detailed initial literature reviews and a priori theory, both of which are not advocated with classic GTM.

Of the six studies, two were conducted prior to the emergence of evolved GTM, which emanated primarily from Strauss & Corbin (1990). Although all were categorized as classic GTM by virtue of their reference to Glaser & Strauss (1967) and other Glaser writings, in some studies there was inadequate elaboration as to how GTM principles and procedures were enacted.

## Perceived epistemological stance

Many researchers do not explicitly state the epistemological stance taken in their studies, which at times makes it difficult for readers to establish this position. In the absence of an explicit statement in the research articles, research was considered positivist where it was adjudged that the research paper assumed ‘an objective physical and social world that exists independent of humans, and whose nature can be relatively unproblematically apprehended, characterised and measured’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). Interpretive research was distinguished by the researchers<sup>0</sup> analysis of participants’ viewpoints, seeking context-based understanding of phenomenon and a general absence of criteria expected from positivist studies (Myers, 1997).

Classic GTM is often alleged to be positivist (Bryant, 2002), while the evolved approach is often associated with interpretivism (Hughes & Jones, 2003). It is important to assess how GTM is used in IS research in order to understand whether these generalizations hold. Table 4 illustrates the differences found in the sample set of articles.

For approaches employing just the grounded theory analysis techniques, 76% were deemed to be interpretive while 24% were deemed positivist. In studies following the classic approach, 67% per cent (4) resonated with an interpretivist epistemology with the remainder positivist. Mixed method studies were deemed 61% interpretive, compared to 28% positivist and 11% adopting scientific realism. Finally, studies following evolved GTM were deemed 78% interpretive and 22% positivist. The findings perhaps lend credence to the view that GTM does not have a specific epistemological position embedded within its tenets (Urquhart, 2002). The predominance of interpretive studies nevertheless confirms the assertion by Hughes & Jones (2003) that the methodology fits well with the interpretive tradition. Glaser (2002) in a critique of constructivist GTM contends that ‘constructivist data, if it exists at all, is a very, very small part of the data that grounded theory uses’, implying that classic GTM should be viewed as paradigmatically neutral, and neither positivist nor interpretivist.

## Discussion and implications

It is acknowledged that for the development of a field of knowledge it is necessary to develop theory about pertinent phenomena (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Several calls have been made to develop theory in IS, rather than relying on theory borrowed and adapted from reference disciplines (Truex et al, 2006). GTM offers a compelling means of achieving this aim. GTM is hence increasingly being used in IS research. Its relevance is easily supported by acknowledging the nature of the discipline, which predominantly deals with IT in social contexts. Indeed, the IT artefact itself is inscribed with social and cultural rules, norms and procedures (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). GTM, which originated in sociology, is therefore an appropriate research methodology to use in IS research, to develop theory grounded in experiences from the discipline.

The analysis has revealed that there are four main approaches to GTM use in IS research – Analytical, Evolved, Classic and Mixed Method approaches respectively. Misunderstandings and myths are common amongst users of all four approaches (Suddaby, 2006; Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006). For each of these approaches further clarity is needed, in terms of criteria for judging the soundness and rigour of the research, and guidelines and principles to be followed.

The majority of IS researchers opt to use just the analysis techniques of GTM, without adhering to GTM principles, or without developing theory. In some of these analytical studies the GTM label is misused, as no explicit evidence of GTM techniques having been employed is provided (Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006). This may be due to a lack of understanding of GTM, and/or attempts at lending legitimacy to poor qualitative research (Suddaby, 2006). Researchers should go beyond adopting GTM techniques only for data analysis and aim to build theory (Urquhart et al, 2010). Generalization and abstraction from empirical data to higher level concepts and theory is important in order to make contributions to knowledge (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). GTM when used in full provides a means for making knowledge contributions in this way.

The evolved form of GTM. particularly that of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) is widely used in IS research due to the clear prescriptions and guidelines it provides (Hughes & Jones, 2003). Researchers need to be aware of the limitations of this method. For example, the paradigm model represents but one of a family of 18 theoretical codes (Glaser, 1992).The ontological and epistemological foundations of GTM are also not explicitly articulated by Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998). It is incumbent then on IS researchers to ensure the paradigmatic stance they adopt is made clear. More recent evolutions of GTM such as constructivist GTM (Charmaz, 2006, 2008) are helpful in this regard. Guidelines such as those proffered by Urquhart et al (2010) are also useful for IS researchers wanting to employ evolved GTM.

Mixed method approaches were found to constitute 24% of the sampled publications. Users of these approaches should have a good grasp and understanding of both GTM and the methods it will be combined with to avoid the potential ontological and epistemological pitfalls of such an endeavour (Mingers, 2001). To build up a cumulative tradition in IS research, previous mixed method formulations (e.g., Eisenhardt, 1989 for grounded case study research) should be built on rather than beginning from first principles each time other methods are to be combined with GTM.

The classic approach to GTM has been the least often used in IS research. In cases where it has been used there is often limited evidence of classic GTM principles and procedures having been followed. More studies should be encouraged in IS that use classic GTM in a rigorous fashion, and articulate the approach clearly and transparently. There needs to be an understanding and appreciation of Glaser’s critique of evolved GTM among those who choose to follow the classic approach. In this regard, researchers would need to go back to the original work of Glaser & Strauss (1967), as well as Glaser’s subsequent writings on GTM. Many researchers rely on secondary reference sources, often compounding myths and misunderstandings about classic GTM (Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006).

In investigating the assumed epistemological and ontological foundations of GTM, researchers often conflate classic and evolved approaches (Mills et al, 2006; Van Niekerk & Roode, 2009; Butler & O’Reilly, 2010). The two approaches are as pointed out in this paper different, and hence epistemological and ontological undertone need to be considered separately for each approach. It would be useful for future research to investigate under what conditions the different approaches are most appropriate, and for what type of IS phenomena. It would also be useful to compare outcomes of the four alternative approaches, so as to obtain a better understanding of the implications of the sometimes subtle differences between them.

## Conclusion

We have highlighted the alternative grounded theory approaches used in IS research and clarified the nature of grounded theory approaches in order to elucidate good practice and help prevent the quite prevalent misuse of the term GTM. The four distinct approaches identified were classic GTM, evolved GTM, the analytical grounded theory approach, and the mixed method approach. The predominant mode of usage in IS research has been the analytical approach. The least often used is classic GTM. The growth in GTM use in IS has been a positive development, capable of contributing to the theoretical core of IS and strengthening the discipline (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). Further work can be done particularly to build better understanding of the process and outcomes of following a classic grounded theory approach, and of mixing GTM with other methods in IS research. The study has limited its investigation to the top eight IS academic journals. For a more encompassing view a larger basket of journals and hence IS papers can be examined.
