## A B S T R A C T

Knowledge-sharing services in virtual communities have enjoyed tremendous growth in recent years. These communities enable content creators (i.e., speakers) to share knowledge by broadcasting lives to which au diences can pay to listen. Our study explores the key antecedents of the sales of paid knowledge products. Data were obtained from the Zhihu Live community to verify the hypotheses. On the Zhihu Live platform, knowledge products are provided in the form of audio broadcasts. A potential listener can choose to purchase a live either before (phase 1) or after (phase 2) it is broadcasted. Accordingly, this study proposes a two-phase approach to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of key factors that a<sup>f</sup>ect the sales of lives in each phase. Our results suggest that price negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects a live’s sales and cumulative prior sales positively a<sup>f</sup>ect next-day sales in both phases. Furthermore, review score and speaker–audience interactions have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on sales after a live is broadcasted. The contributions and implications of the results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Owing to the proliferation of social computing and user-generated content, some platforms that previously provided free knowledgesharing services are now charging their users and providing trading services for knowledge products. These platforms have enjoyed tre mendous growth in recent years. For example, Zhihu Live (www.zhi hu.com/lives), a real-time Q&A paid knowledge-sharing community, had over 200 million registered users in August 2018<sup>1</sup> . According to data obtained from iResearch’s mUser Tracker<sup>2</sup>, in China, the number of people using paid knowledge-sharing applications has grown and the number of active devices each month reached 143 million in December 2017 [1]. In the same year, it was estimated that the scale of the paid knowledge-sharing industry had reached around 5 billion RMB [1].

However, given the increasing demand for knowledge content and the development of knowledge products and services, the online consumption of knowledge products has become increasingly complicated. Moreover, compared to traditional paid knowledge communities (e.g., Massive Open Online Courses, MOOCs), there are four distinctive features of emerging paid knowledge-sharing communities (e.g., Zhihu Live; [1]. First, knowledge content is more abundant and speci<sup>fi</sup>c to satisfy the high demand for knowledge today. For example, icourse163.org<sup>3</sup> o<sup>f</sup>ers courses on diverse subjects that are commonly taught in schools (e.g., computer science and economy management), while Zhihu Live o<sup>f</sup>ers information on broader topics (e.g., travelling and cooking). Second, current forms of knowledge products are more fragmented (e.g., separate short audio messages; [2], which makes them well suited to mobile internet users. Traditional online courses are usually recorded as videos and may last several hours, but new paid knowledge-sharing communities o<sup>f</sup>er short audio clips, which help consumers make better use of their time. Third, the threshold for sharing knowledge is lower, which attracts more knowledge creators. For instance, any veri<sup>fi</sup>ed user can share knowledge with the Zhihu community, while only teachers at famous universities produce MOOCs. Fourth, the interactions between knowledge content creators and consumers create additional value and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for knowledge consumers [3]. Value creation in the context of knowledge used to rely on creators, and knowledge transmission was a one-direction paradigm (e.g., online courses). However, in new paid knowledge-sharing communities, creators and consumers create value together by interacting with each other during the knowledge-sharing process. This is the most distinctive feature of new paid knowledge products.

All four characteristics lead to big di<sup>f</sup>erences between consumers’ perceptions of knowledge products from traditional knowledge communities and emerging knowledge-sharing communities. For example, in emerging communities, the perception of a product’s quality may rely not only on one’s evaluation of the knowledge creator, but also on the interactions between the creator and other customers. Therefore, the purchase behaviors related to emerging knowledge-sharing pro ducts are worthy of study.

Over the past few decades, a number of researchers have in vestigated the determinants of knowledge-sharing behavior in virtual communities (e.g., [4–11]). This research identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some key factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing in virtual communities, including identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, the norm of reciprocity, trust, and commitment [12–15]. In addition, factors related to the knowledge contributor, such as re putation, o<sup>fl</sup>ine social norms, expected outcomes, and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as important in knowledge sharing [4,7,13,14,16]. However, as knowledge products possess a number of speci<sup>fi</sup>c features, the knowledge market is faced with new challenges [1,17]. First, knowledge products can often be categorized as experience goods, whose quality is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate before making a purchase. To address this, emerging platforms have introduced design features to strengthen the interaction between content creators and consumers, and enhance the consumer's absorption and digestion of knowledge [3]. For instance, Zhihu Live includes innovative design functions to boost the sales of knowledge products, including the two-phase mechanism, which are not present in the traditional knowledge market. Second, knowledge products on new platforms are often fragmented and con tain contextual knowledge [2]. Unlike traditional online courses, which last for hours, new knowledge products are more adapted to mobile internet users and fragmented consumption [1]. Because knowledge products and emerging online platforms possess a number of distinct features, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior literature on the knowledge market may not apply [2].

This research aims to address these issues by studying the Zhihu Live platform, where users can act as speakers and share their knowledge/experiences by broadcasting audio lives or as listeners and pay to listen to lives.

A live goes on sale before it is broadcasted. The timeline of a live can be divided into two phases, shown in Fig. 1. Phase 1 (Fig. 2) begins when a live page is created but the live has not yet started, and phase 2 (Fig. 3) begins after a live is broadcasted.

Before a live is broadcasted (phase 1), potential listeners can pay to watch the broadcast when it airs. During the broadcast, the audience can ask questions and obtain feedback from the speaker or express their opinions through a voting system. If a customer purchases a live after it is broadcasted (phase 2), he or she cannot participate in real-time interactions. However, certain details about the live (e.g., average review score, duration, and degree of interaction) can be obtained after it is broadcasted to support purchase decision-making (Fig. 3). Therefore, the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ purchase decisions may di<sup>f</sup>er between the two phases.

This study employs a hierarchical model and a Zhihu Live dataset to investigate aggregated consumer purchase behaviors within online knowledge communities in each of the two phases. In particular, our research attempts to answer the following overarching research questions:

1.1. What factors afect the sales of a live? What are the diferential factors that afect sales volume before and after a live is broadcasted?

The contributions of this research are twofold. From a theoretical perspective, the research contributes to the knowledge-sharing literature, by investigating the purchase behaviors of knowledge pro ducts in a virtual community. In a knowledge-sharing community, everyone can both provide and receive knowledge, while on a traditional knowledge-sharing platform, knowledge can be shared only by experts. The new knowledge transformation model may change purchase behaviors, and it is crucial to understand these behaviors to better develop new knowledge-sharing communities. In addition, the two-phase sales model as a design factor is new in the knowledge sharing and tradi tional retailing literature. An investigation of this mechanism would thus contribute to the understanding of knowledge consumption be havior. From a practical perspective, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide implications regarding how to promote purchasing and sharing behaviors in online knowledge communities. For example, rather than encouraging speakers to improve their recognition (e.g., the number of followers), improving the degree of interaction during a live would more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently boost sales. Alternatively, a speaker could encourage the audience to rate the live while it is being broadcast because the review score is an important determinant for potential listeners making purchase decisions. Overall, as emerging knowledge-sharing platforms are exploring new business models and mechanisms to survive and thrive, in vestigating such mechanisms is of great importance for such platforms.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Social learning theory

The Social Learning Theory (SLT) [18–20] is a theoretical lens with which to explain people’s convergent social behavior. From an SLT perspective, potential listeners’ decision-making could be based on expectancy, or the perceived probability that a particular reinforcement will occur as a function of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c behavior in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c situation [20]. The expectancy regarding the quality of a live might a<sup>f</sup>ect an audience’s decision about whether a live is worth purchasing. Ac cording to the SLT, the process of evaluating a live before it is purchased is essentially an expectancy formation process. In other words, potential listeners’ purchase decisions might be the result of learning about the quality of lives (i.e., forming an expectation about the quality of lives based on information provided on the platform and/or personal experience).

According to Bandura’s SLT, human behavior and its associated expectations can be formed through two ways: direct experience and modeling [18,19]. Learning through direct experience requires the subject to be personally involved in an activity; while modeling is the process of learning by observing others’ responses to an environment and experiencing certain consequences. Modeling includes both vicar ious learning (i.e., observing others) and symbolic learning (e.g.,

reading written information) [18].

In the context of an online knowledge-sharing community like Zhihu, potential listeners do not have direct experience with a live before they purchase and listen to it. Therefore, they have to rely upon modeling through both symbolic learning (e.g., the introduction of a live, the speaker’s past behavior, etc.) and vicarious learning (e.g., others’ behavior and preferences) to form expectancy.

Although the SLT provides a theoretical lens through which to understand consumers’ behavior on emerging knowledge-sharing platforms, it does not explain which types of information play key roles in the development of potential listeners’ expectations of quality. Therefore, we use the signaling theory to analyze the role of di<sup>f</sup>erent types of information

## 2.2. Signaling theory

One of the most critical problems with paid knowledge sharing is information asymmetry. In the emerging paid knowledge-sharing community, everyone can choose to contribute knowledge, and the low threshold for becoming a knowledge speaker contributes to the growth of such communities. However, it also leads to more information asymmetry than other online learning platforms, such as MOOCs, because it is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult for potential listeners to judge the quality of knowledge products, especially before they experience them. Hence, we employ the signaling theory as a theoretical lens to investigate the roles of di<sup>f</sup>erent information in potential listeners’ evaluation process.

Spence [21] introduced the signaling theory to explain that observable attributes of an entity can serve as signals of quality and help to reduce information asymmetry. Signaling theory was originally applied to the labor market; as employers often lack information about the quality of candidates, candidates usually obtain degrees to signal their quality, reduce information asymmetry, and make themselves stand out [21].

Signaling theory has three primary elements: the signaler, signal, and receiver [22]. In our study, the signaler could be the speaker, live, or listeners who can provide additional information about a live. The receiver is the potential listener, who may receive di<sup>f</sup>erent signals from signalers. The signals in our study are observable attributes of lives, such as price and review score, and they play di<sup>f</sup>erent roles in a potential listener’s purchase decision. Observability and cost are two major characteristics of strong signals [23]. Observability refers to the extent to which receivers are able to notice a signal. It is necessary, but not the only characteristic required of a signal [23]. For example, content with a large font is more attractive than content with a small font, and information on the <sup>fi</sup>rst level of a page tends to be noticed more than information at the second level. Other signals are costly, but strong. For instance, obtaining an o<sup>fi</sup>cial certi<sup>fi</sup>cation, like ISO 9000, is time-consuming, but it is a strong signal of quality that helps buyers distinguish between high-quality and low-quality manufacturers. In addition, the credibility of the information source (i.e., the signaler) will a<sup>f</sup>ect the trustworthiness of its signals. Furthermore, some receivers interpret signals di<sup>f</sup>erently from others, applying di<sup>f</sup>erent weights to di<sup>f</sup>erent signals based on preconceived notions about their importance or cognitive distortion [24].

Signals may be strong or weak in di<sup>f</sup>erent scenarios [25]. For example, they di<sup>f</sup>er in strength for search and experience products, which are categorized based on their degree of standardization [26,27]. Search products (e.g., furniture) usually have standard features, which help consumers to more precisely evaluate the quality of products before they make purchase decisions. However, experience products (e.g., movies and books) are more personalized, which makes evaluating a product’s quality more di<sup>fi</sup>cult before purchase because of the limited and ambiguous available information about the product. Therefore, compared to search products, the impact of quality signals is stronger for potential customers of experience products [28]. In the context of our research, knowledge products are similar to typical experience products; audiences who purchase a live before it is broadcasted may receive and interpret di<sup>f</sup>erent signals from those who purchase a live after it has been broadcasted.

Signaling theory has been applied to a wide range of management studies, including electronic commerce research [29,30], online trust building, venture capital <sup>fi</sup>nancing, electronic word-of-mouth ([31], investor decisions [32,33], and P2P lending [34]. Prior researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a variety of quality signals. For instance, the brand [35], reputation of retailers [36], price [37], warranties [38], and store en vironment [39] are commonly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as quality signals that in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ purchase behaviors.

## 3. Hypothesis development

The objective of this study is to identify the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the sales of broadcasted lives. We examined two phases for two major reasons. First, the information available during decision-making di<sup>f</sup>ers between the two phases. Before a live is broadcasted, users have access to very limited information, such as price, start date, and some personal information about the speaker. After a live has been broadcasted, users can obtain more information, such as reviews from other listeners, the number of replies, and the number of attachments. Second, even common variables like price may a<sup>f</sup>ect users’ purchase behaviors in di<sup>f</sup>erent ways, because the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks they present di<sup>f</sup>er between the two phases. For example, a potential listener can purchase a live for the same price—RMB 9.99—either before or after it has been broadcasted. Audiences who pay in phase 1 can join the live broadcast, but incur the risk that the quality of the live will not meet their expectations. In contrast, those who purchase the live in phase 2 will lose the chance to interact with the speaker, but will have access to more information about the quality of the live, before making a purchase decision. On the basis of this scenario, we propose the following twophase model to describe scenarios in which one purchases a live before (Model 1) and after (Model 2) it is broadcasted. Based on the signaling theory [21,40], we identify various signals that are expected to in<sup>fl</sup>u ence consumers’ purchase decisions.

Price might be an important factor a<sup>f</sup>ecting potential listeners purchase behavior of lives as knowledge products. Price is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the monetary cost that a potential listener pays to listen to a live broadcast. A product’s value is based on the trade-o<sup>f</sup> between what the customer receives (e.g., quality, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, worth, and utility) and what she gives up to acquire these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., price and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces) [41–43]. Accordingly, it is conjectured that:

H1a. The price of a live negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of the live before it is broadcasted (phase 1).

H1b. The price of a live negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of the live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

During a live broadcast, audiences can ask questions and post comments with the expectation that the speaker will answer. The number of messages to which a speaker replied is shown on the detail page for a live. The more messages to which the speaker replies, the more likely that an audience will <sup>fi</sup>nd the live useful. Moreover, on the detail page for a live, potential listeners can view the number of attachments (e.g., pictures or lecture notes intended to improve the au dience’s understanding) and the duration of the live. In terms of the signaling theory, attachments represent the e<sup>f</sup>ort a speaker puts into hosting a live and could act as a strong signal of the quality of a live. In line with Connelly (2011), who de<sup>fi</sup>nes signal <sup>fi</sup>t as the extent to which a signal is correlated with unobservable quality, the number of replies and attachments uploaded to facilitate learning are strong indicators of the unobservable quality of a live. In addition, longer duration could mean that the live o<sup>f</sup>ers more content.

From an SLT perspective [18,19], a speaker’s replies (i.e., interactions with the audience) could help potential listeners to learn by observing how others responded to a live and the consequences of this. In the SLT, such learning behavior is characterized as modeling.

Based on the above information, we hypothesize the following:

H2. The number of replies to a live positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of the live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

H3. The number of attachments in a live positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the currentperiod sales volume of the live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

H4. The duration of a live positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of the live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

In the context of Zhihu Live, speakers are the signalers who provide important signals. Positive reputation over time is a strong signal of underlying quality [44]. In the back end of the Zhihu community, the reputation of a speaker is mainly based on the approval of a speaker’s answers to questions, including the number of “gratitudes,” the number of upvotes from other users, the number of times that answers are collected, and the number of times that answers are shared. We expect the degree to which a speaker is recognized, has a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on sales. Therefore, we make the following hypotheses:

H5a. The degree to which a speaker is recognized in the community positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of a live before it is broadcasted (phase 1).

H5b. The degree to which a speaker is recognized in the community positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of a live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

Users can click the “like” button if they are interested in a live be fore purchasing it. As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, when a potential listener investigates a live, the cumulative prior sales and cumulative number of likes are present at the top of the page, both before and after a live is broadcasted. These signals’ prominent position gives them high observability and thus strength. In addition, these signals are relatively costly because they require the investment of other users, making them stronger [21,40].

As quality signals, cumulative prior sales and the cumulative number of likes may lead to the herding e<sup>f</sup>ect and thus a<sup>f</sup>ect potential listeners’ decision-making. The herding e<sup>f</sup>ect is convergent social behavior de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the alignment of the thoughts or behaviors of individuals in a group through local interaction and without centralized coordination [45] because of the strong e<sup>f</sup>ect of others’ decisions [46]. The herding e<sup>f</sup>ect has been widely used by marketing researchers and practitioners, as consumers usually use the evaluations or behaviors of others as quality signals to help them make their own purchase deci sions [47]. For example, the valence of online reviews has been found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect the sales of products like movies, books, and hotel rooms [48 50]. Furthermore, consumer ratings have been found to positively a<sup>f</sup>ect the adoption of less popular software products [51].

The herding e<sup>f</sup>ect has been reported in many circumstances, but it is especially applicable to internet-based or digital products. For example, music consumers prefer to listen to frequently downloaded songs [52]; web visitors will be attracted to popular vendors with a high click count displayed on their webpage [53]; and in online markets, higher sales of a product during a 30-day period is consistently associated with higher sales in the following 7 days [54].

These two signals, i.e., the volume of cumulative prior sales and cumulative number of likes contain aggregated information about others’ behavior. According to the SLT [18], such information might facilitate vicarious learning, leading to the alignment of individuals thoughts or behaviors [45]. We expect that the herding e<sup>f</sup>ect would arise in such circumstances, and we hypothesize the following:

H6a. The volume of cumulative prior sales positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of a live before it is broadcasted (phase 1).

H6b. The volume of cumulative prior sales positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current-period sales volume of a live after it is broadcasted (phase 2). H7a. The cumulative number of likes positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current period sales volume of a live before it is broadcasted (phase 1).

H7b. The cumulative number of likes positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current period sales volume of a live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

Customers who purchased a live can comment on the live after it is broadcasted and give it a review score from one to <sup>fi</sup>ve. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of user reviews is particularly important for experience goods [55] because their quality is often unknown before consumption [26]. In pre vious decades, studies have shown that online review scores signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect the sales of products like movies, books, and hotel rooms [48–50]. A higher review score usually indicates that a live is of higher quality, meaning that this can be regarded as a quality signal. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H8. The average review score of a live positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the current period sales volume of the live after it is broadcasted (phase 2).

For Model 1 (before a live is broadcasted), we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four factors that serve as antecedents of daily sales volume: cumulative prior sales, cumulative number of likes, the degree to which a speaker is recognized in the Zhihu community, and price. For Model 2, the average review score, duration, number of attachments, and number of replies are included as antecedents of live sales. These two models are presented in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data

The data used in this study were retrieved from Zhihu Live (www.zhihu.com/lives), one of the largest paid knowledge-sharing communities in China. We obtained data by crawling the Zhihu Live website from December 17, 2017 to June 1, 2018.

To ensure that the dataset used in our study could answer the proposed research questions accurately and reasonably, we performed several preprocessing steps to remove “noisy” records: (1) remove lives created before December 17, 2017; (2) remove lives that had been promoted because there are various types of promotion programs on Zhihu and the impact of these programs on sales are complex [56], and because there was no available information on the types of promotion that were used; 3) remove the records with missing values; and 4) match live IDs between the datasets for phase 1 and phase 2 to ensure that the objects of analysis are consistent between the models. After removing noisy records, we obtained 460 lives, including 7494 pur chase records for Model 1, and 11,232 purchase records for Model 2. All data concerning the independent variables were normalized before the regression analysis was conducted.

## 4.2. Method

Behavioral and social data are usually nested. For example, repeated observations of attributes at di<sup>f</sup>erent times are nested within the individuals in whom the attributes were observed. Additionally, each person might be nested within an organizational unit, such as a school or workplace.

Observable information on live pages can be divided into two levels based on how often it changes. Information like cumulative prior sales, the cumulative number of likes, and average review score change every day. However, information like price, live duration, and the number of replies changes only between lives. That is to say, some information changes at the day level within a live, and other information changes at a live level.

Because of the nested structure of the dataset, the hierarchical linear model (HLM) was chosen for data analysis to verify our research models. Each level in the data structure (e.g., repeated observations of sales records and sales records within lives) is formally represented by its own sub-model. Each submodel represents structural relations and residual variability at that level. For example, if the research problem consists of data on employees nested within departments, the level 1 model would represent the relationships among employee-level vari ables and the level 2 model would represent the e<sup>f</sup>ect of departmentlevel variables. In our research, for each phase, the level 1 model captured the impact of day-level variables and the level 2 model cap tured the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of live-level factors. Formally, there are i = … 1, , n<sub>j</sub> level 1 units (e.g., daily sales record of live j) nested within j = …1, , J level 2 units (e.g., di<sup>f</sup>erent lives).

Generally, employing an HLM involves three main procedures. First, a one-way analysis of variance is conducted to test whether the variance of the dependent variable di<sup>f</sup>ers between the within-group (i.e., daily) and between-group (i.e., live) levels. In this step, only the dependent variable is added to the testing model (also called the null model). Second, explanatory variables are added to level 1 (i.e., the daily level) to estimate the within-group variance and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of daily-level factors. Finally, explanatory variables are added to level 2 (i.e., the live level) to calculate the between-group variance and the impact of livelevel factors.

Therefore, rather than studying objective design factors, such as text length or other information derived from text-mining techniques, we decided to directly test the e<sup>f</sup>ects of consumers’ a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive perceptions of the live. We adopted the scale developed by [62] to capture both a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive perceptions of lives.

Second, we recruited 100 students from a major university as independent raters. The textual introductions to 460 lives were randomly assigned to 20 groups, meaning that each group contained 23 lives. Each group of lives was assigned to <sup>fi</sup>ve raters, who were randomly selected from the 100 participants. For each live, we computed the average scores of the <sup>fi</sup>ve raters to obtain the <sup>fi</sup>nal scores for both hedonic and utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The ICC was 0.983. ## 6. Discussion and conclusions

This study drew upon the SLT and signaling theory to examine the impacts of di<sup>f</sup>erent signals on the sales of paid knowledge products in online communities. We proposed a two-stage model to investigate signals from multiple sources, including speakers, other listeners, and paid knowledge products, in two separate but related stages. HLMs were employed for data analysis. Table 10 presents the results in relation to the hypotheses.

The objective of this research was to identify the di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects of key determinants on live sales before and after a live is broadcasted. On one hand, for the same factors in two phases, we examine the di<sup>f</sup>erence of the e<sup>f</sup>ects between phases 1 and 2; on the other hand, two separate models are built to examine the di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors in two phases, respectively. Because of the nested structure of dataset, we used HLMs to empirically answer the research question. The empirical results for Model 1 indicate that even though the available information about a live is very limited before it is broadcasted, overall, consumers still prefer to purchase lives during this phase, so that they can join the live broadcast and interact with the speaker rather than passively acquiring knowledge. Additionally, we found that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of several factors that can be observed both before and after a live is broadcasted on sales volume change to varying degrees after a live is broadcasted.

First, the price of a live serves as a cost signal and a<sup>f</sup>ects sales both before and after the live is broadcasted. The higher the price, the lower the sales volume. However, after the live is broadcasted, the negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of price is weaker. A possible reason for this is that more information (e.g., comments and replies) is available to inform purchase decisions after a live is broadcasted. For consumers who choose to purchase a live after it is broadcasted, it is highly likely that they are more con<sup>fi</sup>dent about the quality of the live (based on other quality clues). Therefore, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of price as a clue becomes weaker. Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with traditional marketing literature, which states that price often serves as a cost signal when consumers make a purchase decision (e.g., [40,63]).

Second, replies by the speaker have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on sales. Replies to speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions are likely to contribute to the quality of the knowledge product by providing supplementary knowledge and thus, from a signaling theory perspective, could serve as quality signals that a<sup>f</sup>ect consumers’ purchase decisions. From an SLT perspective [18,19], a speaker’s replies are interactions with other listeners, which could facilitate learning through the observation of others’ responses to a live and the consequences they experienced. The SLT characterizes such learning behavior as modeling, which includes both vicarious and symbolic learning [18]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with a recent study on Chinese college students’ online pay-for-knowledge behavior, which suggested that “in order to strengthen the interaction between user and lecturer, increase the user’s stickiness and optimize the user’s experi ence and enhance the user’s absorption and digestion of knowledge, the related platform should strengthen the interaction between the user and the lecturer, so that the user can feel more about the ‘sense of participation’” ([3], p. 202).

Finally, contrary to our hypotheses, the degree to which a speaker is recognized in the Zhihu Live community does not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on sales in either phase. This could be because of the ob servability of this signal; it is not directly shown on the live page, and potential listeners have to look at other pages to <sup>fi</sup>nd this information, and therefore, it is a weaker signal [23]. Another possible reason is that not all individuals make purchase decisions in a deliberate way. In other words, a certain portion of the audience listens to a live just for fun and makes decisions in an automatic way, leading information with low observability to be ignored. This explanation aligns with the dual processing framework of human behavioral decisions [64–66], which suggests that certain choice e<sup>f</sup>ects can be primarily attributed to rapid, unintentional, and intuitive processing, while others arise from inten tional and deliberate processing. In other words, if some consumers choose knowledge products in a rapid and intuitive way, a signal may become weaker. Exploring consumers’ decision-making in the context of this framework could be an interesting topic for future research.

Some limitations of this research should be noted. First, we col lected data from only one knowledge-sharing community, Zhihu Live, which may have unique characteristics that limit the generalizability of the proposed model. Second, in the Zhihu Live community, some popular lives are exhibited in the Popular Choice and Editor’s Choice columns, which may increase the exposure of certain lives and consequently a<sup>f</sup>ect their sales volume. Our post hoc analysis suggested that it was unlikely our results were biased by the inclusion of this information. Third, the platform is able to analyze users’ browsing habits using big data algorithms and provide better, more customized services. Customized recommendations should play an important role in potential listeners' purchase decisions. However. because of the lack of data on such recommendations, we do not analyze this factor. Fourth, this research collected data from December 17, 2017, to June 1, 2018. Data obtained over a longer period of time as well as more detailed data, such as data regarding customized recommendations, will surely lead to more insights. Finally, textual introductions could a<sup>f</sup>ect sales. Although we conducted a post hoc analysis that included audiences’ perceptions of textual introductions, analyzing the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these introductions (including pictures) could be an interesting avenue for future research.

Our study o<sup>f</sup>ers both theoretical and practical insights into paid knowledge sharing in emerging online communities. These communities di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional knowledge-sharing communities by pro viding more fragmented and speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge products and allowing all users to share knowledge. Previous research on knowledge sharing focused on knowledge contribution behavior [4,12,15,67] or knowl edge seeking [68,69], and there is a paucity of research examining purchase behavior related to emerging knowledge-sharing products in virtual communities. We extend the research on knowledge sharing by investigating the purchase behavior for paid knowledge-sharing products.

In addition, while much prior literature investigates free knowledge sharing, our research focuses on paid knowledge sharing in an online community, which involves a type of transaction. However, this transaction is di<sup>f</sup>erent from normal product sales in that (1) knowledge products are not commodities, but unique, and (2) knowledge products have some characteristics of experience products [26,27], meaning that potential customers have very limited information (or signals) about the products before they actually experience it, leading to information asymmetry [21]. Our research extends the traditional signaling theory by applying it to the emerging phenomenon of online paid knowledgesharing communities.

Finally, knowledge products di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional products in that the knowledge transfer process is crucial for the internalization of knowledge [70]. During this process, the audience rely on interactions with knowledge contributors, as this helps them better internalize the knowledge. Furthermore, from an SLT perspective, potential listeners could gain more information about the quality of the knowledge product by observing others or reading written information (others’ comments and the speaker’s replies) to learn about the quality of a live [18]. Online interactions between speakers and audiences are critical to the success of selling knowledge products.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study o<sup>f</sup>er a few interesting practical implications. First, our research identi<sup>fi</sup>es important factors that a<sup>f</sup>ect the sales of knowledge products, including price, replies from speakers, and review scores. By focusing on these important factors, online knowledge communities can boost the sales of knowledge products. Second, we investigated Zhihu Live, a platform that employs a two-stage sales model. Based on our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings, Zhihu Live could choose to adopt a dynamic pricing strategy that di<sup>f</sup>ers between phases as well as other promotional strategies. Finally, the results of our study suggest that the number of replies (i.e., the interaction between the speaker and the audience) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ects a live’s sales. Therefore, it would be wise to encourage this interaction during the knowledge-sharing process.
