## A B S T R A C T

Enterprise systems represent a strategic asset that enables an organization to integrate business processes and data throughout its organization. Firms often rely on consultants to transfer implementation knowledge to their employees and expect successful project outcome; however, widespread project failures raise concerns regarding whether the use of consultants are effective. This study examines whether mutual trust affects knowledge transfer effectiveness which then impacts project outcome. Building on psychological contract, knowledge management, and trust literature, the results of this matched-pair, field survey suggest knowledge transfer mediates the relationship between mutual trust and project outcome. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

Published by Elsevier B.V.

## 1. Introduction

A firm’s portfolio of information systems (IS) represents a critical and strategic organizational resource. Complex information systems, including enterprise systems, represent a strategic asset that enables an organization to integrate business processes and data throughout its entire organization, as well as with its suppliers, vendors, and customers. Given the size, magnitude, and potential risks associated with enterprise systems implementation projects, there have been many studies examining success factors [1,6,11,26].

Although numerous studies involving ‘‘lessons learned’’ of successes and failures of implementation of enterprise systems have been discussed and documented in the literature, one notable critical success factor is the use of external consultants to aid in the implementation via transfer of knowledge to business clients [4,20,31]. Knowledge required to successfully implement enterprise systems is not typically held by business clients. The most optimal and practical way to fill this gap is to draw on the expertise of external consultants who specialize in the implementation of enterprise systems. The transfer of implementation knowledge from consultants to business clients is critical for meeting the perceived needs of the client organization and for achieving a successful implementation outcome. Yet, the widespread failures of enterprise implementations suggest that practitioners are unsure how to leverage consultant’s knowledge and embed them into work routines and management practices, or the working relationship between external consultants and business clients prevents them from successfully transferring their knowledge which ultimately affects the success of the project.

These widespread failures raise concerns regarding whether the use of external consultants to aid business clients in the implementation projects are effective. Embedding knowledge into work routines and management practices is largely a function of absorptive capacity, or the ability for a business client to recognize and value the importance of externally-sourced knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it [5]. Absorptive capacity implies preexisting ‘‘stock of knowledge’’ that is brought to the implementation project – i.e., effective transfer of knowledge is dependent on existing knowledge base prior to the start of the project. In the implementation of enterprise systems, business clients often lack absorptive capacity; business clients typically do not have the implementation knowledge required to ensure successful project outcome.

It could also be that the development of a positive working relationship between business clients and external consultants plays an important role [32] particularly since implementation of enterprise systems typically last months or years. Relationships characterized by ease of communication and intimacy have shown to facilitate knowledge transfer, and relationships based on competence and benevolence trust have been found to positively impact knowledge transfer [19]. Hence, there is support suggesting that a positive working relationship influences knowledge transfer. However, relationship involving mutual trust has not been explored; prior studies have not examined the level and agreement of trust for one another. A better understanding of mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and their effects on enterprise systems implementation project outcome will aid both consultants and business clients, and the overall management, in delivering successful projects. Therefore, this study seeks to address the following research question: To what extent does mutually trusting relationship impact the transfer of knowledge, and ultimately project outcome? This study appears to be the first to integrate mutual trust with knowledge transfer for examining project outcome in the context of enterprise systems implementation projects involving external consultants. The results of this field survey suggest that knowledge transfer partially mediates the relationship between mutual trust and project outcomes.

The remainder of the paper is organized into five sections. First, a review of prior literature on psychological contract, trust, and knowledge transfer is presented. Second, a research model of mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and project outcome is developed. Third, methodology and the results of reliabilities, validities, factor analysis, and multiple regression analyses are presented. Fourth, implications are discussed. Finally, concluding remarks are offered.

## 2. Prior literature

Several research streams are useful for gaining an understanding about the relationships among mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and project outcome. Psychological contract literature is a useful lens for examining the role of trust in a sourcing relationship context; knowledge management literature provides a framework that examines knowledge transfer and its relationship with antecedents and performance; and information systems literature, specifically enterprise systems implementation, provides a vehicle and a context for understanding the roles of business clients and consultants in their attempt to successfully implement enterprise systems.

## 2.1. Psychological contract and trust

Although the use of external consultants has been identified as a critical success factor, management of IS projects involving external consultants continues to challenge organizations. An inevitable part of IS sourcing strategy involves the use of contracts to ensure expectations are met and obligations are satisfied by external consultants and business clients involved in projects. Researchers examining external consultants have considered the role of psychological contracts – unwritten contracts regarding contractual parties’ mental beliefs and expectations about their mutual obligations in a contractual relationship, and are largely based on perceived promises of a reciprocal exchange [28] – in affecting project outcomes. Moreover, psychological contracts have been considered in terms of unwritten and largely unspoken sets of congruent expectations held by the involved parties about each other’s prerogatives and obligations, consultants’ and clients’ expectations of one another’s obligations, and shared or mutual understanding about parties’ obligations [24].

Psychological contract is an important element of any complex business relationship, the objective of which is to facilitate cooperative work. The literature strongly suggests that trust must be placed in unwritten promises and obligations between the parties, and concludes that trust plays an important role in affecting knowledge transfer and project outcome [16].

Business clients rely on external consultants to transfer knowledge to facilitate successful project outcome, and trusting the other leads participants to work together for achieving project goals. Trust literature provides considerable evidence that trusting relationships lead to greater knowledge exchange – in other words, when trust exists, people are more willing to transfer knowledge with one another. Trust has found to facilitate voluntary exchange which promotes knowledge transfer activities suggesting that knowledge spillover benefits accrue through the development of trust.

Clearly, trust is an important element of psychological contract, and researchers have found it to positively influence transfer of knowledge [29]. In the IS sourcing context, an embedded relationship led to the creation and growth of inter-organizational resources – in other words, trusting relationships aid in transferring knowledge. Thus, it is possible to effectively transfer knowledge and attain project goals by informally regulating the behavior of individuals through the development of trust [8].

## 2.2. Knowledge transfer and performance

While various knowledge transfer frameworks have been proposed, the relationship between knowledge transfer and performance remains largely unexplored. In their theoretical framework, Argote and colleagues [3] note that very little empirical studies have examined the relationship between knowledge transfer and its impact on performance. Much of this literature embodies an intuitively appealing, but untested assumption that effective knowledge transfer improves performance outcomes.

Advocates of knowledge management claim that there are important implications for knowledge transfer and subsequent focal unit’s (i.e., individual, group, or organizational) performance. Researchers have stressed the importance of examining knowl edge transfer involving social relations, ‘‘fit’’ across many domains, and organizational boundaries, to name a few, as means to better understand how to improve focal unit’s performance.

Among the many IS contexts available, transfer of knowledge from consultants is seen as very important to a successful implementation of enterprise systems. Unlike traditional IS development-implementation situations, enterprise systems typically require a tight integration of business processes and data across various functions throughout an organization. Such tight integration among the various functions is made possible in part due to the important roles assumed by both business clients and consultants during the implementation process – consultants provide knowledge related to the enterprise applications whereas business clients provide in-depth knowledge of functional business processes. However, not much is known about the impact of knowledge transfer from consultants to business clients (i.e., users) in the context of enterprise systems – a setting where knowledge is primarily tacit, knowledge structure of the participants is asymmetrical, knowledge crosses organizational boundaries, and business clients assume a larger and more significant role. Hence, understanding the impact of knowledge transfer in enterprise systems implementations is important.

## 3.1. Knowledge transfer

Knowledge management and enterprise systems implementation literatures provide clue regarding the relationship between knowledge transfer and project outcome. In a study of supplier partnerships, researchers found that transfer of knowledge among buyers and suppliers of U.S. firms improved supplier performance [17]. They conclude that exchange of technical knowledge over a length of time promotes supplier performance improvement – in other words, knowledge transfer positively impacted performance. On the other hand, in a study of knowledge transfer of sales proposals in a management consulting firm, Haas and Hansen [9] found that knowledge transfer actually impaired performance – they found that sales teams that had varying learning and differentiation needs impacted performance negatively. These contradictory findings warrant additional research investigating the relationship between knowledge transfer and performance.

In the context of enterprise systems implementation projects, transfer of knowledge is particularly important due to its complexity. Unfortunately, there is a lack of empirical investigation regarding the impact of knowledge transfer on project outcome, prompting a call for additional studies. These implementation projects are commonly done in the context of ‘‘mixed’’ teams of consultants, business clients, and information systems specialists. In using consultants, organizations typically have specific goals, such as the ability to effectively implement a new system, but they also have the less tangible goals of acquiring new skills associated with on-going operations. The successful transfer of this knowledge to business clients is critical for meeting the perceived needs of the client organization, responding to changing business processes, on-going administration and maintenance, minimizing training, gaining new in-house capabilities, and deploying a high-quality system.

The domain of this study involves Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP, a complex enterprise system) implementation knowledge, or a set of rules, tools, and guidelines that accompanies a vendorspecific product (SAP, Oracle). Some examples of ERP knowledge include vendor-specific product, rules for manipulating configuration tables, and guidelines for producing test scripts. Thus, ERP implementation knowledge embodies those activities associated with configuring and testing ERP modules, and training client employees in preparation for on-going operation, maintenance, and support of the system [13].

In comparison to the implementation of internal, customdeveloped applications, where IS specialists assume major roles, business clients play a larger and more significant role in ERP implementations [14]. In fact, business clients typically outnumber IS specialist on ERP project teams. Their in-depth knowledge of functional business processes is an essential ingredient to effective decision-making throughout the implementation effort. To facilitate a successful implementation effort, therefore, transferring consultants’ implementation knowledge to business clients is critical and typically a part of the formal contract with a consulting firm. An effective knowledge transfer, therefore, allows business clients to increase their overall stock of implementation knowledge which effectively and meaningfully provide them the ability to ascertain whether the project met agree-upon objectives.

## 3.2. Mutual trust

A stable cooperative system is based on a strong development of trust, and there has been a growing interest in examining trust from information systems researchers in a variety of contexts. Many researchers utilize McAllister’s [23] trust framework which suggests that trust is comprised of two dimensions – affect and cognitive trust. Affect trust, or benevolence trust, is largely based on ‘‘emotional’’ bonds between individuals; in contrast, cognitive trust, or competence trust, is largely based on competence and responsibility.

Researchers typically conceptualize and measure trust as a single-respondent view, and includes overall or interpersonal trust [18]. One limitation to this approach is that trust operates not as a one-sided perception; rather, trust involves bi-directional perception held by both participants. Though a single-respondent view of interpersonal trust has been shown to be an important predictor to knowledge transfer, it is not clear whether a dual-respondent view of interpersonal trust will have a similar effect. Dual-respondent view differs from a single-respondent view because it involves both participants who may or may not display same level and agreement of trust about each other. Therefore, this study extends McAllister’s trust framework by examining a dual-respondent view of trust, or mutual trust.

Mutual trust represents the extent to which a clientconsultant dyad’s high level of trust for one another is in agreement. Trust is an intangible phenomenon involving two participants, and a reciprocal view plays an important role given their repeated exchange of benefits [37]. In an inter-organizational setting, it is possible that a business client may display competence trust towards a consultant, but the reverse may not be true. Hence, understanding mutual trust is important in determining whether a mutually trusting relationship influences knowledge transfer effectiveness, especially in a context where dependency on another is needed to acquire knowledge and make progress.

Trust is oftentimes developed through repeated interactions until sufficient knowledge is gained about a person to enable his or her behavior to be predictable [25]. Hence, relying on information gathered through repeated interactions, trust develops based on other’s predictability; enough information is needed to choose when to trust based upon ‘‘good reason’’ that another will behave cooperatively or predictably. Cooperative and predictable behaviors developed through repeated interactions increase the degree of risk incurred by each party to match both the perceived benefits and potential losses in their relationship.

Consequently, trust has a direct effect on an individual’s experiences because it implies an expectation from interpersonal interactions. Thus, when individuals trust each other, they are likely to reduce tension and ambiguity on their tasks. They are also likely to experience greater satisfaction in their interpersonal working relationship which then becomes a valuable asset [15]. On the other hand, when experiences do not meet the expectation from interpersonal interactions, held even by one party, not only is trust likely to deteriorate but their interpersonal working relationship become a liability leading to sub-optimal outcomes.

Repeated interactions are a necessary condition, but not sufficient, in developing mutual trust because it involves both parties being in constant contact with each other, exchanging information about needs, preferences, and approaches to problems. Repeated interactions, therefore, contribute to understanding and predictability; they provide opportunities to develop relationship, learn, and gain additional knowledge about the other party [33]. Thus, when a client-consultant dyad displays high levels of trust toward one another, their repeated interactions will help accomplish project tasks and goals.

In the context of enterprise systems implementations, a clientconsultant dyadic relationship crosses organizational boundaries. Differences abound between these two individuals given that they belong to their respective organizations, and include culture, work approaches, standards, incentives, and expertise, to name a few. Though these differences make it especially challenging, repeated interactions assist both business clients and consultants in predicting other’s behaviors. Over time, as they work to implement enterprise systems, their cooperative and predictable behaviors lead to a set of expectations regarding the need to transfer knowledge and accomplish project goals and objectives. Their cooperative and predictable behaviors occur, in part, due to mutual trust. Thus, mutual trust enables the client-consultant dyad to develop a deeper relationship, collaborate, and facilitate voluntary exchange to effectively transfer knowledge and work towards satisfying project goals [34], similar to how mutual trust between top management and IS personnel is important to the development of competitive advantage [21].

## 3.3. Knowledge transfer as a mediator

Mutually trusting relationships influence enterprise systems implementation project outcome primarily because they influence how effectively knowledge is transferred over the course of the implementation project; the effects of mutually trusting relationships on enterprise systems implementation projects outcome are mediated by knowledge transfer effectiveness. Because enterprise systems implementation contracts are typically long-term, trust plays an important role in affecting project outcomes. Trust is a state-of-mind that allows individuals to effectively coordinate and collaborate with one another for accomplishing project goals; however, such coordination and collaboration provide means to fill in knowledge gaps that contribute to successful enterprise systems implementation project outcomes. Therefore, it would appear that mutual trust influences knowledge transfer for improving project outcome.

Business clients draw on the expertise of consultants to obtain the implementation knowledge that is not held within the organization but is needed to deploy the system. Greater trust placed on consultants’ competence suggests an increased level of confidence and credibility in realizing successful project outcome [35]. However, this condition is likely more pronounced when business clients learned a great deal because trusting consultants’ domain-specific competence is directly relevant to receipt of useful knowledge and the task at hand, and the anticipated value arising from applying said knowledge.

Mutual benevolence trust increases interaction frequency, opens up communication, and facilitates cooperation which allows knowledge providers and receivers in an organizational setting to engage in dialogues for accomplishing project goals. In the presence of greater mutual benevolence trust, there is a desire for a trustee (e.g., mentor) to care and help a trustor (e.g., mentee) though there is no extrinsic reward for the trustee; there is a vested interest to care and help a trustor accomplish project goals, much like an advisor working with doctoral students. In other words, a trustee has the willingness and the desire to do good to the trustor above and beyond an egocentric motive.

Egocentric motive implies lacking care and placing one’s own needs first. In contrast, non-egocentric motive indicates that the intrinsic value placed on benevolence trust allows a trustee to go out of her way to help a trustor. Thus, mutual benevolence trust helps reduce risks by developing a ‘‘closer’’ working relationship ‘‘. . .based on loyalty that goes beyond the typical business objective. . .’’ [7] which provides greater opportunities to transfer knowledge required to accomplish project goals.

In implementing an enterprise solution, for example, a consultant displaying benevolence trust will take the necessary steps to help a business client acquire the implementation knowledge needed to successfully apply it. When situations arise, such consultant will take additional measures to help a business client and ensure that his project goals are met. In turn, a business client recognizes that a consultant is acting in his best interest which makes it easier to apply said knowledge with greater confidence in meeting stated goals. Mutual benevolence trust is important to the transfer of knowledge; moreover, high level of mutual benevolence trust means that both parties are willing to go out of their way to help each other accomplish project goals.

Hypothesis 1a. Mutual benevolence trust positively impacts project outcome, and knowledge transfer mediates this relationship.

Hypothesis 1b. Mutual competence trust positively impacts project outcome, and knowledge transfer mediates this relationship.

## 4. Methodology

Surveys can be useful when a researcher needs to collect data on phenomena that cannot be directly observed, assess attitudes, and characteristics of a wide range of subjects, gather information on a sample or population, or determine the relationships between two or more factors – all of which this study attempts to accomplish. To empirically test the research model, a matched-pair questionnaire surveys involving business clients and consultants of ERP implementation projects were designed using existing scales where available. Two separate questionnaires were developed to reduce potential threats from common methods bias. Business clients (recipients of implementation knowledge) were asked about knowledge transfer and project outcome. To assess trust, data were gathered from both consultants (providers of implementation knowledge) and business clients.

## 4.1. Organizations and projects

Respondents were identified by asking IS project managers belonging to a local Project Management Institute (PMI) Chapter in which the author held membership. Those expressing interest in the research project were given participation requirements and asked to identify appropriate projects and survey respondents. Project selection was based on three criteria. The first was the desire to collect data about ERP projects that were in the operational implementation or post-implementation phase [22] within the last twelve months. This timeframe was considered important to ensure that project outcome could be evaluated and with reasonable memory recall. For this study, each project consisted of an implementation of one specific ERP module such as General Ledger. Second, a consultant and a business client ‘‘matched-pair’’ involved in the stated ERP project must have assumed his or her role for at least fifty percent of the duration of the project. Fifty percent was seen as a reasonable length of time for assessing trust. Third, and finally, because some constructs were measured from the consultant perspective and some from the business client perspective, access to completed surveys from both consultant and business client matched-pair for each project was required; data was discarded for those whose surveys were incomplete or where only one participant responded. Surveys were administered through the Internet, and dynamic web pages were utilized to capture consultant, client, and ERP module names. Thus, participant’s name (e.g., ‘‘Consultant Brianna’’), matching-pair participant’s name (e.g., ‘‘Client Alisha’’), and the ERP module name (e.g., ‘‘General Ledger’’) were displayed in the appropriate survey items to improve the specificity of the question and the quality of the data (see Appendix A).

## 4.2. Respondents

To avoid any potential problems, steps were taken to ensure appropriate individuals completed the surveys. The business client was defined as an employee of a client organization, a member of the project team for a given ERP module, a ‘‘power user’’ who is most knowledgeable about the functional business processes, and an individual who would be responsible for the ownership and management of his respective ERP module after implementation. In this sample, business clients held titles including financial manager, inventory manager, and purchasing manager. A consultant was defined as an employee of a consulting or vendor firm, a member of the same project team as its business client counterpart, an individual most knowledgeable with the said ERP module, and one who interacts primarily with the ‘‘power user’’ as described above. Individuals in this sample held titles such as IS Consultant and IS Manager, and they worked three to 20 months with each other on said ERP module (mean = 8.68 months; s.d. = 3.71 months).

## 4.3. Questionnaire development and mutual trust score computation

Instrument items for each scale can be found in Appendix A. Most scales were derived from earlier work and adapted to this study. The measure of project outcome is based on scales described in Wallace and colleagues [36]. The three items reflect whether the project was of high quality, met functional requirements, and satisfied users’ needs.

The measure of knowledge transfer was newly developed due to lack of available existing items. To overcome challenges associated with developing new items, two iterative, parallel steps were taken. First, field interviews were conducted with both consultants and business clients who were engaged in an ERP implementation effort. One primary objective was to create a list of measures for assessing knowledge transfer in the context of ERP implementation. Second, perusal of the knowledge management literature suggests that knowledge transfer, in fact, can be evaluated by assessing changes to performance derived from said knowledge or assessing changes to the stock of knowledge in the minds of recipients [2]. In conjunction with prior literature and interview findings, and consistent with the notion of knowledge transfer used in this study, six items were developed to measure knowledge transfer. The six items reflect whether business clients learned and applied said knowledge – i.e., changes to the stock of knowledge and performance derived from acquiring knowledge. The focus of this study involves transfer of implementation knowledge from consultants to business clients. Thus, items developed and used in this study include knowledge associated with configuration, testing, and training – activities largely assumed by business clients. Technical activities such as installation, network, and data conversion, are usually performed by IS specialists.

The measures of benevolence and competence trust are based on McAllister’s scales. The four items for benevolence trust reflect whether trust is largely based upon ‘‘emotional’’ bonds including honesty, care, reliance, and dependency; the four items for competence trust reflect whether trust is largely based on competence including professionalism, responsibility, and respect.

The measures associated with mutual trust were calculated using benevolence and competence trust measures by adopting an approach used by Johnson and colleagues [12]. To obtain mutual trust scores, level of trust and level of agreement were evaluated. Level of trust refers to the extent to which both a client and a consultant expressed his or her trust for one another, and was computed by summing up the averages of competence and benevolence measures. For example, if Client Alisha’s and Consultant Brianna’s responses to benevolence trust were 3.5 and 4.25, respectively, then their level of trust score for benevolence trust would be 7.75. Level of agreement emphasizes the importance of shared identity, personal ties, and other forms of trust, and represents the extent to which both parties agree about their trust in each other. Level of agreement score addresses the extent to which Client Alisha’s trust towards Consultant Brianna is same or different from that of Consultant Brianna’s trust towards Client Alisha. Using the same example above, level of agreement score for Client Alisha and Consultant Brianna’s benevolence trust is 4.25 (calculated as 5 – ABS(3.5–4.25)). ‘‘5’’ is the upper bound used to denote high level of agreement (e.g., Client Alisha’s ‘‘2.33’’ and Consultant Brianna’s ‘‘2.33’’ would suggest a distance of zero (2.33–2.33 = 0) indicating a perfect agreement, or a score of ‘‘5’’). To arrive at a mutual trust score, level of trust and level of agreement as described were combined using the following equation: mutual trust score = level of trust - level of agreement, where a higher score indicates greater mutual trust, or high levels of trust and agreement.

## 4.4. Validation

Standard psychometric techniques were followed in validating the measures. Initial Cronbach’s alpha scores were all above recommended cut-off of 0.70, suggesting the scales are reliable. Factor analysis was performed to evaluate convergent and discriminant validities. A single factor analysis was performed, using a principle component method of extraction with a varimax rotation. Components with eigenvalues greater than one were extracted. All items loaded on their intended factors.

## 4.6. Data analysis

Correlations between constructs are shown in Table 4. Not surprisingly, we see a significant correlation between Knowledge Transfer and Project Outcome as well a significant correlation between Mutual Benevolence Trust and Project Outcome and between Mutual Competence Trust and Project Outcome. Both Mutual Benevolence Trust and Mutual Competence Trust correlated significantly with Knowledge Transfer.

Before proceeding to examine our research model, several tests were conducted to check the assumptions behind regression modeling. The first test assessed whether the error terms are normally distributed. For the dependent variable, residuals were plotted against their expected values. Each plot produced a reasonably straight line, suggesting that this assumption holds. A second test checked for outliers. Scatter plots of each independent variable against each dependent variable were generated and examined. There were no outliers. Therefore, all data points were retained and the research model examined, as described below. In order to test the research model, a series of regressions were conducted using the Hayes and Preacher [10] approach for mediation analysis. Specifically, their MEDIATE macro for SPSS was used (http://www.afhayes.com/spss-sas-and-mplus-macrosand-code.html). MEDIATE was chosen for two reasons: (1) it has the ability to handle multiple independent variables simultaneously (and the research model has two independent variables), and (2) it uses a bootstrapping approach for testing the significance of indirect effects.

The bootstrapping approach was chosen [27,30] for testing the significance of indirect effects over the more traditional Sobel’s test of indirect effects because the bootstrapping approach has become more popular recently. The bootstrapping approach is viewed as superior because Sobel’s test assumes that the sampling distribu tion of the indirect effect (ab) is normal, when in fact it is often skewed leading to biased estimates. Thus, Sobel’s test has been criticized for being overly conservative. Bootstrapping overcomes this problem. Bootstrapping involves repeatedly sampling with replacement from the dataset and estimating the indirect effect in each resampled dataset. By resampling thousands of times, the sampling distribution for ab can be approximated and used to construct confidence intervals for the indirect effect. In the analysis, the number of samples was set to 10,000.

Following the approach described above, three regressions were performed: (1) the independent variables (Mutual Benevolence Trust and Mutual Competence Trust) and a covariate (Project Size) predicting the dependent variable (Project Outcome), (2) the independent variables and a covariate predicting the mediator (Knowledge Transfer), and (3) the independent variables (IVs), the mediator, and a covariate predicting the dependent variable (DV). Mediation is established when the IVs are found to have significant effects on the DV and the mediator respectively in the first two regression equations. Further, if the IVs have a significant effect on the DV in the presence of the mediator in the third regression equation, and the effect of the IVs on the DV is less in the third equation than in the first, then partial mediation is indicated, whereas if only the first two steps are met full mediation is indicated. ## 4.7. Results

As indicated in Table 5, both mutual benevolence trust and mutual competence trust had significant effects on both project outcome (Model 1) and knowledge transfer (Model 2). Knowledge Transfer had a significant effect on project outcome (Model 3), and the effects of mutual benevolence trust and mutual competence trust on project outcome were significant even in the presence of the Mediator (knowledge transfer), though their effects were weakened relative to the direct effects observed in Model 1. These results suggest that the effects of mutual benevolence trust and mutual competence trust on project outcome are partially mediated by knowledge transfer.

In order to test the significance of the indirect effects, I examined bias corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. The indirect effect of mutual benevolence trust on project outcome was 0.009 and this effect was found to be statistically significant as the 95% confidence interval (0.001, 0.022) did not contain zero. Similarly, the indirect effect of mutual competence trust on project outcome was 0.006 and this effect was found to be statistically significant as the 95% confidence interval (0.001, 0.017) did not contain zero.

The results provide support for the proposed hypotheses. Specifically, Hypothesis 1a was supported; knowledge transfer was found to partially mediate the relationship between mutual benevolence trust and project outcome. The indirect effect of mutual benevolence trust on project outcome was 0.009 while the direct effect in the presence of the mediator was 0.038. Based on the results, 19.0% of the total effect of mutual benevolence trust on project outcome was carried indirectly through knowledge transfer. Hypothesis 1b was also supported; knowledge transfer was found to partially mediate the relationship between mutual competence trust and project outcome. The indirect effect of mutual competence trust on project outcome was 0.006 while the direct effect in the presence of the mediator was 0.023. Based on the results, 20.6% of the total effect of mutual competence trust on project outcome was carried indirectly through knowledge transfer. A control variable, project size, was found to be insignificant.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The results of this study suggest that knowledge transfer partially mediates the relationship between mutual benevolence and competence trusts and project outcome. In addition to anecdotal evidences in the literature, the results of this survey study demonstrate that an effective transfer of knowledge can in fact lead to improved project outcome. This study appears to be one of the first empirical studies to examine the impact of knowledge transfer on project outcome in an enterprise systems implementation setting. Given that knowledge asymmetry and barrier issues are endemic to much of the IT implementation setting, the findings of this study hopefully shed some insight in raising awareness of the importance of effectively transferring knowledge for successfully implementing complex information systems.

The results also indicate that a mutual trust relationship between a client-consultant dyad affects a knowledge receiver’s ability to learn and apply that knowledge held by a knowledge provider. Though the majority of prior research reported on a single-respondent view of trust, this study provides a more comprehensive approach to examining trust – a dual-respondent view – while offering additional insight. Many prior studies examined a single-respondent view of trust; this study offers additional supporting evidence relating mutual trust with knowledge transfer, as well as extend previous findings into IS domain.

Before discussing the results of this study, some of its limitations should be considered. This will be followed by implications for research and practice emanating from this study.

## 5.1. Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. The first limitation is the relatively small sample size. Gathering matched-pair survey data is challenging; however, sample size of 80 compares well with other matched-pair survey studies. Second, cross-sectional studies face a limitation of capturing only a snap-shot of knowledge transfer activities which may change through the implementation process. As such, for example, an assessment of project outcome could only tap into initial benefits derived from the implementation effort. Third, the findings of this study may reasonably be claimed to generalize to the enterprise systems implementation domain. Fourth, business clients responded to knowledge transfer (mediating variable) and project outcome (dependent variable) variables. It was determined that the business clients were the better respondent of the two for assessing these two variables. Consultants may unnecessarily inflate their responses to these two constructs rendering their responses unusable. Although not ideal, it was seen as a reasonable approach to collect this data from the more appropriate respondent – business clients. Fifth, this study examined implementation knowledge–a set of rules, tools, and guidelines that produce effective systems implementation. Accordingly, this study focused on project outcome with emphasis placed on quality (e.g., meeting requirements) instead of outcomes associated with budget and schedule. Sixth, this study examined trust as the only antecedent. Clearly, there are other antecedents to knowledge transfer. However, the goal of this study was to extend prior work by examining mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and project outcome. Seventh, and finally, projects in the operational implementation or post-implementation phases within the last twelve months were considered. Though the outcome of the project could differ after a certain period of time, a twelve-month window was deemed reasonable due to memory recall and two phases ensured that project outcome could be evaluated; nevertheless, it is a limitation to consider when interpreting the results of this study.

## 5.2. Implications for research

The results of this study suggest a number of implications for researchers. Prior studies have discussed the importance of examining the knowledge transfer-to-performance link, and few empirical investigations have emerged. Unfortunately, results have not been consistent. This study found that knowledge transfer plays an important role in impacting project outcome. Equipping business clients with implementation knowledge offered by consultants provided the necessary means to deliver a highquality system and the ability to sustain on-going operations. Additional studies are needed and in different contexts to determine whether the effectiveness of knowledge transfer leads to improved project outcomes.

Knowledge transfer partially mediates the relationship between mutual trust and project outcome. Though prior literature suggests a relationship between trust and knowledge transfer as well as trust and performance, this study provides empirical evidence in the IS domain that mutually trusting relationship indirectly impacts project outcomes through greater knowledge transfer. 19.0% and 20.6% of total effects of mutual benevolence trust and mutual competence trust, respectively, on project outcome were carried indirectly through knowledge transfer; as knowledge providers and receivers engage in professional and social exchanges, about one-fifth of total effects of mutual trusts on project outcome were carried through the transfer of knowledge. Future research could examine other factors that improve knowledge transfer effectiveness for assisting projects to be of high quality and one that meets the requirements and satisfies users’ needs.

The results of this study also provide insight regarding the role of competence and benevolence trust with knowledge transfer and project outcome. Though both mutual benevolence and mutual competence trusts positively influenced knowledge transfer and project outcome, mutual benevolence trust impacted them to a greater extent relative to mutual competence trust. In the context of consulting engagements, there exists a paradox: to successfully implement an enterprise system, external consultants are drawn upon due to their expertise, suggesting the importance of competence trust; yet benevolence trust plays more significant role in effectively transferring knowledge and influencing project outcome. Mutual benevolence trust, or level and agreement of personal attachment and ‘‘emotional’’ bonding, greatly influenced their desire to help one another learn and see to it that project objectives are met. One plausible explanation is that the increased level of interaction, communication, and cooperation on a social dimension puts both members at a greater risk in the relationship when compared to competence trust. Thus, it could be that the importance of benevolence trust outweighs competence trust. Additional studies are needed to explore this interesting finding.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

As IT implementations grow in number, size, and complexity, IS and business managers increasingly need to better understand how to facilitate knowledge transfer within and across organizational boundaries, and improve the success of the project. This study provides guidance for firms undertaking enterprise systems implementation projects with the assistance of external consultants. For example, with a growing number of virtual teams and global information systems development outsourcing activities, this study suggests that managers must consider the importance of developing mutual benevolence trust despite challenges associated with crossing organizational, geographical, and cultural boundaries.

To ensure project success, this study demonstrates the importance of developing and building both benevolence and competence trust between consultants and business clients for effectively transferring knowledge, which collectively impact project outcome. In addition to staffing the most ‘‘knowledgeable’’ members from both firms, care must be taken to manage the tiesthat-bind between the parties. Development of benevolence trust, or personal attachment or ‘‘emotional’’ bond, was found to play an important role in improving the effectiveness of knowledge transfer which impacts delivery of a high-quality system. It is imperative for firms seeking consultants’ expertise to develop and support team-building and social programs that facilitate and nurture personalized relationship. Recall, interaction frequency and open communication assist in developing benevolence trust. Thus, co-locating team members as well as building a team with similar personal interests should ultimately help with the overall project success, especially when it may be tempting to design a virtual team to reduce cost and take advantage of available communication technologies.

Effective transfer of knowledge is an important consideration for ensuring a successful project outcome. The ability for consultants to provide appropriate knowledge to business clients suggests that it is of utmost importance that competent and ‘‘emotionally-reliable’’ consultants are assigned to the engagement if the project is to have a better chance of success. Herein lies a paradox: benevolence trust typically develops through repeated interactions, but it is not uncommon for consultants to develop expertise through redeployment into other projects and clients. For consultants, rotating externally is an important consideration especially for developing industry expertise which is seen as a key success factor. This raises an important question for consulting firms as they develop talent and engage in renewing project engagements: How do consulting firms enhance project success when developing expertise may be at the cost of potentially severing benevolence trust? For client firms, this study suggests that compromising who is assigned to the project at hand may determine the success or failure of the project. Thus, ensuring that ‘‘key’’ consultants remain throughout the project or in a future project will likely reduce the risk of an unfavorable project outcome.

It is also important to encourage a development of strong competence trust if exploration of new ideas and techniques are in order; however, weak competence trust ought to be promoted when exploiting existing ideas. Nevertheless, a delicate balance in managing competence trust is an important consideration for managers overseeing IT projects.

## 6. Conclusion

This study examined the relationships among mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and project outcome involving consultants and business clients in inter-firm complex enterprise systems implementation projects by drawing on psychology, knowledge management, trust, and information systems literatures. The results indicate that knowledge transfer partially mediates the relationship between mutual trust and project outcome. The findings offered in this study augment prior research to an increasingly important information systems context. Given the growing complexity around deployment of IT-based solutions, the findings of this study shed new insight regarding the importance of mutual trust, knowledge transfer, and project outcome; this study provides insight to the importance of knowledge transfer and mutual trust in delivering a successful project.
