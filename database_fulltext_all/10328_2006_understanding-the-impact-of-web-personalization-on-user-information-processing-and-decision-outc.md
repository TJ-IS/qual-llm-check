## Introduction

Companies worldwide are using the web to interact with their customers for brand promotion, product marketing, order fulfillment and after-sales support. Competition is keen among online merchants and content providers. $^{2}$ To remain competitive, online merchants have been adopting differentiation strategies to attract and retain customers.

A common differentiation strategy is to personalize web content to serve users' needs. This is accomplished by deploying intelligent personalization agents to collect and analyze web activities and transactions to generate highly adaptive content for individual users (Mobasher et al. 2000). For example, Amazon.com greets returning customers with a personalized message and offers a hyperlink to book recommendations congruent with their past purchases. Google search engine generates side banners of related items in real-time based on key words entered by the users.

While there is a lot of hype about delivering personalized services over the web and the stakes are high for vendors selling related products, $^{3}$ little is known about the effectiveness of web personalization and the link between the information technology artifact (the personalization agent) and the effects it exerts on users. On one hand, researchers (Ardissono et al. 2002; Kleinmuntz and Schkade 1993; Perkowitz and Etzioni 2000; Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994; Te'eni and Feldman 2001) suggest that a high level of information adaptation will eliminate disruptive interruptions. The result is a shift of information control to users, allowing them to specify and control their interactions with online merchants. On the other hand, recent research demonstrates that the ability to exert influences through web content can indeed empower online merchants. For example, Mandel (2002) reported that even a minor change in the background color of web content can have an impact on purchase decisions.

In contrast to the proliferation of personalized web services worldwide, work by IS researchers in the area is scant. There is little theoretical work in the literature that considers the relationships between personalized content and the cognitive, behavioral, and decision measures of users. Success in devising differentiation strategies through web personalization hinges on a clear understanding of its effectiveness. Research on web personalization will provide online merchants with a deeper understanding on the different personalization approaches to maximize user satisfaction and business opportunities.

In this study, we develop a conceptualization of web personalization that highlights factors underlying personalization strategies and theorize their impact on the different stages of information processing of a user. Our conclusions are drawn from two studies. First, we conducted a control lab experiment that focused on three variables hypothesized to attract users' attention, affect their level of cognitive processing, and bias their decisions. Second, to supplement the lab results, a field study based on a music download site and lasting for 6 weeks was conducted. In the field study, we examined users' behaviors by analyzing their web activities. Contents of the music site were driven by a commercial personalization agent and all activities of the web site were logged for the entire 6-week period. While both studies pertained to web personalization, the variables used in each study were adjusted to reflect the different granularity of measurements and the duration of the study.

This paper is organized as follows: In the next section, we present an overview of personalization research and review the different approaches to web personalization. The theoretical model and related hypotheses are then introduced. The model consists of a high-level conceptualization of the relationships between web personalization and the processing goal, cognitive effort, decision outcome, and evaluation of a user. The fourth section outlines the setup of the two studies. Findings of each study are then presented. The remaining sections discuss the limitations of the work and the theoretical and practical implications of the study, and present the conclusions.

## Web Personalization and Personalization Agents ■

Web content can be conceptualized as a mix of stimuli that take the form of text, images, audio, animations or video. In the course of interacting with a web page, a user is subjected to the influences of these stimuli. There is increasing interest among researchers in identifying relationships between web stimuli and user behavior (Geissler et al. 2001; Hong et al. 2004; Jamiszewski 1998; Kim et al. 2002; Lim and Benbasat 2002; Moon 2002; Shim et al. 2002; Zhang 2000). While not framed in the context of web personalization, these studies shed light on the intimate relationship between web content, processing goals, and user behavior. In addition, research on flow indicates that an interactive and stimulating experience is likely to induce a flow experience, which in turn leads to more exploratory behavior (Chen et al. 1999; Koufaris 2002; Koufaris et al. 2001; Novak et al. 2000; Webster and Hayes 1997; Webster and Martocchio 1992). These findings open up opportunities for online merchants to personalize their web content to the needs and tastes of individual users.

Web personalization generally refers to the process of adapting web content to meet the specific needs of users and to maximize business opportunities (Korper and Ellis 2001). Simply put, the goal of web personalization is to deliver the right content to the right person at the right time to maximize immediate and future business opportunities. To achieve this, a personalization strategy needs to meet two objectives. The first objective is to control the content, presentation format, and timing of personalized messages or offers to induce a favorable response to the merchant's offerings. This pertains to immediate purchase decisions. The second objective is to increase the likelihood of accepting the firm's offerings in the future by implanting marketing messages in the user's mind. This seeks to influence a user's future purchase decisions.

There are different approaches to web personalization, ranging from user-driven personalization to transaction- and context-driven personalization strategies (see Appendix A for a description of each personalization strategy). Presently, the main approaches are user-driven and transaction-driven strategies (Reimers 2000). As online merchants begin to harness the power of personalization agents, we expect widespread adoption of context-driven personalization.

Personalization agents are the IT enabler of web personalization. A personalization agent is a collection of software modules used to generate unique content to individual users. With the help of personalization agents, online merchants can manipulate content-related parameters at a very fine level previously not possible. Increasingly, these agents are able to adapt to the context of interaction, making context-driven personalization a reality. Some examples of context-driven personalization include displaying banners of complementary items by inspecting the shopping cart of a user to maximize cross-sell opportunities; using name-greeting personalized banners to divert the attention of users to induce elaboration of the banner's message; and structuring the index of product pages in real time based on click stream analysis to minimize the user's search efforts. These are just a few examples of the many choices available for online merchants to implement their personalization strategies. However, there is a lack of understanding on the effectiveness of these strategies, and there is no organized way to relate these strategies to a general conceptualization of web personalization. The current work attempts to develop such a conceptualization and to understand the effectiveness of personalization manipulations on the information processes and decision outcomes of users.

## Conceptual Framework and Research Model

To develop the conceptual framework for the current work, we build on the extant literature in social cognition and consumer decision research. Social cognition is relevant because it involves the study of how an individual constructs his/her social reality, which, to a large extent, is the result of the individual's cognitive outcome in processing sensory stimuli. Social cognition forms the theoretical foundation for the study of communication and persuasion (Cacioppo and Petty 1979; Haugtvedt and Wegener 1994), impression formation (Wyer 2002; Wyer et al. 1995), and human judgment (Albarracin and Wyer 2000; Hong and Wyer 1990; Lynch and Srull 1982). According to Wyer and Srull (1989, p. 2), “the focus of social cognition is on the cognitive mechanisms that mediate judgment and behavior.” The cognitive perspective of information processing allows a systematic investigation of the effectiveness of web personalization. Also, findings on the peculiar properties of the self in social cognition are of particular relevance to the current study.

We also make reference to consumer decision research to understand choice decisions in an online environment. Extant work has shown that a consumer's choice is shaped by the way humans process information and the properties of the task environment (Huber and Seiser 2001). We follow this contingent paradigm and focus on how a personalization agent can exert influence on users through the manipulation of web stimuli.

We take the perspective that users respond to web stimuli based on a very adaptive information processing mode as suggested in social cognition research (Wyer and Srull 1989). Figure 1 shows the conceptual framework that serves as the foundation for our theory development. There are two aspects of the model. First, it adopts a stage approach to information processing as postulated in the social cognition literature. Second, it highlights the cognitive constructs that are relevant to the study of web personalization.

The boxes above the dashed line of Figure 1 represent observable items including web stimuli (e.g., personalized banners), behavior (e.g., click patterns), and decisions (e.g., whether or not to accept the offers generated by the personalization agent). The boxes under the dashed line represent cognitive processes that are not directly observable.

## User Information Processing and Memory Organization

As shown in Figure 1, personalized web content in the form of visual or audio stimuli are generated by the personalization agent. They are intercepted by the user's sensors. However, not all stimuli are attended to and processed by the user. Whether a web stimulus catches the user's attention depends on its saliency relative to its context (Chimera and Shneiderman 1994; Nielsen 2000). During processing, the user will try to make sense of the stimulus by encoding and categorizing the stimulus based on prior knowledge. Knowledge is organized in the form of schema in permanent memory. Permanent memory is the location where schemas are stored over the long term. To process a web stimulus, a user will retrieve relevant schema from permanent memory into working memory. Working memory is the location where relevant schemas related to a particular processing goal reside for inferencing and judgment. Entries to working memory are only temporary. In the event a schema needs to be modified or augmented based on the new information received, updating will take place in working memory and the updated schema will be written back to permanent memory.

One schema that is particularly relevant to web personalization is the self schema (Wyer and Srull 1989). The self schema contains information about oneself, including perceptions, attributes, and experiences related to the self. Since the self is involved in cognitive processing and because of its chronic existence in working memory, concepts related to the self are likely to exert a strong influence on memory recall, judgment, and behavior (Hunt and McDaniel 1993; Klein and

Loftus 1988; Kuiper and Rogers 1979). Personalized web stimuli that activate the self schema are likely to attract attention and be processed to a larger extent.

## Preference Structure Construction

Providing individual users with what they want is a core principle of personalization. It follows that understanding users' preferences and providing offers that fit those preferences is a basic premise of any personalization strategy. However, evidence is mounting that preferences are not static and purchases are not necessarily driven by well-formed preference structures (Bettman et al. 1998; Slovic 1995). Instead, preferences are fluid and are constructed on the fly by users, especially in situations where users are not knowledgeable about the choice options (Yaniv 2004). As such, the construction process is subject to influences such as framing of alternatives (Iyengar and Lepper 2000; Levin and Gaeth 1988; Parikh et al. 2001), trust toward the seller (Lord et al. 1979), trust toward the agent (Wang and Benbasat 2005), and the user interface of the information system (Hong et al. 2004; Lim and Benbasat 2000; Schiaffino and Amandi 2004).

Accordingly, to the extent an online merchant can understand the spontaneous preferences of individual users, the factors that shape these preferences will provide the merchant with significant competitive advantage over its rivals. The increasing level of interactivity provided by IT at each touch point (Rayport and Jaworski 2005) and the vast amount of data collected on customers allow online merchants to learn and influence their users' spontaneous preferences. These preference structures do not necessarily reside in permanent memory, ready to be retrieved. Instead, they are created and reside in working memory in a choice process as shown in Figure 1. The notion of a constructed preference structure implies that choices are highly contingent on the construction process, which itself is susceptible to the influence of external stimuli. Bettman et al. (1998) remarked that, "One important property of this view point is that preferences will often be highly context dependent" (p. 188). Given the limited working memory and computational capabilities of humans, preferences are susceptible to influences of web stimuli generated by a personalization agent.

The staged information processing model together with the notion of dynamic preference construction form the basis of our conceptual framework in understanding web personalization. It also highlights the interaction between web stimuli, the highly developed self schema, and the fluid nature of preference structures, which drives behavior and decisions.

## Research Model

Figure 1 presents the major building blocks of our conceptual framework. Given this backdrop, the research model shown in Figure 2 presents the specific variables used to address our research questions.

The research model depicts the different stages of web processing as (1) attention, (2) cognitive processing, (3) decision, and (4) evaluation of decision. It also highlights two sets of variables hypothesized to have an impact on these four stages. They are variables related to (1) web personalization and (2) the user's goal specificity. The two sets of variables are further elaborated in the following sections.

The essence of web personalization is captured by two variables in the model: self reference and content relevance. They form the basis of a wide range of web personalization strategies widely practiced in the industry. We will discuss each of them in turn.

## Web Personalization: Self Reference

The self is a fundamental concept in psychology and has been studied since the dawn of the discipline. From an information processing perspective, the self is found to be special in terms of the way information related to the self is processed. According to Kircher et al. (2000), “Processing of self-relevant information and self knowledge is regarded as distinct from processing objective information” (p. 133). Among the many peculiar properties related to the self is the self-referent effect as reported in the seminal work by Rogers et al. (1977). Self-referent effect refers to the beneficial effects of encoding information related to oneself because self-reference encoding produces a more elaborate memory trace than other forms of encoding.

Self reference in web personalization refers to personalized web content associated with the self or past episodic experience of the user. The self schema is a well-developed schema and is chronically accessible. It captures the different facets of oneself including autobiography, values, experiences, family, social group affiliations, moods, emotions, and attitudes. Associating web stimuli with the self is a common practice of web personalization. It is common to include cues (e.g., the user's name in a greeting message) that connect to personally relevant concepts. A greeting message such as "Dear John, welcome back to giftshop.com. I hope you enjoyed the latest release of Harry Potter in your last purchase" is an example of a self-referent message. It greets the user by his name and refers to a previous experience. Another example is the posting of messages that are relevant to the processing goal of the user such as banners congruent with the user's browsing traits. Also, in a choice setting, web sites frequently put recommendation items under a distinct category labeled as "personalized recommendations" or "unique offers to you." These are self-referent content generated by a personalization agent to activate concepts stored in the self-schema.

Recent neuroimaging analysis indicates that distinct neural localizations of the brain are associated with the processing of the self (Kircher et al. 2000; Turk et al. 2002). Kelley et al.

(2002) noted that “self-referential processing is functionally dissociable from other forms of semantic processing within the human brain” (p. 785). These findings suggest that the sensory mechanism processes messages related to oneself in a different way, resulting in discrepancy in response to self-referent versus non-self-referent messages. For example, the spontaneous reaction to stimuli related to the self is best illustrated by the “cocktail party” effect—the ability of a person to detect his/her name being called above the background noise of a cocktail party. Bargh (1982) has demonstrated that human attention is spontaneously diverted to descriptions or messages that are strongly associated with the self. Given the saliency of self-referent messages in spontaneous responses, we hypothesize the following:

## H1: Users attend to self-referent web content to a larger extent than they attend to non-self-referent web content.

Extant work on self-referent effects is related to the mnemonic aspects of the self. Rogers et al. (1977) were the first to report that information encoded using the self is associated with better memory recall. Two popular explanations are offered in the literature for this mnemonic gain. One has its root in the depth of processing theory based on the work of Craik and Tulving (1975), which postulates that the self is a cognitive construct that facilitates the encoding and retrieval of information. According to the theory, recall is determined by the depth and extensiveness of processing. The better recall of self-referent messages reflects a “deeper” processing facilitated by the self schema. An alternative theory highlights the organizational processing of self-referent messages that underlies the observed mnemonic superiority (Klein and Kihlstorm 1986). According to this theory, organization facilitates recall by (1) grouping self-referent messages in categories, allowing multiple paths for retrieval, and (2) allowing the category label to serve as a retrieval cue. Elaborative and organizational processing do not need to be exclusive of each other. Klein and Loftus (1988) found that both organization and elaboration can be elicited in self-referent tasks. While there is continuous debate on the cause of the self-referent effects, a meta-analysis (Symons and Johnson 1997) of self-reference studies confirms “the expected self-referent effect (SRE) in memory, with self-referent encoding strategies yielding superior memory relative to both semantic and other referent encoding strategies” (p. 371). As demonstrated in previous research, self-referent effect results in better knowledge organization and elaborative encoding that facilitates recall. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2a: Users recall self-referent web content faster and more accurately than they recall non-self-referent web content.

Arriving at a decision is effortful. In choice settings, mental effort is required to evaluate and rank the different choice options. Given the limited working memory and bounded computational capability, consumers resort to different choice strategies to minimize their cognitive efforts (Chu and Spires 2000; Todd and Benbasat 2000). Very often, the choice problem is framed as one balancing the accuracy of the decision and the cognitive effort involved (Hogarth 1987; Payne et al. 1993). A major appeal of personalized offers is that in the absence of a perfectly well-developed preference structure, the customized label can positively affect perceived fit (Simonson 2005). Because of their saliency, self-referent content triggers selective processing. As a result, choice options tagged with self-referent content are more likely to enter into working memory where the preference structure is constructed. Provided these self-referent options have an acceptable level of relevance and diagnosticity, they will facilitate the construction of preference structures. The results are shorter decision time and reduced information search efforts. As a decision aid, web personalization with self-referent content is hypothesized to reduce cognitive efforts in decision making as follows:

H3a: Users exposed to self-referent web content will seek less information and spend less time on decision making than when they are exposed to non-self-referent web content.

Self referencing is reported to enhance people's product evaluations (Debevec and Romeo 1992; Mick 1992). Hong and Zinkhan (1995) found that referencing marketing messages congruent with the target audience's self concept are positively related to brand preference and purchase intention. The saliency of self-referent content over non-self-referent content suggested in H1 and the heightened elaboration from self-referent content may prime users in their evaluation of choice options. As mental resources are limited, non-self-referent choice options will be processed to a lesser extent. The combined effect of priming and a stronger association with the self schema increases the perceived fitness of self-referent options in the preference structure. In other words, self-referent content serves as a decision aid by facilitating the construction of preference structure and at the same time increasing its dominance over non-self-referent content. All other factors being equal, a user's preference structure is likely to be filled with more personalized offers presented through self-referent stimuli than with those presented through non-self-referent stimuli. The result is hypothesized to bias the user's choice toward the agent's recommendations.

H4a: Users accept offers associated with self-referent web content to a larger extent than they accept offers associated with non-self-referent web content.

## Web Personalization: Content Relevance

The second variable of web personalization refers to the relevance of web content to the user's processing goal. Based on persuasion research in social cognition, messages relevant to the processing goal will heighten elaboration to a larger extent than will irrelevant messages (for a review, see Petty and Cacioppo 1986). In particular, the elaboration likelihood model stipulates that persuasion takes place via two routes: a central route and a peripheral route. When recipients are motivated and able to exert cognitive effort to scrutinize a message, the central route will be used, resulting in a higher level of elaboration. On the other hand, when a recipient is neither able nor motivated, the person will rely on peripheral cues to assist him in judging the message. In the context of web personalization, web stimuli relevant to the user's processing goal are more likely to be elaborated via the central route than is irrelevant content. This is consistent with findings from human computer interaction researchers that salient interface features are more likely to attract attention when they are relevant or perceived to be relevant to the processing tasks (Folk et al. 1992; Yantis and Egeth 1999).

We expect relevant and irrelevant content will induce different levels of cognitive processing. Compared with irrelevant content, relevant content is more likely to reside in working memory because it is constantly being assessed in constructing the preference structure. Therefore, relevant content will be elaborated to a larger extent resulting in more and stronger memory traces. On the other hand, web stimuli not relevant to the processing goal will receive less elaboration. The difference in elaboration will be reflected in recall performance.

H2b: Users recall web content relevant to their processing goal faster and more accurately than they recall irrelevant web content.

Relevant content are useful for users engaged in choice decisions (Limayem and DeSanctis 2000; Silver 1990). Relevant content assists users to build up their preferences more efficiently than irrelevant content. It follows that users will expend less effort in arriving at their choice decisions with relevant content, leading to the following hypothesis:

H3b: Users exposed to relevant web content will seek less information and spend less time on decision making than when they are exposed to irrelevant web content.

Following the same line of reasoning, relevant content will bias the choice outcomes of users. For example, in contemplating a purchase decision under a certain product category, a user stands a higher chance to be induced by the personalization agent to consider products that are related to, or fall under, the same product category under consideration than otherwise. Similar to the self-referent effect, relevant content is more likely to be included in the consideration set and to bias the choice outcome. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4b: Users accept offers associated with relevant web content to a larger extent than they accept offers associated with irrelevant web content.

## Processing Goal Specificity

As mentioned earlier, the personalization agent needs to be sensitive to the processing goal and its fluid nature in order to generate the “right” content. As Bettman et al. (1998) noted, “choice among options depends critically on the goals of the decision maker.” (p. 188). The idea of providing relevant content to users is consistent with earlier work in decision support systems. Silver (1990, 1991) highlights the importance of decision guidance in supporting users’ decision-making tasks. Silver remarked that “knowing when to provide guidance hinges on understanding the potential effects and effectiveness of guidance” (1991, p. 120). In addition to the main effects of self reference and content relevance on recall, we expect that processing goal will moderate the effect of content relevance on information processing.

Previous work suggested a dichotomous processing goal—searching versus browsing—among web users (Janiszewski 1998). However, we believe that goal specificity should be more appropriately measured on a continuous spectrum. While self reference is goal neutral, content relevance is goal contingent and is applicable only to users engaged in pursuing a specific processing goal (e.g., the search for a product or making a decision). The higher the specificity of a goal, the more easily the web stimulus can be classified as relevant (or irrelevant) to a goal. Consequently, more cognitive effort will be devoted to processing relevant content as goal specificity increases. While we hypothesize in H2b that relevant content leads to better recall performance than does irrelevant content, we expect that such a difference will be magnified further under a high goal specificity than under low goal specificity. Thus, we hypothesize an interaction effect between goal specificity and content relevance on recall as follows:

H2c: There is a larger difference in recall accuracy and response time between relevant and irrelevant web content for users with more-specific processing goals than for those with less-specific processing goals.

## Evaluation

Whether or not users are receptive to personalized content has important implications for online merchants. It is therefore important to assess how useful personalized content is to users. As reported by Hong and Zinkhan (1995), marketing and advertising messages consistent with the customer's self concept are more likely to generate positive effects on brand preference and purchase intention. Self-referent messages are reported to heighten elaboration of a product's features and benefits, resulting in a positive impact on persuasion (Klein and Loftus 1988). Thus, other factors being equal, we hypothesize the following:

## H5a: Users evaluate self-referent web content more highly than they evaluate non-self-referent web content.

It is expected that relevant content will receive a more favorable evaluation than will irrelevant content. As hypothesized in H3b, relevant content enables decisions to be made with less effort. This saving of mental cost would lead to a positive attitude toward relevant content. This is of course premised on the condition that the content generated by the personalization agent is indeed relevant in completing the user's task.

H5b: Users evaluate relevant web content more highly than they evaluate irrelevant web content.

We conducted two studies to test the above hypotheses. One was a control lab experiment conducted in the context of an online shop where subjects were exposed to different levels of manipulation of the variables introduced earlier. Findings of the lab experiment were augmented by a field study involving a music download site. The two studies are summarized in Figure 3. The research design and results are presented in the following sections.

## Procedure

The experiment consisted of four phases: (1) a 12-minute study phase in which all participants were asked to navigate through an online shop, (2) a 5-minute distraction phase to clear the working memory, (3) a memory recall test, and (4) filling in a questionnaire.

In the study phase, all subjects were randomly assigned to one of the following tasks: product-selection, product-browsing or random-browsing. The manipulation of goal specificity is presented in Table 1. In the 5-minute distraction period, subjects were asked to provide details on their demographics and to take a rest. After that, subjects were asked to participate in a memory recall test using a PC. It was a YES-NO recognition test (Figure 8), which is typical in cognitive research (Ray and Reingold 2003).

Finally, the subjects were asked to complete a questionnaire to evaluate the entire process. All questions used a seven-point Likert scale.

## Study 2: Field Study

## Design

To supplement the findings of the lab study, we conducted a field study with a personalized music download site. This online music site was set up for a closed community of registered users. All users had to logon before downloading. The web site ran on an Apache web server connected to a database server that maintained a large collection of digitized songs.

The web personalization agent used for the music site was a commercial product called Obert (http://www.radicasys.com). It was used in our study because it provided functions that are common in many commercial personalization software packages, including (1) user profile management, (2) collection and analysis of web activities, and (3) personalizing web content based on users' preferences. The personalization agent generated a set of rules that drove the personalized content on the music site. These rules were based on users' preferences for different music categories, artists, and rhythms. The music site had an easy-to-use interface so that users could sample all songs and albums at any time.

Measurements were taken on two groups of users. The between-subject factor was personalized offers that matched the subjects' preferences (relevant content versus irrelevant content). The two groups were exposed to an interface consisting of both self-referent and non-self-referent content (within-subject factor). Goal specificity was not manipulated in the field study. Manipulation of the independent variables and measurement of the dependent variables are described in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

## Procedure

The field study consisted of two phases. First, subjects were recruited in collaboration with the personalization agent vendor. During the first logon, they were asked to fill in a questionnaire to report their music preferences in terms of music category, artists, etc. The collected data were processed by the personalization agent, which then generated rules to drive personalized content to subjects in the treatment group. Each subject was assigned a password and a logon name. Second, subjects were allowed to download songs from the site over a 6-week period. New songs were periodically added to the web site. The subjects could logon the site for music downloading at anytime and from anywhere.

## Manipulation Check

To check the manipulation of content relevance, subjects in the product-selection and product-browsing groups were asked to evaluate the relevance of each banner message in attaining the assigned goal on a seven-point Likert scale (7 being very relevant). Results showed that the average score of the PDA banners (4.37) was larger than that of the snack banners (3.09) $t(206) = 7.19, p < 0.01$ . Thus, support was found for our manipulation of content relevance.

We conducted two manipulation checks on self-reference. First, we asked subjects if they could see their names on the banner messages (i.e., question 1 in Appendix B). Second, the subjects were asked four questions to report how self-referent the banner messages were. We factor-analyzed these questions and calculated the composite score. The reliability of the self reference measure was 0.85. Results showed that self-referent banners (4.77) were perceived to be more personalized than were non-self-referent banners (2.22) $F(1,205)=58.167, p<0.01$ .

To check the manipulation of goal specificity, we asked subjects to evaluate if they had specific goals before and during their interactions with the web in the experiment. Questions used in the manipulation check are shown in Appendix B. Subjects in the product-selection group reported the highest level of goal specificity before (4.57) and during (5.16) their interaction with the online shop. The random-browsing group had the least specific goal before (2.04) and during (4.09) web browsing. As expected, the perceived goal specificity for the product-browsing group was found to be between the product-selection and the random-browsing groups before (2.90) and during (4.55) web interaction. Since the order was consistent with our expectations, the manipulation on goal specificity was deemed to be satisfactory. The reliabilities of the before and during goal specificity measures were 0.86 and 0.80, respectively. Also, the results of one-way ANOVA tests showed that goal specificity before navigation $F(2,204)=49.67, p<0.01$ and during navigation $F(2,204)=10.50, p<0.01$ was significantly different among the groups.

To test for other potential effects, we performed ANOVA tests to examine whether gender, age, or web experience had effects on recall accuracy and response time. Gender had no significant effect on recall accuracy $F(1,205)=1.01, p>0.1$ and response time $F(1,205)=1.58, p>0.1$ . Age was found to have no significant effect on recall accuracy $F(3,203)=1.89, p>0.1$ and response time $F(3,203)=1.61, p>0.1$ . Also, web experience had no effect on recall accuracy $F(1,205)=0.56, p>0.1$ and response time $F(1,205)=0.25, p>0.1$ .

With these results on the control variables and our random assignment of subjects to each group, we believe that the effects of confounding factors were minimized and controlled for. Summary statistics of the dependent variables are provided in Table 3.

Cognitive Processing: In the lab experiment, we measured cognitive processing in two ways. First, we studied the ability of a subject to recall the banners. Second, we focused on the product-searching group only and compared the amount of effort spent during the decision-making process under different variable manipulations.

1. Content Recall: The data collected from the recall tests were analyzed using repeated measure MANOVA. $^{4}$ The dependent variables were recall accuracy and response time for PDA and snack banners. After discarding the 5 warm-up questions, 32 questions remained: 16 questions were related to the PDAs and 16 questions were related to the snacks. Half of the questions on PDAs (or snacks) asked the subjects to recognize the pictures of the PDAs (or snacks), and the other half asked the subjects to recognize the brand logos of the PDAs (or snacks).

We considered all manipulations and performed a $3 \times 2 \times 2$ repeated measure MANOVA to study the impact of self reference on recall. There were 207 observations. The mean recall accuracy of the self-referent group was 0.61 and that of the non-self-referent group was 0.59. The response time in recalling self-referent and non-self-referent banners were 2.46 seconds and 2.45 seconds, respectively. The impact of self reference on recall was found to be insignificant ( $F(1,201) = 1.60, p > 0.1$ ). H2a was not supported. Also, there was no interaction effect between self reference and goal specificity ( $F(2,201) = 1.71, p > 0.1$ ).

Since content relevance is not meaningful in the random browsing group, we took away this group and focused on 142 subjects in the remaining two groups. We conducted a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ repeated measure MANOVA to study the impact of content relevance and the interaction effect between content relevance and goal specificity on recall. The mean recall accuracy for the relevant content was 0.64, whereas that for the irrelevant content was 0.60. Also, subjects could recall relevant content faster (2.08 seconds) than they could recall irrelevant content (2.84 seconds). Overall, relevant content can be better recalled ( $F(2,137) = 76.44, p < 0.01$ ), supporting H2b.

The mean difference between recall accuracy of relevant and irrelevant content for the product-browsing group was 0.01, while that for the product-selection group was 0.04. And the mean difference between the time to recall relevant content and the time to recall irrelevant content for the product-browsing group was 609.53 milliseconds, whereas the mean difference for the product-selection group was 909.38 milliseconds. Overall, the difference in recall between relevant and irrelevant content for the product-selection group was larger than that of the product-browsing group ( $F(2,137) = 3.09$ , p < 0.05), supporting H2c.

2. Information Exploration for Decision Making: Since only the product-searching group had to make decisions, we focused on this group when studying the users decision effort. We counted the total number of clicks during the decision process. The total number of clicks by the self-referent group was 110.73, whereas that by non-self-referent group was 164.57. We conducted a MANOVA test with the decision time and the number of clicks before reaching a choice decision. Results indicate that the subjects with self-referent banners spent less effort in decision making ( $F(2,71) = 4.38, p < 0.05$ ), supporting H3a.

Decision: The subjects in the product-searching group were asked to choose three PDAs. On average, the self-referent group accepted 1.11 PDAs from the banner recommendations, whereas the non-self-referent group accepted only 0.66 PDA. The difference was significant ( $F(1,70) = 4.79$ , p < 0.05), supporting H4a.

Evaluation: The perceived usefulness of banner messages evaluated by subjects in the self-referent group was 3.43, $^{5}$ whereas that by subjects in the non-self-referent group was 2.70. Reliability of the banner usefulness measure was 0.95. This indicates that self-referent content $(F(1, 205) = 9.54, p < 0.01)$ was significant in affecting the evaluation of banner usefulness, supporting H5a.

## Study 2: Field Study

## Subjects

A total of 182 subjects participated in the field study. Of these, 106 were males and 76 were females. Their average age was 23.3 years. Forty of the subjects were randomly assigned to the control group, while 142 were assigned to the treatment group. All subjects received digital music recommendations generated by the personalization agent. The unbalanced sample distribution was due to the agreement with the personalization agent vendor, which provided the software for the field study. Subjects could access the music site from any Internet connection. They signed an agreement that all downloaded music files were for their own enjoyment and could not be forwarded to others.

## Results

Since 43 subjects never logged on after registration, we discarded these records in the analysis, resulting in 139 subjects, 23 in the control group and 116 in the personalized group. All together, the subjects browsed 26,397 pages and navigated through 6,952 item descriptions during the 6-week period. There were 5,843 music file downloads. Some files were downloaded repeatedly within 5 minutes. Duplicate downloads within a very short time were discarded. This resulted in 4,386 downloads for analysis.

Attention: The web interface for the field study was divided into two regions: self-referent and non-self-referent regions. The self-referent region included six recommendations in the central window and five recommendations under the column Our Offers. The non-self-referent region included all hyperlinks on the left-hand side and the 10 recommendations under the columns Market Offers and Other Offers. As expected, users clicked on the self-referent offers (52.37) more often than they clicked on the non-self-referent offers (11.53) ( $t = 7.17$ , $p < 0.01$ ), supporting H1. $^{6}$

Cognitive Processing: We conducted a MANOVA test with the amount of time spent and the number of clicks before downloading a song as the dependent variables. Results indicated that subjects exposed to offers congruent to personal preferences spent less effort on decision making $F(2,136)=23.17, p<0.01$ , supporting H3b. $^{7}$

Decision: On average, subjects downloaded 46.60 songs from the self-referent region during the period as compared with 7.22 songs from the non-self-referent region. We found that the subjects tended to download songs under the self-referent region more frequently than the songs under other regions (t = 6.38, p < 0.01). This indicates that self-referent cues are able to bias a user decision. H4a was thus supported.

Subjects in the treatment group downloaded on average 51.82 songs during the period as compared with 20.30 songs for the control group. However, the difference in the number of downloads was insignificant ( $F(1,137)=3.03, p<0.1$ ). H4b was not supported.

Evaluation: Apart from click stream and download statistics, subjects were asked to evaluate at least 12 of the recommended songs displayed in the central part of the web interface at any time during the 6-week period. Subjects were more satisfied with the recommended songs in the self-referent region (3.72) than those in the non-self-referent region (3.27) $F(1,137)=5.61, p<0.05$ , supporting H5a.

Also, our results showed that subjects in the treatment group (3.73) who received recommendations from the personalization agent were more satisfied with the recommended songs than were those in the control group (3.49) who received random offers $F(1,137)=5.50, p<0.05$ . This provided support for H5b.

While most of our hypotheses related to Study 2 were supported, we realize that the unbalanced sample sizes of the treatment and control groups might have affected the results. The small sample size of the control group is sensitive to violation of distribution assumptions. To assess the stability of the results, we conducted a bootstrapping analysis as suggested by Davison and Hinkley (1997) to reexamine the hypotheses. Bootstrapping is a nonparametric analysis that involves repeated sampling from the original sample to construct a confidence interval based on the resampled means. The bootstrapping analysis was applied to all hypotheses except H1, H4a, and H5a because these hypotheses deal with self reference, which is a within-group factor (all subjects were used in the analyses). The original samples for the remaining hypotheses were resampled 1,000 times and confidence intervals were constructed. The results were consistent with the original results with a 95 percent confidence level for H3b and H5b. Interestingly, the insignificant results of H4b became significant after bootstrapping. The bootstrapping analysis indicated a 95 percent confidence interval of [12.26...52.75]. A closer inspection of the download sample distribution revealed a few subjects in the treatment group were associated with a very large number of downloads. To probe further, we compared the median of the two groups and conducted a sign test on their difference. Results of the sign test indicated significant difference between the two groups for page clicks (Z = -2.358, p < 0.05), decision time (Z = -4.636, p < 0.01), acceptance of offer (Z = -3.603, p < 0.01), and evaluation (Z = -7.562, p < 0.01). Both the bootstrapping and median tests are able to handle skewed sample distributions and the results of both tests were consistent with each other. Furthermore, the results were consistent with those of the ANOVA tests except H4b. The contradictory result was due to the skewness of the sample distribution which generated a large standard error for parametric test. Given the robustness of the bootstrapping and median tests, we revised our conclusion for H4b accordingly.

## Limitations

There are a number of limitations that readers should take into account when interpreting the findings. First, the number of recall tests was 32 (16 images and 16 logos) in Study 1. The number of tests may cause fatigue in subjects and may impair their recall performance. The small difference in recall between different treatment combinations may be the result of some subjects randomizing their responses at the end of the test.

Second, the manipulation of relevance as a within-group factor in Study 1 may overestimate the recall performance of irrelevant information because snack and PDA banners were placed in the same location on a rotating basis. This may explain why snack banners were also recalled even though they were irrelevant. Notwithstanding this potential impact, a significant difference is found between relevant and irrelevant banners.

Third, the experiment was intentionally set up to make observations of user behavior during the entire decision making process in completing a choice task. As such, the dependent variables at each stage were not studied in isolation from each other. There are two advantages to such an approach. First, it simulates an environment in which the usage context is emphasized. Second, it reveals the interdependence of behavior that is difficult to detect in isolated studies of information processing. However, the downside is the additional complexity involved in controlling for factors that may affect these interdependencies. Ideally, a path-dependent experimental design (spanning the different information processing stages) will help to cover all possible scenarios as the actual process unfolds. Practically, this may not be feasible. Our study attempts to take a balanced approach and readers should interpret the findings with this in mind.

Finally, while the two studies were intended to test the proposed model, they were not replicates of each other. The operationalizations of most variables were not the same and the focal points were somewhat different in each study. For example, in Study 1, we benefited from a control experiment and were able to make observations on memory recall. However, because of the longitudinal nature of Study 2, a recall test was not feasible. Also, the product used in Study 1 was a PDA, a personal productivity tool, while a hedonic product (MP3 songs) was used in Study 2.

## Discussion

The results of the two studies indicate that web personalization affects the processing of web stimuli and decision outcomes. Major findings are summarized in Table 4 and discussed below.

Widely practiced personalization strategies in the industry are conceptualized into two variables in the model: content relevance and self reference. The first variable measures the extent to which web content is relevant to the specific processing goal of a user, while the second variable measures the extent to which the content activates concepts related to the self. As hypothesized, self-referent web stimuli divert the attention of users (H1). This is confirmed in both studies.

Both self reference and content relevance are hypothesized to stimulate a higher level of cognitive processing of personalized content, resulting in better recall performance than otherwise. The results are mixed. While relevant content led to better recall (H2b), this is not obvious for self-referent content (H2a).

One plausible explanation for the insignificant impact of self-referent content on memory recall is that while self-referent content elicited selective processing (H1), subjects in the self-referent group actually spent less time in making a choice decision than did subjects in the non-self-referent group (H3a). Consequently, self-referent subjects had less time to process the banners, which might result in less than optimal information encoding and organization via the self-schema. This had a negative impact on recall performance. The shortened exposure time is less of a problem in testing the relevancy effect because PDA banners were processed to a larger extent than were snack banners for both the product selection and product browsing group. Another plausible explanation for the lack of mnemonic gain is the overuse of self-referent messages. Meyers-Levy and Peracchio (1996) found that the persuasive effectiveness of a self-referent message increased initially but decreased when such messages were overused, resulting in an inverted U-shaped effect of self-referent messages. A similar pattern was found by Cacioppo and Petty (1979), Mick (1992), Speier et al. (1999) and Sujan et al. (1993). These authors suggested that when users were overwhelmed with self-referent messages, they generated many idiosyncratic ideas that were related to the message, thus interfering with the processing of the original message. The frequency of self-referent messages in the current study may be relatively high and the results may fall off the downward edge of the suggested inverted U-shaped pattern. Both explanations suggest the importance of the duration and the frequency of self-referent messages in eliciting self-referent effects. It seems that unless users are motivated to elaborate and are given enough time, they may not be able to effectively use the self schema as a mnemonic aid.

In terms of the interaction between processing goal and relevance, goal specificity was found to moderate the difference in recall performance between relevant and irrelevant content as hypothesized in H2c. When a user engages in a very specific goal, he will become more sensitive to stimuli on the web. In the experiment, users were able to recall relevant content (PDA banners) better than they were able to recall irrelevant content (snack banners), and this difference was larger in the product-selection group than in the product-browsing group.

Regarding choice outcome, a personalization agent can serve as a decision aid that assists users in arriving at a choice decision more effectively. In both studies, subjects (those in the product-searching group in the lab study and all subjects in the field study) made a product choice at the end of the experiment. Recent research in consumer decision making suggests that, when faced with a choice decision, a person is likely to construct a preference structure on the fly instead of retrieving it from memory (Bettman et al. 1998; Slovic 1995). The entries to this preference structure and their ranking are susceptible to the influence of the task environment and the person processing goal. Results from both studies indicated that users who were subject to self-referent and relevant content searched for less information and spent less time in arriving at a choice decision (H3a and H3b). The finding provides evidence of the decision-supporting role of self-referent web content.

Furthermore, both studies demonstrated significant self-referent effects on decision outcomes. Users were more likely to accept offers with a self-referent association than otherwise (H4a). Also, content relevance was found to be significant in affecting choice outcome (H4b).

In terms of evaluation, subjects found self-referent content to be more useful than non-self-referent content (H5a). $^{8}$ This is true in both studies. Also, in the field study, subjects in the treatment group rated the songs higher than did those in the control group (H5b). The findings provide evidence that personalization can induce a positive attitude toward the recommended offers.

## Theoretical Contributions

In contrast to the widespread adoption of personalization software and the strong advocacy by management gurus (Porter 2001) for the use of personalization services as a differentiation strategy, little has been done to assess the effectiveness of web personalization. We attempt to bridge this gap by providing a theoretical model to investigate issues related to personalized IT services. Major contributions are summarized below.

First, the current work introduces a conceptualization of web personalization postulating that the effects of personalization strategies are mediated by two factors: self reference and content relevance. So far, the term personalization means different things to different audiences. The proposed conceptualization of web personalization suggests that the effectiveness of personalization is determined by the use of self-referent cues and the timely display of content relevant to the processing goal of the user. Such a conceptualization captures many of the functionalities of contemporary personalization agents such as adaptive content generation, customer profiling, web mining, and click stream analysis.

Second, to understand the impact of web personalization, we theorize the impacts of self reference and relevance on different stages of information processing. Drawing on theories from social cognition and consumer decision research, we hypothesize the effects of the two factors on users' attention, cognitive effort, decision outcome, and evaluations. With the exception of heightening cognitive effort, self-referent stimuli were found to attract more attention, enable users to create preference structures faster, and bias the decision outcome.

Also, users were receptive to self-referent content and found them useful. The idea of leveraging the self as a mnemonic aid is the foundation of many web personalization strategies. The insignificant impact of self-referent content reported in the current study adds to the continuous debate on the cause of the self referent effect (Symons and Johnson 1997). As mentioned earlier, this may due to suboptimal encoding of the content.

Third, while findings on content relevance are consistent with previous work on persuasion (Ahluwalia 2000; Areni et al. 2000; Burnkrant and Unnava 1995) and IT cognitive fit (Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Vessey 1991), they shed new light on the fluid nature of processing goals in the web environment. Studies on IT cognitive fit are typically confined to specific tasks and problem-solving situations in the workplace. In the web environment, users are often engaged in browsing with no clear processing goal in mind. Yet, they may switch to a search mode spontaneously if some stimuli catch their attention. Our findings indicate that there is a significant interaction effect between goal specificity and content relevance on memory recall. To maximize business opportunities, online merchants should adopt a more adaptive personalization strategy that is sensitive to the processing goal of the user.

Fourth, the current work demonstrates the effectiveness of personalization agents as a decision aid. When exposed to personalized content, users seek less information and likely spend less time in making a choice decision. Both self-referent and relevant web stimuli enable users to create preference structures more effectively. There is also evidence that personalized stimuli affect the choice outcome by biasing the entries to the preference structure during its formation. The findings provide a new perspective that the dominant effect of web personalization in choice settings could take place very early in the information processing stage. This is achieved by diverting attention to the personalized items and biasing entries into the preference structure in the early stage.

Finally, the work also develops a number of constructs to measure the extent of self reference and goal specificity (Appendix B). While the constructs are used for manipulation checks in the current study, the reliability of the constructs are acceptable and could be further explored in developing latent constructs in personalization research.

## Practical Implications

We believe the findings of the current work are applicable to a wide range of web-based services that target attracting users' attention and influencing their information processing. Some of the implications for IT developers are summarized below.

Presently, there are two approaches to generating personalized content: rule-based and analytic. The former is based on static rules derived from analyzing customer transactions or based on the experience of marketing executives. For example, a rule could be “If the customer buys a six pack of coke, show him a pack of potato chips (banner ad) for sale.” While such a cross-sell tactic captures a certain purchase context, the context is not fully exploited. Other contextual factors such as the user’s processing goal (directed buying versus hedonic browsing) and spontaneous preferences are not considered. Our findings indicate that detecting the level of goal specificity is important in influencing the information gathering behavior of users. This view echoes the work by Moe (2003) who advocated a typology of store visits based on click stream analysis. The typology includes directed buying, search/deliberation, hedonic browsing, and knowledge building. By mapping click streams to these four behavior categories, a personalization agent could infer the level of specificity of the user’s goal.

Second, there is evidence that self reference as a personalization strategy is fruitful under the following conditions. First, the web content should elicit self-referent effects by developing retrieval cues and memory traces through the self schema. Second, self-referent messages should not be overused as they could stimulate thoughts that are not related to the targeted content. Interestingly, the insignificant impact of self-referent content on recall is consistent with findings by Hong and Zinkhan (1995), who reported “brand preference and purchase intention were influenced by self-congruent advertising appeals, whereas brand memory was not” (p. 74). One implication to marketers in implementing web personalization is that it is important to understand what role self-referent content plays in the personalization strategy. Whether it is used as a mnemonic device or a decision aid has very different outcomes on the user side.

Third, to understand the context and the user's goal, it becomes important to integrate server-side logged data with client-side information. So far, client-side tracking technology is not well developed. Also, many services allow multiple access channels. For example, bank account information can be accessed over the Internet or using a mobile phone. Personalization strategies for fixed-line services could be very different from those for mobile access. As the number of touch points with customers increases, online merchants should take a holistic approach in implementing personalization strategies.

Finally, while web personalization has been shown to be effective in influencing user behavior, its use should be balanced by taking a proactive approach to protecting data privacy on the user side. A survey conducted by the Personalization Consortium reported that while 76 percent of the respondents find it helpful for web sites to remember basic personal information such as names and e-mail addresses, only 50 percent read the privacy statement and only 38 percent of the respondents agree that privacy statements are easy to understand. Online merchants should ensure fair access to individuals' personally identifiable information and should provide a mechanism for users to easily and quickly change inaccurate or unauthorized personal information. Acceptable criteria for "opt-in" and "opt-out" should be provided as consumer privacy options.

## Conclusion

The current work has covered a number of conceptual territories spanning social cognition and consumer decision making. We draw on the extant literature on human information processing and in particular studies on self-referent effects to conceptualize the impact of web personalization on users' decision-making processes. Most of the hypotheses on the impact of web personalization on cognitive processing, behavior and decision making are supported based on a control lab experiment and a field study. Our findings suggest that personalization could offer competitive advantages to online merchants. Users generally perceive personalized messages to be more useful and are more willing to explore the personalized content further. Moreover, personalized communications have the potential to reduce information overload and provide aids to decision making. Personalized IT services have become a ubiquitous phenomenon. More work needs to be done to better understand the issues brought about by personalization.

## Appendix A

## Personalization Strategies

(1) User-driven personalization: A user specifies in advance the desired web layout and content that match his interests and preferences. User-driven personalization provides the user with tools and options to specify information requirements and presentation format. For example, an investor would tailor the contents of a financial portal to match his own investment portfolio. One example is My Yahoo!, which asks the user to enter simple information and collects favorites, such as news, weather, stock prices, sports scores, and movie listings. Personalized content are delivered to the users in their preferred format.

(2) Transaction-driven personalization: Personalized layout and content are generated by the online merchant. Personalization is driven by previous transactions rather than specified by the user in advance. For example, page organization and information indexing is automatically adjusted for different users depending on their preferences inferred from previous transactions. One example is Dell.com, which provides personalized recommendations and maintenance services based on users transaction histories.

(3) Context-driven personalization: In this case, a very adaptive mechanism is employed to personalize content and layout for each individual user. Advances in click stream analysis and web mining have made it possible to understand the context and to infer the user's processing objective in real time (e.g., product inspection versus random browsing). The mechanism is sensitive to the context of the interaction and adapts to the changing context continuously. For example, context-driven persuasion messages in the form of ad banners are dynamically generated to realize cross-sell and up-sell opportunities. One example is Amazon.com, which generates recommendations based on the search pattern of a user.
