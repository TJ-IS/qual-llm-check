# Messaging specifications, properties and gratifications as institutions: How messaging institutions shaped wireless service difusion in Norway and Japan

Lars A. Knutsen <sup>a,\*</sup>, Kalle Lyytinen <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Copenhagen Business School, Department of Informatics/TDC A/S, Howitzvej 60, DK-2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark

<sup>b</sup> Case Western Reserve University, Weatherhead School of Management, Department of Information Systems, 10900 Euclid Avenue Cleveland, OH 44106-7235, USA

Received 10 May 2007; received in revised form 15 May 2007; accepted 23 October 2007

## Abstract

We address the following question: why have a large number of mobile services been successful in Japan but received only lukewarm response in Norway despite Norway’s lead in the late 1990s in wireless messaging? Current explanations are not suficient in explaining this dilemma. We approach mobile service difusion through the lens of new institutionalism by analyzing how messaging insti tutions emerged both on supply and demand sides that enabled and constrained mobile service inno vation. A tripartite framework of institutional fields consisting of architectural service specifications, service properties, and use gratifications is formulated. Using secondary data we show that mobile service specifications in Japan integrated better interpersonal communication and data services and thereby allowed instrumental and aesthetic properties to align with expressive properties of messaging. In Norway disjoint service specifications caused service properties and gratifications to remain disconnected, which inhibited data service adoption and channeled data service adoption toward simple SMS based messaging solutions. Diferences in service specifications and prospective gratifi cations not only account for diferences in overall service difusion, but ofer valuable insights into institutional forces that shape complex mobile service innovation. - 2007 Elsevier Ltd. ## 1. Introduction

Over the last two decades mobile services have become ingrained in everyday life in all Western societies. By mobile service we mean transfer, manipulation and processing of digital information within a mobile device, which is dependent on two way transfer of digital information over a wireless long range link. Though this definition also covers digital voice communications we will focus in this study on mobile services that go beyond mobile telephony like short messaging, mobile e-mail, or mobile browsing.

Distinct paths have emerged for mobile service difusion in diferent regions: Japan has enjoyed enormous success with its mobile internet (Funk, 2001, Working Paper; Ishii, 2004) while similar success is largely absent in the US and Europe. For the latter regions the dominant use of mobile services have remained limited to short messaging (SMS) and its extensions (Knutsen & Lyytinen, 2005). Analyses of these surprising differences abound: they are explained by idiosyncrasies in culture, life-style, community structures, or due to the shrewdness of the NTT DoCoMo leadership (e.g. Elliott & Tang, 2004; Funk, 2001; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003; Sigurdson, 2001). However, scant scholarly attention has been paid to diferences in infrastructural capabilities and institutionally enforced service specifications. Past studies have been largely blind to how ‘‘small diferences in the features of apparently similar tools could be associated with big diferences in usage patterns and social outcomes” (Markus, 2005, p. 19). They neglect how simple diferences in dominant messaging solutions – SMS in Norway and mobile e-mail in Japan – produced significant diferences for mobile service innovation and difusion. Consequently, past analyses miss in what ways mobile infrastructure capabilities can shape and channel mobile service adoption, and how their ongoing and expansive use engenders alternative social institutions on the demand side. Subtle diferences in initial specifications generate variations in how services are commonly perceived and what properties are ascribed to them and what pleasures they bring to users – in short how services institutionalize over time. In this paper we ask: how do infrastructural capabilities shape mobile service adoption and how does the ongoing institutionalization of services and their use further shape service innovation and adoption?

To address this question we will examine how institutional fields and their embedded mechanisms can engender alternative paths of mobile service innovation, and how these disparate innovation paths produce diverse outcomes. We adopt as our theoretical lens new institutionalism (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Nickerson & zur Muehlen, 2006) where institutions are viewed as ‘‘shared rules and typifications that identify categories of social actors and their appropriate activities or relationships” (Barley & Tolbert, 1997, p. 96). Among diferent strands of institutional theory (for a review: Scott, 1987) this perspective is grounded on Giddens (1984) view<sup>2</sup> of institutions as socially manifest and reified rules and resource sets produced by actors over time and space. This view contrasts alternative institutional analyses where institutions are treated as identifiable large organizations and powerful actors (e.g. governments), or distinct societal spheres (e.g. standardization).

By studying the interplay between institutions that shape on the supply side mobile artifact provisioning including among others technology standards and features, and institutions on the demand side that are produced and enforced during mobile service use like meanings associated with them or their use values, we examine how divergent service innovation paths are created by their dynamic interplay. In particular, we examine how diferences in institutionalized messaging specifications become over time embed ded in specific institutionalized service provisioning solutions that will then condition service adoption and use and their institutionalization patterns. We illustrate the value of the framework by analyzing how alternative institutional arrangements at the supply side – original and historic diferences in messaging standards – lead to diferent service configurations in the emerging service ecology which again produced distinct institutionalized use patterns. We show through two case studies covering mobile service difusion in Norway and Japan how the mutually dependent processes of institutionalization in the supply and demand side over time reinforced increasingly divergent mobile service difu sion paths despite very similar starting conditions and Norway’s early lead in wireless messaging.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review studies of mobile service use and adoption and discuss how they account diferences in service difusion. In Section 3 we formulate our institutional framework for analyzing mobile difusion. In Section 4 we analyze mobile service institutionalization during the difusion of mobile services in Norway and Japan, and analyze their diferences. We conclude by discussing the contribution and limitations, and suggest new avenues for future research.

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1. Current models

Research into mobile service innovation and adoption can be classified into three cat egories: (1) models of technology acceptance, (2) models of service adoption and difusion, and (3) ensemble models of the macro environment.

Models of technology acceptance draw upon individual technology acceptance models (see Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003 for an overview). They focus on individuals perceptions of technology (e.g. ease of use and performance) and on other factors such as the social context (e.g. norms) and personal diferences (e.g. age and gender) to predict adoption decisions. These models have been widely employed to explain mobile service adoption (e.g. Bruner II & Kumar, 2005; Hung, Ku, & Chang, 2003; Nysveen, Pedersen, & Thorbjørnsen, 2005), and have been expanded with factors associated with research on gratifications (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001; Leung, 2002) and technology domestication (Haddon, 2001; Ling, 2004; Ling & Haddon, 2001).

Models of service adoption and difusion apply difusion of innovation theory (Rogers, 2003), consumer behavior theories, and sociology of technology. They focus on adopter categories, typical services used in adopter segments, and adopter characteristics (e.g. Constantiou, Damsgaard, & Knutsen, 2006; Ishii, 2004; Mante-Meijer & Ling, 2003; Vrechopoulos, Constantiou, Mylonopoulos, Sideris, & Doukidis, 2002; Aarnio, Enkenberg, Heikkila¨, & Hirvola, 2002). Answers are sought for how mobile service adoption and difusion varies across geographical regions and user segments, how mobile services impact everyday communications, and how adoption processes evolve.

Ensemble models analyze a mixture of industrial, technical, strategic, cultural, social, economic and institutional factors to explain diferences in innovation or difusion rates (e.g. Baldi & Thaung, 2002; Barnes & Huf, 2003; Elliott & Tang, 2004; Funk, 2001; Mizukoshi, Okino, & Tardy, 2001; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003; Ratlif, 2002; Sharma & Nakamura, 2004; Sigurdson, 2001; Srivastava, 2003). These studies have garnered an impressive, yet non-cumulative array of explanations for mobile service uptake. They are often dressed in prescriptive ‘‘success factors” about the mobile internet adoption. Examples of factors that have been identified to influence adoption are cultural idiosyncrasies such as commuting habits, ‘‘gadgetophelia”, limited private space; industry particularities such as NTT DoCoMo’s i-mode business model; or technical diferences at content delivery as the use of de facto Internet standards.

Acceptance models and difusion models assume what Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) call a proxy view of technology. This view is insensitive to diferences in variances in technical features that underlie mobile services. Consequently, diferences that become accentuated with service adoption, e.g. network efects and service interdependencies, cannot be easily accounted. This makes these models inappropriate for explaining variance in innovation paths which result from supply-side service diferences, e.g. specification variance or interdependencies between services. The ensemble models maintain an ensemble view of technology (e.g. Baldi & Thaung, 2002; Barnes & Huf, 2003; Elliott & Tang, 2004; Funk, 2001; Mizukoshi et al., 2001; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003; Ratlif, 2002; Sharma & Nakamura, 2004; Sigurdson, 2001; Srivastava, 2003), generally seeing technology as a production network. However, interdependencies between the institutionalization of technology and institutions of use, as well as interdependencies between service features and use-outcomes are also here neglected.

To sum up, the explanations put forward for mobile service adoption ofer insights about (1) factors that predict the individual acceptance of a mobile service; (2) variances in the difusion of mobile services in diferent socio-economic strata; and (3) factors in the macro environments that influence adoption and innovation rates across regions or markets. However, the models are of limited use when we want to explain diferences in the adoption of families of mobile services across diverse contexts and technical platforms. As Ito, 2005, p. 6 suggests: ‘‘research agendas that survey a wide range of national contexts can have the efect of producing a perception that we are dealing with a single technology deployed across multiple settings”. Tendencies to treat technology as a homogenous matter downplays the tool view of technology (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) and thereby any subtle diferences in artifact features, their relations, and interdependencies arising and potentially becoming augmented when a larger ensemble of factors – technological, natural and human – interface. Mobile services draw upon a complex and ever expanding set of service, network and device features (Knutsen & Overby, 2004) aforded by continuously advancing infrastructures that are institutionally enforced and regulated by standards (Lyytinen & King, 2006). Therefore they often emerge as ‘‘hybrids” (Sarker & Wells, 2003) where content and multiple service features aforded by infrastructure are integrated. For instance, infotainment services merge information and entertainment data while interpersonal location based services blend GPS or other location data with messaging features. Accordingly, careful articulation of the artifact is important in order not to miss features in specifications that become pronounced as technology institutionalizes. We need a way to connect characteristics of infrastructures and services with the demand side innovation dynamics and analyze their joint evolution over time. Next we propose an approach that accomplishes this: it views enacted service characteristics at both demand and supply sides as path dependent institutions.

## 2.2. Institutional shaping of technology

Markus (2005) emphasizes the importance of accounting for how and why diferences in artifact features can influence technology adoption outcomes. By foregrounding features and patterns of use, she wants to explain how ‘‘artifacts purporting to do the same or similar things. . . might be associated with systematically diferent patterns of technology use practices” (Markus, 2005, p. 10). Central for these endeavors is to ask: what are these fea tures and how do they difer, and which level of feature analysis is appropriate for a good explanation?

In general, technology features denote to a broad set of technology characteristics ranging from detailed technical specifications inscribed by engineers and standard setters, properties ascribed by users, to use gratifications. Significant variations prevail for each family of technologies. But each characteristic can also generate varying efects in diferent contexts. To discern these diferentiating efects it is useful to expand Markus’ position by adopting Orlikowski’s (2000) idea of ‘‘technology-in-practice”, which suggests that while varying technical features are enacted across time and space only some of them, through structuration<sup>3</sup> (Giddens, 1984), become established as institutionalized features. These features are then elevated and standardized as generally accepted rule-like expectations about technology, its capabilities and use value. Through institutionalization service features become reified and obtain a taken-for-granted status and span over extended time and space (Barley, 1986; Barley & Tolbert, 1997; Giddens, 1984; Orlikowski, 2000). They start act like a social institution and are chronologically re-produced through a cumulative history of interactions among service producers and users. Thereby they reach an institutional status in the form of manifest rules and typifications that shape and channel social conduct (Giddens, 1984).

By incorporating these ideas from new institutionalism (Barley, 1990; Barley & Tolbert, 1997; Giddens, 1984; Hargadon & Douglas, 2001) we are invited to analyze how technology over time embeds rules and resources into social conduct that will grow into institutions where its interpretation and applications become deeply sedimented in the institutional acts of provisioning and use. Recurrent activations and modifications of technology features permit these institutions to grow, become interconnected, and to increase in their significance, persistency and pervasiveness. These institutions act, in the end game, as powerful social ‘‘filters” or ‘‘virtual templates” for cognition and behavior (Barley & Tolbert, 1997). In this way they constitute a sort of socio-technical grammar that preserves the social order by shaping cognition, generating a sense of a moral order, and legitimating ongoing technology use.

Institutionalization of technologies has a bearing upon how specifications of technologies (e.g. standards) are presented and interpreted, and what they come to signify. Novel rules and resources implicated by the introduction of new artifacts must compete against existing institutional rules and resources, and thus challenge what is dominant, legitimate and well understood. As Hargadon and Douglas note, novel technologies make ‘‘two social forces collide, one accounting for the stability of social systems and the other for change” (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001, p. 476). The life of a new artifact, e.g. a new mobile service, is thus determined by the degree to which rules and resources associated with its provisioning and use sustain or collide with those already in place. For example, introduction of a MMS based service need to be integrated with the standardized and accepted features of SMS and its success will depend to what extent it aligns or collides with the rules and resources associated with SMS use. Consequently, the more proposed specifications align with the prevail ing provisioning practices and associated standards, the stronger is the institutionalization on the provisioning side. Likewise, the larger the number of existing features reproduced and drawn upon during service enactments, the stronger is the institutionalization of the technology on the demand side. The degree to which service and provisioning side institutions are mutually shaped by the novelty depends how the new rules and resources can be merged with the existing orders on both sides. Provisioning organizations and actors – as producers of specifications – thus ‘‘come to mirror or replicate salient aspects of environmental diferentiation in their own structures” (Scott, 1987, p. 506). Likewise, users come to mirror and instantiate the institutional inscriptions put forth by the provisioning organizations and established through widespread service deployment.

Within institutional analysis focal points in explaining technology innovation and diffusion are examinations of ongoing service provisioning and use processes and how they shape and are shaped by institutionalization. Accordingly, new services and their difusion must be assessed relative to how the prevailing powerful institutions shape their evolution. This helps us capture how institutions become ‘‘accretions of past practices and understandings that set conditions on action” (Barley & Tolbert, 1997, p. 99). In particular, we need to ask how new service characteristics relate to and shape both the provisioning and the use side of the institutionalization. Next we suggest a framework that connects institutional shaping of service specifications on the supply side with how institutionalized service properties and gratifications emerge on the demand side.

## 2.3. The tripartite institutional shaping of mobile services

We observe three interrelated institutional processes associated with mobile services: (1) generation of technical specifications; (2) contextual and ongoing sense-making of properties attributed to services; and (3) production and obtainment of use gratifications. This tripartite distinction connects a mobile service specification to its social and institutional context and then narrates how service properties and gratifications become socially entrenched and institutionally implicated during ongoing and expansive use of mobile services. Adoption explanations are thereby extended to ‘‘institutional logics” that connect an individual’s use to the ongoing wider institutionalization of service characteristics. We can analyze each of the three processes in isolation, but the idea of institutions interacting and colliding demands that we probe carefully their joint dynamics. Thereby, we can discern how specifications gradually institutionalize at the provisioning side, and how they enable and constrain the constitution of service properties and gratifications on the demand side.

Whilst most service specifications are produced by standards bodies or key technology provisioning actors (Lyytinen & King, 2006), service properties and gratifications are largely culturally embedded, socially constructed and typified during extended use. They are socially constructed by those who ‘‘materially” enact specifications and instantiate service features. Service specifications thus become instantiated in enactments that are both enabled and constrained by structures of: legitimacy (‘‘Is this correct and acceptable service”?), signification (‘‘What does this service use mean?”) and domination (‘‘What does this service enable and constrain me to do and achieve?”) (Giddens, 1984). These structures afect then the ‘use repertoire’ users build around technology while they monitor their own and others’ technology enactments (Blechar, Knutsen, & Damsgaard, 2005).

## 2.3.1. Service specifications

Specifications refer here to texts about technology which define the service as inscribed by its advocates, e.g. designers, architects and other supply-side actors. These texts are written and agreed definitions of technical elements that pertain to mobile service – not the cultural (Hill, 1988) or hermeneutic (Boland, 1985) interpretations of technology. Service specifications are normally conveyed in de jure standards like GSM SMS messaging standard (Trosby, 2004), or by de facto standards like HTML or WAP standard (Sigurdson, 2001). They can be also promoted by single actors like the i-mode standard by NTT DoCoMo (Funk, 2001; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003). Specifications convey a fact sheet of ser vice characteristics expressed in a technical terminology enlisting their engineering measures and associated design heuristics related to the artifact: payload, type characters (for alphabet), interface support and modality, transfer rates, radio access standards, mark up language standards, decoding standards, format support, etc. Scrutiny of service specifications ensures that service providers can easily record underlying material and functional diferences in services and coordinate their action (e.g. between manufactures or diferent operators).

Written specifications reflect a shared understanding about technology artifacts among those who have the will build the technology and aim to put it into the users’ hands. They form a basis also for users’ sense-making and formation of technological frames (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). During institutionalization these specifications also increasingly penetrate the life worlds of those putting them forward and shape their understanding of the services and their provisioning. The greater the enactment of specifications among providers, the more deeply they become institutionalized. Ultimately, they obtain a ‘‘fixed” nature about how service provisioning should be carried out and what the services mean including their underlying business logics and motives.

Specifications appear most of the time as fixed as they are pursued by the provisioning institutions. At the same time they constitute all the time a new ‘‘text” for potential users. As for any written text, the text will remain essentially the same, but it will be flexibly inter preted across contexts: i.e., technical representation and the social reading of that technology in any institutional environment difer (Bijker, Hughes, & Pinch, 1987).

## 2.3.2. Service properties

Properties refer to what the specification ‘‘text” signifies to users. These properties can be of material and symbolic nature and they are embodied in technology enactments<sup>4</sup> (Orlikowski, 2000). Service properties are material and symbolic qualities of a mobile service that are socially constructed by actors who interact directly or indirectly with the service. Naturally, these properties may difer significantly from the assumptions and intentions inscribed by technology architects when writing their specifications (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

Service properties reflect socially constructed qualities attributed to machine–machine interactions, human–machine interactions, and human–machine–human interactions. Properties are neither about sole technical characteristics, nor about individual’s perceptions. They encompass generic qualities and attributions that arise when mobile services mediate social interactions. Institutionalization of these properties occurs when meanings associated with service qualities coalesce in time and space and start organize patterns of service use. We suggest three categories of properties: (1) instrumental, (2) aesthetic and (3) expressive properties.

Instrumental properties define how a service can serve as a means for human–human and human–computing interactions. They signify functional qualities that support specific user actions: speed of transmission, mass distribution capability, service compatibility, text entry and navigation functions, download/upload time, synchronicity, and latency, etc. In short, they reflect qualities of a mobile service which, in the eyes of the (prospective) user, determine its functional aspects including reliability, use outcomes, performance, ease of use, relative advantage and usefulness.

Aesthetic properties define how a service afects user experience of the service and/or of the aesthetics of the artifacts involved. Diferences in aesthetic properties influence to what extent services evoke varying visual and audible sensations.<sup>6</sup> Examples include: visual quality and clarity, audio fidelity, or personalization options or possibilities for artifact decoration to express and project an identity. Ringtones<sup>7</sup>, messaging tones, call-back tones, picture backgrounds, logos and screensavers are typical mobile services where aesthetic properties are fore-grounded in the service or device – their aesthetic look-and-feel, and individual appeal and appearance critical to user experience.

Expressive properties are features that define how a service enables and/or constrains expressive communications. Expressive communications<sup>8</sup> (Carey, 2004; Gillies et al., 2004) are human communication that convey strongly personal attitudes or feelings. Services can vary significantly in how they, e.g. with multimedia features, can mediate such communications. Overall expressiveness has been defined as ‘‘the degree to which users of mobile services perceive the services as suitable for expressing their emotions and social or personal identity” (Nysveen et al., 2005, p. 332). In essence, the expressive properties afect the impact and intensity of interactions in a virtual world by approximating and even sometimes augmenting the reach and richness of bodily communications. Of particular interest in our study are those service qualities which influence users’ expressive communications. These include among others communication richness (i.e. capacity to express with multiple code systems the information) and the degree of synchronicity (synchronous vs. asynchronous capability). But maybe most important for messaging is its expressive reach (pervasiveness) governed by properties pertaining to diferent socio-technical conditions that arise when many people can use the service – e.g. network and interaction efects.

## 2.3.3. Service gratifications

Gratifications are defined as pleasures, delights, and fulfillments obtained during and from the consumption of a mobile service (Blumler & Katz, 1974; Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001; Leung & Wei, 2000; McQuail, 1987; Staford, Staford, & Schkade, 2004). Examples of service gratifications include relaxation, status/fashion (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001; Leung & Wei, 2000), sociability (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001), reassurance (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001), immediacy (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001), mobility (Leung & Wei, 2000; Nysveen et al., 2005) and enjoyment/fun (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001).

While most view gratifications as an individual level experience, we underscore that grati fications not only embody personal experience, but also gain institutional character by relying upon typified labels about ongoing use experience. Gratifications extend from the individual’s experience and phenomenology towards an institutional level as sets of connotations by which users make sense and establish a common life-world around mobile service use outcomes. Gratifications institutionalize when rules about their obtainment are labeled, become taken for granted, and reach a standardized signification associated with the artifact use.

In order to delineate types of gratifications we adapt on Staford et al.’s (2004) threefold typology of Internet gratifications.<sup>9</sup> The typology distinguishes between content, process and social gratifications.

Content gratifications refer to labeling of pleasures, delights and fulfillments sought and obtained from digital content – e.g. text, audio, and video – use. They arise from consumption of digital content crafted by professional providers, e.g. news, infotainment, ringtones, logos, wallpapers, maps, directions etc. More often, however, content gratifications emanate during consumption of content interchanged via interpersonal messaging. Accord ingly, content gratifications are not only associated with ‘‘industrial” content, but also with a myriad of user-generated content.

Process gratifications signify connotations of pleasures, delights and fulfillments obtained during and from the process of engaging with a service. They emanate from the ongoing experience of using a service (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001; Leung & Wei, 2000; Staford et al., 2004). This covers discovering new services, becoming ‘‘literate”, mastering technology, or personalizing a device or service. Playing, experimenting, learning, navigating and exploring can be gratifying for its own sake. Conversely, this may not be gratifying if plagued by complexity and glitches hampering the rendering of gratifications. Process gratifications are important because mobile services ofer broad latitude to personalized experience and can vary sharply across a myriad of devices<sup>10</sup> and the infrastructure they attach to.

Finally, services are expected to bring social gratifications (Ho¨flich & Ro¨ssler, 2001; Leung & Wei, 2000; Staford et al., 2004). Social gratifications are defined as connotations to pleasures, delights and fulfillments obtained from interactions between physically or virtually co-present users.<sup>11</sup> Mobile services bring social gratifications by enabling people to nurture social relations, by facilitating micro-coordination and organizing, by expressing individual identity and providing social contacts (Ito & Okabe, 2003a; Ito, Okabe, & Matsuda, 2005; Ling, 2004; Matsuda, 2005). They also emanate from sharing and coconsumption of pictures and messages, soliciting social responses, carrying out personal exchanges, and establishing social contacts. Certain mobile services, e.g. SMS, e-mail, chat, instant messaging, and interactive gaming extend gratifications associated with co-present interactions to virtual domains. These extensions augment gratifications through local and peripheral nurturing of social relations and social contacts across social networks.

We emphasize that the analysis of gratifications goes beyond utilitarian calculus, which are typically implicated in constructs deployed in technology acceptance and difusion research including performance, relative advantage, usefulness, etc. (Rogers, 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Content, process and social gratifications are not limited to instrumental use, but often have a hedonic character. Using marketing theory we distinguish between utilitarian and hedonic value dimensions of gratifications (Babin, Darden, & Grifin, 1994; Mathwick, Malhotra, & Rigdon, 2001). Utilitarian gratifications orient towards instrumental goals. Hedonic gratifications orient towards ‘‘the mode of being” and ‘‘mood”; joy, entertainment and pleasure. ## 2.4. Relationships between specifications, properties and gratifications

The three institutional processes connect through feed-forward and feed-back loops as illustrated in Fig. 1.<sup>12</sup> The feed-forward loops determine that (1) specifications are expected to ofer rule and resource sets implicated in the shaping of user-attributed properties; and (2) properties enable and constrain the scope and magnitude of user gratifications. The feed-forward loops represent the supply flows within a ‘‘mobile service-ecosystem” whereby specifications materialize and users construct properties which will render utilitarian and hedonic gratifications that become typified and shared.

The feedback loops are significant because properties and gratifications must become socially sedimented to render the services widely accepted. When institutionalization unfolds, properties become manifest and taken-for-granted, gratifications gain legitimacy and become typified as institutional norms and rules. The feedback determines how: (1) gratifications re-shape and influence users’ understandings of service properties, and (2) how properties and gratifications influence interpretation of specifications and afect their evolution. The feedback impacts to what extent supply side specifications will or will not align with the use institutions. What has become legitimate and dominant provides opportunities for expansive service provisioning, but can at the same time restrict the scope and ease among users to comprehend future specifications. Properties set directions on how specifications fit, extend, or deviate from the current shape of technology. Thus, the growth of institutions around specifications, properties and gratifications come to play a dual role in shaping service innovation and use: they become implicated by influencing the agency of both service providers and users and their interactions. These processes are connected also to institutionalization processes within other institutional spheres (Scott,

1987). Providers are influenced by the institutionalized properties and gratifications being established by users. Any specification they bring forward mirrors also influences from other institutional spheres such as regulatory bodies, governments, standardization bodies, and other industries. Likewise use properties and gratifications are not constructed in vacuum but reflect the institutional influence emanating from multiple spheres outside the providers. These include for instance, family values, social norms, community structures, or general legal structures.

By heeding into the interdependencies between the three processes we can analyze the mutual process of institutionalization between supply-side specifications (standards) and demand-side user properties and gratifications. In particular, we can ask to what extent diferences in specifications and their institutionalization shape property construction and gratifications associated with properties, and how dynamic interactions between specifications, properties and gratifications shape innovation paths by enabling the erection of diferent sorts of use and provisioning institutions.

## 3. Case study: institutional shaping in Norway and Japan

## 3.1. Methodology

We carried out an illustrative theory building multi-‘‘site” case study (Eisenhardt, 1989; Stake, 2000; Yin, 2003) to understand why and how institutional shaping of specifications, properties and gratifications produce distinct mobile service innovation paths. The selected cases are two leading countries in mobile service difusion: Norway and Japan. Mobile service evolutions in these two countries illustrate how diferent innovation trajectories emerge due to diferences in the institutional reproduction of service provisioning and use. Theoretical sampling guided our case identification. Two ‘‘polar type” (Eisenhardt, 1989) cases were selected based on a review of commonalities and discrepancies identified in an analysis of mobile data services across several countries (Knutsen & Lyytinen, 2005). In this analysis Norway and Japan stood out as countries, which not only exhibit two distinct mobile service trajectories, but which in many other respects are quite similar. Both countries are afluent, have a long history of innovating and providing mobile services, and are among the top countries in mobile communication penetration and use. Accordingly, a large number of scholarly analyses both from the technological and social perspectives are available about mobile communications covering user behavior studies, infrastructural analyses and socio-cultural studies. We focused on publications that covered years 1999–2005 in mobile service innovation and use in both countries. During this period significant improvements – though quite distinct – took place in mobile services in these countries so we expected to see how diferences in the respective institutionalization processes yielded diferent outcomes.

While adopting the framework outlined above we carefully analyzed and coded contents of books, journals and reports identified in academic databases; e.g. Business Source Premier, ABI Proquest, and Science Direct. We also included in our analysis published conference papers, magazines, web-sites and newspaper reports. The case data were first analyzed for technological similarities and discrepancies in underlying infrastructural capabilities (technology specifications) of the dominant mobile infrastructural platforms. For both countries we tabulated themes and findings under the categories (1) content and nature of specifications, (2) typical service properties, and (3) emergence and typolo gies of gratifications. These findings were organized in data matrices (Miles & Huberman, 1994) which were then analyzed for commonalities and discrepancies both using diachronic and synchronic dimensions. Before delving deeper into the innovation paths in the two countries we ofer first a brief narrative of the general evolution of mobile services in these two countries.

## 3.2. Similarities and diferences in mobile services in Norway and Japan

Similarities abound in the key events that shaped digital mobile telephony and service difusion in Norway and Japan. These include, as illustrated in Fig. 2, (a) market liberal ization leading to competition among multiple operators during the early 1990s; (b) rollout of 2G digital cellular networks in the early and mid 1990s; (c) aggressive oligopolistic competition fuelling exponential mobile subscriber growth, and (d) mobile messaging epidemic; first among youth, but with a subsequent spread largely throughout the whole population (Ito & Okabe, 2003a, 2003b; Ling, 2004; Matsuda, 2005). A clear diference, however, is found in the types of 2G mobile networks. Telenor and Netcom in Norway both opted for GSM. In Japan, all operators were forced to lease capacity on NTT DoCoMo’s proprietary PDC network. So, rather than an industry-wide consortium like the Group Spe´cial Mobile´<sup>13</sup> (today the GSM Association), network, handset and service standards were more or less decided by NTT DoCoMo. This was decisive for the following data service difusion showing institutional path dependency.

The first handsets (e.g. Nokia 7650 and Sony-Ericsson T68) which integrated MMS and mobile e-mail capability were introduced within GPRS networks in 2003. Thus, almost a decade of SMS adoption took place before multimedia capabilities, on-device e-mail and separate WAP browsing services were introduced. Although these services are functionally similar they are technically distinct. This formed a baseline from which large scale variations in the service ecologies of the two countries transpired. In both service ecologies the profound growth in mobile phone uptake was paralleled by massive increase in messaging (Funk, Working Paper; Knutsen & Lyytinen, 2005; Matsuda, 2005). Messaging scaled fast towards broad institutional acceptance and became a ‘‘killer application”. Beyond plain messaging, however, the mobile service ecologies diverged signifi cantly. Japan witnessed success with mobile Internet services whereas the evolution in Norway remained stagnant for WAP and picture messaging. To understand this divergence we need to take a closer look at the underlying messaging technologies and how their enactment pulled supply and demand side institutions to radically diferent directions.

## 3.2.2. Japan

Japan has become known by her success in ofering mobile data services such as i-mode (NTT DoCoMo), J-Sky (J-phone/Vodafone) and Au (KDDI). By the end of 2001, i-mode had already 30 million subscribers. J-Sky and Au had both 9 million. In 2005 oficial statistics revealed that 86% of the 85 million<sup>20</sup> mobile phone users subscribe to mobile portal services. These portal services have made Japanese operators among the world leaders in data revenue (Funk, 2001; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003; Nokia, 2003). Ringtones and screensavers (39%) followed by entertainment (21%), gaming and horoscope (20%), information services (11%), database services (5%), and transaction services (4%) were announced as the most popular service categories on i-mode in 2002 (Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003).<sup>21</sup>

However, messaging formed the killer application among Japanese data services. It created the initial feed-forward ‘‘push” and became a key enabler for the difusion of other mobile services (Funk, Working Paper; Knutsen & Lyytinen, 2005). Unlike Norway no reliable statistics exist about mobile e-mail growth in Japan. However, several scholarly studies ofer clues of its wide and fast spread. Though browsing with content downloads such as games and ringing tones comprised already about 50% of i-mode activity in 2000 market studies confidently asserted that ‘‘e-mail is the killer app” which ‘‘initially attracted users to i-mode” and ‘‘remains critical to i-mode’s appeal” (Mizukoshi et al., 2001, p. 93). A national study of 3000 respondents reports that 57.7% of Japanese subscribers were e-mail users in 2001 (Habuchi, Dobashi, Tsuji, & Iwata, 2005). Okada (2005) reports for the following year that e-mail comprised of 83.3% of user activity and Ishii found that ‘‘half of all mobile Internet users used e-mail (excluding non-Internet short messages)” and concluded that ‘‘the main usage of the mobile Internet is e-mail” (Ishii, 2004, p. 48). Likewise, Sharma and Nakamura (2004, p. 49) assert that ‘‘Japan’s growing band of e-mail junkies has driven the phenomenal growth in i-mode”. On average, 87 e-mails were sent from mobile phones monthly in Japan in 2002 (Ishii, 2004). This was 19 more than that what was sent via a computer. Given that an average subscriber exchanged 100 mobile e-mails in 2000 (Mizukoshi et al., 2001) and assuming 50/50 send-receive ratio it is plausible that the growth of e-mail use in Japan has been similar to growth in SMS use in Norway.

Multimedia messaging in Japan occurs also via e-mail and not MMS (Kato, Okabe, Ito, & Uemoto, 2005). J-Phone launched picture e-mail in November 2000 and by July 2002, 60% of J-Phone subscribers owned a camera phone and subscribed to their ‘sha-mail’ service (Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003). A similar service was launched by KDDI in April 2002 and had gained 2.5 million subscribers by December 2002. NTT DoCoMo launched their counterpart, the i-shot service, in June 2002. By December 2002, 10% of its subscribers (4 million) had signed up for i-shot (Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003) and in 2003 cameras were more or less a standard feature (Kato et al., 2005). The core e-mail solutions have later been expanded to enable exchanges of video-clips or other types of attachments such as Microsoft Ofice and Adobe files.

## 3.2.3. Comparison and summary

We can observe how strong demand side institutions emerged around messaging in both countries. Yet overall mobile service ecologies evolved diferently. In Norway SMS was introduced as an integral part of the GSM infrastructure, whereas WAP and MMS were later introduced as separate hierarchical protocols-sets on top of the GSM delivery infrastructure. The latter failed to meet industry and user expectations. Instead advances to data services arrived with premium SMS innovations exploiting the current mass use and available network efects that already existed for SMS in Norway. In contrast, mobile e-mail and internet portals have been tightly intertwined in Japan. Leading service provisioning firms created early on a joint platform that spearheaded innovation within new services like content distribution, picture e-mail, location-based services, and mobile payment. Some of these, especially picture e-mail and portal services gained swiftly user footprint and social legitimacy. In 2005 NTT DoCoMo provided access to more than 4500 oficial i-mode sites and 84,000 unoficial sites. Menon, Page, Watt, & Bell (2005) report that 39% of the users used multimedia messaging during 2005. For Scandinavian countries, the corresponding number was 16% (Menon et al., 2005). More importantly, new revenue streams for mobile operators and content providers emerged around the new supply side specifications and promoted ongoing growth on the demand side properties and gratifications that institutionalized fast due to the increasingly widespread use of mobile messaging. These included e.g. extensions to SMS in Norway or additions of pictures to e-mail and hyperlinks to mobile web pages in Japan. The increasingly integrated nature of messaging and mobile Internet services on the provisioning side helped Japan innovate with unprecedented speed and scope around mobile Internet (Funk, 2001, Working Paper; Kodama, 2002). In Norway, strong demand side institutions emerged swiftly around SMS. Further provisioning side innovations followed this path with the introduction of premium SMS. In contrast, the separate MMS and WAP services experienced low adoption rates. Interestingly, revenue for profit seeking operators and content providers were not primarily obtained via MMS or WAP as first expected, but emerged instead around the institutionalized properties and gratifications of SMS. In this way SMS became the primary vehicle to promote and deliver premium value added services. We can now apply the institutional framework to understand how diferences in institutionally enforced platform specifications resulted in divergent service properties and gratifications that made the mobile innovation trajectories pull in diferent directions.

## 3.3. Specifications of mobile messaging

We start by analyzing technical specifications of SMS, MMS and mobile e-mail (Dornan, 2002; Elliott & Philips, 2004; Umar, 2004 NTT DoCoMo and the GSM Association). The analysis includes payload, text character limits, supported formats, transmission protocols and addressing standards as summarized in Appendix 1. For mobile e-mail we focus on specifications of NTT DoCoMo’s mobile e-mail service<sup>22</sup>.

SMS is by far the simplest messaging technology with a fraction of payload and stringent character limits. SMS supports only text format and uses conversion when transferred to/from e-mail over Internet standards such as SMTP and MIME (Umar, 2004). MMS is much richer, but it is still not as advanced as mobile e-mail. Constraints are found regarding payload, e.g. the 300 KB limit on MMS services in Norway is only 60% capacity compared to NTT DoCoMo’s e-mail service. While MMS and mobile e-mail are similar in their format support – both support multimedia formats and use HTML mark up language – the attachment function in mobile e-mail supports additional file formats such as Microsoft Word and Adobe PDF. For addressing SMS and MMS support the E-164 phone number standards. MMS also supports the e-mail addresses of the IETF RFC

2822 standard.<sup>23</sup> Mobile e-mail, on the other hand, supports the latter and includes, if not changed by the user, the phone number as part of the default e-mail address.<sup>24</sup> Finally, the transmission protocols difer. Whereas MMS utilizes either WAP WSP or SMTP- MIME/ TCP/IP, mobile e-mail has utilized only the latter. SMS utilizes X.25 (Trosby, 2004) which demands a message routing application to convert and transfer data from the network layer to the transport layer. MMS also uses a diferent access point name (APN) than WAP. This inhibits simultaneous use of WAP over GPRS/UMTS and MMS in the same terminal. Overall, these diferences suggest that alternative service institutions have shaped around these specifications at the supply side which were reflected in diferences at the demand side in form of properties and gratifications.

## 3.4. Properties of mobile messaging services

## 3.4.1. Instrumental properties

Messaging allows users to exchange messages in both one-to-one and one-to-many mode. However, the central diference is found in how MMS and mobile e-mail facilitate exchanges between mobile phone users and e-mail users. This permits greater flexibility in mediating social contacts across networks. Both mobile e-mail and MMS have a capacity to mediate enhanced HTML and multimedia content exchanges. In the initial versions of MMS this was limited to text and images (Umar, 2004), but has later been expanded to support video-clips, and the MP3 audio format for music downloads, music sharing and podcasts (e.g. PCs, laptops and MP3 players). Relative to the properties of mobile e-mail, with the exception of MP3, MMS does not support exchanges with commonly used third party file formats such as those employed by Microsoft and Adobe. This gives another advantage for mobile e-mail. It supports a larger range of digital exchanges including rich documents, like word processing files, receipts, tickets and other attachments. As such, institutionalization of the enhanced mobile service properties can be mobilized in broader spheres of social life and across both mobile and tethered networks. The limitation of 300 KB payload for MMS also implies a downscaling of pictures of larger size that in turn leaves MMS inferior to mobile e-mail in bridging the worlds of the mobile and the PC.

The messaging technologies all have instrumental properties that allow for eficient data exchange between devices. However, the richer formats supported by MMS and mobile e-mail yield additional properties to support for instance installation and configuration files, e-commerce applications, and images used for bar-coding<sup>25</sup>, ticketing, advertisements or receipts. Moreover, file attachments of mobile e-mail render additional instrumental properties for eficient exchange of electronic documents, receipts, coupons and tickets. Relative to SMS, these properties form a basis for a set of richer instrumental properties.

## 3.4.2. Aesthetic properties

While aesthetic properties have been central for content personalization in Norway (Kristofersen et al., 2005; Nielsen & Hanseth, 2003) and to ringtone/tune purchases via i-mode in Japan (Barnes & Huf, 2003; Hofmann, 2001; Mizukoshi et al., 2001), we can observe how diferences in technical specifications also enhance diferently the aesthetic properties. In particular, varying specifications regarding audible and visual capabilities render variance in the aesthetic service properties. As with instant messaging on the Internet ‘generation txt’ has widely embraced the use of alphanumeric character combinations and symbols to display emotions (e.g., smileys :-) (Ling, 2004; Rheingold, 2002). In Japan, specific words have become attached around such aesthetic properties. For instance ‘kaomoji’ refers to face letters used to form smileys whilst ‘e-moji’<sup>26</sup> (picture character) refers to animated and unanimated proprietary (network restricted) pictograms used in e-mail messages (Wallace, Hofmann, Scuka, Blut, & Barrow, 2002).

SMS imposes severe constraints to these aesthetic enhancements because tailoring is limited to alphanumeric characters or how a certain device reads and graphically displays character combinations. Extended multimedia capabilities (e.g., animated pictograms and attachments of photo and video) with MMS and mobile e-mail can add aesthetic properties by introducing pictures, sounds and animations. For instance, upon launching the 901i series handsets NTT DoCoMo increased e-mail attachment payloads at the same time of adding new ‘emojis’ and the Deco-mail client which allowed users to form and use media intensive emoticons in messages. This is but one example how specifications aford new aesthetical dimensions to service properties. However, the modest use of MMS in Norway along with the almost non-existing use of WAP have constrained the institutionalization of new aesthetic properties and inhibited users from fully embracing such multimedia. In fact, MMS connotes to users more limited picture capability rather than multimedia messaging (Ling, 2004).

## 3.4.3. Expressive properties

All three messaging services have powerful expressive properties to rapidly spread content across user networks. By exhibiting positive network efects (Katz & Shapiro, 1985, 1986) they have become more powerful and valuable as the number of users has increased. With two way connectivity the services exhibit properties to scale exponentially (Reed, 2001). Yet, there exist some important diferences in their expressive properties. SMS and e-mail have an advantage over MMS due to the sheer magnitude of users. On a worldwide basis, with approximately two thirds of the world’s wireless users being GSM customers (over 1.2 billion users)<sup>27</sup> and the fact that SMS is available over other networks – even the Japanese – it has a strong expressive reach. Yet, if we consider the large network of computer based e-mail users, which enables mobile e-mail and wired TCP/IP based e-mail to communicate, this gives a compatibility advantage to mobile e-mail<sup>28</sup> as bridges are built between the installed base of mobile messaging and the tethered internet.

With respect to expressive richness, mobile e-mail has stronger properties. This is not due to its multimedia capabilities, but also because HTML allows links to other content on the mobile internet. For years, NTT DoCoMo has ofered integrated ‘send to’, ‘call to’ and ‘web to’ capabilities so that direct hyperlinks can easily be incorporated into e-mail communications. Together, this can build a helix efect around expressive properties that speed up their institutionalization. Similar capabilities became available for GSM with advanced messaging clients that support hyperlink recognition and WAP Push in WAP version 1.2. Yet, the modest browsing of WAP sites and the small number of sites to link to on the mobile internet has slowed down the institutionalization of these advanced properties and dampened any positive feedback efects. The fact that the character capacity of SMS only occasionally constrains the richness needed for everyday messaging (Ling, 2004) has with this generated a ‘‘suficiency” barrier that further prohibits the growth of more advanced messaging.

## 3.5. Gratifications<sup>29</sup> of mobile messaging services

## 3.5.1. Content gratifications

Mobile service growth has been propelled significantly by individualized messaging where the content is crafted by users. Such user generated content is highly versatile and personalized and can render an almost unlimited set of hedonic and utilitarian content gratifications. Of strongest importance are the gratifications gained from content supporting social interaction, contact, and coordination. (Ito & Okabe, 2003a; Ling, 2004; Ling & Haddon, 2001; Matsuda, 2005). In Norway about two thirds of 882 SMS messages investigated contained statements associated with micro/social-coordination (Ling, 2004). This was followed by content aimed at grooming (17%) – nurturing of friendships, relations and romances, answers (14%), questions (11%), information (6%), and personal news (5%). Similar findings have been made in Japan (Ito & Okabe, 2003a; Matsuda, 2005), where the majority of mobile e-mail supports everyday social contact and activity with number of people in the social vicinity (Ishii, 2004).

Personal specificity, adaptability and richness of messaging are superior to what thirdparty information, entertainment or location based services can ofer. However, as shown by deco-mail<sup>30</sup> in Japan (Ishii, 2004), content providers can accentuate these gratifications by ofering graphical content to make expressions of emotions more animated and vivid. In contrast, the less advanced properties of SMS along with the technical divide between SMS and MMS have constrained a wider adoption and typification of accentuated content gratifications.

## 3.5.2. Process gratifications

On one hand, the eficiency and simplicity of writing short messages constitutes its most significant process gratification. On the other hand, such utilitarian fulfillments can sometimes be foreshadowed by the hedonic process gratifications that arise from being creative, careful, or artistic in crafting appealing messages. Interestingly, as uncovered by text analysis studies, the messaging technologies (MMS and e-mail more than SMS) support both ends of this spectrum (Ito & Okabe, 2005; Ling, 2004). As multimedia capabilities of MMS or mobile e-mail do not necessarily compromise process eficiency, these capabilities can ofer complementary process gratifications. However, the limited support of SMS for graphical decoration constrains wider institutionalization of augmented process gratifica tions associated with the handling of images and multimedia.

## 3.5.3. Social gratifications

Messaging is tightly interwoven in common social practices and has created new patterns of communication and opportunities for new social gratifications. We already pointed out how the majority of SMS exchanges in Norway aim at coordination and grooming (Ling, 2004) and how mobile e-mail is used in Japan among frequently interacting individuals (Ishii, 2004; Ito & Okabe, 2003a; Matsuda, 2005). Both in Japan and Norway messaging serves as a medium to establish and maintain social relationships. Ling (2004) found that the asynchronous nature of messaging lowered barriers in the beginning of relationships, facilitated transitions to synchronous communication (e.g. voice), and enabled people to erect barriers if one party chose to disengage or terminate a relationship. Similarly, Ishii (2004) found how mobile e-mail ofers a double fulfillment. It supports communications of personal feelings while securing that face-to-face interaction can be minimized, when desired.

Although most social gratifications arise from person-to-person communications, i.e. between (boy/girl-) friends, spouse, other family members, and peers, the versatility of messaging introduces hybrid forms of inter-personal communications. Crafting as well as consuming messages take often place as a social activity with physical co-presence (Haddon, 2004; Ling, 2004), i.e. local interactions such as sharing, reading aloud, or passing digital devices around. This is not only significant for social gratifications associated with groups, but is also important in understanding how social networks operate. They often scale exponentially (Reed, 2001), and can have a potent efect on the velocity at which messages traverse. For instance, Ito & Okabe (2003a) found that trails of messaging continue after conversations, social gatherings and events. These patterns constitute new mechanisms to bond people’s physical or social ties. At the same time groups around inter ests and trends build up virtually. Hence, messaging enable gratifications associated with group membership, feelings of belonging, participation and projecting identity. Despite the specification and property diferences neither of the dominant messaging technologies appears inferior at this point.

The freedom of being able to connect while simultaneously disconnecting from burdens of physical space are fundamental to social gratifications of messaging. Ling (2004) reports of how SMS communications among Norwegian teens is significant for their emancipation. In Japan scholars found that the enabling of private communications for people under conditions of limited private space is one reason for messaging success (Ito & Okabe, 2003a; Sharma & Nakamura, 2004). While SMS, MMS and mobile e-mail are all posited to bring social gratifications, limited knowledge is available of how MMS can extend social gratifications of SMS. However, the rapid adoption and use of animation, photo and video in Japan (Ishii, 2004; Menon et al., 2005; Nielsen & Mahnke, 2003) suggest that aesthetic content can augment social gratifications by providing a new range of identity forming mechanisms such as personalized emotions, sounds and images. For instance, picture messaging was found to rotate around things associated with exchanges of everyday experiences with subjects being peers, family and oneself (Daisuke & Ito, 2003). Furthermore, as mobile e-mail provides functions such as ‘mail to’, ‘phone to’ and ‘web to’ users can also draw upon service specifications, which integrate third party content with the message content (e.g. You Tube or Picasa type social networking and sharing). New forms of social gratifications can thus emerge from weaving personal and professionally crafted content into MMS and mobile e-mail messages (cf. Srivastava, 2005 for examples). Socalled moblogging<sup>31</sup> where users post pictures, messages and multimedia from phones onto web for others to access, is but one example of how integration of specifications from other services extend social gratifications.

The strongly institutionalized expressive properties, which characterize SMS and mobile e-mail render them a powerful medium for transmitting social ‘‘epidemics”. Viral spread of multimedia content is strongly enabled with MMS and mobile e-mail. For SMS this is constrained by its limited pay load and format support. In Norway, the weaker expressive reach of MMS, due to its limited network of users, seem to constrain gratifications bound to epidemic spread. We therefore suggest that the wider adoption and institutionalization of mobile e-mail in Japan enables a richer set of social gratifications.

## 4. Discussion

The institutional analysis sheds light how institutional paths can shape the feed-forward and feedback interplay between service specifications, properties and gratifications. We observed how mutually reinforced sets of specifications, properties and gratifications formed service ecologies that institutionalized and thereby enabled and constrained the future paths of provisioning and use of mobile services. Our approach not only meets Markus’ (2005) call for scholarly explanations to how small diferences in technologies render large scale diferences in use outcomes over time. Beyond pinpointing apparent artifact diferences the institutional analysis sheds new light on how the ongoing institutionalization of service characteristics, taking place with widespread service use, elevated seemingly subtle diferences into key diferentiators in innovation trajectories. Institutions on both the supply and demand side establish mutually dependent and reinforcing service characteristics which jointly direct the trajectory of technology change and use. While the adoption of messaging has been in Norway and Japan similar in terms of difusion rates, the subtle diferences in service specifications have set service evolutions to follow idiosyncratic paths. This has arisen from diferences in the (1) open-endedness of institutional alignments, and (2) diferences in the dual bridging between institutions; i.e., bi-directional alignments of feed-forward and feed-back loops between specifications, properties and gratifications.

## 4.1. Open-endedness of institutional alignments

Alternative alignments were formed between the institutionalized specifications, properties and gratifications on the supply and demand sides in Japan and Norway. Diferent standards chosen by operators, device manufacturers, standard bodies and regulators in these countries ofered users with diferent technical specifications around which they constructed rules and typifications about what messaging services could do; their meanings, and what they could ofer. By operating with diferent specifications actors at both sides introduced changes that became institutionalized: providers in terms of specifications and users in terms of properties and gratifications. With the spiraling increase in the use of messaging, subtle diferences in the specifications of messaging fed-forward alternative ‘‘baselines” for sense-making and learning from which institutional variation on the user side could grow up. New alternatives for novelty and unique gratifications emerged at fas pace. This happened when messaging technologies reached a ‘‘killer application” status. Once they became entrenched in practice, the alignments produced strong feed-back loops that channeled ongoing service innovation and increasingly directed innovation and use. Users learned to adhere to the institutionalized specifications that were put forth by provisioning side actors including operators, device manufacturers, and content providers. Likewise these actors were influenced by rules and typifications already institutionalized on the demand side through widespread enactment of available messaging services. Institutionalization occurred thus as much among users in terms of what the services have come to ofer and mean. Ongoing service enactments, provisioning as well as use forged strong alignments which in turn established strong ‘‘path dependencies”.

In Norway the messaging specifications based on GSM standards became early on institutionalized among operators at the supply side. These formed the baseline for the institutionalization of the demand side messaging properties and gratifications. Alignments between supply and demand channeled institutionalization processes towards SMS and its enhancements. As a result operators and content providers met high adoption barriers when they started to ofer a richer set of service properties and gratifications through the separate services such as MMS and WAP. The modest capability of SMS that could not associate with strong expressive properties – especially expressive reach and richness – blocked advanced user generated content interchange over the mobile Internet. Enhanced expressive properties, such as capability to reach internet users and the use of richer media formats, were ofered separately as another provisioning institution around MMS. However, these properties did not naturally align with the institutionalized specifications and practices already and deeply associated with SMS. This institutional divide created a rift between SMS, MMS and mobile Internet services and formed over time a formidable barrier to advance new services. The properties of MMS were foreshadowed by the strength of the SMS user networks and network efects. Within mobile networks the expressive reach of MMS was limited and governed by the installed base of MMS compatible phones and degree of interoperability between operator networks. Lack of openendedness to new specifications in SMS constrained its bridging with multimedia and Internet specifications and associated properties. Instead, new properties and gratifications, e.g. as those emerging with premium SMS, expanded upon the already institution alized specifications and properties of SMS.

A parallel process is found for Japan. However, here we observe greater use, and greater cultural and social embodiment of multimedia and Internet related properties and gratifications. This grew from the initial use of e-mail that provided baseline capabilities, which did not erect barriers between messaging, multimedia and Internet services. The use of specifications of de-facto internet standards (Funk, 2001, Working Paper) allowed specifications, properties and gratifications to institutionalize and expand flexibly. They promoted seamless and organic migration towards advanced services. Users could expand on properties and gratifications associated with mobile e-mail without adopting a ‘‘separate” application or technology. Accompanied with superior expressive properties mobile e-mail ofered novel gratifications that difused by following the trails of group forming communications. Mobile e-mail constituted the baseline for content and hyperlink interchanges that extended messaging properties and gratifications and became a critical diferentiating ecological element. It allowed for ‘‘service fusion” as opposed to ‘‘service fission”. The integration between mobile e-mail and portal services glued together a smorgasbord of services with a broad range of instrumental, aesthetic and expressive properties that could yield a wider range of content and social gratifications. Especially the content gratifications bound to social multimedia exchanges were accentuated by the combined aesthetic and expressive properties of mobile e-mail.

In contrast, the constitution of MMS as a separate service created two silos of messaging technologies which operators and device manufacturers needed to promote and users had to appropriate. Additional learning barriers were erected while users had to make new associations and choices vis-a\`-vis their established communication institutions. This fissure added confusion as to how new services would relate to the established use schemata, scripts and meanings associated with existing services. Rather than evoking ‘‘interpretations among potential adopters that are based on adopters’ past understandings and experiences” (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001, p. 478), the novelty demanded deconstruction of properties peripheral to user’s common knowledge. Deviations from the current generated break-downs in the feed-forward loops of institutionalization and brought continued innovation to a halt. In contrast, the choice of e-mail and standardized specifications in Japan provided less friction during the transformation by organically building upon properties and gratifications and by providing unified pricing. This did not force users to choose either between prices of service A or service B, but formed a situation where costs could be decided based upon gratifications of person(s) generating, receiving and ‘‘consuming” the message. Users were only required to interpret and incorporate novelties around a single technology which allowed the shaping of properties and gratifications to grow as a system with the specifications ofered.

We contend that the success of premium SMS in Norway is governed by the exact same principle: Its success emanates from incremental innovation around strong property and gratification institutions. However, due to the separation between SMS, MMS and WAP, the path beyond value added SMS content remains severely constrained. Limited social anchoring of MMS forms a modest basis for natural discovery and accentuation of its service properties and gratifications.

## 4.2. Dual bridging between institutions

Our analysis reveals that the path of mobile service innovation is directed by the nature of bridging between institutions on the supply and demand sides. A founding father of i-mode, Takeshi Natsuno, proclaimed that: ‘‘The true mechanism of the great success is that the operator has made a function of coordination of the total value chain” (Sharma & Nakamura, 2004, p. 48). The true mechanism of success lies inherently in the institutional orchestration that generates both the feed-forward and feed-backward loops, but also constrains interfacing between specifications, properties and gratifications to appropriate connections.

Coordination of mobile service innovation in Japan has rotated around promulgating links between mobile messaging and mobile Internet across handset providers and content providers. Such organizing around limited sets of specifications is in contrast with the ‘‘silver bullet” thinking adopted by most players in the West. In its essence, the former approach creates horizontal and vertical bridges between specifications and service properties that foster extension, re-construction and expansion to current gratifications. The coordination aligns players in networks that promote shared architectural specifications that will feed new specifications forward by simultaneously leveraging on the feed-back of current properties and gratifications. Content provisioning centered around SMS over the content provider access (CPA) model has in Norway been built around similar cooperative initiatives between the largest operators and content providers (Nielsen & Hanseth, 2003). This efort resembles somewhat the organizing logic of Japanese networks and has enabled bridging of novelty to the existing institutions of SMS.<sup>32</sup> However, the dual interplay between feed-forward and feed-back loops between specifications, properties and gratifications constrain advances to other capabilities, e.g. as MMS and mobile services via WAP, because messaging specifications that were initially institutionalized were much simpler when compared to those of mobile e-mail. Rather than bridging, a rift has been erected between SMS, MMS and portal services that bars the path to easily extend mobile service properties and gratifications.

## 5. Conclusion

This paper reveals how institutional analysis can ofer an alternative lens to understand diferences in ICT innovation and use in complex settings. The institutional shaping view sets focus on specifications, properties and gratifications that become sedimented in the ‘‘deeply” structured practices of social life – and determine paths to how technology ecologies can evolve. The institutional lens unveils difusion processes in ways which cross-sec tional research designs miss: how innovation becomes institutionally entrenched and path dependent both at the supply and demand sides. It allows us to see how mundane institutions interact with novelty to shape mobile service innovation and use paths. Our analysis shows how SMS and mobile e-mail each played a crucial role – i.e. how they became ‘‘reverse salients” (Hughes, 1987) that would enable or block extended mobile data service use and thus constituted diferentiating components in the service ecologies. We show how difusion outcomes depended heavily upon the extent to which specifications were institu tionalized during the use, which would allow for a continued expansion of legitimate properties and socially valued gratifications. In Japan, mobile e-mail became the ‘‘killer application” that enabled this and was thus centrally placed in networks between users and providers. Elsewhere, innovation around mobile Internet failed because specifications were separated from the use logics, which dominated institutional arrangements around messaging innovation and use. After Hargadon and Douglas (2001), the Japanese design was both flexible and rigid as well as extensible and simple.

Our contribution to the understanding of mobile service adoption and use and its impli cations for provisioning organizations is threefold. First, by developing an analysis focused on institutions we show how mobile service characteristics and their relations emerge both as enabling and constraining institutions. The framework overcomes weaknesses in individual focused or culture based explanations in the adoption of mobile services across countries. It accounts for dynamic interdependencies between institutions which develop around provisioning and use and ofers explanations as to why even small idiosyncrasies in specifications and their integration will forge alternative innovation paths. After Markus (2005), we can point out how small diferences in technologies with similar functions can gestate over time large scale institutional diferences decisive for difusion.

Second, the framework is sensitive to specific and novel properties and gratifications of wireless services and goes beyond static analysis of service categories. The framework allows mobile service analysis from three interrelated angles: (1) technical specifications, (2) service properties, and (3) gratifications. Compared to current analyses we not only account what services are used, but how their institutionalized specifications, properties and gratifications determine jointly why they are used, how they contribute to user experience, and how their interactions and interdependencies afect each other. These three angles are important in carving out institutional forces which shape service innovation. Together with specifications of properties (i.e. instrumental, aesthetic and expressive) and gratifications (i.e. content, process and social) we augment analyses based on technology acceptance, difusion models, and models of the socio-economic environment. This is enabled by uniting aspects of the technology shaping (Markus, 2005) and ensemble view (Orlikowski, 2000; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) into and institutional-shaping view which accounts for the role of technology characteristics in shaping institutions, and vice versa.

Third, our analysis has direct implications for the future provisioning of wireless services. We unveiled how pivotal it is to integrate and innovate around ‘‘killer applications” – ‘‘reverse salients” (Hughes, 1987) – to build and advance service ecologies. At the same time we have revealed how technical diferences, when institutionalized, can radically shape innovation trajectories. We contend that the institutionalization of a divide between SMS, MMS and WAP services has led service provisioning in Norway to a crossroads. Institutional change at both the supply and demand side may be required to advance beyond SMS and premium SMS. It is most plausible that this is the case for most countries where SMS gained killer application status.

Bridging between the institutions of internet and the world of mobile phones constitutes the future holy grail which is expected to foster innovation around content, process and social gratifications in mobile services. This can only be made possible by migrating users towards specifications with richer messaging such as mobile e-mail, instant messaging and integrated messaging technologies. Smoreda & Thomas (2001) suggest that due to ‘‘certain proximity between written modes of communication in the worlds of the Internet (e-mail, chats, etc.) and of the mobile phone” the Internet and SMS should be brought together to ‘‘further stimulate the use of SMS”. Our analysis points out that e-mail, due to its horizontal and vertical bridging capabilities forms the most promising technology to facilitate this. But weariness among mobile operators to cannibalize their SMS profits and embrace e-mail easily blocks this. This prevails despite recent findings that e-mail is highly desirable across all age groups (Anckar & D’Incau, 2002; Constantiou, Damsgaard, & Knutsen, 2004). Mante-Meijer and Ling (2003, p. 9) for example conclude that ‘‘apart from email, there is no killer service for the internet”. It is likely that SMS will never ofer a basis for expanded innovation in mobile services. No wonder the GSM Association initiated the ‘‘Integrated Messaging Initiative”<sup>33</sup> where the aim is to create an integrated user experience for SMS, MMS, instant messaging and mobile e-mail. This would suggest a transitioning towards richer specifications. However, overcoming the divide between SMS, MMS and mobile Internet services requires a dismantling of prevailing institutional arrangements both at the supply and demand side. Inevitably, institutional forces preferring the preservation of the existing order and forces advocating change will collide and in future the mobile service industry can prepare for significant shake-ups (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001). Given the current divide, either the integrated messaging initiative or building on – and bridging to – the strong institutions erected around instant internet messaging will be necessary.

This study opens several avenues for future research. We need additional studies of user behaviors as to understand how service properties and gratifications connect or disconnect with specifications. Studies in gratification (Dimmick, Sikand, & Patterson, 1994; Katz, Blumler, & Gurevitch, 1974; Leung & Wei, 2000; O’Keefe & Sulanowski, 1995) and domestication research (Haddon, 2001; Ling, 2004; Silverstone & Haddon, 1996) are here critical. We need to also construct typologies which link service specifications, properties and gratifications. By integrating empirical insights with typologies we will be better equipped to connect institutions at the provisioning and user sides-their synchronic efects and diachronic shaping.
