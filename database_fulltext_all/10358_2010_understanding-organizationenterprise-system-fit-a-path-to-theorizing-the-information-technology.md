## Abstract

Packaged software applications such as enterprise systems are designed to support generic rather than specific requirements, and hence are likely to be an imperfect fit in any particular instance. Using critical realism as our philosophical perspective, we conducted a three-year qualitative study of misfits that arose from an enterprise system (ES) implementation. A detailed analysis of the observed misfits resulted in a richer understanding of the concept of fit and of the ES artifact itself. Specifically, we found six misfit domains (functionality, data, usability, role, control and organizational culture) and within each, two types of misfit (deficiencies and impositions). These misfit types correspond to two newly defined types of fit: fit as coverage and fit as enablement. Our analysis of fit also revealed a new conceptualization of the ES artifact, with implications for IT artifacts in general.

Keywords: Fit, misfits, enterprise systems, IT artifact, critical realism

## Introduction

Over the last few decades, the use of packaged applications software, rather than internally developed applications software, has dramatically increased in organizations (Sawyer 2000, 2001; Swanson 2003). Enterprise systems (ESs), one class of packaged software applications, are large integrated, process-oriented packages designed to meet most needs of organizations including accounting and control, manufacturing and distribution, sales and order entry, human resources, and management reporting. These software packages are of particular interest to MIS researchers because they can have broad organizational effects rather than the localized individual and group task-level effects of many smaller packages. Furthermore, ESs have become ubiquitous, with growth in ES software license revenue of 19 percent in 2007 (Jacobson et al. 2008).

Packaged software raises important theoretical issues associated with its definitional characteristic, namely that it is designed to fit generic rather than specific requirements, and hence is likely to be an imperfect fit in any particular instance (Lucas et al. 1988; Seddon et al. 2003). ES literature explicity acknowledges that an ES package is unlikely to include all the functionality an organization needs. According to one source, “The standard software customization rule of thumb states that 80 percent of the software application package should fit the organization as is, and only 20 percent of the software application should be customized” (Foster 2001, p. 4), and according to another, “It has been estimated that in the best case, integrated enterprise systems only address about 70% of the needs of the average organization” (Markus 2000, p. 20). Despite the theoretical and practical need to understand the phenomenon of fit between an organization and its ES (Org–ES fit), it is infrequently studied.

Our study addresses the research question: What constitutes organization–ES fit? Our answer introduces two new fit constructs. Furthermore, as a consequence of our analysis we also develop and present a new perspective on the information technology artifact.

Understanding Org–ES fit poses several theoretical challenges. First, Org–ES fit is a collective construct, that is, a construct used to discuss concepts applicable to aggregations of interdependent entities, which enables a discussion of phenomena operating simultaneously at multiple levels of analysis (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007; Morgeson and Hofmann 1999). Unlike existing task–technology fit (TTF) studies (e.g., Dishaw and Strong 1998; Goodhue 1995; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Zigurs and Buckland 1998), which focus on individuals and/or specific task categories or domains, Org–ES fit is concerned with the fit between software and multiple elements of an organization's operations. As with any collective construct, understanding its nature involves understanding not only the parts (i.e., the fit between the ES and various individual tasks), but also the sum of the parts, and the interactions between the parts (Hofmann 2002; Kozlowski and Klein 2000; Morgeson and Hofmann 1999).

A second challenge is the complexity of the IT to which the organization is being fit. To properly explore fit from a theoretical perspective, one must be specific about the technology (Monteiro and Hanseth 1996) and examine its material properties (Orlikowski and Barley 2001). Fit studies in the MIS literature rarely include a clear theoretical characterization of the IT artifact. In the MIS literature overall, the IT artifact is underspecified, and often treated as a black box (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). Thus understanding Org–ES fit involves two simultaneous complexities: multiple organizational elements, not single task types or domains, and a complex, multifaceted IT artifact, an ES, not the simpler systems in prior TTF research.

The widespread use of packaged software applications such as ESs increases the urgency for IT researchers to develop a deeper understanding of Org–ES fit. From a practice perspective, until we understand better the phenomenon of fit, achieving organizational benefits from an ES is likely to continue to be difficult and unpredictable. Building on prior ES research and practice, organizations have improved their ability to configure and implement ES packages, but the continued poor fit means that using ESs daily in organizations and generating planned benefits from them remain problematic. From a research perspective, our theoretical argument is that the concept of fit in the MIS literature is underspecified primarily because the IT artifact itself has been underspecified. Our research develops both a theoretical understanding of Org–ES fit as a collective construct and a new understanding of the IT artifact, specifically the ES artifact.

The seminal work of Soh and her colleagues, in studying ES implementations in Singapore, has tackled some of the challenges of understanding Org–ES fit. They started by classifying misfits as data, functional, and output misfits (Soh et al. 2000). By comparing structures embedded in an ES and those inherent in the organization, they subsequently expanded this list to include data ownership, data entry, workflow, job scope, reports, and revenue processing (Soh et al. 2003). They further studied misfit resolution, looking first at whether misfits arise from deep or surface structures in the ES (Sia and Soh 2002) using Wand and Weber's deep and surface concepts (Wand and Weber 1990, Weber 1997), and later at whether misfits arise from voluntarily assumed or externally imposed organizational structures (Soh and Sia 2004, 2005). These dimensions were combined to examine the relative severity of and the associated mechanisms for addressing each misfit type (Sia and Soh 2007). In other words, they looked at where and why misfits arise, and then at choices (namely changing the ES or the organization) for resolving misfits early during ES implementation. Researchers adopting the Soh et al. (2000) misfit definition examined the relationship between fit and project success (Hong and Kim 2002), identified strategies for overcoming misfits (Wei et al. 2005), and recorded difficulties experienced by Asian firms when implementing ESs designed for Western business practices (Davison 2002; Martinsons 2004).

To develop a deeper understanding of Org–ES fit, we conducted a longitudinal case study using grounded theory procedures. Like Soh and her colleagues, we first examined misfits because they were salient in our interview data. Because our field site is a North American manufacturing organization, a context for which the software was originally designed, the misfits we observed during ES use reflected the endemic issues associated with introducing organization-wide integrated packages, rather than being overwhelmed by issues of institutional and cultural differences as in, for example, the Asian hospital studies. As a result, we uncovered a more complete set of misfit domains which contributed to our theoretical understanding of misfit types. Specifically, we identified six domains of misfit from our data within each of which we recognize two theoretically different types of misfit, namely deficiencies and impositions. From this we propose two new types of fit: fit as coverage and fit as enablement.

Our data not only revealed a deeper, more theoretical understanding of Org–ES fit, but also revealed a need to extend the Wand and Weber model of the IT artifact. Specifically, by using grounded theory analysis techniques, we developed an understanding of the ES artifact as composed of four types of structures, the three identified by Wand and Weber (1990)—namely, physical, deep, and surface—plus a fourth identified from our data—namely, latent structures. Whereas prior ES fit research focused on understanding configuration and implementation choices, our study contributes to understanding fit during ES use, which in turn contributes to a better understanding of the ES artifact.

As our study leveraged grounded theory procedures, it does not start from the literature, but rather uses it later to compare the results revealed from data with existing theory (Urquhart and Fernandez 2006). Thus, we do not provide an extensive survey of literature on fit or on enterprise systems, but rather weave in the literature related to our findings and theoretical results later. In particular, after presenting our conceptualization of the Org–ES fit construct, we discuss how it differs from the classic work on fit by Venkatraman (1989) and existing work on fit in the IS and ES literature. The method section below presents our philosophical lens, information about our research site, and the methods we used to reveal misfits and to theorize about Org–ES fit and the ES artifact.

## Method

We approached this research from the philosophical perspective of critical realism (Archer 1995; Bhaskar 1978, 1979, 1998; Dobson 2001; Mingers 2004a, 2004b; Smith 2006). Critical realism bridges positivism and interpretivism by carefully distinguishing between its ontology and its epistemology. While its ontology, like that of positivism, assumes objects, entities and structures have an objective, independent existence, its epistemology, like that of interpretivism, asserts our knowledge of these items is socially and historically conditioned (Mingers 2004a). Objects, entities and structures are “real” (which Bhaskar (1998) refers to as the “domain of the real”), but this reality is not directly accessible to us. Real entities have the potential to generate “events” (referred to as the “domain of the actual”), some portion of which is observed by us (the “domain of the empirical”). Our observations of these events are necessarily subjective (Bhaskar 1998).

Under critical realism, causality is not deterministic, but real entities are assumed to have causal effects. Specifically, entities, by themselves or in combination with other entities, cause events to occur through generative mechanisms, and some of these events are empirically observed. The entities are assumed to exist prior to the events they generate and, in turn, the events may lead to subsequent changes in the entities (Archer 1995).

These two defining characteristics of critical realism—the nested stratification of the empirical within the actual within the real domain, and the horizontal stratification of entities existing prior to the events they generate which in turn precede the changes to the entities—fit well with the methodological techniques recommended for the study of collective constructs. Specifically, a collective construct such as Org–ES fit can be viewed either by looking at the structural properties of the construct, or at its functional outcomes (with the recommendation made to start with the latter) (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007; Morgeson and Hofmann 1999). These functional outcomes are the “observed events” in the empirical domain as defined by critical realism. The events we observe are the misfits that arise as various organizational and ES elements interact when individuals attempt to perform their jobs while using the technology. Furthermore, misfits can be experienced differently by different people, but in all cases will be observed as incidents of individuals feeling that the ES is impeding the proper execution of organizational operations. The task of the researcher, then, is to understand the generative mechanisms that lead to such feelings and so uncover the structures of the construct. Thus we did not start by looking directly at the structural aspects, such as the distance or gap between the technology and the organization, but at the observable events, the misfits, that arise because of such structures. This approach allowed us to recognize that while such gaps do exist, the structure of the fit construct includes more than simply gaps, and enabled us to articulate an abstract conceptualization of organizational level fit. We also note that because the experience of a misfit will lead to subsequent actions, a misfit is not a stable object. It changes over time, whether through changes to the ES or some aspect of the organization, or a reframing of the issues. Regardless of the eventual outcome, the initial experience of a misfit reflects a real problem, and will need to be addressed, whether through concrete changes or education.

A longitudinal case study employing grounded theory procedures is appropriate for studying Org–ES fit because it facilitates the emergence of theoretical concepts directly related to the phenomena being observed (i.e., misfits), while simultaneously considering the context in which those phenomena are embedded (i.e., the organization and its new ES) (Orlikowski 1993). We observed three years of a five-year phased SAP implementation at a global corporation. By following the project over several years, we observed both those misfits that were apparent immediately, and addressed in the early days of use, and those that only emerged over time.

## Field Site

ACRO, our research site, is a multinational producer of high-precision industrial equipment. The company's headquarters and 20 operating units are located in the United States, with additional plants and after-market service facilities in 11 other countries. In 1998, ACRO embarked on a plan to implement ES software from SAP, configured as a single instance, throughout the company. Because of its magnitude, the project involved a number of separate implementations. We observed phase three covering product manufacturing and assembly operations, phase four covering service and repair of customer-owned products, and phase five covering product design and development. When we started our observations in 2000, the ES team consisted of 300 people about equally divided among IT people, people from the business units, and consultants. As the company gained experience, the number of consultants fell. The largest of these implementations affected about 10,000 employees. Overall, hundreds of legacy systems were replaced by the new ES.

## Data Collection

Over a three year period we made 150 trips to four different plants plus the separate ES team location. Traveling singly or in pairs, we collected data primarily through observations, interviews, and informal conversations. Observations were collected in field notes taken as we attended planning and trouble-shooting meetings, sat in on testing and training sessions, and shadowed users and implementation team members as they solved problems. At three points in the study (before, shortly after, and approximately a year after each go-live) we conducted a total of 72 formal interviews, mostly with power users, but also with managers and general users (see Table 1). The timing of the interviews reflected the need, first, to establish a baseline, and then to observe the misfits that evolved over time. Stage models of ES implementation (e.g., Markus and Tanis 2000; Ross and Vitale 2000) and studies of benefit realization (Shang and Seddon 2002) indicate that the year after go-live is a time of uncovering and addressing misfits.

A collective construct and the data source associated with it often reside at different levels (Hofmann 2002; Kozlowski and Klein 2000; Rousseau 1985). This is particularly true of collective constructs such as Org–ES fit, which is composed of an aggregation of individual task–technology fit experiences together with their interactions. Thus we needed to interview and observe individuals in a variety of jobs using different parts of the ES to capture the variety of misfits that arose, and the way the experiences of misfits related to each other.

A number of power users, employees seconded from operations to the ES team to help with testing, training, and subsequent support, but ultimately expected to return to their jobs, were chosen to be our first key informants (for more complete description of the power user role, see Volkoff et al. 2004). Not only were they involved in the project throughout the research period (unlike most users), but their position meant that they were very familiar with the ES, so problems they identified were unlikely to arise from not knowing how to use it. With their help, we identified additional interview subjects, which included customer service representatives, material buyer planners, inspectors, engineers, expeditors, shipping clerks, inventory clerks, plant managers, accounts payable managers, and plant controllers.

Interviews generally lasted from 30 to 60 minutes, and followed an informal protocol focused on eliciting information about the interviewee's job, work processes, and changes to those processes, and what they particularly liked and disliked about the ES. Almost all of the interviews were taped and transcribed. In addition to these formal interviews, we engaged in casual conversation before and after meetings, during testing and training sessions, and in the implementation war rooms. Furthermore, we often had the opportunity to listen in on group conversations, both casual and formal. While documents did not constitute a major data source, we did collect meeting minutes, training material, and some design documents.

Our coding process generally followed the approach recommended by Strauss and Corbin (1998), but took heed of Glaser's concerns about the Straussian approach (Urquhart 1997). While Glaser and Strauss (1967) developed grounded theory together, they parted company over some aspects of implementation of the methodology. Specifically, Glaser objects to what he views as Strauss's overly restrictive guidelines for axial coding, with questions emanating from the researcher rather than the data (Locke 2001). We followed Strauss's recommendation of doing axial coding after open coding, but treated this technique as a method through which to discover relationships in the data, and not as an overly restrictive set of rules (Urquhart 2007).

The first few interviews were coded by three researchers. We then met to discuss the codes, to ensure we all used the initial labels to mean the same thing. Later interviews were coded by the researcher who conducted it, on the assumption that being present at the interview provided information about the speakers' intentions that was not available from simply reading a transcription. As we each added to the list of codes, we discussed the labels and their intended meanings with each other to ensure consistent coding. After familiarity with the research site and the emerging list of codes was well established, one researcher took over responsibility for performing the remaining coding. Emerging insights regarding potential relationships were recorded in memos, and codes were organized into trees. The NVIVO software package was used to support coding and analysis and to manage the large volume of data (Richards 2005).

The first step, open coding, attached descriptive labels to every interviewee statement to capture the core issues, as identified by the speakers, in conceptual language. Where possible these labels were taken from words used in the interviews and included concepts such as misfits, responsibilities, and impacts of SAP. Our final list included 53 open codes covering the broad set of concepts in our interviews.

As the core category misfits emerged, we initiated axial coding to explore the various subcategories and relationships between them, and to develop more precise explanations of what misfits are, what causes them, and the outcomes that arise because of them. Axial coding involves coding for conditions (causal conditions, intervening conditions, and contextual conditions), actions/interactions, and consequences (Strauss and Corbin 1998). Glaser objects to imposing such a precise framework on the data and instead presents 18 different coding families, which he cautions should be treated merely as samples of what might be found in the data (Glaser 1978). That said, the Strauss and Corbin approach resembles Glaser's "six C's" coding family (causes, contexts, contingencies, consequences, covariances, and conditions). We coded following Strauss and Corbin's paradigm, but also included codes based on Glaser's six C's coding family and his dimension family as needed, always taking care to ensure the codes arose from the data, not from an external framework. As a result of axial coding, six key categories of the core phenomenon of misfits emerged (Table 3). Next, in a process Strauss and Corbin call selective coding, we examined all data coded under each main category in turn to further compare incidents and to integrate and refine categories. This permitted us to move beyond description to a more abstract level of conceptualization (Urquhart et al. 2010). It was through this selective coding process that the two types of misfit emerged. In comparing these two conceptualizations of misfit to concepts in the fit literature, we developed two corresponding fit conceptualizations, as well as a new conceptualization of the ES artifact.

## Findings

In this section we present the six domains where users experience misfits (see Table 3), and classify those misfits as either deficiencies or impositions. From a critical realist perspective, classifying misfits as deficiencies or impositions connects problems observed in the empirical domain to underlying generative mechanisms in the real domain, specifically to characteristics of the ES and the organization. Deficiencies are problems arising from ES features that are missing but needed. Empirically, these problems take the form of actions users cannot take because the ES is missing functionality, data fields, controls, etc., necessary for those actions. Impositions are problems arising from the inherent characteristics of an ES such as integration and standardization. While these characteristics offer multiple benefits and are some of the reasons organizations acquire an ES, they also generate misfits. Empirically, impositions take the form of the ES requiring ways of working that are contrary to organizational norms and practices or that negatively affect organizational performance. Imposition misfits are generally more serious because these misfits arise from characteristics that are necessarily present in the ES and cannot be eliminated, whereas deficiencies can be resolved through the vendor adding the missing features in subsequent releases or an organization acquiring bolt-on packages to support the needed actions. Extra features that are present but not problematic are not misfits.

While minor functionality misfits often had easy work-arounds, other deficiencies were more serious. For example there was no functionality to support swaps and loans of parts between products or products between customers. A production manager in the repair plant described the problem as follows:

If there's a part that we need at test...then we would loan swap a part into that [order] in order for the [product] to perform at test. Now if this part has no impact on the specific performance of the test operation, we can do that and we can take it from whatever [order] we need, whatever customer we need, in order to make that work and when the [product] comes back from test, we simply remove that part, put it back on the other customer's [product] and order a new part for [the first order]....It's very difficult to do that now with ERP because the system won't allow you to loan swap from one customer to another, so we have to bypass the ERP system and do it on paper, so that we have traceability and record of it, so that we don't get caught with our pants down, how did this part get on this [product], well here's how, because we have to do a manual swap, because...the system wouldn't allow us to swap or interchange material from one customer to another. We need to have that flexibility and it's not there.

While the many functionality deficiencies lowered task performance, they were the easiest misfits to understand, and future ES releases often addressed them. Because of these misfits, some employees viewed the new ES as a step backward: it did not provide the legacy systems' functionality and was not tailored to ACRO's business processes.

By contrast, functionality impositions arise from salient ES characteristics, such as an ES's ability to integrate and standardize business processes (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005;

Gattiker and Goodhue 2005; Volkoff et al. 2005). ES-enabled process integration entails linking tasks together in a predefined order. Such process integration produced misfits by increasing the interdependence among tasks that, in the legacy systems, had been loosely coupled and so relatively independent. Not only were all steps of such a process mandatory (whether or not a specific circumstance actually required them), but users were also required to perform some tasks in sequence, rather than in parallel, lengthening the time required for process completion.

An example of a functionality imposition caused by integration was the new process for making an engineering change (EC). All ECs required design engineering's approval (e.g., assemblers and process engineers could not substitute one part for another if engineering had not formally listed that part as a valid substitute). Enabling continuous improvement, ECs were frequent and usually scheduled. They served to fix design or assembly problems, reduce product production and operating costs, and improve product performance. With the legacy systems, design engineering could call an EC into process engineering (especially useful for simple part substitutions for a product being assembled), so they could start working it before design engineering's formal paperwork arrived. With the new ES and the automated interface between development and production, design engineering had to enter the EC directly into the ES. Unfortunately, this meant that process engineering could not start working the EC until EC entry was complete. Moreover, the information required of design engineering was more detailed than before, so the time until a correctly entered EC was transferred to production could be several days. This example illustrates the stricter process sequencing in the ES, which caused problems and slowed operations whenever complete EC data were not available or not correctly entered.

Other functionality impositions were due to process standardization (i.e., task sequences that were appropriate for some organizational units, but which created problems for others). For example, in the repair facility, although there are standard processes in the ES based on the production processes, the actual work is not that standard. As one person in repair noted,

It's very difficult to get standard work in this facility. Yeah, there is for some things....It's rare that we work to the product manual today, it's always a customer-driven work scope whether it's a work scope derived from our work scopes or a totally unique customer-supplied work scope we're expected to utilize.

Finally, our data included many examples of how the system worked well only under “sunny day” conditions. While the process integration and strict task sequences characteristic of an ES are the embodiment of various policies, procedures and best practices, which encourage process discipline, these strict process rules reduce the flexibility needed for handling process exceptions.

## Data Misfit

Data misfits occur when data or data characteristics stored in or needed by the ES lead to data quality issues such as inaccuracy, inconsistent representations, inaccessibility, lack of timeliness, or inappropriateness for users' contexts (Wang and Strong 1996) (see Table 5). With its common, shared database and focus on global master data (e.g., the parts master and customer master), the ES provides a very different data environment than in a disparate legacy systems environment. Previous fit literature has considered data misfits in various ways, focusing variously on data quality, locatability, compatibility, and authorization (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), data format and relationships (Soh et al. 2000), or data entry and ownership (Soh et al. 2003). One attraction of an ES is that data only need to be entered once, generally at the point of collection, which should increase both accuracy and availability. On the other hand, since data, once entered, are available for use by many, errors can rapidly have a broad impact and be difficult to undo.

Data deficiencies. One data deficiency was in the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) in the ES, which did not have enough levels of details for ACRO's complex products, affecting, for example, the ability to compare budgets to actuals. Another problem arose because ACRO's repair customers sometimes provided their own spare parts, but there was no way to track and account for these parts separately, and only use them for that customer's orders.

Data impositions. As with functionality, integration and standardization created data misfits. For example, the need for common data definitions meant that Finance and Production had to agree on how to define part location. The decision was made to accept Finance's preference for defining location based on who had responsibility for the part at that moment, rather than its physical location. As a result, parts were difficult to find on the production floor, and employees started to keep spreadsheets of data in a form most useful to them.

Similarly, all parts of the organization had to define serial numbers the same way if their work was to be integrated, but while production needed serial numbers for detail parts, repair used serial numbers for assemblies they were replacing, as explained by a process engineer:

## Usability Misfit

Usability misfits occur when the interactions with the ES required for task execution are cumbersome or confusing, that is, requiring extra steps that add no value, or introduce difficulty in entering or extracting information (see Table 6).

Usability deficiencies. Usability deficiencies include screens that lack default values and inappropriate report designs. ACRO invested considerable resources creating many new screens and reports to overcome what they considered usability deficiencies in the ES as delivered by the vendor. For example, they created a complex shortage report to support decision making at the daily production meeting. Because of ACRO's attention to such issues, we did not observe significant examples of usability deficiencies after system go-live.

Usability impositions. Because the ES served many organizational units simultaneously, screens often contained more than any one unit required. As a result, data that had been available on a single screen were now spread across several screens. Doing any task necessitated more screens and more clicking in the ES than in legacy systems tailored to the task, and thus more time to complete any task. While the ES had more data than had been stored in any legacy system, this larger volume meant that it was harder and more time consuming to extract what was needed. According to one interviewee, “There is data in there, we just don’t always know how to get it out.” In the words of another interviewee, “Now you have to go hunt for it. It [used to be] exposed more. You [could] see all this information on what I call a sheet. Where in today’s environment you need to screen hop.”

Data use was also more difficult because data in the ES are represented more abstractly. For example, instead of using part numbers, which were familiar to users and the way they wanted to search for data, data were often stored using an arbitrary transaction number, such as a PO number or a batch number. As a result, people would phone a repair facility to check whether it had a particular part because it was too difficult to find its status in the ES.

A challenging usability problem arising from integration was that reversing an error often required multiple people to be involved since the effects had propagated to others who would have to help reverse it. For a similar reason, changing data values was much more difficult than in the legacy system environment. To avoid this extra work, users delayed entering data likely to change, which reduced the data's timeliness for others.

## Role Misfit

Roles define who is responsible for performing each task. Role misfits occur when the roles in the ES are inconsistent with the skills available, create imbalances in the workload leading to bottlenecks and idle time, or generate mismatches between responsibility and authority (see Table 7). The task–technology fit literature has generally not addressed role misfits because it primarily focuses on individual level tasks or a group of individuals performing similar tasks. One exception is Soh et al. (2003), who discuss job scope misfits. As is evident in our field data, role misfits include, but also go beyond, job scope.

Role deficiencies. Roles are constructed in the ES as authority to execute a fixed set of transactions. Because roles can be set up however desired, there are few deficiencies. That said, at ACRO the ES did not easily accommodate a traditional shipping role; the associated tasks were diffused across many roles. While those tasks could be efficiently executed in those other places, the lack of a locus of accountability for some shipping functions created problems whenever exceptions arose, e.g., changes in planned shipping dates.

Role impositions. Assumptions built into the software required some role changes that created problems for ACRO. A dramatic example, reported by all sites and by many interviewees, was the increased workload and expertise required for the new material buyer planner role. Before the ES, material planners were responsible for ensuring that the appropriate parts were available for assembly as needed, which generally involved working with the Materials Requirements Planning (MRP) software, the warehouses, and the shop floor. Through its embedded processes, the ES defined a broader role that also included acquiring the material, leading to the role name of material buyer planner. One material buyer planner in the repair organization reported that “in the material buyer planner role, we are responsible for too much. The material buyer planner, they ended up doing everything.” Another person reported that “the ES has elevated the talent; it has created a professional position. Before someone in the material buyer planner role was just a tactical person, now they are a strategic person. Right now the fallout is that some people can evolve into it, others cannot." A buyer planner in the product assembly organization reported "our responsibility is ten times as much as it used to be." He reported several new responsibilities and said, "We do that type of stuff. I never had to do that before." Later in the same interview, he said, "This [ES] has made things different, the whole relationship is different. As a result, this small group now had to cover weekends. The increased workload and lack of expertise made this new role a serious bottleneck in production.

A widespread issue was that roles were simultaneously more interconnected yet more distinct. Typically, because of increased integration, people in each role needed more understanding and knowledge of the network of tasks to be performed and needed to spend more time performing coordination activities. A customer accounts manager noted, “It has increased the work load dramatically, in the interaction necessary with storerooms and with shipping personnel.” Paradoxically this effect was made more severe due to the restrictions on the authority of each role, which limited the scope of what people were authorized to do and increased the number of people involved in any action. Because of these authority restrictions, people were no longer allowed to help out with other jobs where there was a temporary need: “We used to help a lot of people get a lot of stuff done. We cannot do that anymore. We basically just do our jobs and, hey, it is somebody else’s responsibility. You have to contact X in inspection.” In those circumstances, some individuals were overworked while others sat idle. Because ES-specified authorizations were fixed, the ES limited managerial flexibility to dynamically reassign their workforce as needed.

## Control Misfit

Control misfits occur when the controls embedded in the ES provide too much control, inhibiting productivity, or too little control, leading to the inability to assess or monitor performance appropriately (see Table 8). ESs generally enhance managerial control as compared to legacy systems (Elmes et al. 2005), which is one reason for their popularity with managers, but there are often unintended consequences that indicate an underlying misfit. The fit literature has not discussed control misfits, other than Soh et al.'s (2000) inclusion of control as a subtype of functional misfit.

Control deficiencies. As with any software, an ES blindly applies all rules built into it, without regard for specific circumstances. To accommodate a variety of situations requires building complexity into the rule set, which generally makes the system harder to use.

For example, the ES required all parts to be checked into the warehouse when they were newly arrived, returned from repair, or not needed after being issued, rather than allowing direct part transfers. Theoretically this improved inventory control, ensuring that all items were visible. Because returning unused items that could be immediately redirected to another order is extra work, parts were often left on the shop floor, but then were not listed as available in the ES. If they were subsequently used in another order without being returned to the warehouse, they could not be charged to the customer. Those describing these situations all felt that the degree of control was excessive, as it created much unproductive work for little actual control benefit. In other situations they described examples of insufficient control.

Similarly, any parts being moved from one version of a product under development to the next version had to be checked back into the warehouse first rather than being moved directly. Eventually ACRO created a special transaction for development to support direct version to version part transfers to address this control misfit. This new transaction provided a better control balance by maintaining control over inventory movement while minimizing the extra work required to perform a frequent task in development.

Control impositions. Control impositions occurred because there was little flexibility about when, whether, and how much control was exercised. In particular, the ES was built so that rules always had to be followed, whether they made sense in a particular situation or not. As one development engineer noted, “[It] was always a rule, but it was a rule that we were able to work around. So now we’re held to the rules.” The functionality, data, and role specifications in the ES (e.g., task sequences forming integrated processes, the data needed to place an order or to receive a part, and role authorizations) all implicitly became inflexible rules that the ES strictly enforced. Even in routine production processes, this strict enforcement of rules caused problems because special cases routinely arose. For example, material buyer planners could not add parts to a production order, even when something was desperately needed on the shop floor. While this provided a measure of inventory control, it also caused production delays, as described by a material buyer planner:

If we need to issue a part...we have to have somebody...add that part to the production order as a shortage...Sometimes it necessitates us to...go out on the floor, find somebody, say, "Hey, I need you to add this part to the production order for me."... Before we could do that right from our chair. We had no problem, we accessed whatever program we needed, we did the changes, which we knew were correct, and life was good. Now if they're on lunch or if they're on break, that's all the time you lose. They go home at 3:00, we're here 'till 5:00, 6:00, 7:00 at night. Now if we know something scraps out at 4:00, we can't buy it, it's not on the production report, we can't get that 'till morning. Whereas if we had the access we could do it instantaneously.

## Organizational Culture Misfit

Organizational culture misfits occur when the ES requires ways of operating that contravene organizational norms (see Table 9). In the literature, Krumbholz et al. (2000) conducted a thorough study of cultural misfits, considering both corporate and national culture misfits. Other authors have also observed that common ES software such as SAP often conflicts with Asian ways of operating (Davison 2002; Martinsons 2004; Soh et al. 2000) or with organizational culture (Markus 2004). National culture did not surface in our data, probably because we only visited ACRO's U.S. sites, but we did observe corporate culture misfits.

Organizational culture deficiencies. Organizational culture differs from the other domains in that it is holistic, emerging from the totality of the technology and its context. To that extent, we cannot speak of deficiencies, because there is nothing specific that could be introduced to fill a void. Instead, all organizational culture misfits are impositions.

Organizational culture impositions. An integrated system inherently embodies a culture of discipline. This new culture imposed by the ES (doing things the way you are supposed to) differed significantly from ACRO's traditional "cowboy" or "hero" culture (doing whatever was necessary to get the product out the door). Many employees, including managers, identified this change as a misfit. With transaction sequences embedded in the ES, failure to complete every step in a process created a failure further down the line. Furthermore, with the difficulty inherent in reversing errors, there was a high premium placed on doing things right the first time. While increased discipline is one of the reasons organizations (including ACRO) buy an ES in the first place, when confronted by the reality of the need for methodical transaction execution, even in a production emergency or situations of ambiguity or uncertainty, employees complained that the ES was inappropriate for ACRO's operating culture.

Similarly, design engineers had a hard time adopting more disciplined approaches to managing budgets and ordering parts. Specifically, they lost authority for transactions that involved spending money, as the ES enabled centralizing these responsibilities. While management valued the increased and more consistent control, the engineers complained that being unable to create or change budgets and project plans, create external work requests, or approve purchase orders for parts inhibited their ability to deliver innovative new products in a timely way. In general, the ES changed what was valued in the organization. In particular, it moderated ACRO's engineering culture, where innovation and technical knowledge (and hence engineers) were highly valued, by placing much more focus on finance and financial control. Plant controllers gained considerable power and influence. The long-term impact of this cultural shift is not yet known, but a need for continuous innovation and a measure of agility are certainly hallmarks of ACRO's competitive environment.

In summary, our data-driven approach with a focus on the problems users experienced revealed six domains of Org–ES misfits, namely functionality, data, usability, roles, control, and organizational culture. These are a more comprehensive categorization of misfits than found in the literature, and thus provide a contribution to the IT and ES literature on fit and misfits. For managers, the articulation of these misfit domains, especially roles, control, and culture which are rarely mentioned in the literature, will help them better anticipate and plan for such misfits. Furthermore, the six misfit domains and the classification of misfits as either deficiencies or impositions, provide the foundation for theorizing about Org–ES fit and about the ES artifact as presented in the following sections.

## Theorizing Organization–ES Fit

Organization–ES fit is problem-free functioning of an organization as it uses its ES. Although fit is only one criterion for selecting and implementing an ES, it is important because misfits can be costly. Our path to conceptualizing Org–ES fit is via misfits. Misfits are the empirically visible events that help to reveal the underlying structure of the Org–ES fit construct. In general, misfits are more salient to users than fit (i.e., while we asked interviewees about instances in which the ES worked well and worked poorly, they tended to elaborate on problems).

Our data revealed two different types of misfits: deficiencies and impositions. While deficiencies, often called gaps, are discussed in the misfit literature, impositions have not been addressed. That said, they are certainly alluded to: “An enterprise system, by its very nature, imposes its own logic on a company’s strategy, organization, and culture” (Davenport 1998, p. 122, emphasis added). The notion of imposition is also evident in Gosain’s (2004) analogy of an ES as our new iron cage. An example from our data is the imposition of a culture of “disciplined operations.”

The rich data on dimensions of misfits helped us move to an abstract conceptualization of Org–ES fit. Specifically, the two different types of problems encountered during ES use signal two different types of Org–ES fit that ES designers and implementers should strive to attain, which we call coverage and enablement fit (see Table 10). Coverage and enablement fit are both complex constructs, addressing the fit between the many elements of a software package and multiple aspects of an organization's operations. First, they are collective constructs in that they refer to groups of individuals or units. Second, they are multidimensional constructs in that they are aggregated across the six domains of interrelated requirements.

Coverage fit means the ES meets the organization's requirements (i.e., it includes the features that the organization needs to operate and that users need to do their work). Fit as coverage is a collective construct corresponding to the absence of deficiency misfits. Coverage is achieved, that is deficiencies are eliminated, by vendors as they design and develop features of their ES and by organizations as they tailor the ES through configuration options and customization (e.g., with bolt-on packages), to meet needs not achievable by configuration alone. For example, ACRO's development of new screens and reports eliminated usability deficiencies before ES use.

Enablement fit means the ES permits and enables the organization to operate more effectively, and users to do their work more efficiently, than was the case without an ES. Fit as enablement is related to imposition misfits, but unlike coverage and deficiencies, enablement fit is not merely the absence of imposition misfits (i.e., the two are not simple complements), but often emerge together from the same source. Enablement fit means that the organization is better off as a result of implementing the ES, even after accounting for the negative effects of impositions. Vendors discuss these ways in which an ES enables organizations to be more efficient and effective as the industry “best practices” embedded in the ES.

This second type of Org–ES fit is important because it is the source of organizational benefits that cannot be achieved from legacy systems. At the same time, however, those ES characteristics, such as integrated processes and standardized data, that confer benefits (i.e., enable new capabilities) often simultaneously constrain (impose upon) organizational capabilities, even when an ES has all the required features (provides adequate coverage).

The tensions arising from this duality of enablement fit (i.e., the coexistence of desirable outcomes and undesirable side effects arising from the same ES features), comes in various forms. First, the ES may demand trade-offs between different levels of the organization (e.g., integration offers global advantages such as company-wide visibility of inventory, while creating local problems such as data definitions that are not meaningful to users.) Second, the ES may demand trade-offs between different organizational units at the same level (e.g., the ES may enable new oversight capabilities for Finance, but impose operational difficulties on Production). Finally, the software may impose on the organization as a whole (e.g., SAP imposes a culture of discipline on everyone). Our data provide examples of process and data impositions that are unintended consequences of integration and standardization, and usability impositions (too many screens for any task) that are unintended consequences of the many possible ES options. The desirable strict controls enabled by the ES become impositions whenever flexibility or dynamic resource allocation is needed to respond to the many exceptions in any business process (Gasser 1986; Strong and Miller 1995). Increased control through strict role authorizations and integrated workflow through tightly coupled processes individually contribute to improving organizational performance, but they also interact in ways that may lead to periodic production slowdowns.

While achieving coverage fit (resolving deficiencies) requires additions to the ES or changes to ways of working, achieving enablement fit may not be possible unless the ES itself is fundamentally redesigned. As a result, organizations are left with trying to manage the tensions that inevitably arise when technology integrates, standardizes, and controls different areas. Even what is defined as a misfit $^{2}$ or who experiences it most severely becomes a political negotiation among organizational units (Light 2005).

As these two new constructs are used in future studies, researchers will need to develop methods for measuring them. We recommend measuring coverage in terms of the proportion of requirements met, probably distinguishing “must have” from “nice to have” requirements. Another option is to take the next step and evaluate unmet needs in terms of their organizational impact using a performance or criterion measure (e.g., cost or severity level). Because of the duality associated with enablement fit, we recommend measuring it by assessing the net cost or benefit of the specific characteristics embedded in the new system, which are the underlying cause of both the new capabilities and the new impositions.

## Comparison to Venkatraman's Fit Taxonomy

The contribution of our two Org–ES fit types can be understood further in comparison to the ES, IS, and management fit literature. We first consider Venkatraman's (1989) fit taxonomy and then Sia and Soh's (2007) misalignment assessment framework.

Coverage and enablement fit differ from Venkatraman's well-known fit taxonomy in the management literature because his purpose and approach differ from ours. His purpose is to encourage researchers studying strategic fit to match their theoretical propositions about fit with an appropriate statistical measurement. Thus, his six perspectives on fit, developed from an analysis of fit studies in the strategy literature, are based on two characteristics of statistical models for testing fit propositions, namely specificity of the form (two versus many variables) and presence or absence of a criterion (outcome or performance) variable. The contribution of coverage and enablement fit beyond Venkatraman's fit perspectives is illustrated in comparison to the three of his six perspectives that are most closely related to coverage or enablement and that have proven useful in the IS literature: fit as matching, profile deviation, and moderation.

Fit as matching (two-variable, criterion-free form) conceptualizes and measures fit as a deviation between two variables. Cognitive fit studies (Umanath and Vessey 1994; Vessey 1991; Vessey and Galletta 1991) seem to use this form. In the ES fit literature, Soh and Sia (2004) attribute misalignments to differences between package structures and organizational structures, which has some similarities to matching. Conceptually, coverage fit has some similarities to matching (i.e., the matching of ES features to organizational needs), but also some differences. First, Venkatraman defines matching for two variables only, but coverage fit is an aggregation of the coverage of many organizational requirements. Thus, coverage might be considered an extension of matching to multiple variables. Second, matching measures fit as a deviation or distance between variables, whereas as noted above, we suggest measuring coverage as the proportion of needs met or the cost of unmet needs, rather than distance. In part, this is because operationalizing the distance between an organization's many needs and its ES will be extraordinarily complex, although Rosemann et al. (2004) do propose using ontological distance to assess such misalignments. Finally, matching is symmetric, but coverage is not (i.e., unmet organizational requirements are misfits; excess ES capabilities are not). Thus, we might think of coverage fit as an extension of Venkatraman's matching fit, and its tailoring to an IS context.

In the IS context, fit as profile deviation (multiple-variable, criterion-specific) assesses whether performance is associated with how well a technology matches an ideal profile for that technology. For example, Zigurs and Buckland (1998) used profile deviation to assess the association of task outcomes with how well GDSS software matches an ideal profile of what a GDSS “should” look like. Neither coverage nor enablement requires an ideal ES profile for assessing fit; nor does such a profile exist. If future research develops an ideal profile for an ES, the concept of fit as profile deviation could then be used to test whether ESs closely matching this profile generate better organizational performance.

Fit as moderation (two-variable criterion-specific) is frequently used to analyze how much an IT improves task performance as it interacts with (moderates) that task (Dishaw and Strong 1998; Goodhue 1995; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Strong et al. 2006). Conceptually, moderation fit is appealing to IS researchers because it supports our belief that using IT will affect tasks in a way that improves performance, a belief that applies equally well to an ES. Moderation fit has some similarities to enablement fit because both focus on performance changes due to technology. Enablement fit is a richer concept than moderation because it specifically captures the duality of how an ES interacts with an organization (i.e., providing benefits while simultaneously generating negative side effects). As a statistical form, moderation is a two-variable form, although IS researchers have not restricted their moderation fit studies only to two-variable forms, but rather use multiple dimensions of task and technology and assume these dimensions to be additive. This extension to multiple variables requires many interaction effects, even for technologies not nearly as comprehensive as an ES, and thus would likely be impractical for an ES. While Venkatraman's taxonomy has achieved its purpose (e.g., most IS fit researchers mention which of his statistical forms they are using), coverage and enablement fit provide significant additional contributions.

These two types of Org–ES fit provide fit conceptualizations that are natural for the IS domain rather than borrowing fit forms from other research domains. Fit as coverage should sound similar to anyone who has listed organizational requirements or needs and has evaluated a software package to see whether it meets (covers) those needs. Yet, when IS researchers attempt to measure fit, they try to adapt one of Venkatraman's fit forms because that is what is in the literature. Fit as enablement differs from anything in the literature, but it should also resonate with IS researchers and professionals who typically argue that IT is an enabler. Furthermore, coverage and enablement are tailored to IT artifacts, that is, they capture how the IT artifact affects the conceptualization of fit, rather than being fit forms of generic variables as in Venkatraman's forms. Coverage fit recognizes that Org–ES fit is an asymmetric concept focusing on missing ES features. Enablement fit recognizes the materiality of the IT artifact, which permits it to impose ways of working on an organization—for example, through the transactions it allows or forbids employees to perform (Volkoff et al, 2007). These new ways of working may be enabling or constraining, and often both. Finally, our conceptualization recognizes that the two types of Org–ES fit are collective constructs, whereas Venkatraman's conceptualizations, while using organization-level variables, do not consider the nature or form of the variables.

## Comparison to Sia and Soh's Misalignment Assessment Framework

Like Venkatraman, Sia and Soh (2007) also developed a framework, but theirs focuses explicitly on ES misfits, and thus is the most similar study in the literature to ours. The purpose and approach of their framework differ from Venkatraman's and from ours. The purpose of their taxonomy is to explain and predict how organizations will resolve misfits during ES configuration. Their framework and the results from testing it are summarized in Table 11. As they predicted, misalignments that involve externally imposed constraints on organizational structures, such as governmental requirements, are more likely to be resolved during configuration by changing the system than are misalignments involving voluntarily acquired organizational structures.

Sia and Soh's data for testing their prediction are the submitted requests for changes to the ES. These data provide a total set of documented misfits. As they note, such data has the advantage of permitting counts and percentages for their framework, but also the disadvantage of being biased due to only including formally requested changes. In comparison, our data collection methods, based on grounded theory techniques, provide for theoretical saturation, but do not identify every instance of misfits. As a result, counts and percentages are not valid for our data. Our data, however, enables us to capture misfits, such as roles that are too large, or that imposed discipline in actions, that are unlikely to appear in submitted requests for changes.

In the ES fit literature, no studies model fit in a manner similar to our enablement fit.

An additional contribution of Sia and Soh's framework is their use of Wand and Weber's representational model of an IS in analyzing misfits. As discussed in the next section, we also employ this model, and a comparison of our use to Sia and Soh's is provided there.

These two Org–ES fit types not only extend the fit literature, but also provide goals for ES designers and implementers and the basis for developing practical measures of the quality of an ES in use.

## Theorizing the ES Artifact and its Latent Structures

As noted in the introduction, examining organization-ES fit from a theoretical perspective requires being specific and theoretical about the IT artifact, in our case the ES. Consistent with this view, our development of the two types of the Org–ES fit construct revealed a new conceptualization of the IT artifact that includes what we call latent structures.

While conceptualizations of the IT artifact in the fit literature are rarely made explicit, the IT artifact is implicitly viewed variously as a data repository (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), a bundle of functionality to support processes (Henderson and Cooprider 1990), or a means for transmitting information and supporting decision making (Zigurs and Buckland 1998). All of these exemplify what Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) refer to as a tool view of technology. Sia and Soh (2007) are notable in basing their analysis of the severity of misalignments and their resulting prediction of how an organization will respond on Wand and Weber's characterization of the IT artifact as consisting of three types of structures, namely deep, surface, and physical structures (Wand and Weber 1990; Weber 1997).

## Latent Structures: An Extension to Theories of the IT Artifact

As we compared our findings to the literature, we recognized that the deep and surface structures that underlie Sia and Soh's view of the ES artifact are not only very helpful, but since Wand and Weber share our critical realist perspective (Weber 1997, p. 175), are also a particularly appropriate foundation to build on. That said, we found the Wand and Weber framework must be supplemented to capture fully the ES artifact evident in our data. Weber (1997) describes three types of structures. Surface structure phenomena are “the facilities that are available in the information system to allow users to interact with the information system” (p. 78). As discussed by Sia and Soh (2007), surface structure misfits arise when a system’s interface does not support input of or access to information in the way desired by users; this is synonymous with our definition of usability misfits. Deep structure phenomena are described as scripts that provide a representation of real-world systems: the “things,” their “properties” and “states,” and the “transformations” that alter those states (Wand and Weber 1995; Weber 1997). Deep structure misfits arise from missing or inadequate entities and business rules (Sia and Soh 2007)—our functionality and data misfit domains. Weber also discusses a third category, physical structure phenomena, the ways in which deep and surface structures are mapped onto underlying physical technology, but he and Wand do not pursue this topic. Similarly, Sia and Soh do not include physical structures in their model. While we have not included them in our findings because we did not specifically collect data about them, misfits related to physical structures were observed and included problems associated with printers and the network.

To accommodate the remaining three misfit domains—roles, control, and organizational culture—we need to extend Wand and Weber's ontology by adding the concept of latent structures (see Figure 1). We use the term latent because while they are an integral part of the ES, the specific IT artifact of interest to us, they are not designed and scripted in the same way as are the other structures but arise from them as second order structures. Whereas, for example, functionality is given material form in the ES as coded transactions, and data are given material form as tables and relations (i.e., both are instantiated in the system as explicit scripts), roles, control structures, and organizational culture emerge from the way a set of deep, surface, and physical structures are designed. Just because they do not take material form, however, does not make latent structures less real or less a part of the ES. They are an inevitable and unavoidable outcome of the scripts for the other structures, and while not deliberately constructed representations of some real-world phenomena, they do have real-world analogues that they reflect, either well or badly. An integral element of the IT artifact, they exist as part of the delivered package separate from its use, and must be accommodated by the organization. To illustrate latent structures in an ES, we present a concrete and comprehensive example.

## Latent Structures Example

At ACRO, product details are represented in the ES by a bill of materials (BoM), a tree data structure that identifies the major product subassemblies and then explodes each subassembly into its component parts. The data in the BoM enables the ES to determine requirements for parts, to schedule purchase orders for these parts, and then to release parts to manufacturing stations when needed, connecting the BoM data structure with various ES transactions. As such, the BoM and associated functionality for scheduling and generating orders and for releasing parts to manufacturing stations are part of the deep structure of the ES.

The BoM is not, however, simply a data structure within the ES's deep structure; role, control, and cultural structures also emerge in the ES because of the BoM. Different user choices in setting up the BoM data generate controls, often unintended by those users. For example, not including substitute parts in the BoM reduces flexibility to manufacturing when a standard part is not available because parts not in the BoM cannot be ordered, released to manufacturing stations, or recorded as part of an assembled product. A more complex example is storing a BoM as one large tree or several smaller trees. As ACRO discovered, larger trees lead to more manufacturing delays, although this effect was not obvious when designing the initial BoMs. In addition to storing a portion of a product structure, the tree structure is the amount of work released to a manufacturing station at one time, but release is not allowed if any part is unavailable. ACRO discovered that making trees in terms of two hours of work rather than eight hours provided time to find parts needed later in the day, thus avoiding unnecessary manufacturing delays. Thus, a BoM is not only a data structure with useful information, but it also generates controls that define how work can be performed using the ES. The BoM controls which parts can be ordered, the timing of such orders, and when and how material is released to manufacturing. Furthermore, these controls are strictly applied without exception.

At ACRO, a centralized role emerged in the ES to maintain production BoMs and manage the associated MRP process because the BoM is master data used by many transactions. In addition, because of the latent control effects, more expertise was needed to make good design decisions about organizing the BoM data structures. In the legacy environment, many people (e.g., process engineers) could and did have access to maintain the BoM. With the ES, this became a centralized role performed by a small group, which in turn reduced flexibility when members of the BoM control group were unavailable, especially on weekends.

The latent control structures and role structures around the BoM contributed to a culture of discipline. For example, more attention was paid to ensuring correct and timely data in the BoM and the part master because part orders could not be placed if data were not available. Part shortages were carefully managed to avoid failed releases of parts to manufacturing and to avoid problems on the weekends, both of which would cause manufacturing delays.

In summary, a BoM is designed and implemented as a data structure to represent a product's structure. The associated roles, controls, and culture that emerged were not necessarily considered or even known as BoMs were designed, and may only have become apparent during ES use. These latent structures are not the outcome of specifying the BoM itself, nor are they deliberately written in the code, but are the effective outcome of the integrated totality of the software. Just as an organization is more than the sum of its various organizational elements, an ES is more than the sum of its surface, deep, and physical structures.

## Our Representation of the ES Artifact

Our representation of an ES artifact, shown in Figure 1, is an integrated whole formed from physical, deep, surface, and latent structures. The bottom of Figure 1 is Wand and Weber's (Weber 1997, p.79) view of the IT artifact. This base shows how the deep structures, where our functionality and data misfits arise, are embedded and materialized in the physical structures, and similarly how the surface structures, where our usability misfits arise, are built upon the deep structures.

Arising out of this base are our latent structures. They are pictured as separate from the other three as they are not scripted in the same way as deep structures, but emerge from the interactions of all the other scripts combined. The logical clustering of responsibilities that becomes a role, for example, emerges from the deep structure as a set of tasks that are logically connected by virtue of the way transactions are scripted and relate to each other. Because a variety of transactions now share the same BoM data, it became necessary at ACRO to create a centralized BoM maintenance role. Once the role structures are clarified, they are tied back to the deep structures in the form of authorization tables specifying which roles have access rights to each transaction. Similarly, control structures and organizational culture arise from the tight coupling of transactions and from the common database.

While each misfit in our findings was experienced as a problem within a single misfit domain, its causes may lie within several domains because the ES structures that give rise to these problems are interrelated. Thus, resolving any particular misfit, especially if it is an imposition rather than a deficiency, is likely to affect more than one of our six misfit domains and perhaps more than one of the four ES structures.

Our focus on problems experienced by users rather than on differences between the organization and the ES makes the latent structures visible. A focus on differences leads to conceptualizing misfits as deficiencies to be resolved by changing the ES or changing the organization, and a resulting view of fit as coverage. This is consistent with Wand and Weber's conceptualization of an IS as a representation of the world in terms of deep and surface structures, and their criteria for "goodness" of an IS's deep structure, focusing on accuracy and completeness of that representation, which mirrors our coverage concept. Our data on user problems not only reveals deficiencies, but also impositions. These impositions on the way work is performed lead to extending the concept of fit to include how the ES enables and constrains the organization. Thus, we must conceptualize the ES not only in terms of its deep and surface structures that are explicitly scripted and which may or may not be deficient (incomplete or inaccurate), but also in terms of latent structures that emerge from the combination of many scripts which taken together create the inherent ES characteristics that enable and constrain how roles are designed, how control can be exercised, and the overall culture of the organization.

As a result, our research presents a view of an ES artifact as a combination of the four structures (physical, deep, surface, and latent) shown in Figure 1. Our identification and explanation of latent structures as an integral part of the ES artifact is the third major contribution of this paper.

Adding the concept of latent structures to the more common view of the IT artifact as a set of scripted structures, and the concept of impositions to the usual view of misfits as deficiencies, places our view of the ES artifact somewhere between the tool and ensemble views of technology, as defined by Orlikowski and Iacono (2001). The scripted structures provide a set of intended capabilities; this instrumental approach of deliberate design delivering specific outcomes is consistent with the tool perspective. On the other hand, the characteristics of the latent structures emerge from and depend on the other three structures, so cannot be independently designed. Furthermore, unlike the tool view, our conception of the ES artifact is not deterministic. Rather, in taking a critical realist perspective, we consider the four types of structures we identified as the mechanisms that, while real and independent of the organizations that employ them, do not determine outcomes. These mechanisms have the potential to generate various outcomes (but not others) as they interact with other entities (e.g., users or organizations). This puts us closer to the ensemble view. That said, most variants of the ensemble view focus on what the technology is connected to, while black-boxing the structures that constitute the technology itself. Our perspective opens up that black box, and identifies six specific domains that simultaneously have independent form and are directly connected to the context of use. The ES structures both enable and constrain organizational activity. Our view is most like the ensemble view's “technology as structure” variant, grounded in structuration theory. That said, under structuration theory, technology is defined at the moment it is enacted by a user, while under our view the technology has embedded structures, both planned and latent, that exist separately from instances of use.

Consistent with Wand and Weber's view of an IS, an ES artifact is intended, and explicitly designed, to represent the real world. On the other hand, its latent structures emerge from the design of its scripted structures, rather than being explicitly designed. This presents a critical problem for IT researchers and ES designers. While coverage fit is increasing as ES vendors add functionality and industry templates, enablement fit is unlikely to improve until we learn to design latent structures and, in general, to design in a way that reduces the number and severity of imposition misfits.

## Conclusions

This research investigated the fit between organizations and enterprise systems with the purpose of developing a theoretical conceptualization of Org–ES fit, and from this to theorize about the ES artifact, and the IT artifact in general. To accomplish our goals, we conducted an analysis of misfits using grounded theory techniques to assess the ways in which an ES failed to fit the needs of an organization. Our initial analysis revealed misfits in six domains: functionality, data, usability, roles, control, and culture. These domains go beyond the fit domains previously reported in the ES and IS literature.

As we compared our misfits with each other, a more theoretical classification emerged: some misfits were due to system deficiencies, while others were due to system impositions, leading to our conclusion that Org–ES fit should be conceptualized as composed of two different types, coverage and enablement. Fit as coverage captures the extent to which the ES meets the requirements of the organization (i.e., the extent to which there are no deficiencies causing misfits). Fit as enablement captures the extent to which the ES enables the organization to operate efficiently and effectively according to its needs. While fit as coverage should sound familiar to practitioners, neither of these two fit conceptualizations are captured in the general IS fit literature (e.g., Dishaw and Strong 1999; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Vessey and Galletta 1991; Zigurs and Buckland 1998), nor in the specific ES fit literature (e.g., Sia and Soh 2007).

Our new concept of latent structures in the ES emerged as we theorized about the causes of our misfits in the six domains in terms of the associated structures in the ES and compared this understanding to the literature. Latent structures are an extension of Wand and Weber's surface, deep, and physical structures (Wand and Weber 1990; Weber 1997), and represent a new way of thinking about an IT artifact. These latent ES structures, although not necessarily materially specified in the ES, are part of the ES that exists independently of its organizational embedding. Explicitly considering the fit between an ES's latent structures and the associated organizational analogues, and promptly addressing misfits as they are identified should enable more rapid achievement of organizational benefits.

Several limitations may affect our results. First, we studied only one organization, although we studied five different contexts in that organization. We argue that ACRO is exactly the type of organization for which SAP is designed because it has typical manufacturing operations. Although its products are more complex than average, this complexity is exactly why it needed SAP. Overall, SAP was a good fit, as evidenced by the few system deficiencies we observed. Thus our results should reflect inherent Org–ES fit issues. Second, we only studied one ES, which may limit generalization to other ES packages. Evidence from our observations in other companies using a variety of systems suggests, however, that other systems have similar problems, and that the misfits we observed occur in other organizations using SAP or other products. Finally, we have focused on misfits as problems rather than gaps, and have thus focused more on ES rather than organizational structures as misfit causes. Our goal was to use the theoretical knowledge we developed about misfits and Org–ES fit to understand better the ES artifact. The complexity of collective constructs such as Org–ES fit requires, however, a program of research rather than a single study (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007; Morgeson and Hofmann 1999) and we encourage other researchers to build on the foundation we have laid. Further research could, for example, do a similar investigation with the goal of understanding what categories of organizational structures are salient in Org–ES fit.

In summary, our purpose in this paper was to study the misfits observed between an organization and its new ES and, from the findings, to develop new conceptualizations of organization–ES fit and of the ES artifact. Through our grounded study of misfits, we have accomplished that purpose.

## About the Authors

Diane M. Strong, Ph.D., Carnegie Mellon University, is a professor and Director of MIS in the School of Business at Worcester Polytechnic Institute. Her research focuses on advanced information technologies, such as enterprise systems, and their use in organizations. She examines how such systems contribute to the structuring of work, the integration of data and processes across organizational boundaries, and the need for workarounds as organizations seek to fit packaged systems to organizational needs. With NSF funding, she is currently investigating how electronic health record systems affect healthcare delivery processes, and is director of WPI's Center for eHealth Innovation and Process Transformation. She also studies data and information quality.

Olga Volkoff, Ph.D., University of Western Ontario, is an assistant professor of MIS in the Faculty of Business Administration at Simon Fraser University in Vancouver, Canada. Her research focuses on the implementation and use of software to integrate organizational processes across internal and external organizational boundaries. Her recent work has examined the challenges of knowledge transfer during enterprise system (ES) implementation, changes in management control in ES-enabled organizations, the different dimensions of ES-enabled integration and, more broadly, how technology leads to organizational change. She is currently examining how the implementation of electronic health records affects healthcare organizations and the professionals they employ.
