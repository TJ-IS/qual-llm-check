## Abstract

Drawing on sociology of science foundations, we argue that, in order to survive and prosper, healthy applied disciplines must meet the dual demands of academic and practitioner audiences by demonstrating both focus and diversity in their research. First, we use this concomitant modality to explain why previous studies into the structure of the Information Systems discipline have reported contradictory results, with some finding a focused field while others conclude that the field is diverse. In support of our argument, we then present the results of a longitudinal, author co-citation analysis, looking across the full range of journals in which IS research is published. Our results suggest that the IS field has sustained a focus on research within three subfields over a 20-year period from 1986 to 2005: (1) a thematic miscellany of research on development, implementation, and use of systems in various application domains; (2) IS strategy and business outcomes; and (3) group work and decision support. At the same time, the field has demonstrated considerable diversity within and around these core subfields, with researchers responding flexibly to the rapidly changing field by investigating these areas with new paradigms and in new contexts, and by exploring new topics including inter-business and Internet applications, computer-supported collaborative work, virtual teams, and knowledge management. Finally, we demonstrate that, over the 20-year period from 1986 to 2005, the discipline has shifted from fragmented adhocracy to a polycentric state, which is particularly appropriate to an applied discipline such as IS that must address the dual demands of focus and diversity in a rapidly changing technological context.

Keywords: IS discipline, IS research, author co-citation analysis, focus, diversity

## Introduction

An academic discipline that functions in an applied setting, such as Information Systems, faces particular challenges in establishing and maintaining its intellectual structure. Unlike more traditional disciplines, applied disciplines must demonstrate their value in both theoretical and practical arenas. More specifically, they must manage a tension between the demands of building an in-depth body of knowledge on their specialty areas (i.e., the need to establish a core focus of coherence and continuity in the discipline across time) and the demands to remain responsive to new directions arising in their practice contexts (i.e., the need to encourage topical diversity in the research agenda over time in response to new practice directions). Such disciplines often experience protracted debates among their members about their discipline's identity and direction, particularly related to issues concerning whether and how the field can survive and prosper. The IS discipline is no stranger to such debate.

The aim of this article is to provide a comprehensive, empirically based review of the developing structure of the IS research field and a view of the discipline's direction. While intellectual leaders may strive to set a research agenda for a discipline, the identity and direction that the discipline takes are ultimately determined by the collective action of its contributors (Banville and Landry, 1989). Our goal here is to provide an empirically derived answer to the question of how the work of the broad community of IS researchers, publishing across the breadth of journals in the IS domain, reflects the character of their discipline, in terms of its focus and diversity. Essentially, we argue that the health of the IS field, in meeting the dual demands of practical relevance and academic rigor, is reflected by diversity of research topics around a continuous and coherent core of ideas.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We start by providing a brief summary of the key positions that have been taken in the IS discipline debate. We then discuss underpinning work from the sociology of science and introduce more recent perspectives that illuminate the dangers of assuming that the healthy development of a discipline is achieved through a single developmental path, an assumption implicit in previous discussions of how the IS field has been developing. Following this theoretical discussion, we review previous empirical work that has attempted to delineate the topics and boundaries of IS research, and introduce our own approach to understanding how the IS field is developing. We present the results of a longitudinal co-citation study and examine these results through the dual lenses of focus and diversity. Finally we present our conclusion, that the IS discipline has successfully achieved the balance between diversity and focus over a period of 20 years, and discuss recommendations for the future health and development of the field.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Foundations

## The IS Discipline Debate

The development of the IS field has engaged the interest of scholars since the field's inception, nearly 50 years ago. Early discussions focused primarily on carving out a territory for IS clearly distinct from its foundation disciplines and establishing frameworks to guide researchers in the field (Ives et al. 1980; Nolan and Wetherbe 1980). Originally, the major contributing disciplines were considered to be computer science, management science, and organization science (Culnan and Swanson 1986), with lesser contributions from fields such as management accounting, behavioral science, and psychology (Adam and Fitzgerald 2000; Nolan and Wetherbe 1980). More recently, the fields of economics and marketing have been added as contributing disciplines (Grover et al. 2006).

Initially, some leaders expressed doubts as to whether IS could ever be considered a discipline in its own right (Dearden 1972; Keen 1980), but by the mid-1980s, Culnan (1986, 1987) and Culnan and Swanson (1986) were reporting empirical evidence that described the emergence of MIS as a separate field of study that was building a cumulative research tradition distinct from its foundational fields. $^{2}$ By the end of that decade, Banville and Landry (1989), examining the development of the field, characterized MIS as a “fragmented adhocracy.” While they emphasized that their categorization was value-free, the description of the field as fragmented carried with it an implicit threat of disintegration that caused disquiet among many members of the discipline. We will return to Banville and Landry’s characterization of the discipline shortly.

In the 20 years that have elapsed since these early assessments of the field, scholars have continued to debate the nature of the IS discipline and whether the early promise of a distinct discipline of IS research has been fulfilled, or whether the presumed threat of disintegration through fragmentation is eventuating. As the field has grown and established a stronger claim to its own identity, researchers have turned their attention to the concern of identifying the intellectual structure of the discipline. Some (e.g., Orlikowski and Iacono

2001) have argued that IS researchers have strayed from the core subject matter of the discipline, which they define as the "IT artifact," and that diversity of IS research has resulted in an ambiguous and ill-defined core identity (Benbasat and Weber 1996; Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hirschheim and Klein 2003; Weber 2003). In the view of these writers, this failure to develop a stable, integrated, and coherent core research agenda has resulted in increasing fragmentation of the field and threatens the continuity and survival of the discipline. Others, however, challenge the contentions that the IS discipline is in crisis or that a strong theoretical core is necessary for the discipline to achieve legitimacy (DeSanctis 2003; Galliers 2003; Hirschheim et al. 1996; Ives et al. 2004; King and Lyytinen 2004; Lyytinen and King 2004; Robey 1994, 1996, 2003). These researchers have argued for a fostering of diverse and adaptive IS research in order to allow continued growth and knowledge contribution across a range of topics in a broad IS body of knowledge.

These debates have lost none of their momentum in the past five years, and review papers by Agarwal and Lucas (2005) and Teo (2007) provide thorough summaries of recent contributions.

## Sociology of Science Foundations

Much of the debate discussed above has been fueled by Banville and Landry's seminal 1989 article. Drawing on a categorization of scientific fields developed by Whitley (1984a, 1984b), Banville and Landry positioned MIS as a fragmented adhocracy based on the low levels of strategic and functional dependency among members of the discipline, and high levels of strategic task uncertainty, $^{3}$ as shown in Table 1. They concluded that MIS was “an essentially pluralistic scientific field, especially in view of its vocational character” (p. 58). Banville and Landry reinforced Whitley’s statements that “it is neither good nor bad for a discipline to qualify as high or low on any of the variables used for classification” (p. 55), but a less value-laden description, such as pluralistic and adaptable organization, might have proven less provocative. We suspect that many subsequent commentators have been influenced by the pejorative connotations of the descriptions that Whitley chose for his various categories, and have concluded that a discipline that is fragmented risks disintegration.

Concerns about the fragmented nature of IS, the lack of planning implicit in the word $adhocracy$ , and the fear of disintegration of the discipline have permeated the IS disciplinary debate. But as Whitley (2000) and Fuchs (1993) point out, fragmented adhocracies may be a normal stage of development for social science disciplines. While fragmented fields may deteriorate or disintegrate by further fragmentation and isolation, they can also develop and thrive by achieving a balance between overall cohesiveness across the field and the development of specialized subfields within the field (Fuchs 1993). Thus, a prospering applied field is unlikely to remain in the state of fragmented adhocracy, because the development over time of greater cohesiveness and specialties will increase both the strategic and functional dependence among members of the field, resulting in a shift toward the polycentric categories in the top row of Table 1.

As Banville and Landry have noted, the goal of an academic discipline is the production of new knowledge. In discussing the progress of scientific disciplines more broadly, Gibbons, Nowotny and their colleagues (Gibbons et al. 1994; Nowotny et al. 2001) have presented an influential argument that, across most disciplines, a fundamental change is occurring in the research process, both in terms of what types of knowledge are produced and how knowledge is produced and disseminated. These authors argue that a new approach to the process of research and knowledge production (which they term Mode 2) is evolving out of, and coexisting with, the traditional protocols (Mode 1). Table 2 provides a summary of the basic characteristics of the two modes.

Mode 1 research, with its emphasis on the creation of a rigorous body of theory and knowledge and the establishment of the identity and status of the field and its researchers within academia, is contrasted with the Mode 2 priorities of finding solutions to real-world problems and collaborating with and communicating to practitioners. Essentially, the Mode 2 research approach is more application- and context-driven than the traditional mode (Mode 1) and more likely to involve transdisciplinary collaborations, both within academia and with industry and society partners. $^{4}$

Mode 2 studies show greater heterogeneity in terms of the variety of organizations that carry out research, beyond traditional academic institutions, with rapid shifts in the composition of cross-organizational collaborative teams as a diverse range of problems arise and are solved. Gibbons et al. (1994) claim that the context-driven nature of Mode 2 work results in greater levels of social accountability, with researchers showing more reflexivity toward the individuals and groups in the problem space and the social implications of the research outcomes. Given the higher levels of involvement of the broader community of interest, the quality of Mode 2 work is likely to be assessed by additional criteria, such as social utility and market competitiveness, in addition to the traditional peer review judgments that predominate in Mode 1 research. Finally, in Mode 2, the primary target for dissemination of research results is a practitioner audience, in contrast to the scholarly conferences and journals used in Mode 1.

While researchers in a variety of fields have drawn on the Mode 2 concepts to justify arguments for making their disciplines more responsive to change (Hessels and van Lente 2008), we believe that a more tempered approach is appropriate. The presentation of this dichotomy of research modes masks the more probable—and what we argue to be the more ideal—situation, namely that most applied disciplines are likely to include both Mode 1 and Mode 2 attributes in the range of their research practices. Indeed, as Pettigrew (2001) comments, such dichotomies may conceal as much as they reveal, and they present a tempting danger of opting for a superficial categorization rather than exploring the full range of complexity and uncertainty apparent along the continuum. For example, Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff (2000) envisage scientific disciplines as dynamic knowledge systems maintaining a healthy tension in which Mode 1 research could be embedded in Mode 2 work or vice versa, making it difficult to classify particular research projects as entirely Mode 1 or entirely Mode 2 work.

For applied disciplines, maintaining a balance between the two modes is vital: too much emphasis on Mode 1 may result in stagnation and lack of relevance, while too much attention to Mode 2 can lead to research that lacks rigor, that looks indistinguishable from consulting work, or that follows every passing fad (Robinson 2008; Tranfield and Starkey 1998). These two concerns—stagnation versus lack of rigor—essentially encapsulate the concerns of the two sides in the IS discipline debate. The arguments for more focus, and especially the concern that too much diversity will lead to lack of rigor, can be seen as a call to address the Mode 1 concerns of establishing a continuous and coherent body of knowledge that will enable the field to maintain its credibility and status as a traditional academic discipline. On the contrary, the arguments for diversity and adaptation, particularly the concern that too much focus will lead to stagnation and irrelevance, may be seen as a response to address the Mode 2 requirements of flexibility and adaptability to the context, particularly in view of the unusual amount of rapid change in the IS practice environment. This raises the question of whether both sides of the IS discipline debate have fallen into the dichotomy trap against which Pettigrew has warned, aiming solely for one goal or the other and thus failing to recognize the necessity of achieving both focus and diversity, while still maintaining continuity and stability in the discipline across time.

In sum, contrary to the assumption that the health of the field relies on either focus or diversity, we believe that a healthy applied discipline such as IS must avoid this dichotomy trap and accept the need to be continually in tension, maintaining a balance between Mode 1 and Mode 2 approaches to research. The Mode 1 focus on central research themes for the field is essential to the continuity and coherence required of a recognized academic discipline, while Mode 2 diversity, through rising interest in some areas and decline in others, is necessary to provide flexibility and adaptability in response to the IS field's rapidly changing contexts of application. Applied disciplines that succeed over time in maintaining this balance, particularly those operating in rapidly changing technological contexts, are likely to shift toward the poly-centric categories in the top row of Table 1, since the Mode 1 pressures will increase the strategic dependence among members in the field, while the Mode 2 pressures will maintain high levels of strategic task uncertainty.

## Empirical Studies on the Structure of the IS Field

Emphasis on a single end of the dichotomy of research modes can be found in previous empirical examinations of the direction of the IS discipline, with scholars arguing for one of the two perspectives described earlier: either the importance of research remaining focused on a central core of the discipline, or the value of promoting diversity in research. Examples of the first perspective, demonstrating focus and convergence on certain central topics in the field, can be seen in Alavi and Carlson's (1992) and Culnan's (1986, 1987) examinations of the early development of the field (up to the mid-1980s), both reporting convergence and suggesting that IS was progressing to a cumulative research tradition. This view of the field's convergence on a central focus continued in Vessey et al.'s (2002) examination of IS-related articles from five journals during the period 1995 to 1999, with the authors commenting that “compared with topics in the computing field in general, IS is not particularly diverse” (p. 166).

In contrast, two other studies, analyzing the field from 1987 to 2000, concluded that the field was topically diverse. Swanson and Ramiller (1993) analyzed submissions to a single journal, the then newly established Information Systems Research, and reported a topically diverse field during the 1987 to 1992 period between Alavi and Carlson's and Vessey et al.'s studies. Similarly, Larsen and Levine (2005), using a co-word analysis to compare research articles from four IS journals across two time periods, 1990 to 1994 and 1995 to 2000, found a diverse field with a “high degree of change in centers of coherence” (p. 374) over time.

Interestingly, while Swanson and Ramiller concluded that the IS field was diverse, a detailed reading reveals a topically diverse field at the lowest levels of their analysis, with overarching themes displaying more focus at a higher level of analysis. Similarly, Sidorova et al. (2008), using a latent semantic analysis of abstracts of research papers from three IS journals across the 1985–2006 time period, concluded that the discipline has demonstrated a stable core of high level research areas focused on issues of IT development and use, with broad topical diversity at lower levels of analysis. The results of these two studies raise the possibility that conclusions drawn by previous authors about diversity or focus of the field may depend on the level of granularity they chose.

Overall, these prior empirical studies, when taken together and viewed in the light of the dual modality discussed earlier, do suggest support for our expectation that the IS field displays evidence of both Mode 1 focus and Mode 2 diversity and that the focus–diversity concomitant duality would seem to provide a convincing explanation of the contradictory findings in previous research. By having viewed their data through an either/or lens, and seeking to draw a conclusion that contributes to one or the other side of the debate, previous researchers may have blinkered themselves to the possibility that the IS discipline could, and perhaps should, be simultaneously displaying evidence of both focus and diversity in order to meet both the theoretical and the practical demands of an applied discipline.

Conclusions drawn from prior research about how the IS discipline is developing are further limited by the restricted and varied sets of journals that these research teams have used in their analyses. An alternative explanation for these mixed findings relates to the sensitivity of the results to the different sets of journals used for the analyses (often referred to as the “basket of journals” problem). As noted by Chua et al. (2003), the choice of journals may be critical in determining the extent of focus or diversity revealed. Each of these prior studies has examined a different group of journals, and, with the exception of Culnan’s earlier co-citation analyses, the number of journals in each study was limited (between one and eight), raising the possibility that conclusions about trends identified, either toward increased focus or increased diversity, may be related more to the particular foci of the set of journals chosen, rather than to an overall trend in the field. While Culnan’s studies avoided the basket of journals limitation and provide support for the view that, by the mid-1980s, the IS discipline was stabilizing as an independent academic domain, the differing conclusions from the later studies leave us unclear as to the subsequent direction of the field. The mixed evidence from prior studies may be indicative of a healthy field exhibiting both Mode 1 focus and Mode 2 diversity, but alternatively, it may simply be an artifact of differing journal selections in the various studies.

In the next section, we present the current study, an author co-citation analysis (ACA), the aim of which was to provide a comprehensive, longitudinal analysis of the development of the IS discipline. Our overarching goal was to develop a picture, from the perspective of the IS research community, of how the IS discipline has grown and changed over the 20 years since Culnan's (1987) study of the intellectual structure of the field. Using ACA, we examined the breadth of research published across the discipline, over a 20-year period from 1986 to 2005. Examining the full range of journals is critical if an adequate evaluation of the discipline's focus and diversity is to be achieved. By interpreting the results through the dual lenses of focus and diversity we are able to remain open to the possibilities of finding one or both of these modes of research. In addition, by examining the development over time we are able to shed light on the temporal stability of the field. Our study thus makes a unifying contribution to the ongoing debate over the nature and direction of the IS discipline.

## Author Co-Citation Analysis

Author co-citation analysis (ACA) is a set of bibliometric techniques that uses the published body of work of an academic discipline, based on citations to the discipline's leading researchers, to map the intellectual structure of the field (McCain 1990; White and McCain 1998). ACA builds a composite view of the discipline from the perspective of its contributors, by analyzing the frequency with which pairs of authors are cited together. The fundamental premise is that intellectual subfields or centers of influence can be deduced by identifying groups of foundational authors who are often cited together (i.e., co-cited), and then examining the co-citing articles, published across many different journals, to determine how the co-citing researchers are building on the work of the field's leading authors. In contrast to previous scientometric studies that restricted their evaluation to a limited number of journals, the ACA approach allowed us to draw data from the entire citation index database, thus providing a view of the field based on the combined work of the thousands of researchers who have now published IS research across a wide range of journals, rather than being limited to work drawn from a far more restricted set of authors and journals. $^{5}$

ACA can be used to inform the IS field as to whether it is primarily focused, diverse, or achieving a mix of these modes. The extent to which intellectual subfields change, emerge, disappear, or coalesce can give important clues about the field's temporal stability and its development in terms of the focus and diversity distinctions discussed earlier. In a discipline developing at the focus (Mode 1) end of the continuum, the groups of co-cited authors identified by ACA (i.e., the discipline's intellectual subfields) would be expected to show considerable stability over time, with each subfield clearly focused on one or two research themes, as illustrated in Figure 1. In this scenario, the authors in each subfield would largely be specialized in the research themes of that subfield, and there would be few, if any, authors working across subfields. Further, the influence of the co-cited foundational authors is likely to be persistent over time, with a limited number of changes in the foundational author set.

In contrast, as shown in Figure 2, a discipline at the diversity (Mode 2) end of the continuum would show a diverse and evolving set of subfields, changing rapidly over time, with new fields arising and old fields disappearing, and little or no continuity between subfields. Authors working in such a discipline would most likely be associated with more than one subfield, and would move from one subfield to another as new research areas arose. In this scenario, it is quite possible for two different subfields to arise, each focused on the same research themes, but each working independently of the other. Here the influence of foundational authors will rise and fall quickly, as new authors and new research themes catch the interest of researchers in the field.

As noted earlier, we would expect a thriving applied discipline to show some combination of Mode 1 and Mode 2 activity. Thus, as shown in Figure 3, disciplines balancing Mode 1 focus and Mode 2 diversity would show cohesiveness across the field through overlapping clusters of co-cited authors and temporal stability through continuity of subfields over time, combined with development of specialized subfields branching off from the mainstream in response to new and emerging areas of interest (Fuchs, 1993). In this balanced mode, we would expect to see some foundational authors exerting considerable influence over a long period of time, but also changes in the foundational author set as new authors and research interests are taken up.

## ACA Procedures

We used standard ACA procedures, as described in McCain (1990), to examine changes in the intellectual content and boundaries of the IS discipline over four 5-year periods from

1986 to 2005. The key steps in ACA involve (1) identifying the foundational author set and retrieving co-citation frequencies and bibliographies for each time period; (2) analyzing the co-citation frequencies with analysis and mapping techniques (factor analysis and multidimensional scaling) to display specialty groupings of authors (i.e., intellectual subfields) and to demonstrate changes across the time periods; and (3) interpreting the resulting groupings through an analysis of the topics of the articles co-citing authors in each group. For the sake of brevity, we present details of our application of the ACA methodology in Appendices A through E and focus here on a presentation of the key results. We leave the interpretation of the results, in terms of the need for both focus and diversity in the development of a healthy field, for the “Discussion” section.

As detailed in Appendix A, we used a combination of publication count and reputation in order to identify the most representative set of 100 foundational authors across the many constituencies of the discipline, and then extracted co-citation counts for pairs of authors from citation records retrieved for this set. The co-citation counts and bibliographies obtained for the foundational authors provided the input data for factor and multidimensional scaling analyses, which were used to identify the groups of co-cited authors that are associated with intellectual subfields. We discuss the results of the factor and multidimensional scaling analyses in the next two sections, with full details of the factor analyses and factor loadings provided in Appendix B, and the analysis of the research themes of co-citing articles in Appendix C. Two further appendices describe the analysis of journal outlets for articles co-citing authors in each factor (Appendix D) and the analysis of changes in factors and authors associated with factors over time (Appendix E).

## Factor Analyses

Once the final set of authors had been identified and the co-citation counts had been prepared, standard procedures for applying factor analysis (more specifically, principal components analysis) in the co-citation context (McCain 1990; White and McCain 1998) were used to examine patterns of co-citation frequencies and to establish the relational links between groups of authors in each period (see Appendix B for more details). The active intellectual subfields in each time period were then derived from an analysis of the co-citing articles associated with each group of authors (see Appendix C). Examining the continuity and changes in the factors appearing in each time period provided for an evaluation of the extent of focus and diversity in the development of the field over time.

The factors identified in each time period are shown in Table 3 and comprise clusters of foundational authors who are frequently co-cited together. These clusters are interpreted as intellectual subfields or centers of influence (White and McCain 1998). A group of foundational authors clustering together may be co-cited for a number of different reasons, including their common influence related to specific research themes within a content domain, a specific research methodology, a specific theory, a specific context of inquiry, or a combination of these reasons. The subfields emerging for each time period thus include some factors where the influence of foundational authors on subsequent co-citing research has been primarily related to research themes and other factors where the foundational authors' influence is evident in terms of research methodologies or contexts of investigation.

The IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor represents a research theme based subfield, anchored by co-citations to several strong foundational authority figures whose influence is evident across all four time periods. The co-citing articles to foundational authors in this factor encompass a diverse range of system- and relationship-oriented mainstream research into information systems—their development, their implementation, their impact, and their use by individuals and within organizations.

The Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany factor illustrates a center of influence based on research methodology: it is associated with research themes similar to those in the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor but is distinguished by the foundational authors' association with qualitative methodologies, and the use of these methodologies by the co-citing researchers. The qualitative methods emphasis of this subfield is also reflected in the Information Systems Journal's position as the third most frequent publishing outlet for researchers citing authors in this factor, a rank that this journal does not hold for any of the other factors (see Appendix D). Similarly, the Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factor could not be distinguished from the other Thematic Miscellany factors on research themes alone. However, the context-of-inquiry influence of the foundational authors in this factor was apparent in the emphasis of the co-citing articles on the new context of e-commerce-related applications.

In contrast to the wide range of themes in research citing the three Thematic Miscellany factors, the co-citing articles for the IS Strategy factor showed a more concentrated interest in themes related to IS strategy and strategic business outcomes, with an emerging interest in the last 5-year period in the strategic applications of interorganizational systems. This recent interest in interorganizational systems, especially in electronic data interchange systems, e-business, and supply chain management, is also evident in the themes of articles co-citing authors in the Inter-Business Systems factor. While we regard both the IS Strategy and the Inter-Business Systems factors as primarily representing research theme-based centers of influence, the Inter-Business Systems factor has a stronger context-of-inquiry emphasis on information systems research in the business-to-business context. As shown in Table D1 in Appendix D, the appearance of two new journals—the International Journal of Electronic Commerce and the Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce—in the top 10 journals for the Inter-Business Systems factor reveal the strong emphasis on business and electronic commerce of researchers co-citing foundational authors in this subfield.

The major research themes of articles co-citing foundational authors in the Group Work & Decision Support factor also showed a more concentrated focus, centered on group decision support systems and decision support systems, with interest in knowledge management, and teams and virtual teamwork increasing in the 2001–2005 period. The Group Work & Decision Support factor is an example of a subfield where the foundational authors' influence spans both research theme and methodology, with many of the co-citing articles applying decision-making theories and problem-solving and model-based methodologies in their investigations of decision support and group decision support systems. The distinctiveness of this factor is also reflected in its journal outlets, as shown in Appendix D, with the third most frequent publishing outlet being Decision Support Systems. Additionally, two journals—International Journal of Human-Computer Studies and the European Journal of Operational Research—feature in the top 10 outlets for this factor, but do not appear among the journals listed for the other factors.

Finally, the research co-citing foundational authors in the Foundations & Reference Disciplines factor reflected both an interest in decision support systems and a strong emphasis on foundation disciplines, especially management science and operations research. As shown in Appendix D, the journals publishing articles citing papers for the Foundations & Reference Disciplines factor include an almost entirely different set from the journals listed for the other factors, with the top ranked journal being the Journal of the Operational Research Society, a journal that does not appear in the top 10 for any of the other factors. The different group of journals for this factor reflects both the earlier time period of this factor, and its focus on reference disciplines. As the IS discipline has evolved and developed its own journal outlets, it is likely that these reference discipline outlets became less appropriate for IS specialist research, and researchers moved away from citing the foundational authors in the Foundations & Reference Disciplines factor toward citing IS specialist authors in other factors.

We now turn to the multidimensional scaling results, which provide a visualization of the structure of the field.

## Multidimensional Scaling Analyses

Multidimensional scaling (MDS) provides a visual representation, or map, of the intellectual distance between the foundational authors, in terms of the perceptions of the co-citing researchers. The intellectual distance between pairs of authors in each time period is calculated as the arithmetic inverse of the correlation between the authors, as derived from the co-citation matrix. Hence, authors that have highly correlated co-citation patterns are separated on the MDS maps by a correspondingly low intellectual distance. The MDS maps present similar information to the factor analyses, but they also provide a clearer picture of the extent of high cross-loading authors between factors, and of the underlying dimensional structure of the author groupings. Additionally, a comparison of the MDS maps across time periods, shown in Figures 4 through 7, presents a visualization of changes and developments in how our foundational authors' research is used by the wider IS research community. The foundational authors' names on the maps act as proxies for co-citations to their work, with the circled groups illustrating the clusters of authors being co-cited in each factor. $^{6}$

Examining the MDS maps (Figures 4 through 7) in light of the extracted factors we discussed above, we see that the vertical axis reflects a quantitative to qualitative dimension, while the horizontal axis shows a span of subject areas. Thus in Figure 4, the authors that subsequently split into the Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany factor already appear at the lower (qualitative) end of the vertical axis, and in the subsequent maps in Figures 5 through 7 we see the increasing distinction of this factor on the vertical axis, reflecting a growing interest on the part of researchers in cociting this group of authors for support for their choice of research approach. Similarly, the horizontal position of the Group Work & Decision Support factor on the MDS maps clearly illustrates its separation as a relatively self-contained subfield, reinforcing the distinctiveness of this factor's themes and journal outlets. $^{7}$

Authors appearing in the intersections of the circles on the maps also typically cross-loaded on both factors, meaning that they are co-cited with other authors from both factors. These intersections highlight areas of commonality and cross-fertilization between factors. The considerable overlap through all time periods of the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany and IS Strategy factors on the MDS maps reflects the high number of co-cited authors cross-loading on these factors. Likewise, as can be seen from Figure 7, the intellectual distance between authors in the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany and Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factors, as perceived by the co-citers, is small, in that the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor of “older” authors is embedded within the larger Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factor.

## Changes in Subfields over Time

In order to examine the evolution of the discipline over time, we developed timelines to integrate the factor analyses and MDS maps, tracking the high-loading authors from each of the subfields in our 1986–1990 period backward to the 1980–1985 factors identified in Culnan's study (1987), and then working forward to trace who continued in each subfield, and who joined or left subfields for each subsequent period. A summary of these relationships of subfields over time is presented in Figure 8. (See Appendix E for a tracking of the highest loading authors across the time periods.)

Typically, a core set of foundational authors anchored each subfield across the time periods, with newer authors joining in later periods as their co-citations with the earlier foundational authors and each other increased. As shown in Figure 8, the IS Strategy and the three Thematic Miscellany factors provide continuity and temporal stability in the discipline, with the extensive overlap between these groups of cocited authors demonstrating a strong thread of cohesiveness for the field over time. The three Thematic Miscellany factors are especially interesting, in that while they demonstrate continuity and stability over the 20 year period, at the same time they contain substantial diversity in the research themes, methodologies, and contexts begin investigated. Additionally, the separation of the Group Work & Decision Support factor, together with its continuity through all four periods, suggests that this factor has developed as a specialized subfield of the discipline.

## Discussion

Returning to the debate about the nature of the IS discipline, we now examine the insights that can be drawn from these results. To date, the debate has tended to take an either/or view of the structure of the field, arguing for either focus or diversity, without considering the possibility that both focus and diversity may be needed and may indeed exist together. In addition, previous empirical work has been hindered by differing basket of journals choices, which raises the possibility that any trends identified were related more to the particular foci of the set of journals chosen, rather than to an overall trend in the field.

The current study addresses these gaps by examining how the broad community of IS researchers, drawn from the entire citation index database, co-cite foundational IS authors. We view this body of research through the dual lenses of focus and diversity to provide empirical evidence showing how the structure of the IS field is developing over time. Finally, we return to Whitley's (1984a, 1984b) categorization of scientific fields and examine whether Banville and Landry's (1989) positioning of the IS field as a fragmented adhocracy is still supported.

## Evidence of Focus

Issues of focus have two perspectives: whether research in a field is concentrated on a central core of research themes and whether the field demonstrates temporal stability through continuity of research themes over time. As shown in Figure 8, three factors are clearly distinguishable through all periods—IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany, IS Strategy, and Group Work & Decision Support—suggesting that there is a strong continuity of research ideas across the 20 years of the study. The IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor and its two offshoots, Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany and Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany, lie at the heart of the field, providing a continuous and coherent polycentric core over the 20 year period of the study. While the persistence of the Miscellany factors provides evidence of temporal stability in the discipline, the diffuse nature of these factors, comprising a broad range of research themes centering around development, implementation, impact and use of systems in various application domains, suggests that the core interest of the discipline has become more system- and relationship-oriented around the “nature of work that surrounds IT” (DeSanctis 2003, p. 369) than specifically addressing the IT artifact (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001).

In contrast, the IS Strategy and Group Work & Decision Support factors demonstrate both temporal stability and topical focus in the field. The IS Strategy factor, with its focus on strategy issues and business outcomes, is closely linked to the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor, as can be seen from their similar central positions on the horizontal axes of the MDS maps in Figures 4 through 7. The considerable overlap between these two factors through all time periods reflects both the high number of co-cited authors cross-loading on these factors and similarities in research themes across the two factors. Thus, while these factors do reflect differing bodies of co-citing researchers within the field, there is a high degree of commonality between them, suggesting that, together, they provide both temporal stability and a polycentric topical focus for the field.

In contrast to the high integration between the Thematic Miscellany and IS Strategy factors, the third focus of the field, Group Work & Decision Support, appears as a clearly separate and relatively self-contained subfield through all periods. The distinctiveness of this focus is revealed both in its separation on the MDS maps in Figures 4 through 7 and its journal outlets (see Appendix D), and reflects the co-citing researchers' perspective of a tight focus in the research interests of the foundational authors in the factor and a high level of co-authorship among these authors. The research themes of the Group Work & Decision Support factor are also more focused, with a primary interest in group work and decision support issues, and a closely related interest in computer-supported collaborative work and teamwork.

Thus, viewing the intellectual structure of the discipline over time from a focus perspective, our results demonstrate temporal stability and a polycentric focus on the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany, IS Strategy, and Group Work & Decision Support subfields, revealing strong continuity and coherence across the field. In particular, the areas of IS strategy and IS development and use are central research themes for the field, with group work and DSS providing a consistent sub-specialization through all periods.

## Evidence of Diversity

The focus perspective provides a convincing picture of the continuity of a set of core concerns for the IS field. However, when we view Figure 8 with a diversity lens, we see not only a polycentric core, rather than a single focus, but also changes around the core, with new and fading factors over time, and changes within the core, through splits in the factors and changes in research themes within factors, suggesting continual adjustments of research interests as new contexts arise. Specifically, in 1991–1995 we see a split in the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor with the emergence of a separate Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany factor. This factor's emphasis on context-driven, socially embedded, and reflexive research approaches presents a view of the field developing the diverse methodologies often associated with the heterogeneous and transdisciplinary attributes of Mode 2 research.

Similarly, the second split in the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor in 2001–2005 produces the Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factor, reflecting a response to a rapidly changing practice environment. While the new Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factor continues the core themes—system development, implementation, and use—of its parent factor, it shows adaptation to the changing application environment by examining these topics within the context of e-commerce-related applications. The embedding of the foundation IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor within the larger Internet Applications factor shown in Figure 7 provides support for the view that the field is maintaining the healthy tension expected of an applied discipline, with the continuity of focus provided by Mode 1 work embedded within Mode 2 diversity and adaptation to changing context. Thus, even as these factors appear to define one consistent, albeit polycentric, focus of the field, within the factors we see a continuing diversity of research interest as the IS application context changes.

While the IS Strategy factor initially appears to maintain a steady focus on strategy issues and business outcomes, there is also evidence of the development of increased diversity both in research approach and research topics. In the 10 years from 1996 to 2005, the IS Strategy factor's position on the MDS maps extends further down toward the qualitative end of the vertical axis, reflecting the introduction of a newer group of foundational authors to the factor and revealing a growing interest on the part of co-citing researchers in investigating IS strategy with a wider and more diverse range of research methods. While the core focus of this factor remains on IS strategy across all periods, the last 10 years reveal that increasing attention is being paid to the inter-organizational systems theme, reflecting interest arising in practice in new strategic directions for collaboration between businesses.

Further evidence of diversity in the discipline can be found in the emergence of a separate Inter-Business Systems factor, which is comprised of newer authors and appeared in the last two periods with interorganizational systems and e-business as its predominant theme. While strong interest in the inter-organizational systems research theme was also apparent in the IS Strategy and Internet Applications Thematic Miscellany factors, the emergence of a separate Inter-Business Systems factor suggests a strong move toward increased diversity of research interest, reflecting important developments in the context of application.

## Balancing Focus and Diversity

Our ACA analysis of the development of the IS field over time provides an explanation for the conflicting evidence about focus and diversity of previous studies. Comparing Figure 8 with Figures 1, 2, and 3, we see that the IS discipline's development best matches the illustration in Figure 3 of a field balancing focus and diversity as it develops over time. As Figure 8 reveals, the IS discipline is characterized by focus in the continuity of central subfields over time together with diversity as new subfields emerge and others decline.

This tension between focus and diversity is apparent not only at the subfield level, but also within the subfields themselves. Thus, diversity in research themes, methodologies, and contexts is embedded in the three Thematic Miscellany factors. These three factors have encompassed a diverse range of mainstream IS research themes throughout the 20 year period, even as they have provided a continuous polycentric core for the field over time.

Similarly, the Group Work & Decision Support factor is interesting in that it initially appears to provide a distinct subfield with a very consistent focus through all four periods. However, close analysis of the co-citing articles in this factor revealed that, in many of the studies, highly theoretical work (Mode 1) and practice-driven work (Mode 2) are tightly intertwined: Mode 2 research on building decision support systems is informed by Mode 1 work on decision-making theory and vice versa, reflecting Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff's comment that "when one opens the black-box [of the scientific knowledge system] one finds Mode 1 within Mode 2, and Mode 2 within Mode 1" (2000, p. 119). In this factor, also, we see evidence in the last period of increasing diversity in response to new practice areas, with greater interest in teams and virtual teamwork, and increasing attention to knowledge management.

## Information Systems as a Polycentric Field

We now examine the present-day structure of the IS discipline in the light of the evidence we have for positioning IS on each of Whitley's (1984b) three dimensions for the classification of intellectual fields: the degree of strategic dependence, the degree of strategic task uncertainty, and the degree of functional dependence. In 1989, Banville and Landry assessed IS on these dimensions as a fragmented adhocracy, with low strategic dependence, high task uncertainty, and low functional dependence, fueling concerns over the subsequent 20 years about the potential disintegration and isolation of the field. Has the field changed in the subsequent 20 years?

Strategic dependence refers to the extent to which researchers in a field are dependent on each other for recognition, reputation, and resources, and relates to coordination, control, and conflict in the field. We have demonstrated the presence in the IS field of strong and persistent centers of influence, represented by clusters of foundational authors whose influence was first identified in Culnan's (1987) study of the 1980 to 1985 period, and who continue to be co-cited in researchers' work across the 20 years from 1986 to 2005, both across the IS Strategy and the three Thematic Miscellany factors, and within the Group Work & Decision Support factor. The cohesiveness and continuity evidenced by the ongoing influence of these older foundational authors speaks to the development of high strategic dependence of the IS field in the period since Banville and Landry's article.

Strategic task uncertainty refers to the conceptual coherence within the field, and a high degree of strategic task uncertainty is associated with loosely coupled schools of thought. Here the diversity in the IS field, both within the factors we have identified, and with the emergence of new factors, indicates that the high strategic task uncertainty identified by Banville and Landry in 1989 has continued through to 2005.

Functional dependence describes the extent of technical and procedural coherence within the field, and the standardization of research tools, procedures, and results interpretation. Here again, Banville and Landry viewed the IS field as demonstrating low functional dependence, with little consensus among researchers about appropriate methods of inquiry. In the present study, we have shown the emergence of a distinct center of influence formed around research methods, the Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany factor, suggesting a growing functional dependence in the field. We categorize the current level of functional dependence as medium, because we believe further standardization is likely in the future. For example, while design science has not appeared as a center of influence in the time periods of our study, we believe that it is highly likely that such a center will emerge over the next few years, indicating a further increase in the degree of functional dependence in the field.

Referring back to Table 1, we can see that this categorization of high strategic dependence, high strategic task uncertainty, and medium functional dependence suggests that the IS field has shifted from the fragmented adhocracy state described by Banville and Landry, and is now more appropriately situated as a polycentric field.

## Looking Forward: Focus and Diversity as the IS Discipline Develops

Is this shift from fragmented adhocracy to polycentric field, as evidenced by our analyses, sufficient to address prior researchers' concerns of potential disintegration on the one hand and isolation and stagnation on the other? We believe it is, but with a caution. First, if the discipline were indeed declining or disintegrating, we would expect that researchers would move away to other fields, resulting in a decline in the number of researchers co-citing the foundational authors within the IS field. Thus, we examined the total number of co-citing articles extracted for each period, and the total number of unique authors of these articles for each period. As shown in Figure 9, the robust health of the field is reflected in the steady increase in the number of researchers working within the IS field and co-citing work from a large set of leading IS authors for support of their research. In addition, IS research is reaching a wide audience, as evidenced by the range of over 170 journals publishing the co-citing articles retrieved for this study, while still maintaining a central body of research in the core journals of the field. Thus, from this first perspective, the growing number of contributors and articles suggests that the adaptive pluralism of the discipline is not a concern.

There may be a concern, however, from a second perspective, which arises when we examine the extent to which subfields are becoming increasingly isolated. In particular, the combination of parallel research interests with increasing intellectual distance between the Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany and the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factors suggests that researchers are increasingly limiting their attention to prior work on their topics within their own paradigm, ignoring contributions within other paradigms. In a similar vein, the researchers citing the four authors in the new Inter-Business Systems factor are working on research topics that feature highly in the work of researchers citing foundational authors in the IS Strategy factor. While the appearance of Inter-Business Systems as a separate factor at this stage may simply be an artifact of the introduction of more recent foundational authors, it is of concern that the researchers co-citing authors in the Inter-Business Systems factor are apparently not making connections to the work already done by authors in the IS Strategy factor.

Thus, while it appears that IS has successfully addressed the challenges of an applied discipline by developing from a fragmented adhocracy to a polycentric field, the evidence of increasing isolation of some factors suggests that we cannot be complacent. Constant attention is required to maintain the balance between the dual demands of academia and practice, if the discipline is to sustain a continued and healthy presence in both arenas. This dual modality is particularly critical for a field such as IS, which is experiencing such rapid technological change in its context of practice with the consequence that practice often leads academia in terms of identifying key new areas and changing dimensions of the existing topic areas (Benbasat and Zmud 1999).

There is a need for even greater collaboration with practice to continue developing Mode 2 transdisciplinary research approaches in order to respond quickly, adaptively, and relevantly to the changing environment (Gill and Bhattacherjee 2009). Such collaboration has the added benefits of increasing the field's visibility and credibility in the practice arena and generating greater enrollments and funding within academic institutions (Gill and Bhattacherjee 2009, Klein and Rowe 2008). This, in turn, can help the IS field to sustain its identity as an academic discipline with continuing emphasis on Mode 1 concerns of developing a rigorous and coherent body of theory and knowledge. Here, the increasing separation of the Qualitative Methods Thematic Miscellany factor from the IS Development & Use Thematic Miscellany factor and the emergence of the Inter-Business Systems factor completely independent of the IS Strategy factor are two areas of concern that do require attention. The development of separate paradigmatic or thematic streams of research on similar topics could result in key connections between findings from different perspectives being overlooked. Researchers' familiarity with, and citation of, work done by others on the same topics using different research paradigms could make an important contribution to the integration of the IS field in the future. Thus, we hope that a future co-citation study of the field would show an increase in co-citations to foundational authors across methodologies in the Thematic Miscellany factors, and across themes in the Inter-Business Systems and IS Strategy factors.

## Limitations $^{8}$

Our goal was to examine the IS field through the dual lenses of focus and diversity, and we have noted throughout the parallels between focus and Mode 1 research on the one hand, and diversity and Mode 2 research on the other. However, the co-citation tool can only help in the investigation of some aspects of the Mode 1 and Mode 2 characteristics shown in Table 2, primarily the academic versus application context and the variety of methodological approaches. Further work is needed to examine the extent to which IS research spans the continua of the other characteristics of homogeneity versus heterogeneity, autonomy versus reflexivity, and traditional versus novel quality control, and we believe that an evaluation of IS research on these criteria could prove interesting.

In particular, in the context of knowledge transfer to practice, which is claimed to be addressed by the Mode 2 attributes of heterogeneity, reflexivity, and novel quality control, it is also important to note that the focus of this study is on journal publications as the primary means of dissemination of research results: citations to proceedings of conferences are not included in the co-citation counts. In a field that is rapidly changing, conference proceedings are often a more timely indicator of new and emerging research interests, and are also often more accessible to practitioners. An examination of research themes in the major IS conference proceedings through the dual lenses of focus and diversity would be a worthwhile area of future research.

Finally, and consistent with Sidorova et al.'s (2008) findings, it is notable that, up to 2005, design science has not emerged as an independent factor, or even at the fine-grained level, as a research theme. We believe this is most likely because, as of 2005, no leading design science researchers had met the threshold for inclusion in the foundational author set. In the future, as more researchers publish articles on the application of design science methodologies, it is likely that some design scientists will join the foundational author set, and we may well see the emergence of another methodological factor—Design Science Thematic Miscellany.

## Conclusions

This study has drawn on the perspective of researchers across the breadth of the IS field to understand the key topics of interest in the field, and to identify continuities and changes over time. Over the 20-year period of the study, we have shown that IS researchers have maintained a sustained focus on a polycentric core of research areas: a thematic miscellany comprising research on development, implementation, and use of systems in various application domains; IS strategy and business outcomes; and group work and decision support. At the same time, we have demonstrated on-going diversity in research methods, topics, and contexts, with the increasing prominence of qualitative research approaches and new, emerging application domains including Internet and Internet users, interorganizational systems, virtual teams, and knowledge work.

Our examination of IS research through the dual lenses of focus and diversity provides a unifying approach, accommodating both sides of the IS debate. The trends over time reveal that IS is a healthy applied discipline, demonstrating diversity through researchers' flexible responses to a rapidly changing domain of interest, while still maintaining consistency in focus on the core concerns of the discipline. The view of dual focus and diversity presented here provides a pathway for the discipline to continue its development, meeting the demands of both the theoretical and practical arenas in which it operates. The key challenge for the IS discipline in the future is to continue to nurture the dual modality necessary to maintain the polycentric field structure indicative of a healthy applied social science discipline operating in a rapidly changing technological context.

## About the Authors

Hazel Taylor is an assistant professor at the Information School, University of Washington, Seattle. She holds a Ph.D. from Queensland University of Technology, Brisbane, Australia. Prior to joining the Information School, Dr. Taylor taught at the University of Waikato in New Zealand, and at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and conducted research in Hong Kong on risk management and tacit knowledge in IT projects. Her teaching and research focuses on IT project management and risk management, and information systems analysis and development, with an emphasis on tacit knowledge and decision making in these areas.

Stuart Dillon is a senior lecturer and chairperson of the Management Systems Department, University of Waikato, where he also received his Ph.D. and master's degrees. His research interests include electronic commerce with a particular interest in e-local government, decision problem structuring and descriptive decision making, and he has published in all of these areas. His doctoral research focused on understanding the naturalistic problem structuring processes of executives.

Melinda Van Wingen is a history specialist at the Everett Public Library in Everett, Washington. She holds a Master of Library and Information Science from the University of Washington's Information School and a Master of Arts in History from the University of Maryland at College Park.
