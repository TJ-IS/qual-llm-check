# The roles of social identity and dynamic salient group formations for ERP program management success in a postmerger context

Stefan K. Mueller<sup>1</sup> | Jan Mendling<sup>1</sup> | Edward W.N. Bernroider<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Institute for Information Business, WU Vienna, Vienna, Austria

<sup>2</sup> Institute for Information Management and Control, WU Vienna, Vienna, Austria

Correspondence

Stefan K. Mueller, Institute for Information Business, WU Vienna, Vienna, Austria. Email: skm@skmis.com

## Abstract

Large enterprise resource planning (ERP) programs and their critical success factors (CSFs) have been subject to intensive research in recent years. One of the key challenges is their complexity in terms of their duration, context, and social dynamics. The aim of this study is to investigate these matters with the help of an interpretative case study using the coding techniques of the grounded theory method. We extend extant ERP research by introducing a new theoretical perspective that helps to clarify how stakeholder perceptions and CSFs are interrelated, and evolve throughout the life cycle of a large ERP implementation conceptualized as a program in a postmerger context. Using Social Identity Theory as a metatheory for interpretation, we find that different perceptions of opposing salient groups are particularly important. Our result offers a dialectic process view of ERP program implementation success, which (1) considers the different perceptions of salient groups (2) at different points in time (phases) and (3) proposes that a low perceptual fit in relation to a specific CSF contributes to program failure as opposed to (4) a high perceptual fit in relation to the respective CSF, which contributes to program success. We offer a new theoretical lens and propositions for future work highlighting the importance of salient groups and their CSF perceptions for ERP program success. Practitioners can use our findings as a guide towards increasing the probability of success throughout the course of their ERP program life cycles.

## KEYWORDS

case study, critical success factors (CSFs), ERP implementation, ERP program, intergroup relations, Social Identity Theory

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

An enterprise resource planning (ERP) system is a standard software package that integrates various business functions and stores data in an integrated database. Benefits such as cost savings (Holland & Light, 1999; Seddon, Calvert, & Yang, 2010) tend to be as substantial as implementation costs. The average cost of an ERP implementation has been \$6.5 million with an average duration of 16.1 months. Fifty‐four per cent of all projects have exceeded their projected budgets, while 72% have gone past their planned durations (Panorama Consulting Solutions, 2014). On persistent problem in managing ERP implementations is associated with stakeholder influences and changing perceptions. For example, already in the initiation of the ERP implementation project, stakeholder groups tend to affect th design and course of implementation to their advantage (Bernroider, 2013; Boonstra, 2006). Further complicating implementation is dynamically changing stakeholder perceptions over the life cycle of ERP implementation, which can be related with resource and change conflicts negatively affecting implementation success (Bernroider, 2013; Besson & Rowe, 2001).

While the extant literature on critical success factors (CSFs) of ERP implementation offers important guiding principles and activities that should be regarded by managers to reach related obiectives (Bullen & Rockart. 1981: Caralli, Stevens, Willke, & Wilson, 2004), prior studies insufficiently clarify the effects of existing social networks, social identities, and stakeholder perceptions that alter during the extensive ERP implementation process (Schwarz & Watson, 2005). This calls for shifting the focus of research from the outcomes as a result of the perceptions on the interplay between human action and technology (Leonardi, 2011; Schwarz & Watson, 2005) towards the dynamics of evolving stakeholder perceptions (Bernroider, 2013; Besson & Rowe, 2001; Grainger, McKay, & Marshall, 2009). We assume that dynamically changing perceptions and social influences are major determinants of ERP implementation success. Additionally, we believe that a specific focus on program management considering a set of underlying projects with the common goal of ERP implementation provides additional clarity. Only recently, ERP implementations have been organized as programs in order to coordinate competition on resources and the interre lations between projects (Jiang, Chang, Chen, Wang, & Klein, 2014; Seddon et al., 2010). Such program management and its governance mechanisms are an important locus for studying the course of implementation and the related organizational change (Cabinet Office, 2011: Pellegrinelli, 1997: Proiect Management Institute [PMI], 2008), Only scant information systems (IS) research to date has explicitly considered programs in the ERP implementation contex (Chang, Jiang, Klein, & Wang, 2014; Jiang et al., 2014; Ribbers & Schoo, 2002; Seidel, 2009). Against this backdrop, it is of particular importance to investigate links between both perspectives, ERP program management and the dynamics of stakeholder group formations, and consider implications in regard to CSFs, which to date have not been con sidered in the related IS literature

This study aims at filling this gap in literature by focusing on the dynamics of perceptions on CSFs in different phases of a large‐scale ERP implementation represented by a contemporary program management design in a postmerger integration context. We highlight the importance of considering the dialectic process nature of ERP programs, investigating how the temporal aspect of perceptions aimed at CSFs changes the trajectory of the program and its implementation success. Our contributions are propositions and their implications for explaining the change of implementation success throughout the course of a large‐scale ERP implementation. We rely on an interpretive single case study supported by an adapted version of the grounded theory method (Sarker, Lau, & Sahay, 2001; Strauss & Corbin, 1998) and consider the multiplicity of involved groups from a social identity perspective and poten tially changing stakeholder perceptions. Specifically, we address the following research questions in the context of a large-scale ERP program, which took place between 2010 and 2013 at the leading Austrian Telecommunicatior Provider A1 Telekom Austria (A1/TA): (1) What is the role of dynamic stakeholder group formations and their perceptions in relation to CSFs over the course of the ERP program? (2) How can diverging perceptions between stakeholder groups be resolved to foster ERP program success? Using Social Identity Theory (SIT) (Tajfel & Turner 1986) as a metatheory for interpretation, we argue that different perceptions of opposing salient stakeholder groups which can develop informally in relation to CSFs, are particularly important and inhibit ERP program success, and propose remediations to resolve these opposing views to the advantage of the ERP program. Next, we elaborate on prior research in relation to ERP program management with a focus on CSFs and stakeholder perceptions. Then, we introduce SIT for interpreting the dynamic interplay of CSFs and stakeholder perceptions. Our interpretation of the case leads to the description of research propositions. Finally, we discuss implications for research and practice before we conclude.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | ERP program management

The concept of program management has been developed in recent years for large‐scale projects (eg, Cabinet Office 2011; PMI, 2008), in particular to support control objectives over IT project management (Bernroider & Ivanov, 2011). Pioneering research on the specifics of program management of ERP implementations was conducted by Ribbers and Schoo (2002, p. 45), who define a program as “a portfolio of projects, defining a set of related activities both for the IT and the business side, that have defined goals and benefits, and need to be controlled as a whole.” In their view, a program is the controlling instance of the transformation process. This definition is very comprehensive and includes various aspects of an ERP program.

Enterprise resource planning programs are a recent and complex phenomenon, and several insights have been reported in the literature. Ribbers and Schoo (2002) describe how different contextual factors such as the degree of change, the number of sites included, and the degree of concurrency shape the design of the program. Seide (2009) develops a model intended to predict the probability of a successful ERP program but without considering the actual implementation phases. Markus, Tanis, and van Fenema (2000a) use the term multisite ERP implementa tion, which shares characteristics with a program, and describe elements such as architecture, business strategy, software configuration, technical platform, and management execution. Grainger et al. (2009) use the term “umbrella project” for a series of implementation projects, with a dedicated single overall project coordinator and multiple project leaders. A recent study by Jiang et al. (2014) highlights the positive effect of shared commitments and understandings of program goals between key managers as one specific program‐related aspect. Chang et al. (2014) have a similar focus using a case study approach. Apart from these contributions, ERP program management was rarely explicitly considered in the past.

The life cycle of ERP programs is complex and often undergoes various changes and evolutions, including organizational change and the adaptation of business processes. Lyytinen and Newman (2015) stress that the majority o previous research explaining the success of ERP implementations only considers antecedents and static elements of the implementation context. Largely ignored is the role of process events in explaining the outcomes. An exception here is the account of Parr and Shanks (2000), which considers CSFs during different phases of two investigated implementation projects; nonetheless, they focus on projects not programs. Markus and Tanis (2000) explicitly con sider different implementation phases and stress that the starting conditions may not remain the same over the life cycle of the implementation. Grainger et al. (2009) discuss two implementation attempts and highlight that the assessment of outcomes depends on the point in time of observation. We therefore conclude that a view spanning the entire program life cycle is beneficial for a comprehensive view of an ERP program and might describe its course bevond the formally defined life cycle phases

## 2.2 | Stakeholder perceptions and CSFs

In comparison with single projects, ERP programs bear a higher risk of diverging perceptions, because they usuall involve a large set of diverse stakeholders (Cabinet Office, 2011). Lyytinen and Newman (2015) emphasize the critica influence that divergent actions of different stakeholder groups might have on the ERP implementation process and outcomes. Their statement is consistent with the views of other authors (Bernroider, 2013; Besson & Rowe, 2001; Grainger et al., 2009; Markus, Axline, Petrie, & Tanis, 2000b) who stress the importance of stakeholder actions and perceptions for a successful implementation. For these reasons, authors including Sarkis and Sundarraj (2003 emphasize the need to appropriately manage user expectations and satisfaction for a large‐scale ERP implementation.

In relation to CSFs, prior ERP research has looked at the consideration of stakeholder perceptions but is limited to specific stakeholder groups. Mostly, perceptions of managers are investigated like in the case provided by Nah, Zuckweiler, and Lau (2003) on CSFs and associated perceptions of chief information officers. A few studies deal with perceptions of different stakeholder groups. A prominent account in that direction is from Amoako‐Gyampah (2004), later replicated in China (Lin & Rohm, 2009), on differences between the perceptions of decision‐makers and end‐users<sup>1</sup> on implementation factors. He investigates seven factors for potential differences in perceptions. The largest differences in perceptions were found with regard to shared beliefs and a shared sense about the project, whether the user satisfaction for the new ERP system is higher than for the legacy system it replaced and the appropriateness of project communication. Understanding differences in these perceptions is described as the key for developing effective interventions (eg, training and communications) to help steering the project into a successfu direction (Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004).

Finney (2011) extends specifically the point on communication and the perceptions of four stakeholder groups (managers, users, IT staff, and consultants). She hypothesizes that without engagement and acceptance by stakeholders, it is unlikely that any change associated with an ERP introduction will deliver the promised benefits. Furthermore, she finds that particularly, the communication strategy requires a tailored approach to meet the stakeholder needs (Finney, 2011). This is in line with empirical results of Nandhakumar, Rossi, and Talvinen (2005) on perceptions of the ERP system by different stakeholder groups. Even if contextual factors and technology drift do not directly related to ERP success, they influence the project trajectory (Nandhakumar et al., 2005).

All these accounts document the importance of stakeholder perceptions in relation to CSFs and ERP programs. However, they are limited in four dimensions (Table 1). They either (1) focus only on a limited amount of stakeholder groups (eg, managerial perceptions), or (2) investigate a limited number of CSFs, (3) follow a static instead of a dynamic approach, and (4), most importantly, assume homogeneous perceptions of stakeholders within stakeholder groups. Lastly, all accounts do not relate their research to ERP programs that are typically larger or longer as traditional implementation projects and include a higher number of stakeholder groups. This means that it is not clear from prior research whether the dialectic process of ERP implementation is restricted to diverging views of exactl the formal groups identified in these prior studies and how stable these diverging views are.

We observed in practice that such homogeneity is rather the exception and that views are often not consistent. This is presumably a major challenge for an ERP program. We address this research gap using the salient group concept taken from the SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). Furthermore, we consider the dynamic structuring of stakeholders into salient groups beyond the usually considered formal groups. The identification of these salient groups is grounded in their perceptions of CSF at a specific phase of the ERP program

## 2.3 | Social Identity Theory

During our coding procedure (outlined in the next section), we realized that different perceptions in relation to CSFs emerged and led to social classifications as these perceptions were shared by different persons. Hence, the usage of SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986) appeared to be useful for interpretation of these phenomena, which helps to explain socia identification and salient group identification. We found from the data that group perceptions were shaping th outcome of a CSF and that our core category “stakeholder and communication management” was important to explain how and why these perceptions changed.

Social Identity Theory defines a group as a collection of individuals who perceive themselves (and are perceived by others) as members of the same social category (a group). The members share emotional involvement and a socia consensus in relation to the evaluation of their informal group membership (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). “One's identity is an amalgam of loosely coupled identities,” and one person might define himself or herself in terms of a most salient social identity (Ashfort & Mael, 1989, p. 30). This is what is also referred to as in‐group. Salient groups are formed according to three main principles of SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986, p. 16):

1. Individuals strive to achieve or to maintain a positive social identity.

2. Positive social identity is based to a large extent on favourable comparisons that can be made between the ingroup and some relevant out‐groups; the in‐group must be perceived as positively differentiated or distinct from the relevant out‐groups.

3. When social identity is unsatisfactory, individuals will strive either to leave their existing group and join som more positively distinct group or to make their existing group more positively distinct

This means that the most salient social identity is often connected with different perceptions between in‐groups an out‐groups in the given social environment. According to SIT, such social classification implies that the definition of the self and others is to a large degree relational and comparative in terms of certain idiosyncratic characteristics (Tajfe & Turner, 1986). In our context of ERP programs, the most social classification is investigated in relation to a particular CSF in different phases of the ERP program. The resulting salient identity (or perceptions) does not necessarily reflect a formal group within the organization. This means that not only the CSFs evolve but also the social classification in terms of salient groups themselves, since they are a product of the underlying dynamically changing perceptions

In terms of prior literature, the viability of our theoretical lens is exemplified in a study by Schwarz and Watson (2005), to the best of our knowledge the only study in the ERP context that explains intergroup relationships with SIT. They investigate the role of perceptions on IT‐enabled change and how affiliations to salient groups alter as the situation changes. Critical success factors are not considered. Our lens is also related to Boonstra's (2006) work on stakeholder salience theory<sup>2</sup> (Mitchell et al., 1997), which investigates the change of perceived problems and interests of different stakeholder groups in relation to an ERP implementation. He finds empirical evidence for dynamics of power relations between stakeholder groups. Again, CSFs are not considered.

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

To answer our research questions, we conducted a single in‐depth case study and adopted an interpretive epistemology, which assumes that our understanding of reality is gained through social constructions in a complex world of lived experience (Díaz Andrade, 2009; Walsham, 1995). As interpretive researchers, we are thus presenting “interpretations of other people's interpretations” (Walsham, 1995, p. 78) and seek to reveal the complexity of interests and perspectives of stakeholders in their sense making processes. This interpretive philosophical stance was particularly useful for our purposes around capturing dynamic, context‐, and time‐dependent social phenomena (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991) and to access the subjective and intersubjective meanings of individuals as they interact with the world around them (Klein & Myers, 1999). Interpretivism has become widely accepted in information systems research (eg, Berente & Yoo, 2012; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). In our analysis, we particularly consider temporally evolving phenomenona in relation to stakeholder perceptions unfolding over the lifecycle of the ERP program. We thus apply process thinking (Langley, Smallman, Tsoukas, & Van de Ven, 2013; Mohr, 1982) by developing a causa explanation of the sequence of actions and events assigned to three implementation stages and create a story about how and why ERP program implementation evolved as a result of the temporal ordering and interaction of numerous events. By focusing on how and why, we were also able to contribute to practice (Bizzi & Langley, 2012) and secure quality in terms of plausibility of the story and the argument (Myers, 2009).

## 3.1 | Data collection

As interpretive researchers, we attempted to understand the perspectives of stakeholders regarding actions and events, which took place during the ERP program at A1/TA. and aspirations of themselves and other participants Consequently, interviews were the primary data source (Walsham, 1995). An interpretive case study is particularly useful in identifying how perceptions and actions of stakeholders are changing over time and to understand why they act in the way they do (Boonstra, 2006). Table 2 depicts the interview schedule and the roles of the interviewees within the ERP program at A1/TA. It also shows salient group memberships for the precrisis phase, which we will discuss in the following sections. We conducted 12 semistructured interviews (besides internal conversations and eight informal interviews) with an average duration of 1 hour. Our single point of contact was the program manager with whom we conducted our first and last interview. He proposed other representative participants for the program from different stakeholder groups. As concepts emerged in a constant comparison of data and analysis (Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Urquhart, Lehmann, & Myers, 2010), the interview questions were posed flexibly and the original guideline served primarily as an orientation device. In order not to lead the interviewees, we asked generic questions. For example, to understand how ERP success was perceived, we asked: “How did you perceive the success of the program at its various stages?” If there were instances of mismatches between perceptions between stakeholders or changes over time, we then queried the reasons. We considered social classifications by, eg, asking “To which extent did you perceive a common identity at the different stages of the program?” If there were such groups, we investigated their characteristics. the location of the self and others, and comparisons. We also accounted for organizational culture and asked: “Which role did potential cultural differences play throughout the course of the program?” These example questions also reflect our constant focus on time‐related phenomena. This helped us obtain a perspective on the dynamics of perceptional patterns and whether people were divergent or convergent in their perceptions over the stages concerned.

All interviews were recorded and fully transcribed (230‐page transcript) and added to a hermeneutic unit of th computer program ATLAS.ti7, which we used for our qualitative data analysis. By the time we coded our seventh interview, we had already reached a point of theoretical sufficiency (Dey, 1999; Díaz Andrade, 2009), ie, the point at which the same themes are observed in data analysis (Guest, Bunce, & Johnson, 2006), and therefore deem the number of 12 interviews as sufficient for our case (see further details on data collection and use in Appendix F). In addi tion, the interview data were supplemented with other internal program sources such as meeting protocols presentations, risk registers, diagrams, and public data as proposed by Walsham (2006).

## 3.2 | Data analysis and coding procedure

In line with the intent of our study, we implemented a less rigid, more interpretive variant (Sarker et al., 2001) of the Straussian grounded theory method (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). While this adaption keeps close to the Straussian coding procedures, it also uses integrative memos to describe relationships between categories and allows for including alternative metatheoretical frameworks instead of the “paradigm model” from the Straussian method during the selective coding of data. The use of such an adapted and more flexible approach is in line with recent and established reviews in information systems literature showing that the grounded theory method is subject to idiosyncratic interpretations and flexible deployment (Matavire & Brown, 2013; Seidel & Urquhart, 2013). To ensure rigour<sup>3</sup> in our implementation of the research method, we followed the guidelines for conducting grounded theory studies in information systems of Urquhart et al. (2010), an account that bears many general recommendations and basic principles inherent to the original variant (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), and consider also the subtleties of our adapted approach (Sarker et al., 2001). Whereas the first three guidelines deal with the degree of conceptualization necessary for good theory building, the final two guidelines compare the outcome of the first three guidelines with existing theories with the aim of theoretical integration (Urquhart et al., 2010). All three steps of the coding process were discussed and aligned during a large number of sessions between the authors. In the next section, we turn to our case, the ERP pro gram at A1/TA.

Three researchers were involved with the coding. The first author conducted the initial coding. Seven sessions were conducted throughout the coding phase with all three researchers, in which codes and alternative interpreta tions were discussed and resolved. First, we begin with open coding and label data chunks referred to as open codes (see examples of frequencies in Appendix A). Second, during axial coding, we build major categories by bundling th data chunks of step 1 into categories and relate them to each other through (1) a hierarchical, graphical representa tion of the concepts (see an example in Appendix B) and (2) describing their relationships in integrative memos (see examples in Appendix D). Third, during selective coding and further iterations in the continuously ongoing data anal ysis process, we identified that perceptions played an important role in the case and we used the SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986) as a metatheory for interpreting the data and for creating the storyline (Sarker et al., 2001). Finally, we chose the category “stakeholder and communication management” as our core category, as this category closely related to the perceptions of different stakeholder groups. We related the other categories to “stakeholder and communication management” and checked how the perceptions of stakeholders changed (or remained constant) over the course of the program, thus shaping the CSFs perceptions. In order to accomplish this, we did further iterations and went back to open and axial coding with a particular consideration on perceptions. We looked in relation to which CSFs the per ceptions changed, and how and why this happened. This was followed by a new round of selective coding

## 4 | THE CASE AT A1/TA

This section presents the results of our interpretive case study of an ERP program at A1/TA. We describe contextual information, the social groups involved in the program, and their social identities. Then, we discuss the different phases of the program: implementation attempt 1 (precrisis phase) was characterized by different perceptions. After a consolidation (crisis), the perceptions in relation to the CSFs converged and led to shared perceptions during imple mentation attempt 2 (postcrisis). Finally, we present our interpretation of the case and develop a model that relate the phases of the program to the perceptions of salient groups on CSFs.

## 4.1 | Contextual information

We conducted our interpretive case study between July 2013 and April 2014 within A1/TA, the Austrian subsidiary of the Telekom Austria Group. The group operates in eight countries in Central and Eastern Europe, with almost 23 million customers, 16 000 employees, and revenues of approximately 4 billion Euro in 2014 (Telekom Austria AG, 2015). The largest market operated by A1/TA has 5 4 million customers for mobile services and 2.3 million customers for landline services, 8600 employees, and revenues of about EUR 2.5 billion in 2014 (A1/TA AG, 2015). The industry is highly competitive and was marked by consolidations in the previous years. A1/TA was founded in 2010 as a ful service provider when the previously independent sister company Mobilkom Austria AG (mobile services) was merged into Telekom Austria (landline services). A1/TA is only operating in the Austrian market and is a subsidiar of the Telekom Austria Group. The ERP program at A1/TA served as a high priority postmerger integration initiative sponsored by the board.

The ERP program at A1/TA encompassed several related projects (Table 3) and spanned a period of 2.5 years. In total, 600 persons (including testing staff and 100 external consultants) were involved in the program. The ERP anc reporting systems were intended to serve 5000 users with more than 700 interfaces to other systems. The main goa was the development of a new ERP system where the processes of the legacy systems were either harmonized (eg, in the functional area Financials, the total number of processes were reduced by 50%, from 1200 to 600) or technically consolidated (eg, Logistics). The same procedure was intended in the reporting area with a new common user interface. A further harmonization was planned for later system releases, yet the conception of future releases was already part of the ERP program The proiects were accompanied by a separate change management proiect and eventually a documentation project was also affiliated within the program. The numbers reflect how great the challenge of establishing a successful program was for A1/TA.

## 4.2 | Social identities and groups shaping the ERP program

The ERP program involved different stakeholders and groups of stakeholders. It is important to note that such stakeholder groups can be either formal groups that are anchored in the organization chart of A1/TA or salient groups that informally develop based on shared perceptions on specific CSFs.

Figure 1 depicts a hierarchical view of the formal groups in the ERP program at A1/TA. It shows a simplified view of the different formal groups involved in the program and assigned to a level; note that an individual is always part of a formal group. Nevertheless, it is important to stress that a formal group may contain individuals sharing the same perceptions, but this is not necessarily the case. Salient groups in our model are characterized by the shared percep tions of individuals (representing their most salient social identities) in relation to this CSF. As we will see, these per ceptions of the different groups allow us to explain how the perceptions shaped the perceptions of CSFs and th salient groups themselves

On the highest level, Figure 1 shows stakeholder groups that manage the strategy, enterprise architecture, the portfolio, and the corresponding program sponsors. On the next level, there is the program management group (at A1/TA, it includes, among a dedicated program team, the project managers) and the steering committee. Below are the different projects organized in different streams/slots (depending on the functional area), the internal and external IT. Furthermore, due to the postmerger phase, the organizational cultures of A1 and TA still played a role as informal groups, mainly on the stream level. Note that not all groups can be clearly assigned to one level. eg “External IT” (depicted as dotted box) is on the one hand part of the projects and the program, but on the other hand also part of the environment, or the program sponsors, which interface the program and the organizational level.

To illustrate the difference between formal groups and salient groups, we use one example. First, salient groups can be bigger than formal groups. The salient group “Basis,” consisting of the streams, internal and external IT, shared certain perceptions regarding the CSF “Realistic Planning of Time Schedule.” This led to the emergence of a salient group consisting of different stakeholder groups sharing the same perceptions in relation to a CSF. Second, salient groups can be smaller than formal groups. When different perceptions exist within one stakeholder group, the members of the stakeholder group could belong to different salient groups. In this way, the concept of salient groups over comes limitations of previous research that consider only formal groups. The salient group concept allows us to distinguish divergences in perceptions of CSFs beyond the boundaries of the formal stakeholder group membership

## 4.3 | Implementation attempt 1 (precrisis)—low fit of perceptions

In this part, we discuss perceptual fit in relation to three different CSFs marked by a low fit of perceptions and on CSF characterized by a high positive fit in the precrisis phase. We relate the perceptions of salient groups to a particular CSF and identify the overall fit for this phase, which finally led to the failed implementation attempt 1.

## 4.3.1 | Low fit of perceptions: Ensure Realistic Planning of Time Schedule

The intended goal of this CSF was the definition of an ambitious but realistic time schedule. It is not important to evaluate if the time schedule was in fact realistic, but rather, we want to highlight how the low fit of perceptions with regard to this CSF led to the formation of two opposing salient groups (Believers and Skeptics). Table 2 also illustrates salient group memberships of the interviewees pertaining to this CSF and implementation stage.

After the legal merger of the previously independent companies Mobilkom and Telekom to A1/TA in 2010, the new company consisted of two different organizational cultures with two different ERP systems still in place. In this context, the ERP program was, besides the technical aspects, a high priority postmerger integration initiative in which the program sponsors prescribed an ambitious implementation schedule. Most program and project managers across the two organizational cultures perceived this plan as doable and therefore initially committed to this goal. According to SIT, one's identity is an amalgam of (loosely) coupled identities and that particular social identities are activated by relevant settings (Ashfort & Mael, 1989). The newly formed social identity could be described as the coalition of Believers with a common positive perception regarding the time schedule. The next statement from a project managers (Q1) shows that managers at different levels of the ERP program hierarchy shared common perceptions

Q1: The initial go‐live date was defined strictly by the board, CTO and I think also CFO for the 1st of May 2012. The program manager and I committed to this date, as well as the project managers. (ID9: Technical coordinator program, project manager ASAP, 206 ff.)

This early perception of the Believers with respect to the feasibility of the time schedule was not shared by other stakeholders, who together conflicted with the Believers and could be assigned to a newly initiated and opposing salient group (“Skeptics”). Members of the project implementation teams and stream leads at the very beginning o the program seriously doubted the go‐live date. Opposing views were engrained in micropractices of translating and overcoding (Rouleau, 2005): Believers spoke of a realistic, but critical schedule, while Skeptics overcoded their perception of the schedule to be unrealistic with a metaphoric use of the term sporting schedule, which is used in

German to refer to overly ambitious. The following statement by an ASAP stream reflects these concerns and, additionally, emphasizes the intergroup comparisons, which are endemic in SIT.

Q2: But the original go‐live date in May, which was initially planned...when I saw the plan the first time I thought anyway, this not realistic …. The [initial] go‐live date was specified by the program sponsors, and the program manager tried to stick to this date. (ID5: Stream leader, stream 1, functional area 1, 1322 ff.)

The following statement (Q3) confirms similar perceptions existed early on also within the project BSAP and within functional areas of the line organization resulting in a further strengthening of the salient group “Skeptics.” Since th go‐live date directed by the management coalition was tacitly accepted, the salient group was also given a certain tem poral continuity for its firm establishment before the disparities were made explicit allowing the conflict to break out

Q3: The line management was too weak, right from the start … nobody could resist the unrealistic time schedule. The CTO wanted an unrealistic time schedule, that's why he got one …. Because nobody could say clearly: “this is not feasible what you want.” (ID2: Technical coordinator of BSAP, 1095 ff.)

Retrospectively, the management coalition, partially Believers, accepted the critical view of their out‐group (“Skep tics”) on the feasibility of the time schedule. It is interesting to note that the interviewee still justified his in‐group's (Believers) perceptions, which turned out to be wrong. We offer two possible explanations. Based on SIT, this is an indi cation of favourable bias towards the in-group he belonged to and also a matter of self-esteem. The other explanation is that the individual has internalized distinct values and attitudes, which, again according to SIT, does not conflict with the Believers and Skeptics joining into one group once their perceptions (and therefore the group barriers) have been harmonized. Social identification is distinguishable from internalization. In other words, the same internalized values an attitudes are not necessarily associated with each members of a certain salient group (Ashfort & Mael, 1989).

Q4: Well, it was certainly ambitious, and in large projects it will be always like that, that not everyone is convinced at all times about the feasibility of the time schedule. Eventually, it turned out that it [the plan] was not doable, after all it was not wrong …. One has to draw on a planned assumption, and it was according to a project plan and also the target of the sponsors. (ID1: Program manager, interview 2, 20 ff.)

It is important to note that the Skeptics also included persons on the directions level. The next interviewee puts blame on his out‐group of Believers for the failure, which highlights another aspect of SIT. Social identification is here maintained ex post in regard to the implementation schedule.

Q5: I believe it is a major mistake to make unrealistic time schedules…also in regard to the staff perspectives and their motivation. If I say I will never make it, then I have a different engagement or non‐engagement. I mean unrealistic time schedules are very serious sources of failure. (ID6: Director, functional area 2, 794 ff.)

The next statement (Q6) again illustrates the social categorization and comparisons between the two opposing salient groups (“basis” and “we”) with their different perceptions, which according the interviewee should have been addressed by more effective communication strategies. All two aspects are important in considering social identification through SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). The actual reason for the missing belief in these CSFs by the Skeptics was i particular attributed to a lack of schedule acceptance.

Q6: The basis [stream leaders and implementation teams, that is the skeptics] did not believe that this date is feasible right from the start … you need to convey this plausibly to the basis, otherwise the date won't be accepted. And we did not manage to communicate this properly. Therefore, we lost the basis for a certain time. That was one reason why we had to skip the first go‐live date. (ID9: Technical coordi nator program, project manager ASAP, 210 ff.)

Note that the last statement reflects a strong separation of the self and others, which conforms to the relationa and comparative attributes of SIT (Ashfort & Mael, 1989, p. 21) and that the opposing views were endured for months until the crisis and reflection stage. Taken together, we explain the two opposing groups (Believers and Skeptics) with the differing perceptions in relation to the CSF “Ensure Realistic Planning of Time Schedule”. Despite the fact that each of these two salient groups shares members of similar status or position in the ERP program and organizational hierarchies, this positioning was not the contrasting factor explaining the formation and later resolution of these social identities. This is consistent with SIT stating that one's identity is an amalgam of (loosely) coupled identities and that particular social identities are activated and deactivated by relevant settings.

## 4.3.2 | Low fit of perceptions: Alignment of Harmonized/Consolidated Business Processes

One major goal of the ERP program was the definition of harmonized and technically consolidated business processes. Since the company applied a greenfield approach, the intended alignment was even more difficult to achieve. Also, in this regard, the perceptions of the salient groups (salient groups: A1 Division vs TA Division and Stream X vs Stream Y) with regard to this CSF resulted in a low positive fit of perceptions for this dimension. Thus, seen togethe with its perceptions, the target of this CSF was not met during this phase.

From the management perspective, it was still evident that the two premerger worlds continued to exist in the program. Instead of the new postmerger group A1/TA, the premerger groups Mobilkom (A1) and Telekom (TA) were clearly dominant in many minds and thus the most dominating salient groups. The strategy from an SIT perspective t positively differentiate the in‐group from the out‐group was a social competition (Tajfel & Turner, 1986), which mad process agreements difficult. Such a retention of premerger identities and the maintenance of their values and practices were also stressed by Drori, Wrzesniewski, and Ellis (2013). Consequently, the perceptions were not compatibl with each other and the future to‐be processes.

Q7: Indeed, two worlds collide, two worlds which are different also from their processes, but also differen from the mindset of the people, … and those two mindsets one recognizes how they collide …. … [A]ll [both] present their positions, all [both] are right, this is exactly the dilemma, all [both] are right, all [both] had good reasons in history to develop their processes in that way, and until they find themselves again in the new process, find themselves together, this is an effort one must not underestimate. (ID2: Technica coordinator of BSAP, 182 ff.)

In regard to this CSF, the situation for A1/TA was clearly challenging due the postmerger context. In line wit prior postmerger research, cultural differences between premerger identities (Stahl & Voigt, 2008) and status‐based concerns (Hogg & Terry, 2000) created resistance and tensions in regard to the harmonization and consolidation of end-to-end business processes. Retrospectively, more effort should have been dedicated to this special situation as the statement of the change manager below illustrates. The roles of the premerger identities (Mobilkom A1 vs Telekom TA) in the new postmerger identity and their reflections into the new system (functional areas vs IT) were not clear in the beginning.

Q8: However, I believe that certain things would have been necessary earlier … bringing together func tional areas and IT right from the start … you see? Because misunderstandings persisted for a long time … “what is your world, what is the Telekom world, what is the Mobilkom world, how do your processes work, how do my processes work” … this cost us an incredible amount of time … we should have invested more in this common understanding. (ID7: Project manager, CSAP, change project, 2:8)

This is an indication that too little attention was giving to micropractices such as disciplining and justifying change (Rouleau, 2005) in the precrisis phase. The challenge to align the new common processes was made more difficult because of a missing agreement on process ownerships especially considering the end‐to‐end view. As a integrated system was required, this tension about responsibilities between different salient groups (Stream X v Stream Y) impacted major harmonized and consolidated processes likewise.

Q9: Topics across Streams! This is related to process accountability, who is responsible, where does it [my accountability] start and where does it [my accountability] end? [……] In regard to process governance …. there is not really something defined, who is the process owner and mainly responsible? … We had functional areas like WILLNOTMENTION1 and WILLNOTMENTION2, they checked everything [unit tests], but that the entire process across streams works … was one of the main challenges. (ID3: Member of IT core team, member of program management office, 137 ff.)

We conclude that this lack of a common understanding (in relation to the merger and accountability criteria) and the low positive fit of perceptions with regard to the harmonization and consolidation of business processes had negative effects on the ERP program's success.

## 4.3.3 | Low fit of perceptions: Ensure Data Migration/Accuracy

The major goal of the data migration was to provide a common database for A1/TA. Only necessary data (eg, open items) should have been migrated so that harmonized and consolidated processes could run properly in the new system (according to the salient group IT as opposed to the salient group Business). This included the merging of mas ter data including a new chart of account, data cleansing, and a reduction of master data. A lot of conversion rules, interfaces, and custom developments are needed to be considered. For this CSF, we also recognized the low fit of perceptions, which negatively impacted the ambitious goal and led to a low fit of perceptions for this dimension of our model. Thus, the target of this CSF was not met during this phase.

The complex data migration was a huge challenge for the program team, and it turned out very quickly that dif ferent perceptions regarding the data migration worsened the situation. During the first data migration test cycles, these different views had already manifested into a significant backlog. The migration tools that were expected to be technically mature did not bring the planned results (salient groups: Internal vs External). Furthermore, the process views could not be sufficiently considered with the available data quality and the intended migration scenario. Thus the functional areas were very insecure regarding the workability of the envisioned system set‐up

Q10: We were always one data migration test cycle behind. The vendor expected a technical migration, we expected already a first test migration … these two perceptions did not fit each other … we expected that they provide technically mature migration tools, which was not the case …. The functional areas real ized that not all the data will be migrated … and they asked themselves: “how can I work with that data?” (ID9: Technical coordinator program, project manager ASAP)

Three months before the go‐live was planned the situation did not change for the better. Again, the ongoing data migration problems were stressed.<sup>4</sup>

Q11: The expectations for Mock1 [Data Migration Cycle 1] were not met. The major reason for the thus far resulting delays in the data delivery, are attributed to not realized migration rules within the migration tools of Vendor 2. (Management meeting, minutes of 03 February 2012)

Furthermore, it turned out that the amount of data intended to be migrated was not sufficient to serve the business needs. The functional areas (salient group Business) expected more data than the ERP groups (salient grou IT) initially intended to migrate into the new system. These two contrary positions of the two salient groups did not reconcile and contributed strongly to the problems regarding the data migration.

Q12: The data migration, originally with only compulsorily needed data for the new fiscal year, respectively master data, we needed to extend it since more historical data was necessary to keep existing processes alive …. …[T]his was also the crux why we needed the postponement. We saw that the migration as required in the specification was not sufficient, and would have led to major problems. (ID9: Technica Coordinator of the program, project manager ASAP, 109 ff.)

## 4.4 | Crisis and reflection

The crisis was triggered due to a lack of ERP program progress and the overall realization that the actual can no longer be aligned with the plan. Tracing back the reasons in a processural view, the crisis and reflection stage coul have been anticipated early on from the negative perceptions of opposing salient groups. For example, the following statement (Q15) shows that the negative perceptions of the Skeptics regarding the feasibility of the envisioned time schedule have become explicit in meetings, which in turn negatively impacted on ERP program commitment and consequently implementation progress:

Q15: It is for sure negative …. When one loses trustworthiness. I mean, if you pick plans on the wall, and nobody believes that they are feasible...in the precrisis the [project] staff looked at the plans and they thought, “nice plan, nobody believes that, we cannot do it, absolutely not realistic.” …[T]hen this has an impact on the motivation and the output … and the output declined before the crisis, the cancellation, sig nificantly. (ID7: Project manager CSAP, 47:12 ff.)

Additionally, the ERP program faced other conflicts due to opposing groups, eg, in terms of the alignment of harmonized and consolidated business processes (see Section 4.3.2) and data migration and accuracy (see Section 4.3.3 leading to the decision to cancel the originally intended go‐live date in May 2012.

In the course of the crisis and reflection stage, the overarching principle was to emphasize a common and given identity (that is, the ERP program team as a whole) through reshaping ERP program communication and decisionmaking. This overall group became salient due to inclusive strategies in communication and collectively deciding on effective remediations impacting CSFs. Table 6 depicts the remediations, which we deemed most important, and the respectively influenced CSFs. Different data migration and cutover scenarios were prepared and presented during a large buy‐in meeting with 26 relevant stakeholders (CFO, CTO, the program management and the project managers, directors of functional areas, and stream leaders). Additionally, program rooms were established, which again brought stakeholders together on a regular basis allowing for the swift exchange of vital information in one place and brainstorm together on remediations. These rooms also proofed valuable in resolving implementation issues in the next stage. These helped to develop what Rouleau (2005) calls disciplining micropractices. The following statement (Q16) illustrates the change in perceptions and subsequent social identification due to these actions

Q16: The crisis had a positive effect on the entire program. We used the time to reflect what went wrong, what we need to improve, where we need to change the structure, how we change our collaboration …. A new quality of collaboration …. The team found together…. (ID7: Project manager CSAP, 6:1)

In terms of actual remediations, the data migration was seen as the main challenge and therefore the migration team was reinforced with additional team members and an external migration manager. Furthermore, quality gates for the data migration test cycles were established. An issue‐tracking tool was also introduced to allow for a more systematic prioritization of incidents and effective reporting. Again, the main aim of these actions was to improve the collaboration between the groups and the communication flow, intertwined with continuous micropractices of justifving change (Rouleau. 2005) to the business units The achieved alignment of positive perceptions resulted ir a strengthened commitment and paved the path for a successful implementation attempt 2. The former intergroup differences in perceptions were effectively minimized

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Our dialectic process view of ERP implementation contributes to our understanding of the role played by dynamic salient group formations for ERP program management success in two major ways. First, we clarify the importance of diverging perceptions of CSFs of salient groups that can emerge at any time during ERP implementation. The qual itative analysis was designed to discover how stakeholders perceive ERP success, locate themselves in relation to these groups, and can change their social identities. We thereby add to prior related work that has considered static perceptions (eg, Finney, 2011; Lin & Rohm, 2009) or the role of diverging views of CSFs between formal stakeholder groups only (eg, Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004; Nah et al., 2003). Second, as an important further contribution, we highlight the dialectic process of resolving tensions in a postmerger integration from an ERP program perspective by emphasizing a common identity and reshaping communication and decision‐making. Prior research has insufficiently approached the complex implementation context in a process view and suggested resolving stakeholder tensions outside the ERP program perspective (eg, Besson & Rowe, 2001). We also provide specific remediations that have been related to Rouleau's (2005) micropractices. Next, we attempt to generalize by formulating propositions from our interpretations, apply the principle of abstraction and generalization to categories, and relate unique instances (A1/TA context) to ideas and concepts that may apply to multiple situations (Klein & Myers, 1999). Thus, we seek to increase the theory scope through integration of the substantive theory in a more formal theory (Urquhar et al., 2010). The given implications of our work should support further research on the dynamics of CSFs throughout the ERP program life cycle, management of different groups in the program, the application of SIT, and the operation of program management. In the last subsection, we note limitations.

## 5.1 | Salient groups and diverging perceptions

The importance of perceptions was stressed by several authors in the past: in the ERP context (eg, Bernroider, 2013; Besson & Rowe, 2001; Boonstra, 2006), in relation to mergers (eg, Drori et al., 2013; Schwarz & Watson, 2005), and as a relevant factor for success of an ERP implementation (Grainger et al., 2009). It is a contribution of our study to clarify the importanc of salient groups that can emerge at any time based on a low fit of CSF‐related perceptions. This perspective complement prior research on CSF perceptions of formal stakeholder groups (Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004; Finney, 2011; Lin & Rohm, 2009), which tend to take a more static view of stakeholder groups and perceptions. The observation of diverging views between managers and end‐users (eg, Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004; Nah et al., 2003) is accordingly only one specific instance of a low perceptual fit between salient groups in an ERP program. Based on our case study, we observed that a range of salient groups can form at any time of an ERP program when perceptions of relevant CSFs diverge.

Our results show that CSFs do not contribute to ERP program success, if there are differing underlying perceptions of stakeholders in regard to these CSFs leading to (opposing) salient group formations. This finding addresses missing considerations of prior studies that largely assume homogeneous perceptions of stakeholders within forma stakeholder groups (Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004; Besson & Rowe, 2001; Finney, 2011; Lin & Rohm, 2009; Lyytinen & Newman, 2015). This assumption does not hold in our case where perceptions can differ and change over time. Since time matters (Abbott, 2001; Grainger et al., 2009), we considered ERP program implementation as a process and dynamic salient group formation through SIT (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). The number of phases of this process, CSFs and salient groups is defined by the context and the underlying perceptions with regard to the CSFs. To more clearly operationalize and understand how to manage these perceptions, we have introduced perceptual fit to capture the deviation between perceptions in relation to one CSF of the respective salient groups. We believe that these ideas are open to further analyses and therefore can be potentially adapted beyond the case at A1/TA. The following propositions can be applied and tested regularly during all phases of the ERP program and relate to each CSF separately

Proposition: 1: In order to allow CSFs to contribute to ERP program implementation success, program management efforts need to ensure that for each CSF, there are no salient groups with members from heterogeneous formal stakeholder groups with negative perceptions regarding the respective CSF.

## 5.2 | Resolving divergent perceptions

Prior research has emphasized the difficulties of measuring ERP implementation success and the importance of an early identification and correction of problems (eg, Lyytinen & Newman, 2015). The A1/TA case supports this view: While the ERP implementation was far away from a success in the crisis phase, corrective measures brought it back on the path towards success. A precondition for the operationalization of Proposition 1 is to continuously assess if different perceptions in relation to a CSF exist or not. As long as there is a collective agreement in relation to a CSF with a fit of positive perceptions, the particular CSF contributes to implementation success and no immediate corrective actions are required in this regard. In case of a negative perception from at least one salient group, remediations are required, which should include corrective actions towards the CSF and effective stakeholder and communication management. The same is applied, if different salient groups are identified on the basis of their differing perceptions in relation to a specific CSF. Hence, opposing views require remediation to set corrective actions towards the respective CSF, which leads us to formulate our second proposition

Proposition: 2: As soon as one CSF in an ERP program implementation is associated with negative perceptions of one or more salient groups, remediations in relation to the particular CSF are required together with effective stakeholder and communication management to resolve the negative perceptions and thereby the opposing salient groups.

In this way, using terms of Van de Ven and Poole (1995), the teleological process of establishing a new ERP system by the help of a program appears to unfold as a dialectic process of thesis and antithesis of salient groups, which had to be reconciled in a thorough replanning as a synthesis. Dialectic process is the notion of perceptual fit. The presented case is generally interesting for SIT research, because it shows a case of resolving tensions. Augoustinos and Walker (1995, p. 117) state that tensions “are only likely to diminish or disappear when the dimensions and outcomes of intergroup comparisons are judged, especially by the unfavoured group, to be legitimate” and the dominant outgroup will be resistant to attempts of the negatively valued in‐group to alter its status. “A group does not relinquish its favoured position easily or voluntarily—for political reasons as well as reasons of maintaining a favourable social identity.” In the case of A1/TA, after the cancellation of implementation attempt 1, the dominant group “Skeptics” became “Believers,” thus altering their social identity and salient group. It is an interesting question for future research, in which circumstances such a resolution of two opposing salient groups is possible so quickly as observed in this case.

## 5.3 | Further implications

The findings from the case study have implications for clarifying when and how program management should be used beyond a classical project organization. The complexity and the heterogeneity of involved stakeholders are a driving factor calling for program management. The more stakeholders are involved, the more interdependencies between the different streams of work, and the more heterogeneous the participants, the more likely appears the chance of disagreement on specific CSFs. Program management is the function to monitor corresponding perceptions and trigger corrective action when required. Our results describe a low fit of perceptions as a concrete trigger for such corrections. Since CSF perceptions change and salient groups can emerge at any time, there is a need to continuously monitor CSF perceptions. This means that communication is not only needed from program management to all parties involved (eg, Amoako‐Gyampah, 2004; Finney, 2011) but also the other way around. Program management must be aware of emerging salient groups and take actions towards establishing a high fit of perceptions among all parties involved. This perspective also complements work emphasizing the importance of perceptions for goal commitment (Chang et al., 2014) and for conflict management (Jiang et al., 2014) in the context of ERP program management.

Finally, our case study also adds to the understanding of postmerger integration from an ERP program perspective While prior research on social identity processes in postmergers has in particular considered status‐based (Hogg & Terry, 2000) or cultural dimensions (Stahl & Voigt, 2008), which may resist or foster the merging of firms, we specifically demonstrate how ERP program management can manage the respective identity dynamics by shifting the focus on the perceptions of CSFs as described above. The premerger identities were clearly evident as the most dominating salient groups in failed implementation attempt 1 in relation to the necessary alignment of business processes. Onl through engaging in corrective actions related to CSFs and by improving collaboration between the groups and the communication flow, premerger identities captured by the salient groups were changed and boundaries resolved. Our findings are consistent with the view of flexible boundaries in postmerger integration and stakeholder perceptions that are fluid and in a constant change (Drori et al., 2013). We demonstrate that opposing salient groups that constitute legacy firm identities in a first stage of ERP implementation can be resolved and transformed into identities that are not rooted in legacy firms. These dynamic shifts of salient groups need to be understood and can be controlled b an ERP program to achieve a successful organizational change.

## 5.4 | Limitations

Beyond the general limitations of qualitative research, there are limitations that are specific to our case. First, we had access to the case based on interviews with persons who worked on the group lead and management levels of the ERP program. Second, the interviews mostly engaged the persons to give a retrospect account of the ERP program. Therefore, we know about the formation and dissolution of salient groups only through the account of these persons working on the management level. A consequence of this fact is that the confrontational nature in different phases o the program was not directly accessible. For future research, it is desirable to conduct quantitative longitudina research on CSF perceptions. In this way, the formation and dissolution of salient groups and their relation to ERP program success measures can be tested.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Our study has extensively engaged with SIT arguments to clarify how stakeholder perceptions of CSFs change throughout the life cycle of a large ERP program and may lead to the dynamic formation of opposing salient groups inhibiting program success. By engaging into corrective actions triggered by a negative fit of perceptions of these salient groups in regard to specific CSFs, we have demonstrated how an initially unsuccessful ERP implementation can be turned around. Enterprise resource planning implementation was only effective after the resolution of tensions that resulted in changed social identities and subsequently the resolution of formerly opposing salient groups. We have shown that the means for realizing the needed ongoing assessments and resolutions of different perceptions can be the ERP program, which is a contemporary management approach more and more used to achieve large‐scale ERP implementations. Among already established requirements, ERP program management should provide the essential function of including all stakeholders and continuously monitoring their perceptions and related dynamic salient group formations. Thus, we seriously question previous studies focusing on static and formal groups only in large‐scale ERP implementations where changing perceptions of stakeholders in regard to a multitude of CSFs endanger the difficult task of achieving the required organizational transformation. We hope that we have provided a fertile incubation ground for future IS research on ERP to build on, extend, or test our proposed ideas and for practitioners to increase the probability of imple mentation success from the viewpoints of program management and salient group formation

## APPENDIX D

## INTEGRATIVE MEMOS (CSF REALISTIC TIME FRAME)

## Axial coding: Realistic time frame (subcategory)

The first go‐live date was defined by the board and supported by the program management, program office, and project leaders (management coalition). The decision was perhaps influenced by the premise that only certain go‐liv dates were possible, regarding year‐end closing or quarter closing (functional director 2)

The first go‐live date was seen as absolutely not doable by many key players among “the Basis” (several statements …). Statement of program group: “very, very ambitious”. In certain cases, also, timelines regarding the change project were not seen feasible (stream lead).

The conception and blueprint phase already had delays. The plan was seen by some players as already being unrealistic in this phase, according to a functional director 1, even before the implementation phase was started. One aspect that was raised is the fact that the ERP vendor changed and perhaps would have needed an assimilation phase (functional director 2). Although some players also defended the initial plan of the program (and top) management, they perceived the plan as not doable, as functional director 2 said “it is always difficult, since when you would have stretched the time plan by 6 or 9 months, then perhaps other problems had occurred, due to less pressure although the daily work would have been more comfortable." The unrealistic time frame was stretched several times in the lessons‐learned workshop and its minutes.

Nobody wanted to say something against the first time plan, as high top‐down pressure was perceived and the go‐live date was clearly defined (two persons of management coalition and one person of the Basis).

Retrospectively, also the program management admitted that the partial greenfield approach and the time frame of implementation attempt 1 was not realistic (several quotations).

The unrealistic time frame had also consequences on the engagement and commitment of the staff (change manager, functional director 1), and problems appeared during the data migration test cycles 1, 2, and 3, where nothin worked out properly.

The Basis (implementation below project leads) did not believe the plans anymore, and the program management lost credibility. This lack of commitment, engagement, and believing in the feasibility of the plan led to the replanning. Although the rumors had been circulating for a certain time, the official cancellation and postponement decision were officially taken by the steering committee only late (March 2012), as a decision with that impact needs a proper and structured analysis in advance.

In the buy‐in meeting (26 June 2012), a realistic but ambitious time frame, with different cutover variants and migration variants, was presented to representatives of all impacted stakeholder groups. This plan was seen as real istic; the decision was taken in unison. Afterwards, the new plan was communicated from the stakeholder represen tatives to their groups. As such, the absolute commitment of these groups was secured for the second go‐live date This ambitious time frame and commitment manifested in weekend work and night shifts (“it was an enormous effort” (one person from the Basis). Functional people and business experts worked together and made the secon go‐live date happen. This willingness to meet the second go‐live date and the absolute commitment were als stressed in the lessons‐learned workshop and its minutes.

Functional areas as logistics reported that the second time schedule worked out properly, with all core functionalities, and it was good that not earlier alternatives were pursued. Some areas as Customer Services really needed this time to meet all the requirements

The pressure was after the cancellation of implementation attempt 1 a little bit lower for a limited time but increased as the second go‐live date approached (two persons from the Basis). The pressure on the second go‐live date also effected that certain positions of stakeholder groups were weakened and the stakeholder groups (Mobilkom and Telekom) worked cooperatively together with the commonly accepted date and found appropriate solutions (management coalition).

## Axial coding: Stakeholder and communication management (core category)

In the beginning, not all stakeholders were properly identified (eg, line management). It was difficult in any case to receive the commitment of the functional areas. The functional areas had the wrong perception that it was solel an IT endeavour. Many stakeholder groups needed to be involved as stated by one member of the PMO, eg, portfoli management and enterprise solution architecture.

At a later stage (perhaps also impacted by the fact that the project manager of the change project was exchanged and the new project manager had more experience regarding communication), change impacts were clearly commu nicated as well as the errors that might be expected. Different channels were used (eg, meetings, newsletter, and roadshow) and transported mainly to the managers. The managers transported it to the next levels. In that relatio also, a meeting intended for the line management was scheduled (very late) as recorded in the meeting minutes of 09 November 2012 and 28 November 2012. To a certain extent, the information meetings were scheduled right from the start, but stronger in implementation attempt 2.

A key user concept was developed, and single points of contact regarding training were defined. The key users should transport the information to lower levels. The responsible persons for training were trained from training con sultants. During the replanning, it was explicitly defined and recorded that the “results must be conveyed to lower levels by line manager.” The line managers acted as multipliers

Only at a later stage was it possible to communicate to the functional areas, which expectations (again perceptions) they should have towards the new system. They realized which data they could expect in the new system (data migration). Furthermore, the implementation team realized that the process could only be observed and tested together with the data (which was not there in a sufficient state during implementation attempt 1) The data migration strategy was not properly communicated during the blueprint phase. This should have been done (technical director program, project manager ASAP), although it would have been “abstract” and hard to comprehend for the functional areas. As a consequence, also the process view is needed to be corrected several times

During implementation attempt 1, daily SCRUM meetings were scheduled as it is recorded in the minutes of 0 February 2016 (perhaps as a final means to meet the deadline of implementation attempt 1)

A functional director clearly asserted that the communication and the communication and meeting of milestones, as well as quality, improved over the course of the rollout.

The change impact was not clearly transported “not enough project‐marketing, no pure accounting‐endeavor,” expectations transported to the management. Not all stakeholder groups were identified; eg, one major area and it impact were not clearly identified. Only commitment of the top management is not enough. Commitment is als needed within the functional areas. In areas where the stakeholders were identified, they trusted the project members

The quality of collaboration and the expectations to each other were reflected during the replanning. In the beginning partially, too many persons were involved in the meetings (too large rounds). No clear definition of accountability. Later, during implementation attempt 2, small, empowered teams were colocated (in separate rooms) The collaboration significantly improved as recorded in the lessons learned document.

The reporting function (to the management) benefited significantly through the program construct. Overview of several projects and transparency were mentioned several times.

The buy‐in meeting 29 June 2012, where all major stakeholders were involved, contributed significantly to a better work situation and a successful implementation attempt 2. The reflection led to a new definition of the commu nication strategy (managers transport info to subordinates, to secure the involvement of all stakeholders), separate project rooms, smaller, empowered teams, data migration scenarios, cutover scenarios. Very important was the def inition of a new go‐live date (several scenarios discussed).

Approval of all stakeholder groups secured strong commitment during implementation attempt 2.

Tools contributed to better communication, in implementation attempt 2.

Post go‐live: Hypercare support was planned during the cutover planning. Errors and expected solution times were properly communicated. Training sessions were partially repeated and skills and training materials optimized Finally, during the lessons learned workshop (end of hypercare phase), the communication (internal and external) was seen positively. Emphasized was the target group–specific communication and the communication plan.

## Selective coding: Realistic time frame regarding core category (stakeholder and communication management)

During implementation attempt 1, the go‐live date was defined by the board and supported by the program management (Believers). It seems that other stakeholders (line managers and implementation team starting from stream leads) were not much involved in the definition of this date. The go‐live was tacitly accepted from these Skeptics, but had was not perceived as doable (realistic). This had an impact on commitment and engagement, and the management coalition (Believers) lost trustworthiness. Nobody of the Skeptics believed in the plans “they put on the walls” and its feasibility As a consequence, a negative fit of perceptions towards the CSF “realistic time frame” existed. The CSFs were not met

During the replanning, several scenarios were discussed, and all stakeholder groups were identified properly and involved. At the buy‐in meeting, the scenario of a “new time frame, new go‐live date” was formally approved. The managers transported this in a new (better) communication strategy to their subordinates, securing that all levels are involved. Several other actions (eg, regarding decision‐making and empowerment of smaller teams) helped to save time

During implementation attempt 2, all stakeholder groups perceived the feasibility of the time frame, showing strong commitment and wanted to stick to the go‐live date at all costs. They worked long hours and weekends The time schedule where all stakeholder groups were “bought in” through effective stakeholder and communication management was perceived as ambitious but doable (realistic). As a consequence, the group borders blurred; all stakeholders had the same perceptions towards the CSF, leading to a high positive fit towards the CSF “realistic time frame.” The CSF was met. This results in the only remaining salient group Believers.
