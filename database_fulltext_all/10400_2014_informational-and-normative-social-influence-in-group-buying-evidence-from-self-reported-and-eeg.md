Abstract: This study examines two types of information commonly used by groupbuying sites to induce purchasing. The first study indicates the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information). The second one indicates Facebook friends who “like” a deal (“like” information). The effects of the group-buying information on opinions (attitude and intention) and emotions were examined using a controlled experiment. Our results show that positive and negative “buy” information has an asymmetric influence on attitude and intention, whereas “like” information has a positive influence on intention. The presence of “buy” information is associated with EEG activity that is generally linked to negative emotions. However, the addition of “like” information is associated with EEG activity that is generally linked to positive emotions. The different effects of the two types of group-buying information can be explained by the different social influences exerted by the information.

Key words and p hrases: electroencephalography, emotion online, group buying, informational social influence, NeuroIS, normative social influence.

Group -buy ing sites, such as Group on, LivingSocial, Plum District, and BuyWithMe (now Gilt City), have emerged as popular platforms in social commerce that have received tremendous interest from both researchers and practitioners in the information systems/information technology (IS/IT) community in recent years [6, 59]. For example, Amazon.com sold approximately 1.4 million units of \$10–\$20 gift cards in 2011 through LivingSocial, a popular group-buying site [71]. Groupon, regarded as “the fastest-growing company ever,” amassed assets of \$1.5 billion in only 18 months since its establishment in 2008 [77]. Furthermore, there has been a steep increase in the number of deal merchants, from only 12,468 in the first half of 2010 to 135,247 in June 2011 [65].

In addition to offering products with deep discounts for a limited time, a prominent characteristic of group-buying is the heavy reliance on social influence to promote deals. Social influence refers to the modification of an individual’s responses in reaction to the actions of another [58, 75], and its effects on buying decisions have attracted tremendous interest from researchers and practitioners [33, 77]. For example, it was found that knowing the songs downloaded by other users has an influence on the songs downloaded by a buyer [81]. In group-buying, deals are often promoted by highlighting the number of people who have bought the deals. The presence of such information is found to have a significant influence on the buying decision [40, 51].

The increasing popularity of social networks such as Facebook allows companies to promote products and services through social marketing that is carried out implicitly through promoting interactions among consumers and their worth-of-mouth-induced behaviors [5, 51, 59]. To group-buying sites, social networks provide an effective channel for disseminating information on deals among personal friends [23, 88]. Sharing deals among socially connected peers can induce social influence and stimulate unplanned purchases [86].

Drawing on the social influence literature, this study looks at how purchase decisions on group-buying deals are socially influenced by two types of group-buying information: (1) the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information) and (2) Facebook friends who “like” a deal (“like” information). The first type, “buy” information, is the most basic information provided by all group-buying sites, whereas the second type, “like” information, is increasingly used by group-buying sites to influence purchase decisions. Furthermore, we look into the role of emotion in social influence and purchase decisions [26]. In fact, purchase decisions have been shown to be seriously influenced by consumers’ emotional states, sometimes even more than their cognitive thoughts [30, 55].

This study adopts the neuroscience approach, in addition to the traditional selfreported approach, to investigate the effects of group-buying information on emotions and purchase decisions. As with other business disciplines, such as economics and marketing, the IS discipline has relied heavily on psychometric self-reported data in understanding user decisions and the consequent behavior resulting from their decisions. The common premise of the self-reported approach is to treat the information-processing activities in the brain as a black box [36]. In addition, the use of psychometric self-reported data is hampered by limitations pertaining to objective measurements. The psychological mechanisms can only be indirectly inferred from the user behavior through experimental manipulations (e.g., [73]) or statistical modeling (e.g., [37]). As a result, research in neuroeconomics, neuromarketing, and NeuroIS has emerged in recent years to apply the neuroscience methods to gain better insights into human information processing and decision making [35, 36, 39, 78]. By measuring the brain activity directly and objectively, hence opening the “black box” in the brain, the neuroscience discipline is able to provide new insights underlying the interplay between IT and human users in IS research [35, 36].

Among the most popular neuroscience methods is brain imaging, which takes “snapshots” of the brain’s activities while it is in the process of performing experimental tasks. Brain imaging tools such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission topography (PET), and electroencephalography (EEG) are useful for exploring cognitive and mental processes inside a person’s subconscious mind. In particular, the EEG uses electrodes attached to the scalp to measure electrophysiological responses to stimulus events. Compared to the MRI and PET, the EEG is relatively unobtrusive and portable. Moreover, it is equipped with better temporal resolution (on the order of one millisecond), making it particularly useful for studying cognitive and emotional responses in natural settings.

Specifically, this study uses a commercial EEG device, the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset (www.emotiv.com), to observe the emotional responses of consumers when exposed to group-buying information. Released in December 2009, the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset has 14 electrodes, compared to 19 electrodes covered by the international 10–20 system, and up to 256 electrodes used by high-density EEG systems. It is lightweight, convenient, and cost-effective, in comparison to other mainstream EEG tools that are designed for medical applications. It is ideal for unobtrusively measuring consumers’ brain activities while they are carrying out normal Web browsing and online shopping activities. Triangulating the neurological data captured by the neuroheadset with the self-reported subjective data hence facilitates tracking the emotional effects of social influence exerted by the group-buying information. This study aims to explore and showcase the potential of commercial neuroimaging products in testing the effects of mental stimuli (e.g., group-buying information) on the subconscious mind (e.g., emotions).

The objectives of this study are twofold. First, we are interested in understanding the social influences (informational versus normative) exerted by the two types of group-buying information and their effects on opinions (attitude and intention). Second, we examined the effects of the two types of group-buying information on emotions.

Emotional patterns reflected in brain activities will be explored and identified to explain the effects of group-buying information on attitude and intention.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Next, we present the theoretical foundations of our study and provide theoretical support for hypotheses development. Then, the controlled experiment used to test the hypotheses is described. Then, the results of the data analysis are presented. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of our findings and implications.

## Theoretical Background

Social influence has been generally referred to as the conformity of going along with or agreeing with others or a visible majority [47]. Burnkrant and Cousineau [19] highlighted two forms of social influence: informational social influence and normative social influence. Both forms of social influence lead to conformity, which involves a change in attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors due to the real or imagined influence of others [7, 45]. However, the conformity effects resulting from the two forms of social influence are driven by different needs.

## Informational Social Influence in Group-Buying

Informational social influence refers to one’s tendency to conform to the opinions of others, based on information obtained as evidence in judgment [49, 52]. Information social influence has been studied under the bandwagon effect (and other related concepts such as herd behavior and social proof), which is a social phenomenon that reflects one’s tendency to follow the actions of others, even when one’s private information suggests otherwise [14, 16, 19]. For example, when faced with two apparently similar restaurants, people tend to choose the one with more customers.

Informational social influence leads to conformity because people view others judgments and behaviors as sources of information and assume that it is informing an appropriate decision that is better than individual judgment [56]. The underlying assumption is that others may possess different and arguably better information on which to base a decision. Being influenced by others mutually, people tend to conform to make decisions similar to those of others [20].

One type of information provided by group-buying sites is the number of people who have bought the deals (“buy” information). The idea of providing such information is to exert informational social influence to induce other hesitant buyers to order [50, 60]. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 1a: Providing information on the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information) has a positive effect on attitude toward the deal.

Hypothesis 1b: Providing information on the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information) has a positive effect on intention to purchase the deal.

## Normative Social Influence in Group-Buying

Normative social influence refers to one’s tendency to comply with the expectations of a group or other individuals [49, 52]. Under such an influence, people modify their own judgments to emulate the actions of other group members or significant individuals in order to socially identify with them, even if this implies a letting go of personal justification [1, 19]. In other words, the conformity resulting from normative influence is driven by the need to be identified with reference groups and gain social rewards such as being liked and accepted by others [19, 52, 56, 64].

Another type of information that is increasingly provided by many group-buying Web sites involves Facebook friends who “like” the deals (“like” information). The idea of providing such information is to exert normative social influence to induce people to buy a deal because their friends like it. It is common for normative social influence to influence purchase behavior in social networks, where individuals often perform actions similar to those of their friends [4, 83]. By using the “like” mechanism of Facebook to provide information on Facebook friends who like a deal, group-buying can exert normative social influence on the opinions of individuals. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 2a: Providing information on friends who “like” a deal (“like” information) has a positive effect on attitude toward the deal.

Hypothesis 2b: Providing information on friends who “like” a deal (“like” information) has a positive effect on intention to purchase the deal.

## Emotional Effects of Group-Buying Information

Emotion is defined as a biological state of feeling derived from cognitive appraisals of events or thoughts [57, 69]. As innate and instinctive responses to stimuli, emotions are found to affect decision making [10]. Emotions are especially crucial when reason is an insufficient guide to decision making [11]. In that case, emotion serves as a functional equivalent for rationality [61].

The role of emotion has been studied extensively in consumer research [10]. Specifically, emotions are found to be positively associated with attitudes [11, 67]. With the high correlations between emotions and attitudes, researchers have sometimes inferred the presence of emotions from revealed attitudes [54]. In some cases, attitudes are even treated as instances of emotion, and assessed with the same measures (e.g., semantic differential items such as pleasant/unpleasant, happy/sad, or interested/bored) [12].

The role of emotion in social influence has also been suggested by the literature. For example, providing social influence information as an emotional stimulus has been found to be effective in enhancing the usability of group-buying sites, and subsequently inducing repeated use by consumers [85]. In relation to informational social influence, research shows that decisions involved in bandwagon behavior are associated with shorter decision times, thus suggesting that the decision is either an automated decision-making heuristic or an instinctive, emotional response [9, 10]. In relation to normative social influence, it was found that the feeling of being liked and approved by others evokes positive emotions [91], with the latter being linked to unplanned purchases [29, 90]. Furthermore, images of other people to which a person can relate are effective stimuli that would evoke emotional responses, and subsequently affect buying decisions [87, 91]. These studies suggest the involvement of emotion in informational social influence, which is arguably exerted by the “buy” information, and normative social influence, which is arguably exerted by the “like” information. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 3: Providing information on the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information) is associated with a higher level of emotional response.

Hypothesis 4: Providing information on friends who “like” a deal (“like” information) is associated with a higher level of emotional response.

## Emotion and Electroencephalography

Advances in neuroscience offer new opportunities to observe emotions directly in IS research. EEG tools are found to be capable of recognizing emotions with significant accuracy [89], and the potential of the tools to detect the current emotional state of a user is increasingly being recognized in the neuroscience discipline [76]. In fact, the role that emotions play in economic decision making has been confirmed by neuroscientific evidence appearing in EEG signals [41, 84].

In general, EEG is effective in identifying the brain activities triggered by quick stimuli in providing up to 2,000 measurements every second [8, 62]. Other advantages of EEG tools is their low costs and portability, which renders them suitable for and frequently applied in studying the effects of different stimuli in consumer research [70, 79, 80]. The study of emotions using EEG tools involves recording the fluctuations of the brainwaves at different frequencies within the neurons of the brain. Different emotions engage different neural structures from the brainwaves, and these are reflected as different brainwave patterns [10, 82].

EEG measures the brainwave patterns as sound waves in cycles per second (hertz [Hz]). EEG tools involve sensors or electrodes that are evenly positioned on the scalp to measure the power of brainwaves from different areas of the brain. Brainwaves are generally classified into five categories, according to the frequency bands: delta (below 4 H z), theta (4–8 H z), alpha (8–12 H z), beta (12–30 H z), and gamma (30–100+ H z). Beta and alpha are the frequency bands of interest in decision making. Generally, a higher frequency band (e.g., beta) is associated with more complex brain activities (e.g., cognitive processing), whereas a lower frequency band (e.g., alpha) is associated with less complex brain activities (e.g., emotional processing) [66].

In line with prior studies, the alpha rhythm in brain activities is used in our study to measure emotional responses resulting from visual stimuli in decision making. Using EEG recordings in humans, previous studies have found that brain activity within the alpha frequency band (i.e., 8–12 H z) fluctuates in reaction to different emotional states triggered by visual stimuli [72, 74]. Moreover, alpha bands are further divided into low-alpha (8–10 H z) and high-alpha (10–12 H z) bands. The high-alpha bands are mostly associated with high-level emotional processing, whereas the low-alpha bands are mostly associated with alertness and attention [28, 53]. Moreover, positive and negative emotions are expressed asymmetrically between the left and right brain hemispheres. Relatively speaking, left frontal activation is involved in experiencing positive emotions, such as joy and happiness, whereas right frontal activation is involved in experiencing negative emotions, such as fear and disgust [31, 42]. Hence, in this study, we focus on the alpha activity in the frontal region of the brain. Given that brain activation is associated with low alpha activity, we expect to observe low alpha activity in the left frontal region for positive emotions, and low alpha activity in the right frontal region for negative emotions.

## The Experiment

The social influence exerted by the two typ es of group-buying information was examined using a controlled experiment. The experiment consisted of two sessions that were one week apart, and each session lasted approximately 1.5 hours. In each session, the subjects were asked to view and evaluate 30 group-buying deals adapted from a popular group-buying site in Hong Kong. The design of the experiment is depicted in Figure 1.

## Procedure

In the first session, the 30 group-buying deals were manipulated and presented to the subjects in the baseline format (described in the next subsection). The deals were randomly grouped into five sets, with six deals in each set. Each deal was displayed for 20 seconds, and the presentation of each set took two minutes. After viewing each set, the subjects answered a questionnaire on their evaluation the six deals they had just viewed. Then they were given a one- to two-minute break before proceeding to the next set.

The second session was conducted one week after the first session to minimize the memory effects in the evaluations carried over from the first session. In this session, the 30 deals from the first session were manipulated and presented to the subjects in one of the two group-buying formats (described in the next subsection). The deals were equally divided into two groups (A and B). The 15 deals in each group were randomly divided into three sets, with five deals in each set. The subjects were presented with one group (A or B) in the first group-buying format, and the other group (B or A) in the second group-buying format (described in the next subsection). Each deal was displayed for 30 seconds, and the presentation of each set took 2.5 minutes. After viewing each set, the subjects answered a questionnaire on their evaluations on the five deals they had just viewed. Then they were given a one- to two-minute break before proceeding with the next deal set.

## Measurements

Both EEG measures and self-reported measures were captured during the experiment. EEG measures were captured using the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset that has a wireless interface with 14 channels (International 10–20 locations: AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7, 01, 02, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4) and a sampling rate of 128 H z. EEG measures were captured using the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset. Self-reported measures, including attitude, intention, and familiarity, were captured using the questionnaire presented in the Appendix. Multiple-item measures for the dependent variables, that is, attitude and intention, were adapted from prior studies [17, 18]. They were examined for construct reliability and validity, and the results are presented in Table 1. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. All of the alpha values were found to be above the recommended threshold of 0.7 [68]. Construct validity was evaluated by performing an exploratory factor analysis. Factors with an eigenvalue greater than unity were extracted and rotated using the varimax criterion. All the item loadings were found to be high on their corresponding factors and low on other factors. Hence, convergent validity and discriminant validity were considered to be satisfactory. Single-item measures used for other controlling variables, including brand familiarity, product familiarity, and deal familiarity, were adapted from previous studies [63]. The correlations of the measures are found in Table 2.

## Participants

The experiment was conducted with 18 undergraduate students from a major university in Hong Kong.<sup>1</sup> All of the subjects were enrolled in the same IS course. They are socially acquainted with each other in real life and actually connected as friends on Facebook. The subjects included 7 males and 11 females, ranging in age from 20 to 24 years (mean = 21.72, S.D. [standard deviation] = 1.54). All the subjects had previous experience on group-buying sites. Seventeen subjects had visited at least one group-buying site on a regular basis, and 12 subjects had made at least one purchase from one of the sites. Participation in the experiment was voluntary and they were given \$50 for participation.

## Effects of “Buy” Information on Attitude and Intention

One basic feature of group-buying is to induce purchasing by providing information on the number of people who have purchased a deal (“buy” information). The effect of this basic feature was assessed by examining the changes in attitude and intention with the information provided using paired t-tests. The results are presented in Table 4. Overall, by providing “buy” information in the deals, attitude increased from 4.152 to 4.222, a change of 0.072; and intention increased from 3.383 to 3.515, a change of 0.132. However, both changes are not statistically significant.

## Effects of “Like” Information on Attitude and Intention

With the ever-increasing popularity of social networks such as Facebook, many groupbuying sites have started using social networks to provide information on friends who “like” a deal (“like” information) in addition to the number of people who have bought the deal (“buy” information). We were interested in the overall effects of enhancing the basic “buy” information with “like” information on changes in attitude and intention. The effects were examined in terms of changes in attitude and intention for deals with both types of group-buying information provided (enhanced group-buying format). The effects were evaluated using paired t-tests, and the results are presented in Table 6. ## Emotional Effects of Group-Buying Information

The emotional effects of social influence exerted by the two types of group-buying information were assessed by analyzing the EEG activity captured by the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset during the first and second experiment sessions using the EEGLab for Matlab application [32]. EEG activity for the first 10 seconds of each trial was extracted. Furthermore, the alpha (8–12 H z) frequencies were extracted for analysis because they are areas of interest in EEG research on emotions [13, 31, 42]. Also, by extracting only the alpha frequencies, much of the noise caused by EEG artifacts could be avoided [22, 38, 93]. For example, physiological artifacts such as electrooculography (EOG) artifacts (e.g., eye blinking) are most dominant below 4 H z, electrocardiography (ECG ) artifacts (e.g., heartbeats) at approximately 1.2 H z, and electromyography (EMG) artifacts (e.g., muscle reaction) that have a wide frequency range (0–500 H z) but maximal at frequencies 30 H z [22, 38, 93]. Nonphysiological artifacts caused by 50–60 H z power lines could be avoided. The alpha frequencies were extracted using bandpass filtering with the fast Fourier transform filter. In addition, we examined the EEG activity in the frontal region, which is primarily responsible for emotional and other high-level processing [48]. Hence, our analysis focused on alpha frequencies from the channels located in the frontal region (i.e., AF3, AF4, F3, F4, F7, F8, FC5, FC6).

“Like” information is increasingly provided by group-buying sites in addition to “buy” information to influence purchasing. ## Discussion

This study examined two typ es of information commonly used by group-buying sites to induce purchasing: (1) “buy” information, indicating how many people have bought a deal, and (2) “like” information, indicating Facebook friends who “like” a deal. We argue that the social influence exerted by the two types of group-buying information are different in nature, and therefore have different effects on opinions (attitude and intention) and emotions.

## Informational Social Influence of “Buy” Information

Among the most basic features of group-buying is providing information on the number of people who have bought a deal (“buy” information) to influence purchasing. The basic idea is to make use of the information to trigger the bandwagon effect and induce herding behavior. Contrary to the hypothesis that “buy” information has a positive effect on opinions (attitude and intention), positive and negative “buy” information were found to have asymmetric effects on attitude and intention. Specifically, positive “buy” information, suggesting that a deal has been bought by more people than expected, does not seem to increase attitude and intention. However, negative “buy” information, suggesting that a deal has been bought by fewer people than expected, has a negative influence on attitude and intention.

We argue that the role of “buy” information in social influence is primarily informational in nature, that is, driven by the need to be “right” [19, 24]. People use “buy” information (i.e., the number of people who have bought the deals) to gauge the opinions of others and compare them with their own. Furthermore, the informational influence exerted by “buy” information is asymmetric for positive and negative “buy” information. Given the general attractiveness of group-buying deals, which results in generally favorable initial attitude and intention, positive “buy” information simply serves as a confirmatory signal, suggesting that the initial favorable opinions are “right.” Hence, positive “buy” information does not necessarily lead to a further upward adjustment in opinions. Negative “buy” information, however, serves as a disconfirmatory signal, suggesting that the initial attitude and intention may be too favorable, and requires a downward adjustment. In fact, the asymmetric effects of posi tive and negative information, with negative information generally having a stronger influence than positive information (i.e., negativity bias), has been well-documented in information influence literature [3, 27, 46].

The informational role primarily associated with negative “buy” information explains the overall negative emotions associated with “buy” information. Our results on the EEG data show that providing “buy” information in the group-buying deals is associated with a higher activity in the high-alpha band (10–12 H z) near the F3 area. Informational social influence is primarily associated with negative emotions because the influence is primarily driven by the need to be “right.” Disconformity in opinions creates cognitive dissonance [15]. The dissonance, while cognitively induced, can be emotionally uncomfortable, and is associated with negative emotions. In other words, when an individual holds an opinion conflicting with the opinions of others, the need to resolve the conflicting opinions is emotionally stressful.

## Normative Social Influence of “Like” Information

With the rapidly increasing popularity of social networks such as Facebook, an increasing number of group-buying sites are using social networks to provide information on friends who “like” a deal (“like” information) to influence purchasing. Whereas negative “buy” information has a negative influence on attitude and intention, “like” information has a positive influence on intention only.

We argue that the social influence exerted by the “like” information is primarily normative, and motivated by the need to be liked (versus need to be right) and socially approved by our peers [4, 21]. To be socially identified with the social peers, a person would want to buy a particular deal that is well received by his or her social peers, without necessarily liking the deal more him- or herself. Furthermore, the normative influence exerted by “like” information is primarily positive. The “like” mechanism of Facebook allows positive opinions held by social peers to be reflected with a “like” vote. However, the mechanism does not provide for negative opinions to be reflected with a “dislike” vote. Hence, the presence of “like” votes is an indication of positive opinions held by social peers, but the absence of “like” votes is not a direct indication of negative opinions held by social peers.

While the emotional effect of the informational social influence exerted by “buy” information is primarily negative, the emotional effect of the normative social influence exerted by “like” information is different. The informational influence exerted by “buy” information is emotionally stressful, particularly with negative disconfirmatory information. However, the normative influence exerted by “like” information appears to be emotionally relieving, given the positive nature of the influence motivated by one’s internal need and desire to be liked and socially approved by social peers [83]. Our findings show that providing “buy” information in the deals is primarily associated with negative emotional responses in terms of increased high-alpha activity (10–12 H z) in the left frontal region (F3). However, providing “like” information in addition to “buy” information is associated with positive emotional responses in terms of increased high-alpha activity (10–12 H z) in the right frontal region (F4). This suggests that the normative social influence exerted by “like” information alleviates the negative experience exerted by “buy” information, rendering the negative emotional responses insignificant. At the same time, providing “like” information triggers the need and desire to be liked and approved by peers and induces a positive motivational drive.

## Implications and Limitations

This study contributes to the literature on the roles of social influence in group-buying from both theoretical and methodological perspectives. Our study opens the black box pertaining to the types of information commonly used in group-buying, the types of social influence exerted, and the resulting effects on opinions and emotions. While both types of information are used in group-buying sites to exert social influence, the nature of the social influence exerted by each type of information is different. Information social influence, as arguably exerted by “buy” information, is driven by the need to be right. However, normative social influence, as arguably exerted by “like” information, is driven by the need to be liked. The different driving forces behind the two types of social influence, as exerted by the two types of group-buying information, result in different effects on opinions (attitude and intention) and emotions.

For practitioners, the findings of this study provide insights on how Web sites can use social networks to exert different types of social influence to engage customers. This has been a challenge for many companies. Our findings show that providing “buy” information can influence purchase decisions differently, depending on whether the information is considered positive or negative. Group-buying Web sites may consider whether, or how, such information should be provided in every deal. For example, group-buying sites may consider whether the “buy” information should be provided before it has reached a predetermined minimum level. Our findings suggest that providing such information may prematurely discourage other customers from participating in a deal. Similarly, group-buying sites may also consider whether it is a good or bad idea to cap the “buy” information at a certain number once it has reached a sufficiently high level.

This study demonstrates the potential value of commercial EEG devices such as the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset for IS research. In our case, complementing EEG measures with traditional self-reported measures has proved to be useful for understanding the emotional aspects of social influence in group-buying [78]. Relative to the medical-grade EEG equipment and other neuroscience methods such as MRI, the EEG device used in this study is both cost-effective and portable. These advantages make it attractive for studying many behavioral issues in IS and facilitating the integration of neuroscience and IS theories in understanding how IT supports human decision processes [35].

In fact, many companies, including Google, CBS, and The Walt Disney Company, are exploring the possibility of deploying EEG tools in their marketing strategies in order to gain a better understanding of their customers. NeuroFocus, a Nielsen subsidiary, has begun to use the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset in neuromarketing research. While EEG technology has been traditionally limited to academic and medical applications, this study serves to showcase how commercial EEG devices can be used to test the effects of various mental stimuli (e.g., group-buying information) on the subconscious mind (e.g., attitudes, intentions, and emotions), which are crucial to decision outcomes (e.g., purchase decisions). This enables a new product development paradigm, from one of merely observing the decisions to looking into the ways the decisions are made.

The findings of this study should be interpreted together with its limitations. Like most EEG studies, our findings are based on repeated trials with a relatively small number of subjects. Furthermore, the number of trials conducted with each subject is not considered high. The generalizability of the findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the Emotiv EPOC neuroheadset used in this study is a commercial EEG device that has a relatively lower spatial resolution of only 14 channels and a temporal resolution of only 128 H z, compared to medical-grade EEG equipment. This lower resolution may affect the sensitivity in detecting the stimulus effect under study. The interpretation of EEG activity can be further complicated by EEG artifacts potentially in the alpha frequency range. For example, while EMG artifacts (e.g., muscle activity from teeth clenching) are generally maximal at frequencies above 30 H z (i.e., outside the alpha frequency range), they can potentially range from 0 H z to 500 H z [22, 38, 93]. However, the encouraging results of this study suggest that the neuroheadset can still provide valuable insights to research, especially when the EEG data are examined together with the self-reported data. Nevertheless, future studies are encouraged to replicate our findings with a higher number of subjects and trials, and using other neuroscience methods.

## Conclusion

Group -buy ing has been traditionally attractive because of the generous discounts. With the increasing popularity of social networks, social influence is playing an increasingly important role in group-buying decisions. This study serves as a starting point in understanding the different roles played by informational and normative social influence in affecting attitude, intention, and emotion. The availability of low-cost, portable EEG technology facilitates observing variables such as emotions generated by the human brain. The combined use of self-reported and psychophysical measures has been shown to provide much richer insights into how different information stimuli affect human decision processes than the use of the two measures independently.

## Appendix: Measurement Items

Attitude (on a 7-point scale; adapted from [17, 18])

I find this deal \_\_

AT 1: Unattractive/Attractive

AT 2: Bad/Good

AT 3: Unfavorable/Favorable

AT 4: Worthless/Valuable

## Intention (on a 7-point scale; adapted from [17, 18])

I do/am \_\_\_\_\_ to buy/in buying this deal.

INT1: Not interested/Interested

INT2: Unlikely/Likely

INT3: Not intend/Intend

Familiarity (on a 7-point scale; adapted from [63])

Brand: Are you familiar with the brand in this deal? (Not familiar at all/Extremely familiar)

Product: Are you familiar with the product in this deal? (Not familiar at all/ Extremely familiar)

Deal: Were you familiar with this particular deal before? (Not familiar at all/ Extremely familiar)
