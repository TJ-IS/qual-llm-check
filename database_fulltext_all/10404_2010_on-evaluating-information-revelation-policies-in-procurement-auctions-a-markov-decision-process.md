# On Evaluating Information Revelation Policies in Procurement Auctions: A Markov Decision Process Approach

Amy Greenwald Department of Computer Science, Brown University, Providence, Rhode Island 02912, amy@cs.brown.edu

Karthik Kannan

Krannert School of Management, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907, kkarthik@krannert.purdue.edu

Ramayya Krishnan Heinz College, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213, rk2x@cmu.edu

ach market session in a reverse electronic marketplace features a procurer and many suppliers. An important attribute of a market session chosen by the procurer is its information revelation policy. The revelation policy determines the information (such as the number of competitors, the winning bids, etc.) that will be revealed to participating suppliers at the conclusion of each market session. Suppliers participating in multiple market sessions use strategic bidding and fake their own cost structure to obtain information revealed at the end of each market session. The information helps to reduce two types of uncertainties encountered in future market sessions, namely, their opponents’ cost structure and an estimate of the number of their competitors. Whereas the first type of uncertainty is present in physical and e-marketplaces, the second type of uncertainty naturally arises in IT-enabled marketplaces. Through their effect on the uncertainty faced by suppliers, information revelation policies influence the bidding behavior of suppliers which, in turn, determines the expected price paid by the procurer. Therefore, the choice of information revelation policy has important consequences for the procurer.

This paper develops a partially observable Markov decision process model of supplier bidding behavior and uses a multiagent e-marketplace simulation to analyze the effect that two commonly used information revelation policies—complete information policy and incomplete information policy—have on the expected price paid by the procurer. We find that the expected price under the complete information policy is lower than that under the incomplete information policy. The integration of ideas from the multiagents literature, the machine learning literature, and the economics literature to develop a method to evaluate information revelation policies in e-marketplaces is a novel feature of this paper.

Key words: auctions; information revelation; MDP; game theory simulation

## 1. Introduction

In procurement auctions, suppliers compete to offer their products or services to the procurer. Milgrom and Weber (1982) note that these suppliers, by repeatedly competing against one another across multiple procurement auctions (also referred to as market sessions or periods), learn about the nature of the competition. Each supplier’s ability to learn is dependent on the information revealed at the end of each market session. In certain government procurement auctions, statutes require that the winner’s bid be revealed at the end of the market session (McAfee and McMillan 1988). In certain other cases, such as in municipal construction contracting, all bids are revealed at the end of the market session (Thomas 1996). The different levels of information revealed affect what bidders learn and how they bid in the future, which affects the expected price paid by the procurer in turn.

Understanding the role of revelation policies is especially important for a procurer in electronic marketplaces such as Ariba<sup>1</sup> where she is required to choose one from a wide range of available policies. For example, a procurer can choose to accept sealed bids from the suppliers, or provide rank information as feedback to them, or conduct an open-cry auction (Jap 2002, 2003). Suppliers in these marketplaces typically face two types of uncertainties, which they attempt to overcome by learning across market sessions. The first type is the cost-structure uncertainty, which is the uncertainty that a supplier has about its opponents’ cost. It arises in both physical and electronic marketplaces. The second type, the marketstructure uncertainty, naturally arises in technology enabled electronic marketplaces and it refers to the uncertainty that geographically distributed suppliers face about the number of competitors. The presence of both these uncertainties poses interesting managerial questions about the choice of revelation policies. To our knowledge, prior work has not considered both these uncertainties together, leaving the procurer with little guidance in choosing the appropriate revelation policy when both forms of uncertainties are present. By providing insights on the revelation policies, our work contributes to the growing body of literature on transparencies in electronic markets (e.g., Koppius 2002, Zhu 2004, Granados et al. 2005) and, more generally, to e-market design (e.g., Gupta et al. 1996, Bakos and Nault 1997, Kleindorfer and Wu 2003, Wu and Kleindorfer 2005).

The impact of revelation policies on the expected price paid by the procurer<sup>2</sup> is different under marketstructure uncertainty from that under cost-structure uncertainty (see Arora et al. 2007, Kannan 2003), necessitating the analysis involving both types of uncertainties. However, revelation policy comparisons are analytically intractable when both the uncertainties are jointly considered (Kannan 2003). To overcome the problem, we develop an alternate approach that combines ideas from economics and the distributed learning literature to characterize the bidding behavior of suppliers in the marketplace. Note that each supplier’s bid in procurement scenarios stochastically affects the payoff of other suppliers. To model such scenarios in distributed setups, Markov formulations are specifically well-suited because they can combine optimization with stochastic models (Sutton and Barto 1998). In our context, we develop a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) model of supplier bidding behavior under two different, yet widely used, revelation policies. We use multiagent e-marketplace simulation, in which supplier-agents use the POMDP model to bid, to computationally analyze the expected price paid by the procurer when jointly considering market-structure uncertainty and cost-structure uncertainty. Based on the analysis, we develop managerial guidelines about which revelation policy to use in the presence—independently or jointly—of these two types of uncertainty.

While there is literature on applying computational models to economic contexts (e.g., Kephart and Greenwald 1999), our work is distinctive in its choice of the problem as well as in validating the computational methodology by benchmarking it to known game-theoretic results when available. We show that results from our POMDP model approximate known game-theoretic results very well. Our model not only allows us to consider each type of uncertainty separately but also simultaneously. Thus, it permits us to gain insights in to games that are analytically intractable. Furthermore, we can analyze settings that are closer to reality and that are beyond the scope of the abstract game-theoretic models. For example, to examine the effect of learning from the first period on the second period bids, Thomas (1996) and Arora et al. (2007) restrict their analyses to two periods with the same set of suppliers participating in both periods. We can extend their analyses and study the impact of revelation policies beyond two periods. We also relax the assumption that the same set of suppliers participates across periods and consider the more realistic case, in which the correlation of the supplier set across market sessions is not perfect. The research questions we address are:

1. How does the presence of market-structure uncertainty have an effect—in direction and magnitude—on the expected price paid by the procurer differ from that of cost-structure uncertainty?

2. How does the number of times the suppliers repeatedly compete against one another affect the procurers’ expected prices when considering each type of uncertainty separately and when considering them jointly?

3. What is the effect of changing the correlation of the supplier set across periods on the expected prices paid by the procurer under different revelation policies?

Although our methodological approach is general in nature and can be used to analyze any number of revelation policies, we chose to focus on the following two sealed-bid policies, commonly adopted in both electronic (e.g., available to a procurer using Ariba) and traditional marketplaces:

1. Incomplete information policy (iip): At the end of a market session, only the winner’s bid is revealed to all bidding suppliers. This policy is similar to government procurement auctions, where statutes require the winner’s bid to be revealed (McAfee and McMillan 1988).

2. Complete information policy (cip): At the end of a market session, all bids are revealed. This policy is followed while awarding municipal construction contracts (Thomas 1996).

We are interested in these two policies for the following reasons. First, such policies are used in e-marketplaces (e.g., they are used in Freemarkets; we became aware of that from conversations with the company’s representatives).<sup>3</sup> Second, these policies have already been studied in prior works (e.g., Arora et al. 2007, Thomas 1996). Moreover, as we discuss later in §6, useful insights about other policies can be generated based on our analysis of these two policies.

## 2. Literature Review

The problem studied in this paper is similar to those in market microstructure theory and also overlaps three other literatures: learning models in game-theory, the information systems (IS) literature, and the distributed decision-making literature. First, we briefly differentiate our work from that on market microstructures and following that, we review the other related literatures in detail.

Market microstructure theory studies the impact of revelation policies in double-sided auctions such as financial markets (see, for example, Bloomfield and O’Hara 1999, Madhavan et al. 2005, Boehmer et al. 2005). Those results are not directly applicable to electronic reverse markets because of the institutional differences between the two. Typically, financial exchanges are double-sided auctions<sup>4</sup> whereas electronic reverse markets are single-sided auctions. Information revealed in a double-sided auction not only affects the behavior of suppliers but also that of procurers. In a single-sided reverse auction, information revealed only affects the behavior of suppliers.

## 2.1. Learning in Games

There is extensive literature on learning in games, which is motivated to explain the inconsistency between experimental results (e.g., Cox et al. 1982, Rassenti et al. 1982) and analytical results. Only a few papers in this domain have analyzed games where the opponent’s payoff—which is analogous to the opponent’s cost structure—is not known (e.g., Snir et al. 1998, Dekel et al. 2004). To our knowledge, even these papers have not explicitly considered the impact of different levels of information on learning. More relevant are prior papers that compare different sequential-auction mechanisms because the choice of a mechanism also implies the choice of information revealed.

Hausch (1986) compares the expected auctioneer’s profit in a common value setting between an auction that uses a revelation policy similar to cip (see Appendix A in the online supplement<sup>5</sup> for glossary of acronyms) and an auction with no information being revealed between market sessions. In his model, there are two bidders each of which is aware of the number of opponents it faces. Each bidder obtains a high- or a low-value signal about the commonly valued object. Although each bidder is aware of its signal, it is not aware of its opponent’s. The equilibrium of this game is such that the supplier with the lowvalue signal always truthfully reveals its type. However, the supplier with the high-value signal has an incentive to fake under cip. Because of this faking, the expected profits for the auctioneer can be lower under cip than when no bid information is released between market sessions. Unlike in Hausch (1986), the procurement scenario we consider involves independent valuations. Further, we also consider market-structure uncertainty.

Thomas (1996) compares cip and iip in a framework with two suppliers, where each supplier is certain about the presence of its opponent but uncertain about its opponents’ cost structure. Each supplier faces an opponent which is a high-cost type with a probability of 1/2. Under this specific setting, he demonstrates that cip generates higher savings for the procurer than iip. Thomas (1996) considers only a specific case of cost-structure uncertainty and ignores market-structure uncertainty.

Note that although these prior works have addressed cost-structure uncertainty, they have not modeled market-structure uncertainty. In contrast, our paper addresses a broader problem—one where both types of uncertainty are jointly considered—using a novel POMDP-based computational model. In addition, our model permits the analysis of the effect of correlation among supplier participation across market sessions, an element that is an important facet of real world marketplaces that is usually ignored in analytic models owing to intractability.

## 2.2. Relevant Literature in Information Systems

Prior IS works have used machine learning techniques in various problem domains. Sikora and Shaw (1996) develop a distributed learning procedure and focus on the effectiveness of the rule-learning technique. In another paper, Sikora and Shaw (1998) extend Malone and Crowston (1994) to present a multiagent learning framework to coordinate and integrate IS processes. They apply this framework to the production of printed circuit boards (PCB) and demonstrate the superiority of their approach against the traditional Silver-Meal heuristic approach (Silver and Meal 1973). Purao et al. (2003) develop a learning technique to automate the conceptual design of databases. Our paper also uses machine learning techniques, but it is different from these prior works in integrating the learning literature and the economics literature to study different information revelation policies in e-marketplaces.

In the IS literature, the following two papers are most closely related to ours: Koppius (2002) and Zhu (2004). Koppius (2002) uses experimental data to analyze the impact of revelation policies in a multidimensional reverse auction. He shows that the policy with the least level of uncertainty for the suppliers about the weights that the buyer places on each attribute generates the highest profit for the bidders. Our paper is different from his in that we focus on learning across auctions. Zhu (2004) studies the informational effects of exchanges by focusing on participants’ ability to infer their rivals’ costs under price and quantity competition. He demonstrates that only a few firms participate in electronic markets because the loss that high-cost firms suffer when their costs are revealed to others outweighs the benefit of learning about the costs of others. Note that our paper is different from Zhu (2004), who focuses only on cost-structure uncertainty. In contrast, we focus on learning across market sessions in the presence of market-structure uncertainty as well.

## 2.3. MDP Literature

The theory of MDPs (Howard 1960, Puterman 1994), which is used to model a single agent in a stationary but stochastic environment, has been applied in different contexts. For example, White (1993) discusses applications using fully observable MDPs to manage inventory and job schedules. Similarly, Bagnell and Schneider (2001) use a POMDP to control a helicopter. In these papers, the agents use reinforcement learning techniques such as Q-learning and SARSA learning to determine their optimal course of actions (Sutton and Barto 1998).

The idea of using reinforcement learning techniques in Markov games—multiplayer games, where each player’s decision is modeled in an MDP-like environment—has been increasingly drawing attention since Littman (1994). For example, Hu and Wellman (1998), Greenwald and Hall (2003), and Bowling and Veloso (2001) have all designed algorithms intended to learn to play best responses in Markov games. Greenwald and Hall (2003) introduce an algorithm to learn correlated equilibria. Both Hu and Wellman (1998) and Bowling and Veloso (2001) study games involving mixed-strategy (Nash) equilibria. The algorithm developed by Hu and

Wellman (1998) can identify mixed-strategy equilibria in restricted classes of games, such as common-interest Markov games, where all agents have identical profit functions. Although the focus of Bowling and Veloso (2001) is to advocate rationality and convergence as desiderata in multiagent contexts, they also propose a reinforcement learning algorithm that can identify mixed-strategy equilibrium in zero-sum Markov games. Apart from such restricted classes of games, researchers have had difficulty developing multiagent reinforcement learners that can identify mixedstrategy equilibria (see the conclusion of Bowling and Veloso 2001). In these aforementioned works on multiagent learning in Markov games, agents are assumed to observe, if not the actions, at least the rewards, of the other agents playing the game.

Before we differentiate our MDP model from those in prior works, we highlight some of the key aspects of our approach. Using the game we will describe in §3, our first objective is to identify the equilibrium strategy profiles—bid distributions—used by suppliers facing the different types of uncertainties under each of the two policies. This is so that, based on the equilibrium profiles, we can then compare the expected prices paid by the procurer under the two policies. However, as we will demonstrate in §3.1, except for certain special cases, obtaining closed form expressions for bid distributions is not always possible because of analytical intractability, limiting our ability to analytically compare the expected procurer prices. To overcome this limitation, we seek alternate methods capable of numerically identifying the strategy profiles. Similar to the aforementioned works on Markov games, we chose to model each of our agent’s decisions as an MDP. However, unlike in those games, an agent in our model does not always know the rewards of all other players (the only scenario when our agents know the rewards is under cip when suppliers face only the market-structure uncertainty). We capture the notion of the incomplete information by characterizing each agent’s decision as a POMDP and the resulting game as a partially observable Markov game. To implement such a game, we construct a computational market (described in §4), where the protocols used by the agents to interact are the same as the sequence of events in our basic game definition in §3. By running market sessions on the computational market, we demonstrate that our POMDP model can identify the analytically calculated mixedstrategy equilibrium for those analytically tractable special cases. Having validated our POMDP model for the special cases, we proceed to use the POMDP formulation to simulate market sessions and provide insights into the expected price paid by the procurer in §5 while jointly considering both types of uncertainties (i.e., the analytically intractable case). Thus, our paper is closely tied to the analytical framework, while at the same time providing opportunities to extend beyond models that are analytically tractable.

## 3. Preliminaries: The Basic Game

We begin the section by briefly describing a typical electronic reverse marketplace for coal. This description will also serve as an illustrative example for the rest of the paper. Among all the suppliers that mine coal, only a few of them choose to sell through the reverse marketplace. Procurers sequentially arrive at the reverse marketplace to initiate each market session. Among the suppliers that choose to sell in the marketplace, a subset of them participate in each market session. Note that supplier participation is, therefore, a two-stage process. In a market session conducted by Freemarkets, three to five coal suppliers participate. Typically, the same set of suppliers repeatedly participate to provide coal to the procurers (from one geographic region). The continuity of the same set of suppliers may sometimes be lost because of exogenous factors such as the type of coal that the procurer demands, or restrictions that procurers, from one period to the other, may impose on the set of suppliers chosen by the broker. For example, a procurer may specify that it does not want a particular supplier to participate. This observation by Freemarkets about the similarity of the set of suppliers participating (referred to as correlation) across market sessions is consistent with the anecdotal evidence in Milgrom and Weber (1982). In any case, participating suppliers face both market-structure uncertainty (about the number of competitors) and cost-structure uncertainty (about opponents’ cost structure) that they attempt to overcome across market sessions. In a market session, each participating supplier submits a multidimensional bid that includes coal content, ash content, water content of its coal, and the price quote. Recall that we focus only on sealed bids in each market session. After reviewing all bids, the procurer chooses the “best” bid and awards the contract to the winner. At the end of the market session, bids are revealed according to the information revelation policy.

In our setup, the parameter for the first set of Bernoulli processes is to determine the effective probability of participation, while the second one determines the continuity of the set of suppliers. Specifically, the first process involves $N _ { l } + N _ { h }$ Bernoulli trials, one for each of the suppliers. The parameter for each trial is the supplier participation probability $\theta ,$ which we assume to be common knowledge.<sup>11</sup> Even though 	 is common knowledge, no supplier is aware of the outcome of the Bernoulli trial for the others.<sup>12</sup> Thus, it effectively captures the uncertainties faced by each supplier. Let M be the total number of both high- and low-cost suppliers participating in a market session.

As mentioned earlier, the second Bernoulli process influences the continuity of the set of suppliers participating across market sessions or the correlation of supplier participation across market sessions.<sup>13</sup> Prior works (e.g., Hausch 1986, Thomas 1996, and Arora et al. 2007) have explicitly set the correlation to be perfect, motivating their assumption from the anecdotal evidence in Milgrom and Weber (1982). We relax this perfect correlation assumption by considering a Bernoulli trial with probability k. With probability k, the same set of suppliers that participated in one market session also bids in the next; and with (1 k), a new set of suppliers to participate in the second market session is drawn using the first Bernoulli process. Thus, note that our model considers two extreme cases: Exactly the same set of suppliers participates or a completely new (independent) set is chosen in the next period. Note also that some suppliers in this new set may have bid in the prior session as well and so, a new set of suppliers does not mean that all members of the set are new. Knowing k, we use the result from DeGroot (1991) to compute the participation correlation across market sessions, $\rho = k ^ { 2 } / ( k ^ { 2 } + ( 1 - k ) ^ { 2 } )$ . Values k 0 and k 1 correspond to independent random draws and perfect continuity, respectively. The values of k and  are assumed to be common knowledge. Given this game, our objective is to develop methods to determine the equilibrium strategy profiles of the suppliers, which can then be used to compare the expected prices paid by the procurer under the two policies.

## 3.1. Insights from Prior Papers and Intractability of the Game

Although the main objective of this section is to motivate the analytical intractability of the gametheoretical model, we also present key insights from prior works, which are useful in developing hypotheses in §5.1. Initially, with a view to studying both types of uncertainty, we began analyzing the implication of the information policies under each type of uncertainty separately. In Arora et al. (2007), we set $T = 2 , \rho = 1$ and analytically compared the expected prices paid by the procurer under cip and iip using a framework where homogeneous suppliers with identical cost structures faced only market-structure uncertainty. cip generated higher savings for the procurer than iip. The intuition for this result follows. Recall that at the end of the first period in cip, all bids are revealed, allowing suppliers to learn about the market structure independent of whether they win the first period. In contrast, only the winner’s bid is revealed at the end of the first period in iip, which implies that all suppliers except the first-period winner are aware of the presence of at least one competitor. However, the first-period winner continues to be uncertain about the market structure, even in the second period. To avoid this uncertainty in the second period, suppliers bid higher in the first period, leading to a higher expected price paid by the procurer in iip than in cip.

In Kannan and Krishnan (2003), we set T 2 and $\rho = 1$ with a view to compare the same two policies using a framework where heterogeneous suppliers with different cost structures are unaware of their opponents’ cost structure but are aware of the total number of competitors. Even under coststructure uncertainty, suppliers in iip behave in a manner similar to that under market-structure uncertainty. However, in cip, cost-structure uncertainty incentivizes low-cost suppliers to fake their costs when the probability of having a low-cost opponent is high. For an illustration, consider a marketplace with two low-cost suppliers and many high-cost suppliers. If a low-cost supplier fakes its cost structure, the other low-cost supplier, if present, may be tempted to bid a higher price in the following period. This higher price gives an opportunity for the faking supplier to “lowball” its opponent in the second period and generate higher total profits over both periods. As a result of faking, the expected price paid by the procurer can be higher in cip. In fact, cip is always found to have a higher expected price paid by the procurer than iip only for high values of 	.

If both of these studies had favored the same policy, the outcome of the policy comparison under both types of uncertainties would be obvious. Unfortunately, this is not the case (see Table 3). The problem studied in our current paper is equivalent to introducing market-structure uncertainty to the cost-structure uncertainty framework considered in Kannan and Krishnan (2003). However, the analysis under cost-structure uncertainty, even when the total number of participating suppliers is fixed, is executed numerically in Kannan and Krishnan (2003). In this paper, we adopt an alternate approach of simulating a marketplace with software agents, while retaining the core idea of profit maximization derived from economics. Our computational marketplace uses a distributed decision-making approach based on Markov decision processes instead of closed-form, game-theoretic analyses. Markov decision processes, which facilitate numerical optimization of stochastic models, incorporate strategic agent behavior. They permit analysis of more realistic and detailed scenarios than are possible using game-theoretic models. We provide a brief introduction to POMDP before presenting our formulation.

## 4. The Markov Modeling Approach

We use the game-theoretic formulation in §3, including the common-knowledge assumptions, as a guide to develop the computational marketplace. Each of the actors—the procurers, the broker, and suppliers— is modeled as an agent, which is a software implementation of a decision procedure. The procurer-agent chooses the lowest-priced supplier as its winner after obtaining bids from all participating suppliers in its market session. The broker-agent simply intermediates the marketplace. We also let the broker perform the Bernoulli trials that determine the set of suppliers that participate in a market session. A software implementation of a supplier-agent needs to incorporate the strategic bidding behavior. However, as mentioned in the previous paragraph, except for certain special cases (e.g., Arora et al. 2007), equilibrium strategies are not always available. Fortunately, there is active literature on distributed multiagent decision making that has examined alternatives to the analytic gametheoretic approaches. We draw from this literature to model each supplier-agent’s action of choosing a bid price as a POMDP and incorporate in the agents the capacity to learn.

## 4.1. The POMDP Model

Markov games, a generalization of Markov decision processes to the multiagent setting, provide a natural way to model the interactions of multiple agents, where joint actions can affect rewards stochastically (e.g., Littman 1994, Hu and Wellman 1998, Kephart and Greenwald 1999). Indeed, our setting is naturally modelled as a Markov game, because the price bid (action) by a supplier stochastically affects its own profits (reward) and the profits of others. Similar to Littman and Stone (2001), we model each supplier’s bidding behavior as an MDP, specifically, a POMDP.

Any Markov model formulation involves characterization of an objective function, an action space, and a state space (Howard 1960). Each agent faces a decision problem, namely, to optimize its objective function by choosing an action from its action space. At each decision point, the available actions depend on the state space (or the information available).

In our context, each supplier-agent chooses a bid price so as to maximize its total expected profit. The value of the bid submitted can be trivially shown to be $p \in \left[ c _ { l } , c _ { h } \right] . ^ { 1 4 }$ It can also be shown that each supplier will not bid less than its cost. Thus, the high-cost supplier will always bid $c _ { h }$ while a low-cost supplier may choose any $p \leq c _ { h }$ . The specific value of the bid should depend on the following state variables: the time period, the presence of a low-cost opponent in the current period, and the presence of a high-cost opponent in the current period.

4.1.1. State Space. Not all of the aforementioned state variables are observable. Specifically, the presence of a low- or a high-cost opponent is not directly observable. Hence, we use a POMDP model of bidding behavior, where each supplier forms beliefs about the unobservable variables. These beliefs are updated depending on the bids revealed at the end of the previous market session, which in turn depend on the revelation policy. Note that because the updated beliefs have an impact on future bids, agents may have incentives to fake their costs and manipulate the beliefs of their opponents. Therefore, when beliefs are part of the state space, the impact of a supplier faking also needs to be considered.

The state space for a supplier-agent in our POMDP model would be exhaustive with: a vector of beliefs about the number of opponents of its own type; another belief vector for the total number of opponents, the T periods so as to identify the ongoing period; and, last, if the supplier had faked in the previous period, whether its faking had any impact on its competitors’ beliefs. Instead, we consider a reduced set of state-space variables shown in Table 4. The reduction is achieved by taking into account the information required to bid under cip and iip under the different types of uncertainty, and we explain below why the reduced set of state-space variables is sufficient in our POMDP model.

## 4.2. Ratio-Q Algorithm

The optimization problem in Equation (2) can be solved analytically if the expressions for $\Omega _ { S  S ^ { \prime } }$ are available in terms of $\theta , k ,$ and T . Because the equilibrium bid distributions (i.e., the F p) are not known ex ante, $\Omega _ { S  S ^ { \prime } } ^ { p }$ is not known ex ante either. Also, because we cannot always obtain closed-form expressions for the bid distributions (as mentioned in §3.1), $\Omega _ { S  S ^ { \prime } } ^ { p }$ is not always available. In situations, where closed-form expressions for $\Omega _ { S  S ^ { \prime } }$ are unavailable, reinforcementlearning (RL) algorithms have been used to computationally solve Equation (2) (Sutton and Barto 1998).

The typical algorithm proceeds with the agent choosing one of the actions. The probability of an action choice increases with the Q-value of the action for a given state. The agent observes the response to its action, which is dictated by the environment (i.e., the bids placed by other suppliers in our simulation). This response feedback adjusts the Q-value of the action taken. By interacting with the environment over a number of iterations and by repeatedly adjusting the Q-values, these algorithms empirically “learn” the transition probabilities and the optimal strategy profile. Typically, RL algorithms such as soft-Q and Sarsa are used to identify pure strategy equilibria in singleagent settings. However, our multiagent framework, where the response from the environment is determined by the actions of the other agents, requires an algorithm that identifies a mixed-strategy equilibrium because closed-form expressions even for special cases are found to involve mixed-strategy equilibria. (e.g., Narasimhan 1988 derives a mixed-strategy equilibrium in a cost-structure uncertainty framework corresponding to $T = 2 , \ \rho = 1$ , and $M = 2 ,$ , and so do Arora et al. 2007 and Kannan 2003 for their respective setups). This requirement leads us to develop a variant of the Q-learning algorithm, ratio-Q. Both ratio-Q and the standard soft-Q algorithms are shown in Figure 1 and the details are presented below.

The input is shown in step 1 of Figure 1. Even in our case, the algorithm learns by repeatedly adjusting the Q-values (step 17) over multiple iterations (steps between 10 and 23 in Figure 1 correspond to one iteration). Given that the current period is also a state variable in our formulation and learning in one period is dependent on the learning in the other periods, each iteration consists of T market sessions. The key point to note is that the impact from the information revelation occurs in step 16. Reward $R _ { S  S ^ { \prime } } ^ { p _ { i } } ,$ independent of the revelation policy, takes either 0 if the agent loses or $p _ { i } - c _ { i }$ if it wins. So, the actual difference is in how the state changes with the information revealed. All of the state variables in Table 4, except for the belief terms, undergo a straightforward update. The Bayesian update of the beliefs, in this computational environment, has to now be executed empirically. For this, we assume that the suppliers are aware of the bids submitted in the previous iterations. Note that we are not assuming knowledge about the bids from the previous periods in the current iteration. The knowledge available to a supplier-agent is similar to the common-knowledge assumptions in a standard gametheoretic model, where a player can infer the optimal strategy profiles of other players.<sup>15</sup> Therefore, revealing bids submitted in the previous iterations does not interfere with analyzing the effect of an information revelation policy. As an illustration, reconsider Example 1. Here, b 0 is determined as the ratio of the number of market sessions in which the agent was the only supplier to the number of market sessions in which the agent would have won with a bid of $p _ { 1 }$ . Similarly, for the Example 1 scenario but under cip, b 0 is updated to 1 or 0, depending on whether the other supplier is observed. Details on the update procedure are provided in Appendix D, step 17, over multiple iterations,<sup>16</sup> sets the Q-values to the expectation of the total reward. In that step, the second term is the feedback to adjust to the Q-value. By comparing the numerator of that term and the Bellman equation, it can be seen that the first two terms define the reward obtained from being in state S. Even though transition probability $\Omega _ { S  S ^ { \prime } }$ appears to be missing, the state transitions are computationally accounted for over multiple iterations as is typical with any RL algorithm (Sutton and Barto 1998). The eventual output of the algorithm is the bid distribution learned by the agent. A bid distribution is simply the probability distribution with which prices are bid. See Appendix E for a numerical illustration of the ratio-Q algorithm.<sup>17</sup> In a market session with agents bidding prices from these distributions, the procurer chooses the bid with the lowest price. The average of these prices over the market sessions is the expected price that we use for our comparison.

As a first step, we are interested in comparing the bid distributions learned by supplier-agents implementing the soft-Q and the ratio-Q algorithms. As we demonstrate below, ratio-Q determines mixedstrategy equilibrium better than soft-Q. Thus, it also serves to make a case for the validity of results obtained from implementing the ratio-Q algorithm.

Why does ratio-Q perform better than soft-Q? From lines 27 and 30 in Figure 1, it is clear that the algorithms differ only in the manner in which prices are selected.<sup>2021</sup> Because the prices chosen are such that higher probabilities are assigned to those with higher Q-values, the proof of the convergence for soft-Q also holds for ratio-Q. However, because the standard-Q algorithm plugs the Q-value of the action directly into the exponential term, even very small variations in the Q-values can result in huge differences in the probability with which the actions are chosen. In a scenario involving mixed-strategy equilibrium the Q-values corresponding to actions that are part of the strategy set should be the same. ## 5. Results

Recall that we are interested in understanding the impact of the information policy on an important market design consideration, namely, the price paid by the procurer. Specifically, our interest is in how the difference in the expected price paid by a procurer under cip and iip varies with  and T when each type of uncertainty is considered separately and together (note that $\rho$ only captures the explicit correlation between suppliers participating across market sessions). We first hypothesize about the expected differences in §5.1. We develop the hypotheses based on insights gained from prior works that are described in §3.1. In §5.2, we test the hypotheses using the data collected from market sessions executed on our computational marketplace. The metric used in our analysis is the average price paid to the suppliers across all the procurers in the T periods conditional on the presence of at least one supplier. Formally, if $p _ { \phi ,  }$ <sub>t</sub> is the expected price paid by the procurer at period t under the policy $\phi$ (where $\phi \in \left\{ \mathrm { C I P } , \mathrm { I I P } \right\} \mathrm { ) }$ , our comparison metric is $\begin{array} { r } { P _ { \phi } = ( 1 / T ) \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { T } p _ { \phi , i } } \end{array}$

## 5.1. Hypotheses

Most related prior works have not analyzed the impact of varying participation correlation, the factor that determines how much suppliers should value learning across market sessions. Their assumptions correspond to $\rho = 1$ (i.e, exactly the same set of suppliers repeatedly participate across market sessions). When $\rho = 0 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ each market session is independent of the other), there is no value in learning. As discussed before, the expected price paid by the procurer in cip is lower than in iip when $\rho = 1$ under market-structure uncertainty. This stems from the incentive that suppliers have in iip to avoid winning the first period. As $\rho$ decreases, suppliers in iip have reduced incentives to increase their first period price to learn their market structure. This leads to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Under market-structure uncertainty, as participation correlation decreases, the difference between the expected prices of iip and cip decreases; i.e., $\partial ( P _ { _ \mathrm { I I P } } - P _ { _ \mathrm { C I P } } ) / \partial \rho > 0$

In multiperiod games (reverse auctions), the total number of periods considered dictates the bidding behavior (Engelbrecht-Wiggans 2001). Under marketstructure uncertainty, recall that suppliers in cip learn about their market-structure at the end of each market session. Because their learning is independent of the outcome of the market session (i.e., whether they won or lost), their bidding behaviors are not altered. However, only the winner’s bid is revealed to all participating suppliers in iip. Thus, the value that suppliers obtain from losing initial market sessions to gain informational advantage is higher as T increases and therefore we submit the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. When suppliers face market-structure uncertainty, the difference between the expected prices paid by the procurer in iip and cip increases as T increases; i.e., $\partial ( P _ { _ { \mathrm { I I P } } } - P _ { _ { \mathrm { C I P } } } ) / { \partial T } > 0 .$

Next consider cost-structure uncertainty. Recall that, in iip, suppliers have incentive to learn about their opponents and this leads to increased expected prices. In cip, suppliers may have an incentive to fake their opponents. However, their incentive to gain an informational advantage under both policies is lower when the set of opponents is not consistent across market sessions. Thus, for reasons similar to that presented for Hypothesis 1, we expect the following hypothesis to be the case.

Hypothesis 3. Under cost-structure uncertainty, the difference in the expected prices paid by the procurer in cip and iip decreases as participation correlation decreases; $i . e . , \partial \big ( | P _ { _ \mathrm { I I P } } - P _ { _ \mathrm { C I P } } | \big ) / \partial \rho > 0 ,$

The key difference from Hypothesis 1 is that, because cip here may also generate higher expected prices, we only hypothesize about the absolute value of the difference and not the actual value.

Note that suppliers value learning or faking only if they gain informational advantage for future periods. So it is obvious that these advantages increase for earlier market sessions as T increases. However, the advantage realized is restricted in cip because all bids are revealed. Faking does not serve any value if at least two suppliers reveal their low-cost types. As T increases, the chance of at least two of them revealing their low-cost types is higher. In contrast, iip does not encounter similar issues because only the winner’s bid is revealed. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 4. When suppliers face cost-structure uncertainty, the difference between the expected prices paid by the procurer in iip and cip increases with T ; i.e., $\partial ( | P _ { \mathrm { I I P } } - P _ { \mathrm { C I P } } | ) / \partial T > 0$

Similarly to Hypothesis $^ { 3 , }$ we only hypothesize about the absolute value of the difference.

Last, it is interesting to observe whether cip is better than iip when both types of uncertainties are combined and we hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 5. When suppliers face both types of uncertainties, the expected price paid by the procurer will be higher in iip than in cip; i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { P _ { \mathrm { I I P } } > P _ { \mathrm { C I P } } . } \end{array}$

The reasoning is as follows. As mentioned earlier, the effect of learning in iip under both marketstructure and cost-structure uncertainties is expected to augment each other when considering both types of uncertainty; but the effect of faking in cip occurs only under specific conditions of cost-structure uncertainty. We expect this to be the only effect under cip when both types of uncertainties are combined. So when combining both these types of uncertainties, we expect the augmented effect of learning to override the faking effect.

## 5.2. Hypothesis Validation

We test these hypotheses by executing market sessions on the computational marketplace, described in §4.2.1, with supplier-agents implementing the ratio-Q algorithm. Recall that the outcome from executing market sessions is the bid distribution, which determines the expected price paid by the procurer. Parameters for this computational model are derived from real marketplaces and based on interactions with Freemarkets. Specifically, they are set to be similar to the coal reverse-marketplace. Typically, about 12–15 suppliers can provide coal to a region. Consistent with that observation, our simulation assumes the number of low-cost suppliers to be $N _ { l } = 5 ,$ and the number of high-cost suppliers to be $N _ { h } = 6$ . We set the costs incurred, $c _ { l } = 0$ and $c _ { h } = 1 0 0 _ { \AA }$ , and $\lambda = 1 0 . ^ { 2 2 }$ When we deal with market-structure uncertainty in isolation, participating suppliers are of the same type and no uncertainty about opponents’ cost is assumed to exist. Only the low-cost suppliers are assumed to participate. When we model cost-structure uncertainty, we assume that the uncertainty about the number of competitors does not exist and we retain the number of competitors to six (approximately the same number of coal suppliers in a market session convened at Freemarkets). The design of the experiment involves simulating market sessions for every combination of $\theta \in \{ 0 . 2 5 , 0 . 5 , 0 . 7 5 \} , \ \rho \in \{ 0 . 6 , 0 . 8 , 1 \}$ , and $T \in \{ 2 , 3 , 4 \}$ We believe that the quality of the insights is fully supported by testing these combinations. We sample the range of 	 to understand its impact. However, we focus only on high values of $\rho$ because offline experiments indicate that much of the variation in the expected prices is found to occur only at high $\rho$ values. Last, we restrict ourselves to smaller T values because $T$ is part of the state-space and increasing it would imply a significant increase in learning time. For each combination of parameter settings, the simulation runs for about 3–5 days on a 3.06-GHz Pentium processor with 1 GB of RAM.

Observe that our hypotheses deal with the difference $D = P _ { _ \mathrm { I I P } } - P _ { _ \mathrm { C I P } }$ or the sensitivity of D under the three scenarios—market-structure uncertainty, coststructure uncertainty, and both. In each case, the results are tabulated with the numbers in the parentheses indicating the standard deviation of the mean. The differences are validated for statistical significance using the t-test, and the significance level is 5% by default.

## 6. Managerial Implications and Summary

This paper addresses an important IS-enabled realworld problem. A procurer wishing to use an electronic reverse-marketplace needs guidance to choose the appropriate information revelation policy. In line with this motivation, our paper studies the effect of information revelation policies using a framework that is close to reality—where suppliers face uncertainty about both their market structure and their opponents’ cost structure. Specifically, we compare the expected price paid by the procurer in cip and iip, two of the many policies available to a procurer in a reverse market hosted by Ariba.

We execute these comparisons computationally. Similar economic problems have been studied using computational models in other contexts (e.g., Kephart and Greenwald 1999). Our setup is unique in being closely tied to the analytical framework while at the same time providing opportunities to extend beyond the limitations of the analytical model. The ratio-Q algorithm we present can approximately identify the analytically optimal bidding behavior for each supplier under the same assumptions as the analytical model. However, when extending the game-theoretic framework by combining both types of uncertainties or relaxing the assumptions of the game-theoretic model, we cannot analytically derive the optimal bidding behavior of the suppliers. Given that our machine learning algorithm traces the true optimal behavior under the assumptions of the analytical model, it offers a valid methodology to study the multiagent bidding behavior under realistic assumptions. Note that our computational model and our results are applicable even to forward auction setups involving such types of uncertainties. However, we chose to retain the reverse-auction context because of the widespread use of information revelation policies under that context.

Using the computational model, we learned that the reaction to market-structure uncertainty dominates the reaction to cost uncertainty. This implies that cip should be preferred over iip from a procurer surplus perspective. In addition to this, our analysis provides valuable insights into how the participation correlation across market sessions affects the expected price paid by the procurer. For example, consider a reverse marketplace for metal castings. There are about 2,000 U.S.-based metal-casting suppliers registered with Freemarkets. In a typical reverse-auction market session for metal-castings, about 20 of them are invited to participate. The participation correlation across market sessions for these suppliers is observed to be low. In this case, our results suggest that suppliers in cip and iip behave identically and the procurer should be indifferent between the two policies. On the other hand, in a marketplace for coal, there are a few suppliers and only a subset of them participate. The participation correlation across market sessions is higher and suppliers value learning. In this case, the procurer is better off choosing cip over iip. This example illustrates the applicability of our results to real-world contexts.

Even though we consider the analysis of cip and iip to be a contribution, some of the insights gained from our analysis are useful in understanding the implications under other revelation policies as well. For example, it is probably clear that our cip is quite close to a policy in which information is openly fed-back to the bidders. It may be less clear how our paper provides insights into the bidder behavior when information about ranks is the feedback to the supplier. Note that we find that the expected prices are higher if the winning supplier is not aware of the presence of other low-cost buyers. Such a scenario can happen when information about the rank is the only feedback. Given that our analysis points to the effect of market-structure uncertainty dominating the effect of cost-structure uncertainty, one should expect higher expected prices when information about rank is revealed. This observation is consistent with Koppius (2002). It is also consistent with

Freemarkets’ observation that bidders try to bid very high initially when rank information is the feedback.

The interesting feature of our computational model is that it has provisions to combine both types of uncertainty and to alter the time period and the participation correlation across market sessions. These are not feasible game-theoretically. However, note that our computational model lends itself to comparing other types of information revelation policy as well. In that sense, the computational approach acts as a bridge between theoretical and experimental study, an important benefit as noted by Simon (1990) and Geoffrion and Krishnan (2002).

Another interesting contribution of the paper is the ratio-Q algorithm. Our algorithm is capable of identifying mixed-strategy equilibria. We believe that its capacity is not restricted to our specific setting; it should be applicable to other settings as well so long as common knowledge assumption is valid and they can be accommodated in the learning mechanism. Making arguments about the validity of the algorithm in other settings constitutes an interesting future research problem.

Our paper is not without its limitations. The primary limitation is our inability to endogenize participation probability. To understand this better, note that suppliers in our model learn by trying different prices and observing the resulting profit outcome. When participation probability is also endogenized, suppliers cannot tell whether the change in the profit outcome they observe stems from the change in the prices bid by their opponents or from the change in the opponent’s participation probability. It is one of the key extensions that we are considering as a part of future work. Other possible extensions are as follows: (a) This paper compares only two of the many policies facilitated in reverse marketplaces. We intend to extend this work to study other policies facilitated in electronic reverse marketplaces. (b) In this paper, we were focused on analyzing the procurer’s expected price paid. One can analyze the variations in the equilibrium bidding behavior as an extension. (c) In the current model, the procurers do not have the opportunity to learn; it would be interesting to consider the strategic actions of the procurers. (d) Other factors such as reputation effects can also be studied. (e) In addition to these extensions, we also intend to focus on the ratio-Q algorithm and study its properties. (f) Another important extension is to empirically validate the results in this paper.
