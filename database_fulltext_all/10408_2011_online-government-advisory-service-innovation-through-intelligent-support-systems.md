# Online government advisory service innovation through Intelligent Support Systems<sup>§</sup>

Manning Li \*

Department of Computing, Faculty of Science, Macquarie University, NSW 2109, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 5 February 2009 Received in revised form 18 April 2010 Accepted 1 July 2010 Available online 21 December 2010

Keywords: Online advisory tools e-Government Decision making Empowerment Transparency

## A B S T R A C T

Motivated by lessons learnt from countries with a high e-government readiness index, we used the lens of ‘empowerment’ to understand the ways in which online Intelligent Support Systems can be successfully developed. We found that decision transparency and decision making satisfaction played key roles providing a higher sense of empowerment to the public, including their having a greater perceived power relationship with the government agency and a better feeling of being in control. This approach has improved citizens’ positive perceptions of the government agencies as service providers. These findings make a strong case for the investment in the ISS and have provided useful information for government agencies who wish to develop or improve their online advisory services.

## 1. Introduction

The public administration sector has recently started to provide online Intelligent Support Systems (ISS) on government agency portals, especially in countries like Australia and Canada whose egovernment readiness index is high. Online ISS are web-based advisory systems that assist citizens in obtaining and interpreting information about their country’s e-government material, to selfassess their eligibility for aid, etc., and to make informed decisions on whether to proceed to the next stage of their application process. In practice ISS range from relatively simple tools, like smart financial calculators, to complex pension advisory systems incorporating compound rule bases.

When appropriately designed, ISS can improve the public’s experience with the government agency and help in establishing a long term engaging and trusted relationship. Such tools can be an effective way to ease the strain between the citizens’ need for more efficient and higher quality public service and the previously cumbersome, inconsistent and arbitrary decision making provided by government officials [5].

Although ISS have been extensively studied, most research has focused on organizational users; there has been a dearth of studies on the value of ISS tools for public use. Our study was aimed at filling this gap. Therefore, we took a citizen’s perspective to answer three questions:

(1) Can online advisory services supplied by government agencies lead to potential benefits for the public?

(2) If yes, what are the benefits?

(3) Do the optimized explanation capabilities of ISS increase the benefits?

To limit the scope of the study, we did not consider the digital divide or related issues such as access and convenience of the ISS. Despite their importance, we were concerned with how the advisory systems compared with other means of decision support that might be available, such as online manuals. We therefore limited our investigation to situations where alternative means of advice were accessible to Internet users.

## 2. Research framework and hypothesis development

We developed a mid-range theoretical model: such models are moderately abstract, have limited scope, and can easily lead to testable hypotheses [10]. This process involves the four activities:

a) understanding the problem area based on reports or practica experience;

b) examining relevant literature and theory;

c) preliminary data collection to ground understanding; and

d) iterative development of the model, where each results in adjustments in thinking, understanding, and refinement of the model.

An initial exploration of the problem was carried out to ensure that the proposed framework was grounded in reality. This involved examining papers and reports from industry and studying online ISS systems deployed on government portals, together with informal interviews with government agency staff to provide insights into the value of having online ISS for the general public. A notable practical paper by Zeleznikow [18] on online decision support systems in the UK used case studies to show that the provision of web-based legal support systems helped citizens, especially those who were unrepresented and vulnerable, to negotiate their legal disputes. Such systems can improve people’s access to justice and avoid potential problems in the legal systems.

Informal interviews with government agency staffs (Centrelink, Australian Taxation office and the Department of Health and Aging), who had been regularly interacting with and receiving feedback from citizens on their opinions of online services, frequently mentioned that there was a citizens’ sense of ‘‘empowerment’’ when using online ISS services on government agency websites.

By scrutinizing the nature and use of online ISS or practices on government portals we determined that such systems are used over a period of time. To a certain extent, this suggests that they are seen as useful by some citizens and deliver value. The systems that we examined included ELMNet (see Dayal and Johnson’s [5] paper) from the Department of Veterans’ Affairs (DVA), the Eligibility Points Test and Requirements calculator from the Department of Immigration and Citizenship, various eligibility testing tools on the Centrelink website and different tax calculators from the Australian Taxation Office (ATO). Among these, eTax was regarded by citizens as a very popular online self-service. Also, based on a study by Centrelink, online self-service tools were accepted by citizens and there were millions of self-service transactions in 2005 and 2006.

Some systems were highly usable, especially in the way that provided explanations. Initial discussions of the value of online ISS with users of government websites showed that such explanations were very helpful. Thus, the government’s provision of appropriate explanations raised the value of the online ISS.

Consequently our review raised several suggestions:

\- Online ISS can be valuable to the general public: they improve the data collection process, reduce complexity, enable selfservice, increase the public’s decision making satisfaction and create a sense of citizens’ ownership in the decision making process.

\- Self-services allow for an increase in the public’s sense of control of the decision making process and therefore help to boost the government agency’s image as a service provider.

\- The current online advisory systems that remain popular contribute value to the user.

## 2.1. Empowerment theory

Empowerment plays a very important role in virtual contexts where stakeholders do not have face-to-face interaction [11]. Due to a scarcity of systematic research on empowerment in the IS field, we drew on empowerment theory and psychology. There are two complementary perspectives to empowerment, the macro-level, which focuses on the social-structures that enable it, and the micro-level, which reflects the individual’s psychological experience. In our study, we examine psychological empowerment (PE), representing the process, through which individuals in organizations or communities gain mastery over issues of concern to them.

Among the commonly addressed dimensions for empowerment, sense of impact (i.e., influence) and self-determination (i.e., autonomy or choice) are deemed important and relevant. These dimensions caused us to include citizen’s perceived power and sense of control over their own destiny, during the exploration stage of the study.

Empowerment theory can be a useful theoretical lens to assist in understanding how to minimize the gap between different power groups and balance their powers. In the literature, the empowerment construct has been widely used to examine the powerlessness of minority groups such as women, blacks, or the handicapped. Frequently, sharing of knowledge and decentralization of decision making power is central to the empowerment process. In our study, the government agency’s provision of online ISS facilitated distribution of the decision-making power and knowledge sharing. However, such a decentralizing process can be extremely challenging, since it involves the empowerer’s delegation of authority as well as the acceptance and appropriate use of this authority by the person who has been empowered.

It is also possible to analyze the power dynamics of the key stakeholders in a problem context. Power and inequality go handin-hand, organization actors who possess more power can more easily achieve their desired outcomes. In the context of administrative decision-making in the public sector, the public is not likely to consider that they are in a good position to influence the outcomes. Further, decision outcomes are not perceived as favorable for people having little power. Online ISS can thus increase the perceived power of citizens. A well-designed online ISS tool also reduces the complexity of interpreting complex rule bases, assisting the public in progressing from having the right to know to the capacity to understand and influence.

## 2.1.1. Empowering process: decision support effectiveness

Accessing resources is one of the key aspects of the empowering process: the effectiveness of advisory tools on a government portal becomes the empowering enabler of the effect. By providing the public with more effective online advisory services, the government achieves its goal of making the general citizens feel empowered. Prior research on decision support and knowledgebased systems suggested that services that are of high decision quality, perceived as more satisfactory and transparent, will be more effective in leading to empowerment outcomes [5,12]. Congruent with the concepts raised during exploratory stage, our research therefore examined decision advice satisfaction, decision advice transparency, and decision quality as empowering enablers.

## 2.1.2. Empowerment outcomes: public’s perceptions of the government–citizen relationship

The government agency’s providing effective online selfassessment tools to citizens are expected to lead to empowerment outcomes: power and control. Thus they should result in: (1) better sense of perceived power relationships and (2) a greater sense of control by citizens over their own destiny in application prospects.

## 2.2. Preliminary interviews

To triangulate our research findings and enhance our understanding of the key constructs, we conducted semi-structured interviews with the public who utilized such services. This ensured that our findings were grounded in practice and gave us confidence in pursuing the topic

## 3. Research hypotheses and model

## 3.1. Types of advisory support

Our study involved the use of three types of advisory tools on egovernment websites:

(1) Online ISS with optimized explanations;

(2) online ISS with limited explanations; and

(3) text-based systems.

This was the major variable manipulated in our experiment

## 3.2. Decision making satisfaction (DMS)

User satisfaction has long been considered an important surrogate for IS success [17]. Decision making satisfaction was defined as users’ subjective state of satisfaction gained from decision making using online ISS [16]. An appropriately designed ISS can boosts users’ confidence and problem-solving effectiveness, which have been seen as key dimensions in DMS [1]. Thus, compared with text-based systems, online use of ISS should improve the DMS of the public. Further, optimized explanations have been shown to be positively correlated with higher user perceptions [8]. Therefore, ISS with optimized explanations is expected to outperform ISS with limited explanations in terms of decision making satisfaction, resulting in:

H1a. Online ISS leads to higher DMS on government portal than text-based systems.

H1b. Online ISS with optimized explanations leads to better DMS than ISS with limited explanations.

## 3.3. Decision making transparency (DMT)

DMT refers to the extent to which the users perceive the underlying reasoning of the decision-making process to be comprehensible. It requires the ISS to present the decision maker with the premises (normative and factual) and to explain the reasons leading from them to the decision so that the user has sufficient information to understand the process. Apart from DMS, the added maturity of the user in understanding the decision can also be seem as a major result of using the ISS systems. Compared with text-based systems, ISS can absorb the complexity of rules and regulations, reduce potential human bias, and improve transparency. Further, many researchers have found that the provision of explanations contributes to learning, understanding or informed decision making [8]. Since understanding and explanations are tied, ISS with explanations are expected to improve users’ understanding of the decision-making process and enhance DMT.

H2a. Online ISS leads to higher DMT on a government portal than that on text-based systems.

H2b. Online ISS with optimized explanations leads to higher DMT on a government portal than ISS with limited explanations.

## 3.4. Decision quality (DQ)

DQ refers to the performance of the system with respect to its accuracy in decision making; while perceived decision quality refers to the situation where the decision maker feels that the difficulty of the decision making process lowers the quality of the decision. DQ was chosen as a dependent variable of interest because quality should be of utmost concern in an ISS. We chose to scrutinize objective decision quality rather than perceived decision quality, because objective decision quality, compared with user evaluations of the system (perceived system quality), is a better surrogate for the objective performance of the system.

In the literature, there have been confounding results reported on the impact of ISS use on performance. Some researchers have found no significant improvements in decision quality through the use of ISS, while others saw an increase in decision quality by users [14]. Our study therefore examined this issue by trying to determine what kinds of ISS would lead to better performance.

We expected an increase in decision quality if the decision maker utilized online ISS provided by government agencies. Gregor and Benbasat found that explanation use in ISS was positively correlated with better user performance. Online ISS may help reduce user errors in reasoning, for instance those due to poor understanding.

H3a. Online ISS lead to higher decision quality on government portals than text-based systems.

H3b. Online ISS with optimized explanations lead to higher DQ than ISS with limited explanations.

## 3.5. Sense of control (SoC)

SoC refers to the extent that an individual believes his or her voluntary activity can lead to a desired outcome. A SoC is critical for the general public when using online government services especially during self-assessment. It is one of the key outcomes of empowerment. Dayal and Johnson claimed that one of the compelling benefits that self-assessment could bring to clients was SoC, because people are worried about filling in an application and ‘‘being held to account for any mistakes they make on the forms’’; thus the apparent risk can be drastically reduced when all questions are fully explained. Hence, online ISS, can also provide timely and relevant feedback, increasing the user’s sense of control.

Research on online marketing behaviour has demonstrated similar results. Koufaris studied a company which provided recommendation agents for customers to easily locate choose and purchase favorable products online. These site features were demonstrated to result in online consumers enjoying high levels of control and convenience. Thus on government agency portals, ISS with effective decision support capabilities that increase the predictability of the assessment outcomes of citizen’s application, were expected to be positively related to SoC.

H4a. Higher DMS of online ISS leads to higher SoC for citizens using it on government portal.

H4b. Higher DMT of online ISS leads to higher SoC for citizens using it on government portal.

H4c. Higher DQ of online ISS leads to higher SoC for citizens using it on government portal.

3.6. Perceived power relationships (PPR)

PPR is the other key empowerment outcome; it refers to a member of the public’s perception of his or her power situation compared to that of the government agency. Having online advisory services such as ISS on government portals can impact the general public’s perceived power in decision making relative to that of the government agency. Dayal and Johnson felt that online ISS can fundamentally influence the power relationship: when people visit a government agency with very little knowledge of the process, they perceive themselves as having little power compared to that of the government officials. Whereas a properly designed online advisory tool provide a transparent and satisfactory decision making process. This leads to the hypotheses that effective online decision support (i.e., high DMS, DMT and DQ) can lead to better perceptions of PPR.

H5a. Higher DMS of online ISS leads to improved PPR for citizens using it on government portal.

H5b. Higher DMT of online ISS leads to improved PPR for citizens using it on government portal.

H5c. Higher DQ of online ISS leads to improved PPR for citizens using it on government portal.

## 3.7. Government service provider image (GSPI)

GSPI refers to the public’s perception of the government agency as a service provider because of their use of online portals. It is closely related to service quality, a dynamic construct that varies over time. Early research efforts extensively investigated service quality in a series of focus group interviews. Others conceptualized service quality including the Kano model [13] and e-SERVQUAL [4], though with little consensus. Our study was intended to capture the public’s initial impressions of the service quality of a government agency when using a web portal without face-toface contact.

The extension of online ISS to GSPI of the government can be explained by the Halo effect, which is a term used to describe phenomena in which a person tends to make similar evaluations in all dimensions based on a general overall impression. One example occurs when a job applicant does not know much about the organization, then organizational website design characteristics are likely to influence their choice [3]. Similarly, providing effective online ISS on the government portal may be expected to lead to citizen’s perceptions that the government agency strives to deliver a higher quality of service to the general public. Therefore, we hypothesize that the better the decision support effectiveness of online decisions, the better the GSPI will be for citizens using it.

On the other hand, it is also reasonable to argue that through the sense of empowerment, citizens can form better perceptions of the government as a service provider. Indeed, prior studies found that the public’s self-assessment of being in control could insulate them from the adverse effect of stress and consequently enhance their commitment and trust to government [2]. Similarly, increase in the SoC may lead to constructive impact on the public’s perception of the government agency as a service provider. Also, using a government agency’s online service, citizens who perceive themselves to be in a better power situation are more likely to have a better perception of the GSPI.

H5a. Higher DMS of online ISS leads to better GSPI on government for citizens using it on government portal.

H5b. Higher DMT of online ISS leads to better GSPI on government for citizens using it on government portal.

H6a. Higher SoC leads to better GSPI on the government agency.

H6b. Better PPR leads to better GSPI on the government agency.

Based on these hypotheses, the Government Advisory Service Delivery Model (Fig. 1) synthesizes feedbacks from practice and literature and is underpinned by the theory of empowerment.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Participants

One hundred and seventy one people participated in the experiment. They were recruited from several companies and educational institutions in Shenyang, China. Participants were informed by their supervisors about the opportunity to participate.

This sampling strategy ensured that respondents were Internet users from a wide range of ages, cultural background, and occupations. Of the 171 questionnaires gathered, 9 were removed due to problems such as errors found from consistency checking or incompleteness. The remaining 162 (93 males and 69 females; 140 Asian and 22 Westerners; 53 younger than 20, 82 in their twenties and 27 more than 30 years old) shared similar distribution characteristics to the general internet user profiles in terms of gender, age and education levels. Subjects were given a free gift in appreciation of their effort. Experimental sessions were supervised.

## 4.2. Experiment design and procedures

A between-subject design was used. The treatment variable was the type of support available on the e-government website. There were three treatment groups. The first was forwarded to a website that provided ISS with optimized explanation aids to help the subjects. The second was forwarded to a website that provided an ISS with limited explanation. The third was forwarded to a website that provided text-based explanations. Participants could choose the language with which they felt more comfortable (English or Chinese) by simply clicking on a link at the top-right corner of the tool. Subjects were randomly divided into one of the three groups. Appendix A outlines all the independent and dependent variables.

Experiments were conducted in computer laboratories. The experiment supervisors first reminded all subjects that their participation was the voluntary and told them the objectives of our study. Then, subjects were given a hypothetical case scenario, containing information such as the date of registration of their business, which they used while filling out the self-assessment. They were first asked to read the case scenarios, after which, they followed the instructions and used one of the experimental websites to complete their task. At the end of the study, the participants completed a questionnaire intended to capture their feelings about their experience.

The three websites simulated self-assessment tools provided by the Ministry of Commerce of a hypothetical country; a hypothetical government agency was used to remove, as much as possible, any pre-existing perceptions of the participants. Removing this pre-imposed attitude allowed us to be more accurate in capturing the effect of providing different decision aids in measuring the citizen’s perception of the hypothetical agency.

The websites were designed after scrutinizing existing online advisory tools in Australia. All the decision rules underlying the tool were developed by consulting real government agency assessment rules. Thus the impact of using a hypothetical government was minimized.

This online ISS tool was developed with the needs of the customer in mind: clear and concise explanations automatically appeared immediately below a question, once the ‘‘explanation’’ button next to it had been clicked. Further, this structured and organized way of presenting explanations also reduced the respondents’ cognitive load. Discussions with pilot participants showed that the explanations were easily understood and helpful.

The second tool, providing an online ISS with limited explanations, differed from the first by removing justifications and explanations. Users had no way of understanding the reasons beyond the final assessment outcomes. This low level of transparency was obviously an obstacle for applicants in carrying out the self-assessment task. There were no other interfering factors that could impact on the results of the experiment.

The last online self-assessment tool used a traditional electronic PDF document still commonly used on many government portals. The document, titled ‘‘provisions for Labor Service Cooperation with Foreign Parties’’, listed all the rules and regulations in a single file. It took the participant ‘‘much patience and energy’’ to find out his or her eligibility, as stated by a pilot test respondent. Overall, this text-based support system carried identical underlying rule bases to the other types of support tools.

Pilot tests were carried out to ensure that the process was smooth and that the level of difficulty of the case scenarios was appropriate. Nine people participated in the pilot test. The whole process, including briefing, conducting self-assessment (15– 20 min) and filling in questionnaires (5 min), was less than 40 min in total and deemed reasonable. Different case scenarios with varying levels of assessment difficulty had been incorporated into the pilot to examine the impact of ‘‘task complexity’’, which was a controlled variable. Pilot subjects provided valuable feedback.

## 4.3. Measures

Except for GSPI, all constructs were derived from existing scales in the reviewed literature, which exhibited good psychometric properties. DQ was measured as ‘‘the number of correctly answered questions’’ by each participant divided by ‘‘total number of self-assessment questions’’. GSPI was a new construct based on systematic construct development guidelines in the IS field: a three stage model began by exploring the domain and dimension of a construct, continued by purifying the items and then statistically validated and finally confirmed the measurement scales. The resultant construct exhibited good psychometric properties in terms of validity and reliability

## 5. Data analysis and results

To improve the performance of the statistical analysis, several aspects of the data screening process were performed. Checking for incompleteness or failure to pass the consistency check required us to remove nine surveys from the 171 cases collected. Further, the relationships between demographic factors (gender, language used and age) and dependent variables were investigated through independent T-tests and ANOVA using SPSS 15.0 (see Appendix B). The result showed that among different demographic groups there was no significant difference in their response about the dependent variables of interest.

After going through data screening, the hypotheses and research model of the study were ready to be tested. First, hypotheses H1, H2, H3 were examined using ANOVA to reveal the impact of providing different online self-assessment tools (i.e., type of support, a categorical variable) on DMS, DMT and DQ. Second, given the complex nature of the later three hypotheses and the existence of intervening variables, PLS analysis in SEM form was employed. PLS was used as the best tool for dealing with small sample sizes, since it made few assumptions about the distribution of data. It is also a very robust tool when multicollinearities exist in the data.

## 6. Conclusions and discussion

The exploratory stage of our study revealed six key constructs of interest in the empowering process: decision making transparency, decision making satisfaction, and decision quality, empowerment outcomes (power and control), and their impact on the government agencies’ service provider image. The experimental stage showed that online ISS, especially those with optimized explanation features yields higher decision satisfaction, transparency, and quality. Among these effectiveness indicators, transparency is the most important predictor of the public’s sense of control over their destiny, attitude towards their power relationship, and the perceived government service provider image.

Explanation and transparency are key to the success of online advisory systems; an appropriately designed online advisory tool provides a more transparent decision making process due to the publication of its decision criteria, and this process empowers the citizens. In contrast, decision quality was not shown to have significant impact on the dependent variables of interest. This disparity from theory prediction might be a result of the participants not being aware of their actual performance, which is often the case in real life, when people use online government services to conduct self-assessment.

Our mixed-method approach allowed us to integrate theory and practice. The initial phase of the study involved a series of exploratory techniques, through which the authors managed to develop a practical understanding of the phenomenon, despite the dearth of published work. The second phase of the study used experiments, through which we were able to quantitatively examine the proposed research model. This multi-method approach enhanced both the rigor and the applicability of the study and triangulated the research findings.

Some limitations of the study must be addressed: one is related to the use of explanations by participants in the group with optimized explanations. As pointed out by Gregor [9], the availability of an explanation is not equivalent to explanation use, or reading, and understanding of the explanations; the difficulty in measuring actual explanation use has been acknowledged by various researchers; protocol analysis techniques, such as concurrent verbalisation has to sacrifice experimental realism and run the risk of sensitising participants. However, our study took an extra step with the optimized explanation group only of asking participants ‘‘how frequently did you access the explanations?’’ at the end of the questionnaire. This factor was found to have minimal impact on the results of the study.

The second limitation is related to the threat to experimental validity due to experiment control. There were a number of supervisors involved in the experiments and although they were provided with consistent protocols, it is still possible that some procedures might not have been followed as intended. The potential impacts of experiment supervisors could affect the generalizability of the result.

Practically, our findings provide insights that benefit both the public and the government agencies. From the public’s viewpoint, effective online advisory tools have the potential to reshape governance and empower them. From government agencies viewpoint, a more relevant success indicator of the government, whether at the municipal or state level, is the increased appreciation of the general public rather than its performance. Having effective online self-assessment tools is a good starting point to improve public service delivery and responsiveness for its citizens.
