# MULTI-HOMING REVISITED: LEVEL OF ADOPTION AND COMPETITIVE STRATEGIES<sup>1</sup>

Anitesh Barua

McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin Austin, TX, U.S.A. {anitesh.barua@mccombs.utexas.edu}

Rajiv Mukherjee Mays Business School, Texas A&M University College Station, TX, U.S.A. {rmukherjee@mays.tamu.edu}

Aided by the increasing ease of use, lower adoption cost, and higher network benefits, consumers are demonstrating a strong propensity to concurrently use competing firms’ products or services. Depending on their relative preference for a firm, such “multi-homing” consumers may adopt each firm partially and therefore contribute to the network benefits of no firm fully, as would be the case with single-homing. Consumers’ level of adoption of competing products is a key feature of multihoming, which, while observed widely in practice, has not previously been studied in the literature. Through a series of analytical models, we demonstrate the important role of this construct in the pricing and capability-related decisions of competing firms. Our results provide several new insights, which suggest that as multi-homing (M) settings become common across industries, technology strategists and managers should exercise caution against simply extrapolating insights from singlehoming (S) settings, where consumers adopt only one firm, or from M settings, where the level of adoption is not accounted for. Specifically, in markets where competing products are not well differentiated, contrary to intuition, we find that under price competition, a firm’s profit can be hurt by high levels of adoption by multi-homing consumers; further, in markets where prices are inflexible, a firm with a higher level of adoption can succeed even with a lower level of capability innovation relative to that of an S setting. In contrast to single-homing settings, we show that firms in M settings need to mitigate uncertainty regarding network benefits if the level of adoption is low. Finally, we explore the role of adoption level in two-sided markets and demonstrate that if one side does not have a strong preference for a platform, then, contrary to prevailing wisdom, the latter need not strongly subsidize the other side of the market.

Keywords: Network effects, multi-homing, single-homing, level of adoption, competition

## Introduction

Unlike single-homing (S) settings, where consumers exclusively adopt a firm and its technology network, in multi-homing (M) settings (Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005), consumers may be more inclusive and “partially” adopt competing firms and their corresponding technology networks.<sup>2</sup> Consumers derive intrinsic benefits from the firm(s) they adopt; in addition, in technology-driven products and services, there may be network effects (Katz and Shapiro, 1985), causing the value of the product/service to increase as more consumers adopt it. For example, in the personal computer industry, operating systems (OS) provide consumers intrinsic benefits through their file systems, data processing and software support, while innovative network effects enhancing features (e.g., Windows OS Office 365), among others, enable file sharing and collaboration. Indeed, in information systems studies of multi-homing (e.g., Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006; Athey et al. 2016), the focus has been on products that exhibit network effects.

A major impediment to multi-homing has been the significant cost that a consumer has to bear to adopt multiple products simultaneously (e.g., purchasing a dedicated client machine for each OS). Therefore, most consumers were forced to choose a single OS (or single-home) in the past. With technological innovation (e.g., virtualization technology to adopt multiple OS on a single client machine), multi-homing costs have been significantly reduced, allowing consumers to be more inclusive and to adopt competing products at the same time (e.g., Windows and Mac OS). Further, the propensity of inclusive consumers to adopt one firm and its technology network to a larger extent than another—despite adopting both—is a unique feature of multi-homing. Although commonly observed in practice, this feature has not been modeled in the multi-homing literature and thus motivates our research. For instance, students may use virtualization software (e.g., Bootcamp or Virtual PC) to use Windows-based applications with OSspecific add-ins for assignments and projects, while using the Mac OS in their leisure time to edit music recordings and videos. Indeed, in some scenarios, a consumer’s relative preference for an OS may be driven by exogenous factors (e.g., requirements for school work and availability of resources such as time), whereas in other cases a consumer may seamlessly switch between OS to optimize the value from such environments. In our model, the extent or level of adoption is a parameter that characterizes inclusive consumers’ relative preference for competing firms. As consumers engage in higher levels of multi-homing, and as many industries transition from S to M settings, it becomes increasingly important for competing firms to understand how the level of adoption by inclusive consumers may impact their strategic decisions.

The lock-in role of network effects is well studied in the context of competing firms (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007; Shankar and Bayus, 2003) in the technology sector. Network effects create a “protective moat” (Kass, 2013) around a business, and firms use pricing and innovation as strategic levers to successfully compete in such environments. While the above studies have analyzed the role of network effects in the context of single-homing, how do firms adapt their strategies in industries where consumers can easily multi-home? To illustrate the challenges firms may face when transitioning from S to M settings in an industry, let us examine the personal computer business. Until the early 2000s, desktop consumers could install only one OS on a client machine. Whereas Windows OS was supported by the PC architecture, the Mac OS enabled Apple’s Mac family of computers. As the desktop computer market grew, there was an increasing need for virtualization capabilities<sup>3</sup> among consumers. Should firms support consumers to be inclusive of competing products (e.g., by offering virtualization capabilities)? In multi-homing settings, do firms need to proactively educate or inform consumers about features (e.g., Office 365) that enhance network benefits? Does the presence of inclusive consumers require competing firms to deliver more innovative products? Clearly, further analysis is required to answer these and related questions pertinent to many industries, where technology has significantly reduced multi-homing cost, and where multi-homing is driven by consumer preferences and network benefits. Thus, we explore the following research questions:

How do characteristics of the market (e.g., product differentiation), firm (e.g., uncertainty regarding network benefits), and consumer (e.g., relative preferences for competing firms) impact strategic decisions—pricing and capability innovation—of firms and their profits in the presence of inclusive consumers?

• What role does the level of adoption by inclusive consumers play in such settings?

• How do our insights regarding pricing and capability innovation from the M setting differ from those in S?

We model a duopolistic competition of firms based on the Hotelling model (Hotelling, 1990), where firms offer horizontally differentiated products or services, and where consumers are heterogeneous in their preferences for competing firms located at the extremes of the Hotelling line. While a single-homing consumer exclusively adopts a firm, a multi-homing consumer may adopt both firms, albeit partially.

We derive several interesting results from our analysis. First, we demonstrate that as product-level differentiation increases, the level of adoption has contrasting impacts on demands and prices in the two settings. Specifically, competing firms may be better off in M compared to S, if there is low product differentiation and if inclusive consumers’ level of adoption of a superior firm (that offers better network benefits) is sufficiently low. In other words, as it becomes easier for consumers to multi-home, and if the inferior firm still holds sufficient appeal for consumers, both firms benefit in the M setting. This result is in contrast to single-homing, where firms can become stronger local monopolies as product differentiation (or competitive intensity) in the market increases (decreases). We contribute to the multi-homing literature by demonstrating the importance of the level of adoption. With moderate levels of adoption, competing firms in M settings may be better off (relative to S); however, very high levels of adoption may reduce a firm’s profit. Our insights may partially explain Microsoft and Apple’s initiatives to provide free virtualization technology (i.e., Microsoft’s Virtual PC and Apple’s Bootcamp) to their multi-homing consumers, which makes it more profitable for both firms to cater to multihoming consumers who do not have strong preferences for either firm.

Although not studied in the multi-homing literature, consumers may be uncertain about the network benefits available post-adoption. Such uncertainty may exist because of a firm’s inability to educate or inform consumers about the benefits features that enhance network effects (Chellappa and Mukherjee, 2021). We show that although consumers can mitigate such uncertainty by choosing to multi-home, a firm may benefit from resolving uncertainty about its network benefits in M settings. By contrast, in the S setting, doing this may hurt a firm’s profit. In the post-virtualization era in the personal computer industry, our results support increasing investment in physical stores and collaboration with retail outlets by both Microsoft and Apple in order to demonstrate their OS features and convey network benefits to consumers.

Further, we show that even when firms compete on capability innovation rather than price, depending on the level of adoption, firm(s) may not require as high a capability in M as would be necessary if it operated in S. Thus, technology strategists not familiar with such nuances of multi-homing should not rely on insights from a singlehoming setting and must adapt their strategies as they transition to M settings. Extrapolating competitive strategies from S to M may mislead firms to underestimate the competition, which may help the latter to improve its capabilities over time. Our result justifies the increase in innovation levels of OS features by both Microsoft (beginning with Windows 7 OS) and Apple (beginning with Tiger) in the post-virtualization era.

In addition to the transition from S to M settings, another shift that many industries are witnessing involves platform models, which may serve two sides of a market. We examine how the level of adoption impacts the pricing strategy of a platform. We find that the platform may have to significantly reduce prices on one-side of the market (to attract the other) if multi-homing consumers strongly prefer one platform over another. Alternatively, a large price cut may be unnecessary if multi-homing consumers have similar preferences for competing platforms. Thus, if technology strategists rely on existing models of multihoming that do not account for consumers’ level of adoption, their pricing (subsidy) strategy may fail to successfully attract both sides of the market. We extend our model to verify the robustness of our results from the baseline model (without uncertainty) by analyzing heterogeneous and endogenous adoption levels of competing firms.

In summary, our research demonstrates the role played by the level of adoption by inclusive consumers in M on strategic outcomes of competing firms, and how such strategies may differ from S settings. In the following section, we review the relevant literature and then describe our model setup in the subsequent section. The Analysis section comprises the analysis of our baseline model (with an exogenous level of adoption) and the impact of uncertainty regarding network benefits. In the Model Extensions section, we analyze firm strategies when they compete on innovation and discuss insights from two-sided markets. We summarize our findings and discuss the managerial implications of our results in the Conclusion.

## Literature

Our research spans two related streams in firm competition: network effects and multi-homing. The network effects literature suggests that positive network effects (Katz and Shapiro, 1985) from an installed base provide a firm with a protective moat that is difficult to breach (Shapiro et al., 1998). This literature has generally considered consumers adopting one firm exclusively, or single-homing (Cheng et al., 2011; Dou et al., 2013). In recent years, however, consumers have demonstrated an increasing propensity to simultaneously adopt multiple competing products that demonstrate network effects. The literature (Rochet and Tirole, 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Armstrong, 2006; Koh and Fichman, 2014) has assumed that multihoming consumers adopt multiple technologies entirely and derive full benefits from their corresponding networks.

However, in practice, multi-homing consumers may not be able to fully contribute to the network benefits of adopted firms. There has been some discussion in prior literature regarding the inability of multi-homing consumers to extract full benefits from competing firms. Doganoglu and Wright (2006) assume nonduplication of capability, which suggests that using multiple technologies limits a multi-homing user’s ability to leverage the intrinsic benefits. Athey et al. (2016) examine the media industry, where multi-homing consumers equally adopt competing publishers. While not explicitly modeling it, Armstrong (2006) acknowledges that a multihoming consumer’s level of adoption of technology networks may indeed be different. We consider a parameter in our model that captures a multi-homing consumer’s relative preference or the level of adoption of competing firms. Analyzing the impact of this parameter on firm strategies allows us to understand how such strategies may change in terms of a consumer’s propensity to multi-homing increases. We also consider other modifications to existing multi-homing models.

Although the single-homing literature (Liebowitz and Margolis, 1995; Zhu et al., 2006; Arthur, 1989; Farrell and

Saloner, 1986) has primarily considered users to be homogenous, we analyze the applicability of insights from such studies when users are heterogeneous in their ideal preferences and when network benefits to consumers are uncertain. Unlike Griva and Vettas (2011), who assume that firms have a symmetric strength of network effects, we analyze the impact of such asymmetric abilities on competitive strategies. Sen et al. (2010) consider exogenous prices to understand the role of network effects. However, in our model, such decisions are endogenous in firm, market, and consumer characteristics. The multihoming literature (Rochet and Tirole, 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Armstrong, 2006; Koh and Fichman, 2014) has examined two-sided markets (e.g., video games, etc.) where multi-homing consumers do not derive network benefits from each other. In our analysis of two-sided markets, we also consider the same-side network effects of the multi-homing side of the market.

Academics and industry practitioners have been interested in the role of multi-homing consumers in pricing and capability decisions of both single-sided intermediaries and two-sided markets (platforms) that are transitioning from single-homing to multi-homing settings. We contribute to this growing stream of research by examining the role of adoption level by multi-homing consumers on firms’ pricing and innovation strategies, and compare our insights to those from single-homing settings and existing multihoming models.

## Model Setup

We model a duopoly between Firms A and B, located at the extremes of a Hotelling line (Hotelling, 1990) of unit length: Firm A (B) at location 0 (1). Each firm offers a product or a service to consumers, who vary in their ideal preferences; consumers located closer to Firm A in the Hotelling line prefer Firm A over B. Products/services offered by the firms demonstrate network effects; a consumer derives a higher value from a firm as more consumers adopt it. The firms<sup>4</sup> are identical in the quality of their offerings, and make strategic decisions about pricing. Competing firms set prices for their products/services taking into account consumer expectations regarding network benefits. Consumers observe such prices and make adoption decisions, and their expected network benefits are fulfilled in equilibrium. The timeline of events is shown in Figure 1.

We consider two market settings: S, where consumers exclusively choose either firm, and M, where consumers can exclusively choose a firm or be inclusive and adopt both firms. A consumer’s utility depends upon (1) the intrinsic quality of chosen product(s) or service(s), (2) network benefits generated by the adopted firm(s), and (3) the fit cost incurred by the consumers, which is the disutility from adopting a nonideal product.

Since we are interested in scenarios where multi-homing is motivated by network effects, we assume non-duplication (Doganoglu and Wright, 2006) of intrinsic benefits for multi-homing consumers.<sup>5</sup>

## Conclusion

In recent years, lower costs and ease of adoption have prompted consumers to simultaneously use multiple products and services offered by competing firms, a phenomenon known as multi-homing. While multi-homing (inclusive) users derive intrinsic benefits from each firm they adopt, they also realize network benefits due to other consumers adopting multiple firms. For example, using virtualization technology (e.g., Bootcamp) one can adopt multiple operating systems (e.g., Windows and Mac OS) and get access to their respective networks; entertainment seekers can use affordable streaming hardware (e.g., Roku and Apple TV) and subscribe to both Amazon Prime Video and Netflix, deriving network benefits in the form of consumer reviews (in Amazon Prime Video) and accurate suggestions based on viewing patterns of consumers (in Netflix); and mobile phone users can use dual SIM phones (e.g., Samsung Duo) to subscribe to multiple telecommunication service providers and benefit from network-friendly features of each service they adopt (e.g., free texting and data sharing). Since many industries are currently witnessing an increasing willingness of consumers to multi-home, it is imperative for firms to analyze suitable strategies involving pricing and capability innovations for multi-homing settings.

The literature (Rochet and Tirole, 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Armstrong, 2006; Athey et al., 2016), has studied competition between firms in the context of multihoming. However, such studies have largely assumed that consumers adopt competing firms completely and have failed to analyze settings where consumers may adopt competing firms to different extents when multi-homing. We incorporate this construct in our stylized analytical model as the extent or level of adoption, and this feature constitutes a critical element in prescribing how firms should transform their single-homing strategies as their industries transition to multi-homing.

Our analysis suggests that in multi-homing, when firms compete on price in markets with low product differentiation (or high intensity of competition), a firm can derive higher profits than in single-homing for moderate levels of adoption by inclusive consumers. Our insight is in line with Apple and Microsoft’s decision to offer virtualization software (Bootcamp for Mac OS, and Virtual PC for Windows) that allow consumers to multi-home. Often consumers are not sufficiently informed about the network benefits of a product or service. Although reducing uncertainty regarding network benefits is detrimental to a firm’s profit in single-homing, with low levels of adoption in multi-homing setting, it is in the best interest of firms to inform consumers about features that enhance networkeffect features. This result has implications for firms’ decisions to advertise, announce product information, and/or offer product demonstrations to educate consumers about network value propositions. Microsoft’s decision to collaborate with Best Buy to demonstrate Windows 8 features to its prospective customers, and decisions by both Microsoft and Apple to invest in physical stores where consumers can interact with their devices and also learn more about their OS features, are steps that can mitigate uncertainty regarding network benefits. Such strategies were rare before virtualization technology became pervasive, and our analysis emphasizes the importance of educating consumers about network benefits.

Firms may not always compete on price. In several industries, prices may be standardized or less flexible to a firm’s choice, in which case firms may compete on capability innovations (e.g., novel digital content by an OTT service provider, high quality of service in telecom, high performance of an operating system, etc.). Our results suggest that managers must pay due attention to consumers’ levels of adoption to develop the required innovation in the intrinsic capability of their products and services. We find that in markets where firms’ capability offerings are well differentiated, a higher level of innovation is required in the presence of multi-homing consumers. Thus, the surge of innovation by both Microsoft and Apple in their respective OS features, after virtualization technology was widely adopted (post-2004), may have been strategic responses to multi-homing. While our model is static, a possible extension of this research is to develop a dynamic model to analyze temporal variations in a consumer’s level of adoption over time, and the impact of such variations on the competitive landscape of capability innovation.

We extend our analysis to a two-sided market (e.g., operating systems open to external developers) with multihoming users and single-homing supply side (e.g., developers). Consistent with prior literature, we observe the effect of a firm reducing the price of one side of the market to attract the other. However, by incorporating the level of adoption, we note that as the level of adoption increases, the price paid by the single-homing side can be non-monotonic, whereas the price paid by the multihoming side increases. This result is in contrast to a common platform strategy to subsidize one side of the market to attract the other. In our model, if a firm is strongly preferred over another, both firms may have an incentive to offer deep price cuts to the supply (single-homing) side. However, if such strong preferences do not exist, firms can charge high prices to the single-homing side as well. This result implies that understanding the level of adoption by multi-homing users is an important consideration for Microsoft or Apple before deciding their licensing agreements with the developer community.

Overall our results indicate that as consumers pursue multihoming in many industries, managers and platform strategists must exercise caution in extrapolating strategies from single-homing as well as existing multi-homing literature that do not account for the level of adoption. In particular, a firm’s competitive strategies (pricing and innovation) must incorporate the role of a multi-homing consumer’s level of adoption, product differentiation, and the strength of network effects of competing firms, to adapt to the changing landscape in many industries.
