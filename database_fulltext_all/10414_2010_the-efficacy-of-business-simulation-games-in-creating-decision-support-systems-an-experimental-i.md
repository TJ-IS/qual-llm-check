# The ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of business simulation games in creating Decision Support Systems: An experimental investigation

Tal Ben-Zvi

Wesley J. Howe School of Technology Management, Stevens Institute of Technology, Hoboken, NJ 07030, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 December 2008 Received in revised form 12 December 2009 Accepted 16 January 2010 Available online 25 January 2010

Keywords: Decision Support Systems Business games Pedagogy Bloom's Taxonomy Simulation

## a b s t r a c t

This study discusses business simulation games as teaching tools in Information Systems (IS). The discipline's traditional teaching methods, while appropriate for the dissemination of foundational knowledge, do not provide the optimal platform for students to implement IS concepts. We suggest that business simulation games are an effective way to engage students in Decision Support Systems (DSS). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we discuss our experience with a game designed to channel students into a stream of entrepreneurial decision-making. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that business simulation games represent a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently novel approach to teaching and learning.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Background

In the past few years there have been growing concerns over a perception gap between Information Systems (IS) and Technology Management (TM) academics and IS/TM practitioners (Lee et al. [19]; Sutcliffe et al. [33]). The 21st century mandates a multidimensional teaching approach, where teachers (1) transmit core competencies and (2) provide a forum for students to think independently, to challenge assumptions and widely held beliefs (Prince and Stewart [29]). Lee et al. [19] argue that IS programs do not adequately prepare students to understand and cope with the ambiguities they will inevitably face in industry. The authors suggest that the teaching and learning models that dominate current academic practice do not provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient preparation for students. This is particularly problematic for graduate students to the extent that adult working students typically enroll in such programs as a direct result of their lived experience in organizations (Dehler [8], p. 637).

IS is about people, data and processes. Thus, teaching methods based solely on lectures and cases that ignore important practice and implementation topics may be of little use in the real world. Knowledge in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is a means to an end not an end in itself, and the acquisition of foundational factual knowledge is an early and important phase of the learning process but should not be the only one. An innovative approach of learning in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is doing; that is, performing empirical tasks (Léger [20]). We believe that the approach of using simulations to teach

Decision Support Systems (DSS) has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant advantages over other approaches.

## 1.2. Research objectives

The key objective of this study is to suggest general-purpose business simulation games as an educational environment for learning and implementing of effective Management Information Systems (MIS) and DSS and to report on an experiment with one such game.

Several studies argue that business schools do not adequately prepare students to understand and cope with the ambiguities they will inevitably face in real-world organizations (e.g., Lee et al. [19]; Tesch et al. [34]). Therefore, the main argument of this article is that business simulation games are important motivational and learning tools that present a novel approach for teaching IS. We show that business simulation games present an effective alternative to traditional teaching methods; that they provide a link between abstract concepts and realworld problems; that they offer additional practicality to the learning setting and give students the chance to practice decision-making cases, which represent the temporal and process characteristics of the realworld environment.

Furthermore, an investigation of DSS literature reveals that research has mainly focused on the effects of design, implementation and use on decision outcomes (e.g., Ariav and Ginzberg [3], Bharati and Chaudhury [5]; Garrity et al. [14]). Only few DSS studies investigated variables such as user beliefs and attitudes (e.g., Kamis et al. [16]; Lilien et al. [22]; Poston and Speier [28]; Todd and Benbasat [35]). We consider DSS in a learning environment and examine various variables in a social context. Therefore, another objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of the developed DSS. ## 2. Learning and business games

## 2.1. The nature of business games

From an educational perspective, games are important motivational and learning tools (Garris et al. [13]), a link between abstract concepts and real-world problems, a “learning by doing” or “handson” approach to learning (Martin [24]; Kolodner et al. [18]). From a technical perspective, games help remedy education's long-standing struggle to maintain <sup>fl</sup>uency in end-user computing, the world-wideweb, distance learning and cooperative learning (Erkut [11]). Educators desire to work with technology to create more meaningful learning experiences (Souza e Silva and Delacruz [32]). Therefore, exploring novel approaches to technology-friendly games for teaching and learning is eminently justi<sup>fi</sup>able.

A general-purpose business game is a highly complex man-made environment. Its objective is to offer students the opportunity to learn by doing, engaging them in a simulated experience of the real- world, to immerse them in an authentic management situation (e.g., Garris et al. [13]; Martin [24]). In 2003, a special issue of Communications of the ACM, named “A Game Experience in Every Application”, was dedicated to simulation games in diverse applications. Furthermore, the application of simulation gaming as a learning tool is occasionally described in IS literature. For example, Nulden and Scheepers [26] suggested a system development simulation in which failure and escalation are introduced to Information System students. Draijer and Schenk [9] and Léger [20] used a business simulation game to teach Enterprise Resource Planning concepts. Parker and Swatman [27] explored an Internet-mediated business game simulating an electronic commerce environment; Yeo and Tan [36] used a simulation in supporting a course in decision technology; Ben-Zvi [4] studied DSS in business games.

## 2.2. The model

Our model examines the transformative nature of experiential learning and its interplay with simulation games. This may help us respond to various criticisms over the educational bene<sup>fi</sup>t of business games (e.g., Neuhauser [25] states that “the many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for business games are not supported by any but anecdotal evidence”). Experiential learning emphasizes the interaction between experience and learning by exploiting the subjective nature of the learning process (Kolb [17]) and creating a transformation of experience that engenders knowledge (Mainemelis et al. [23]). Business simulation games are regarded as a method that epitomizes experiential learning (see for example, Anderson and Lawton [2]; Faria and Wellington [12]; Garris et al. [13]; Ruben [31]).

An assessment framework known as the Revised Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (hereafter Revised Taxonomy) typi<sup>fi</sup>es the relationship between experiential learning and business games. The Revised Taxonomy is a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives [6]. It is a two dimensional matrix that juxtaposes knowledge and cognitive processes. The knowledge dimension represents a continuum from concreteness to abstraction. The cognitive process dimension represents an assumed hierarchical continuum of cognitive complexity. Each cell in the taxonomy corresponds to an educational objective (Anderson and Krathwohl [1]). Table 1 illustrates the structure of the Revised Taxonomy.

The knowledge dimension includes four knowledge types: factual, conceptual, procedural, and meta-cognitive. Concrete, factual knowledge includes the introductory concepts, skills and details of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c discipline. Conceptual knowledge represents a synthesis of factual knowledge and movement towards an understanding of principles and theories associated with a given discipline. Procedural knowledge involves one's grasp of how to study something. This may include knowledge of subject-speci<sup>fi</sup>c techniques and methods or informed judgments for determining when to use appropriate procedures. Meta-cognitive knowledge is summarizing knowledge; theoretical and conceptual knowledge that synthesizes the lesser dimensions. It is knowledge of cognition in general but also knowledge of one's own intellectual prowess (Anderson and Krathwohl [1]).

The cognitive process dimension ranges from remember, a basic cognitive retrieval process to create, an advanced planning process. The categories between remember and create represent a transition from recognizing and recalling facts to theory generation and successful learning habits (Anderson and Krathwohl [1]). These middle categories are understand, apply, analyze and evaluate. Understand relates to one's ability to determine the meaning of instructional messages through several modes of communication. Apply is the ability to execute or implement a procedure appropriate to a given situation and closely linked to procedural knowledge. Analyze relates to deconstruction: one's ability to break material into constituent parts and determine how those parts relate to one another. The evaluate category involves making judgments based on criteria and standards: checking and critiquing. Those judgments lead to one's ability to create, to generate hypotheses, to engage in research planning and to effectively articulate research outcomes (Anderson and Krathwohl [1]).

This framework represents a practical heuristic for exploring the interplay between teaching, learning, assessment and business games. Thus, we discuss a speci<sup>fi</sup>c business game we used in the context of the Revised Taxonomy.

## 3. The business game simulation

## 3.1. The game

This study utilizes the International Operations Simulation Mark/ 2000 (thereafter, INTOPIA). We use the game to establish a managerial decision-making context. The game involves the participants in the executive process, motivates their need for decision-making aids and forces them to adopt a managerial viewpoint associated with MIS and DSS.

The game is highly realistic, meant to simulate the total environment. Participants are divided to teams (companies) and immerse themselves in an arti<sup>fi</sup>cially created hi-tech industry, where companies conduct research, produce and market chips and PCs. Students participating in the game submerge themselves in this experience. They form small groups, allocate responsibilities for speci<sup>fi</sup>c functions, and work to achieve common goals which they themselves de<sup>fi</sup>ne. ## 3.2. Participants

The study was conducted in a university accredited by the Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB). The participants were senior graduate students. They were divided into groups of <sup>fi</sup>ve. We conducted six runs of the game: (1) Run I, with 18 teams; (2) Run II, with 20 teams; (3) Run III, with 20 teams; (4) Run IV, with 17 teams; (5) Run V, with 16 teams; and (6) Run VI, with 15 teams. Overall, 520 students participated in this experiment. The formation of groups and the allocation of executive roles proceeded without external intervention or manipulation.

## 3.3. Instructional activities

The game commences after an introductory lecture, at which point the instructor adopts a rather passive role. That is, the students are expected to apply classroom knowledge to the game with little direction. This approach is designed to challenge the students' ingenuity and creativity. The decision-making process is based on (1) analysis of the company's history presented to players when the game commences; and (2) interaction with other companies.

These instructional activities promote learning at several levels of the taxonomy. The introductory lecture, for example, <sup>fi</sup>rst emphasizes knowledge of terminology. Then, it progresses to integration of factual knowledge from different disciplines. Thus, instructional activities promote remember factual and conceptual knowledge. However, when the instructor adopts passive roles and the students play the game and use their systems, they are required to engage in progressively more independent strategic decision-making and therefore, learn at a higher level of the taxonomy. Students (teams) have to analyze different management situations and evaluate their decisions based on their knowledge of procedures articulated in the lectures; to ana lyze and evaluate procedural knowledge. Further, at a more abstract level, the less invasive instructor role mandates that students understand how and why they make decisions. Such conditions make it important for students to understand their strengths and weaknesses; to understand meta-cognitive knowledge.

## 3.4. Instructional activities II: developed systems

In each run when the game commenced, a requirement to develop a DSS was communicated to the participants. The participants themselves, without any intervention from the instructor, had to decide who would develop the system and determine its scope. At the end of each run, each group was required to present its DSS, highlighting the following: (1) the scope of the system; (2) a decision analysis; (3) a system design; and (4) a discussion of the contribution of the system in achieving the group's objectives during the game. In addition, each of the students was asked to complete a short individual questionnaire on the DSS assignment (see Appendix A for the text of the questionnaire). The questionnaire, following similar questionnaires used by Ein-Dor and Segev [10] and Ben-Zvi [4], focuses on the practical aspects of DSS and measures variables related to the system's perceived success, functionality and use.

We present an example of the systems developed by the simulated companies in Run III. Twenty companies were created, most of which developed a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet-based DSS. The major characteristics of the systems developed are exhibited in Table 2.

For this study, the most relevant aspect of the DSS presentations is the extent to which the companies differed on their systems. Companies, in all six runs, adopted different application areas with models including various statistical analyses, spreadsheets — and even linear regressions. Sixty <sup>fi</sup>ve companies developed data analysis tools (mostly statistical or engineering analyses) for their systems (approximately 60% of all companies). Only 33 companies developed graphic outputs (about a third of all companies), while the remaining 73 did not. Finally, the sophistication and complexity of the models employed varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly from simple spreadsheet analyses to a complex linear model. While it cannot be claimed that the distribution of attributes of systems exactly measures that in the real world, the degree of diversity of systems developed, based on existing tools, does appear to be very real.

Relative to the development and use of a DSS, learning occurs at several levels of the taxonomy. First, to use the DSS effectively, students must <sup>fi</sup>rst apply: they implement procedures they have acquired in previous courses to this new task of creating a DSS. Then, they analyze: using the system, they distinguish between important and unimportant information and determine how different data elements <sup>fi</sup>t together. These activities also promote the acquisition of conceptual and procedural knowledge, as follows: In terms of conceptual knowledge, the development of the DSS requires comprehension and analysis of the interrelationships among the basic data elements that enable them to function together within the larger structure, provided by the DSS. That is, students must understand their company and the industry (where to position the company within the industry, where to operate, etc.) Pursuant to procedural knowledge, the students learn and practice the use of particular methods and techniques. Fig. 4, for example, shows a nonlinear regression, which appears to be an appropriate method for forecasting R&D investments. This method aligned with the company's principal strategy of R&D superiority.

As the learning process occurs in the intermediate levels of the taxonomy, the DSS serves as a tool to bridge the lower and higher levels of the taxonomy. The DSS promoted the acquisition of factual knowledge and the execution of relatively simple tasks like remembering and understanding. In addition, it promoted more complex intellectual activities such as planning, designing, creating and producing new features or ideas.

## 3.5. Instructional activities III: presentations and reports

At the end of each run, in addition to the oral presentation, the students were also asked to submit a written report. Both the presentation and the written report necessarily build upon the application of DSS. Teams submitted a strategic analysis, market analysis, operational analysis, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis based upon the game's results. The expectation was that each team would learn from one another, given the different backgrounds of students.

We categorized the presentation as analyze meta-cognitive knowledge, as analysis is the highest level required for the presentation. However, we suggest that the culminating report requires learning to occur at the highest level of the taxonomy, based on two factors. First, teams were required to articulate strategic knowledge and to hypothesize about subsequent business periods that are not actually played. Those analyses necessitate the use of the teams' DSS. Second, the <sup>fi</sup>nal report was a vehicle for teams to explain their command of the structure and function of the game. Thus, we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the <sup>fi</sup>nal report as create meta-cognitive knowledge.

## 3.7. The taxonomy revisited

Pursuant to business games, the Revised Taxonomy is an important tool. It allows educators to analyze the interaction of students with course materials. Simultaneously, it allows educators to analyze the ways in which an individual's knowledge is structured. These two activities are fundamentally important in education (Anderson and Krathwohl [1]). Table 7 illustrates our understanding of how the knowledge and cognitive processes relate to the INTOPIA business game.

Our integration of the Revised Taxonomy indicates that business games allow for learning to occur at multiple levels; but not all. Furthermore, our experience with the Revised Taxonomy suggests that business games satisfy a long-standing need in IS: curriculum integration. Integration occurs because learning in business games spans from the lowest to highest levels of the taxonomy. First, students must remember, understand and apply their knowledge from previous coursework. Second, they must analyze their own strengths and weaknesses, relative to other teams. Last, they must create and simulate a corporate reality using a DSS. These three factors, among others, underscore the importance and potential of business games for learning through integration. And more practically, the Revised Taxonomy con<sup>fi</sup>rms that business simulation games are an effective way to engage students in IS topics; that they compel students to understand and cope with the ambiguities associated with real-world organizations.

## 3.8. Simulation evaluation

Our experience suggests that the INTOPIA game is an ideal platform for the gaming method because it enables students to understand information needs through participation in a real decisionmaking process. At the end of each run the students were asked to complete an evaluation questionnaire. The students were also asked to compare this exercise to courses in their curriculum.

Analyzing their answers, student reaction to the game was favorable. Most reported that they enjoyed the exercise and the realism that it brought to the classroom; yet, we also experienced some negative comments, such as “working with four other teammates is tough”. Over time, the simulation earned high ratings in relation to other courses (means of responses from all runs are exhibited in Table 8; questions are based on the Likert scale (Likert [21]) from 1 to 7).

In addition, we conducted a follow-up survey a couple years after the students had graduated: we asked the students that had taken the course to evaluate the game again, with their added perspective of time and experience in the industry. Our analysis exhibits a correlation of 0.79 between the evaluations given by the same students at the end of the game and those given after a couple of years. This validates the robust impact of the game as it shows that added perspective does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly alter the positive evaluations given at the end of the game.

Furthermore, the use of simulation games for enhancing participatory learning and teaching aligns well with the levels (depth) of knowledge aims articulated for IS education (see Gorgone et al. [15], Appendix 4).

Traditionally, one of the greatest challenges of teaching DSS has been the lack of readily-available and integrated DSS creation tools, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to move from theoretical discussions about DSS to more practical aspects of building a DSS (Ragsdale [30]). Nowadays, even the frequently used spreadsheets are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient tools to create extremely powerful, useful and practical DSS.

In addition, the results presented in this article have implications for practitioners. We showed that user factors do impact DSS implementation success; that the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using a DSS and user satisfaction are strongly related to company performance. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to future implementation of successful DSS in several important ways: First, using a simulation as a training technique can become useful in the design and implementation of DSS in practice. Second, this article attempts to make a case for a more thoughtful consideration of DSS effectiveness; speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, by examining the relationships between perceived measures of DSS effectiveness and company performance. We emphasize the fact that the two are not always positively associated with each other as suggested by several MIS/DSS studies (e.g., Bharati and Chaudhury [5]; Garrity et al. [14]). By better understanding the perceived measures of DSS effectiveness and company performance, practitioners could interpret data pertaining to those measures more accurately. Third, we have shown that when system complexity increases, perceived usefulness and user satisfaction may start to decrease. This inverted U-shaped relationship between complexity and performance signi<sup>fi</sup>es that multifaceted system does not guarantee a better outcome. Therefore, as perceived usefulness becomes increasingly important, more and more businesses should concentrate on that aspect of their IS. And employing a simulation game method may provide a strategic opportunity to seamlessly transition the study of DSS from theoretical concepts to more practical and relevant contexts. The ultimate result will be more successful MIS and DSS systems in the real world.

A future line of research might concentrate on how business games can be applied in studying various aspects of IS. For example, the use and performance of IS can be measured and evaluated by using questionnaires. We believe that documenting and quantifying the students' experiences may yield new ideas that substantiate alternative and promising pedagogical approaches in IS.
