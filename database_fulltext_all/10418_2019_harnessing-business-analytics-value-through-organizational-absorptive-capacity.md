## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Business analytics Absorptive capacit Competitive advantage

## A B S T R A C T

Business analytics (BA) is a resource-intensive, risky endeavor. However, the literature has not addressed the important question of how to assimilate BA technologies for competitive advantage. We build a research framework and outline the BA assimilation mechanisms through organizational absorptive capacity (AC). Data were collected from 600 companies across six countries and analyzed using partial least squares. The findings revealed that organizational AC plays a crucial mediating role between BA competency and BA assimilation leading to competitive advantage. This study contributes significantly to both information technology assim ilation and BA value research by incorporating the role of organizational AC

## 1. Introduction

Business analytics (BA) refers to “the techniques, technologies, systems, practices, methodologies, and applications that analyze critical business data to help an enterprise better understand its business and market and make timely business decisions” [1], p.1166]. Examples of BA technologies include data warehouses, online analytical processing, data mining, data visualization, and interactive dashboards [2]. Re cently, the big data trend has generated considerable interest from both industry and academia in BA [3,4]. A worldwide survey of 2053 chief information oficers (CIOs) by Gartner has revealed that BA tops the list of technology priorities [5], and academic research on the subject has increased significantly over the past few years [4,6].

While many organizations devote significant financial and human resources to implement BA systems, some organizations face dificulty in harnessing anticipated business value. For example, Marchand and Peppard [7] report that one global materials management company spent US\$44 million dollars over nine years on various BA initiatives and yet achieved few benefits, calling into question the overall value of BA investment. Researchers suggest that while it is clear that BA can provide value, the ways in which it does so need further examination [6,8] to avoid these types of large-scale failures.

The current literature on the business value of BA has not fully answered the question of how to assimilate BA technologies for competitive advantage. Most such research focuses on examining the success factors of BA implementation [9–11], but the process of BA assimilation (BAA) after implementation is not extensively studied. For example, the literature review of Seddon et al. [12] indicates that researchers approach BA business value through process and variance models. A process model defines the path from BA technology use to insight generation to organizational action and finally to the achievement of organizational success. A variance model studies the factors that contribute to organizational benefits, such as top management support, analytics culture, and analytics expertise. The authors summarize 16 factor models and conclude that process models are less studied than variance models. The present study argues that understanding the BAA process is a key to explaining business value generation because assimilation builds BA-enabled organizational capabilities that can help the firm achieve sustainable competitive advantage. Calls for analyzing the BA value mechanism have constantly appeared in the recent BA literature [4,13].

Moreover, national diferences in the BAA process remain unexplored in the BA value research. Exploring national diferences is meaningful because national economic cultural environments may enable or constrain an organization’s ability to assimilate BA technology. For example, prior studies [23] suggest that diferent attitudes toward analytics-based decision-making style between China and North American countries can play a role in shaping an organization’s cap ability to assimilate the benefits of BA-related systems.

Given the above argument, the present study addresses the ques tions of how BA technologies are assimilated by organizations to create sustainable competitive advantage and how BAA mechanisms difer across countries. Because of the interest in the link between BA and competitive advantage, this research adopts the theoretical lens of the resource-based view (RBV) [14,15] and its advancement in dynamic capability. The RBV argues that organizational resources and organi zational capabilities that are valuable, rare, nonimitable, and nonsubstitutable are sources of organizational advantage. The dynamic capability literature [38] further distinguishes between functional and dynamic capabilities, and argues that dynamic capabilities play a key role in enabling and renewing functional capabilities and are especially important in dynamic business environments. The RBV and its complementary literature on dynamic capability provide valuable guidance for us to understand how key BA resources and capabilities confer strategic value to firms. The RBV literature also provides theoretical foundation for cross-country comparisons of BAA mechanism because as per the RBV the importance and readiness of resources and capabilities required for competitive advantage can vary with the national contexts [22].

On the basis of RBV [14,15,38], this study builds a research fra mework to explain the mechanisms through which value is derived from BA through assimilation and to compare the diferences that might exist across diferent countries. We propose both direct and indirect paths of BAA process. On the one hand, BA technical capability is di rectly intertwined with organization business processes to form BAA, which is routinized use of BA in supporting functional business activ ities and a manifestation of lower order BA-enabled functional capabilities that confer BA-enabled competitive advantage. On the other hand, we suggest that general organizational absorptive capacity (AC), a higher order dynamic capability, bridges the initial acquisition of BA technical competence and ultimate BAA. Organizational AC is the ability of a firm to acquire, assimilate, transform, and apply new knowledge $[ 1 6 , 1 7 ] .$ . It is especially important to BAA because of the dynamic nature of BAA. Successful BAA requires a firm to manage a dynamic state in which insights are created and exploited on a con tinuous basis and business intelligence (BI) applications need to be evolved $[ 6 , 1 3 , 1 8 ]$ . Furthermore, we explore how the importance of BAA paths difers across diferent national contexts, drawing on the diferential importance of organizational AC as an enabler of innovation [16,17] in diferent economic-cultural environments.

The present study makes important contributions to the literature on information technology (IT) assimilation. First, it reveals BAA paths and enhances our understanding of BA value creation mechanism. Second, the research model sheds light on the diferences between the assimilation of process-based technologies (e.g., transaction processing systems (TPS) and enterprise resource planning (ERP)) and data technologies (e.g., BA). Prior research [19,20] mainly studied process-based technologies and concluded that IT $\mathrm { A C } ,$ defined as prior related IT knowledge, facilitates the assimilation of the technology when process knowledge is transferred from vendors/consultants to the clients on a one time basis during implementation [19,20]. BA, by contrast, is a generative technology. The BAA involves not only knowledge transfer for learning BA functionalities and tools but also continuous knowledge creation and exploitation, and the evolyability of BA tools and applica tions to fit changing user requirements [21]. This characteristic of BAA demands dynamic capability (i.e., general organizational AC) to facilitate organizational insight generation and BA tool renewal. Third, exploring the national diferences of BAA process enriches our understanding of BAA value mechanisms across countries and enhances our framework’s practical implications.

The following sections of this paper expand on the observation noted above. In Section 2, we present the theoretical background of this study. In Section 3, we explain the proposed BA-AC framework. In Section $^ { 4 , }$ we outline the research methodology. In Section $^ { 5 , }$ we present the data analysis and results. In Section $^ { 6 , }$ we discuss the contributions to research and practice, and in Section $^ { 7 , }$ we present the conclusion and proposals for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Information technology assimilation and the resource-based view

IT assimilation is the extent to which the use of technology difuses across organizational projects or work processes and becomes routinized in the activities of business processes [24] such as marketing, customer service, operations, and production. Liang et al. [25] suggested that ERP assimilation is the extent to which ERP technology is used to support business processes in terms of the diversity, volume, and depth of the support provided. Assimilation studies are postadoption studies that assume the decision to adopt a technology has been made and that acquisition and implementation of the system are complete [26–28]. These studies focus on whether a technology has been integrated and used in a manner that provides operational and strategic benefits [29]. Hence, in this sense, the IT assimilation process also conceptually reflects the process of IT-enabled organizational capability building.

The RBV is a valuable theoretical base for understanding the ways in which information systems (IS) resources confer strategic value to organizations [22]. According to the RBV [30], any resource can deliver value if it enables the organization to seize opportunities or nullify threats and is dificult for competitors to copy. Resources include organizational assets and capabilities. Assets include physical, financial, or human assets. Assets do not provide competitive advantage unless they are valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable [30] or are integrated or combined with other assets and routines to form organi zational capabilities [31]. Organizational capabilities refer to the ability of a firm to perform functions in a reliable way [31,32] and are combinative in nature. They consist of routines that are repeatable patterns of activities typically involving multiple actors [33,34] and that rely on assets available within the firm. Because routines are complex multiactor phenomena, the capabilities they create can be path dependent, causally ambiguous, or socially complex, thus becoming dificult for competitors to imitate [26], and are bounded to the organization. Or ganizational capabilities, owing to inimitability, imperfect mobility, and nonsubstitutability, are considered valuable resources that help organizations sustain competitive advantages [14].

Capabilities can be static or dynamic. Static organizational capabilities are also called operating or functional capabilities and are formed through routines related to certain business domains such as marketing capability [35], production capability [36], and IT capability [22,37]. Organizational capabilities are stable over time and sometimes criticized as rigid. Teece et al. [38, p.516] conceptualized dynamic capability as the ability of firms “to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments.” Dynamic capabilities are still routines, but they are “routines of learning routines” and can change the organizational resource base, according to Eisenhardt and Martin [39]. Examples of dynamic capabilities are $\mathbf { A C } ,$ learning capabilities, and strategic decision-making capabilities [39]. Because dynamic capabilities drive the creation, evolution, and recombination of other resources into new resources and organization routines, they are often considered higher order capabilities [39–41] that drive the renewal of organization functional capabilities (also called substantive, or first-order, capabilities [42]). Organization functional capabilities are application specific and contribute directly to competitive advantage. Dynamic capabilities are especially important for organizations in highly dynamic markets, where renewal and creation of organizational func tional routines and capabilities are frequent [39].

We argue that organizational AC is a higher order dynamic cap ability that plays an important role in the BAA process, allowing the organization to weave the BA system into business processes and form BA-enabled functional capabilities, thus rendering the process of BA use within the organization dificult to imitate.

## 2.2. Absorptive capacity as a dynamic capability

Organizational AC is a dynamic capability particularly relevant to our research. Eisenhardt and Martin [39] suggest three types of dynamic capabilities with one type focusing on resource integration (e.g., new product development), one on resource reconfiguration within the organization (e.g., resource allocation), and another on the gain and release of resources (e.g., acquisition and merger). AC is a dynamic capability focusing on resource (i.e., knowledge and skills) integration [20,40]. It is the ability of a firm to recognize the value of new external information, assimilate it, transform it into new knowledge, and apply it in various ways [16,17,20] such as creating innovative products and services [43,44], reconfiguring existing new product development capabilities [40], promoting the assimilation of new technology [20], and enhancing the knowledge creation and creativity of the IS development team [45,46]. AC essentially consists of an organization’s learning and innovation routines that can renew the organizational resource base. Jansen et al. [47] suggested potential AC and realized AC as two dimensions of AC. Potential AC, which includes knowledge ac quisition and assimilation, renews an organization’s knowledge stock. Realized AC, which includes knowledge transformation and exploitation, renews the resource base for innovation exploitation because it encompasses deriving new insights from the combination of existing and newly acquired knowledge, and incorporating transformed knowledge into operation.

AC has both outward-looking and inward-looking components [16,48]. A firm’s AC depends on not only the organization’s direct interface with the external environment but also the transfer of knowl edge across and within subunits [16,48,49]. Some identified outward looking organizational mechanisms that enhance AC are the relatedness of knowledge and strong learning relationships with knowledge sources [50–52]. Inward-looking organizational mechanisms include coordination capabilities (e.g., cross-functional interfaces, participation in decision-making, and job rotation) and socialization capabilities (e.g., connectedness and socialization tactics) [47]. Jansen et al. [47] suggest that coordination capabilities enhance potential AC by enhancing knowledge sharing, while socialization capabilities that lead to shared norms, beliefs, and values enhance realized AC by enhancing commit ment to knowledge exploitation.

IS research is informed by the AC research in two major directions: IT assimilation and IT business value [20]. In the IT assimilation research, AC is conceptualized as knowledge stocks rather than dynamic capability [20]. A key assumption of this research stream is that the information system itself represents new knowledge, and this knowl edge, sometimes referred to as best practices [53,54], is transferred from a system vendor or consultant to a client organization [19,55,56]. A firm with higher AC in the form of its prior related knowledge, knowledge diversity, and managerial IT knowledge enhances users’ ability as well as motivation to understand and exploit the technology [57], which contributes to assimilation of IS into organizational processes [20,50,54,58–62]. In the IT business value research, organizational AC is conceptualized as a dynamic capability [20], and identifying IT artifacts that improve the AC of an organization has been a central concern of this research stream [20,40,63–66]. Because of the nonlinear and unstructured activities involved in organizational AC, supporting AC requires a collection of IT tools. For example, Roberts, Galluch et al. [20] suggest that externally oriented outside–in IT capabilities (e.g., electronic data interchange), IT capabilities that span organizational boundaries (e.g., knowledge management systems), and inside–out IT capabilities (e.g., ERP systems) collectively afect an organization’s capacity to acquire, assimilate, transform, and apply external knowledge.

The review of the organizational AC literature suggests that IT assimilation studies have not incorporated the role of AC as a dynamic capability. This is because prior research has mainly studied processbased technologies in which one-time knowledge transfer is suficient. However, in the case of BA technologies, where continuous knowledge creation and organization resource renewal are important, the role of organization AC as a dynamic capability becomes prominent.

## 3. Research framework

This research draws on the RBV and the complementary literature on dynamic capability to propose a research framework to explain the BAA process. As shown in the research framework (Fig. 1), BA technical competency (BAC), defined as BA technologies and expertise, afects BAA either directly or indirectly. In the direct path, the BA assets are integrated directly into organization process through the assimilating prepackaged software, and the resulting BAA is bounded to the organization and dificult to imitate, conferring competitive advantage to firms. In the indirect path, BAC afects BAA through a higher order dynamic capability, organization AC, which drives both the renewal of business processes and BA tools to unleash the full potential of BA technologies in helping organization achieve competitive advantage. We also analyze how the importance of direct and indirect paths varie across diferent national contexts.

## 3.1. Business analytics competency and assimilation

BAC is a subset of IT capability that is managed by the IT department or BA department, depending on the company’s organization structure. It is a combination of BA assets, resources, and processes that a firm acquires, which includes BA objects, operations, and expertise [66]. BA objects refer to artifacts assisting in the “acquisition, processing, storage, dissemination, and use” of BA [57]. They include the hardware, software, database, and applications that a firm either purchases from IT vendors or builds in-house. BA operations refer to BA processes, work practices, and routines. BA expertise is the technical knowledge of how to maintain the BA data infrastructure and to de velop, use, and implement BA applications as well as a general understanding of trends related to BA technologies.

BAC contributes to BAA, which refers to the extent to which BA is efectively used to support the activities and decision-making in different business functional areas such as operations and production, customer service, and marketing and sales [66]. In other words, BAA is a manifestation of the BA-enabled organizational functional capabilities because BA use is integrated into organizational functional routines [33,34].

BAC can contribute to BAA directly because it represents physical BA assets and routines that enable organizational functions and form

BA-enabled functional capabilities. According to the RBV, organizational capabilities are combinative in nature [31,32] and the combination of resources leads to the embeddedness of resources within the organization as a source of competitive advantage. During the direct assimilation process, BA assets—mainly the acquired prepackaged BA software with built-in applications and functionalities—are combined with and integrated into users’ existing decision-making routines in various functional activities. The knowledge of how to use the applications is transferred from the BA vendor to BA business users through the support of BA technical staf. A mutual adaptation of BA applications and decision-making routines may occur during the implementation. The embeddedness of BAC resources in functional activities leads to resource immobility. Higher quality BAC and associated assets (i.e., higher-quality prepackaged software and higher-quality BA expertise) can contribute to better knowledge transfer, resource combinations, and organization adaptation, and therefore greater assimilation of BA into diferent functional areas. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a). Business analytics competency (BAC) has a direct positive relationship with business analytics assimilation (BAA).

However, BAA requires adaptation and evolvability beyond the capabilities conferred by prepackaged software. The direct assimilation of BA limits a firm’s capability to fully leverage the strategic benefits from BA. The BA applications provided by vendors or even developed by BA personnel often cannot satisfy all user requirements. For example, Norfolk Southern Railway, a US railway company, observed during their BI journey of 20 years that, “[h]owever, there is a chasm between IT people who build applications and the people who use them” [67]. BA user requirements are essentially business questions, which can be idiosyncratic, creative, diverse, and dynamic. They change as the external and internal events evolve, rendering the existing BA applications obsolete. A frequent complaint is that the responses from IT staf are slow. This is especially a problem when the scope of BA becomes complex and BA is expected to support strategies and innovations, which are claimed to be its real benefits. In this case, the BA applications are tightly coupled with organization routines renewal and creation to capture the benefits of BA-enabled innovations and strategic renewal.

This discussion highlights that BA departments face great uncertainties on which specific BA applications to provide and how the organization functional capabilities will be renewed as internal and external events evolve, as the relationship between the required BA applications and the renewed organizational functional capability is not linear. To address the challenge, some companies minimize the ofering of special purpose applications and focus on building high-quality, accessible, and easy-to-use general purpose data infrastructure and tools that can empower business users to analyze the data as they require and create BI applications that fit their local needs [68,69]. This approach leverages the characteristics of BA as a generative technology. Generativity is the capacity of a technology to allow its users to develop new things out of it [70]. Generativity denotes a technology’s overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences.

The key issue with regard to this approach is that employees and organizations have limited capabilities to fully leverage the solution. We suggest therefore that in addition to its direct role in assimilation, BAC is also leveraged to enhance the AC of the firm, a higher order dynamic capability, which can equip organizations and its employees with motivation and capabilities to create more BA applications as well as renew organization functional capabilities. According to the dynamic capability theory [38,39], dynamic capabilities are the drivers behind the creation, evolution, and recombination of other resources into new sources of competitive advantage, and are especially important for firms in a turbulent market. In the case of BA, organizational AC facilitates an organization and its employees to engage BA assets to explore and create organizational capabilities and BA applications, resulting in BAA into various organizational functions. Therefore, we propose that the relationship between BAC and BAA is mediated by organizational AC. H2 and H3 provide more details about the indirect link, but here, we propose:

Hypothesis 1b (H1b). BAC has an indirect positive relationship with BAA mediated through organizational AC.

## 3.2. Business analytics competency and organizational absorptive capacity

IT business value research suggests that a range of IT artifacts can enhance dynamic capability and organizational AC [20,40,63–66]. The dynamic capability research also suggests the unique nature of IT support. According to Eisenhardt and Martin [39], the development and evolution of dynamic capability is an experiential process that is nonlinear and features learning by doing (vs. learning before doing), especially in the highly dynamic markets. The BA technologies are a collection of data analytical services, including data, data models, analytical tools, and algorithms, and supplementary functionalities, such as social media and knowledge-sharing forums, which provide an ideal environment for an organization to cultivate organizational AC. Following Roberts et al. [20], we argue that BAC can provide three types of support.

First, BA technological competency has an outside–in component that facilitates the ability of the firm to acquire and assimilate external knowledge. For example, in the big data era more data sources in BA applications are gathered from outside the company, through which organizations can gain insights on their customers, products, and markets [20]. The process of determining the data to collect is the process of understanding the value of the data. Second, BA technological competency has a spanning component that facilitates both potential AC (acquisition and assimilation) and realized AC (transformation and application). For example, data warehouses aggregate data from all functional areas, integrate data across business silos, and provide employees more access to data as well as a better context to understand the potential application of the data [62]. The communication-driven decision support systems and the social media func tionalities in some BA products support socialization among employees, therefore contributing to realized AC [47]. Third, BA technological competency has an inside–out component that facilitates knowledge application. At present, new product development and commercialization are performed in an iterative way. BAC enables a company to closely monitor market reactions after the initial new product launch and to respond quickly in refining products in a cost-efective way [71,72].

Dynamic capability is combinative in nature, as is organization AC [39]. By leveraging BAC, a company can integrate the BA assets, tools, and capability in the organizational AC routines with the support of IT staf, resulting in BA-enhanced AC routines. The organization’s socialization and coordination capabilities are enhanced. Employees’ individual AC is also enhanced. For example, in the case of Norfolk Southern Railway, they commented that “Norfolk Southern bridges this chasm through hybrid employees who have both domain knowledge and the technical skills to develop applications” [67]. The accessibility and adaptability of the data and tools provide employees a low-cost approach to experiment with the data and tools and cultivate employees’ individual AC. BA expertise may be involved in helping individual employees to master the tools. In sum, BAC provides a technical environment and cultivates organizational AC. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 2 (H2). BA technical competency is positively related to organizational AC.

## 3.3. Organizational absorptive capacity and business analytics assimilation

As suggested by the RBV and dynamic capability theory, the key role of dynamic capability is to create and renew organizational re source bases [39–41]. The organizational AC literature also suggests that AC is an organization’s learning routine that enables it to innovate through knowledge resource integration, generating new knowledge, new products, and new business processes and routines $\left[ 1 6 , 1 7 , 3 9 \right]$

BA-enhanced organizational AC can help an organization renew its BA applications and organizational functional routines to capture the business opportunities opened up by BA technologies. Further, such renewals are evolvable because of the generativity of the organization and its employees. In a company with BA-enhanced $\mathsf { A C } ,$ employees develop hybrid knowledge on innovation through experimentation and data analytics, BA data and tools, and the business domain knowledge. With or without the support of BA staf, they are capable of analyzing data, generating insights, and applying these insights to commercial ends. These activities may result in new organization routines and capabilities. Employees are also capable of creating new BI applications for reuse by other employees as a by-product of their own data analysis activities because they have the technical knowledge. The generativity nature of data technologies has been studied by some researchers who note that once the data infrastructure is set, most of the BA applications are built bottom-up by business employees, and not IT staf and vendors [21,67]. The socialization mechanisms in place in organizations with high AC also help with the difusion of the BI applications among employees. Through this process, the BA is assimilated into the business process.

The importance of organizational AC as a dynamic capability in promoting BAA can be justified by the unique characteristics of BA technologies. Process-based technologies, such as ERP and TPS, are static in nature. Changes occur once during the implementation stage when knowledge is transferred from the vendor to the client, and then the same business process is used until the system is upgraded or changed again. This is the reason some studies describe ERP to be “like pouring concrete into a company” [73]. Employees only need to accommodate a one-time change, and then remain passive in the post implementation stage. They are forced to use the system to complete the processes.

However, BAA is dynamic and evolving in nature. Changes occur not only in the implementation stage but also in the post-implementation stage [67]. Any aspect of the BA-enabled routines can change the BI applications, tools, data, insights, and company resource bases for capturing the discovered opportunities. Employees need to be cogni tively active to create insights and applications and socially active to embrace continuous organizational changes for capturing business opportunities. Without AC, an organization is less likely to evolve BA applications and BA-enhanced organizational functional capabilities and enjoy the key benefits of BA. For example, generating and exploiting insights are nonlinear, experimental, and risky activities. Without AC routines to guide employee data analytics, the failure rate will be high, which is especially a concern because BA is used by average employees rather than trained R&D researchers. Without the evolvability enabled by organizational AC, BA will have limited value to the organization, and given time, it is less likely to be assimilated into business processes. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). Organizational AC is positively related to the level of BAA

## 3.4. Business analytics assimilation and business analytics-enabled competitive advantage

BAA is the routinized use of BA to support activities in diferent functional areas. BAA results in BA-enabled functional capabilities in which BA assets and routines are integrated into the users’ decision making routines either directly or indirectly. According to the RBV, such capabilities are sources of competitive advantage because they are socially embedded in the business process and context, bounded to the organization, and dificult to imitate [30].

Indirect assimilation through AC will have an especially high potential for sustaining competitive advantage. In the direct path, the prepackaged solutions are accessible to all companies and the knowledge on how to integrate them into the business is more prone to be deciphered and shared; hence, the competitive advantage achieved this way may be eroded sooner or later. However, BAA, or the BA-enabled new organizational routines through AC, is deeply embedded in the business process, complex, hard to imitate, and capable of evolving. First, the BI applications are born and therefore naturally embedded in the employees’ normal business activities and routines. Second, considering the number of employees and their social connections, the BA application’s embeddedness can be complex and causally ambiguous. Third, BA-enabled routines are evolvable because BI applications are developed by local users in a timely, cost-efective manner. In sum, we propose that BAA, regardless whether achieved directly or indirectly, contributes to the company’s overall BA-enabled competitive advantage (BACA).

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The level of BAA is positively related to business analytics-enabled competitive advantage (BACA).

## 3.5. Cross-country comparison

As suggested by RBV-based IT business value research [22,74,75], IT value creation mechanisms can be moderated by environmental factors. Wade and Hulland [22] suggested that the macro environment, denoting country and meta-country specific factors, shapes IT application for the improvement of organizational performance. Jarvenpaa and Leidner [74] suggest that companies in developing countries face constraints in applying IT in the areas of education, expertise, infrastructure, and culture.

In this research, we propose that the importance of BAA paths can be contingent on the economic and cultural environment of diferent countries. A microenvironment that encourages and demands innovation will favor the indirect path more than the direct path of BAA be cause the indirect path relies more on the innovation capability (i.e., organizational AC) of firms. This research compares two groups of microenvironment, Western (such as the United States and Canada) and East Asian (such as China and Malaysia) countries, because of their similarities in culture and economic environments. Such a grouping has been widely used in cross-cultural analysis [76–78]. Chu, Luo, and Chen’s [78] research indicates that the United States and China are the two most representative countries in each group. A general hypothesis is that the Western countries put less constraint on and more pressure on innovation than do the East Asian countries, and therefore rely on the indirect path for BA-based competition.

First, from an economic perspective, Western and East Asian countries are under diferent pressure for innovation because of their diferences in development stage and sources of competitive advantage [79]. Compared with East Asian countries, Western countries are advanced in their developmental stage, markets are highly eficient, and the economies are developed into a service economy that relies on innovation (vs. assets) as the diferentiator [79]. In the case of BAA, there is less room and profitability for a firm to compete in a highly eficient, competitive, and developed market by using BA packaged solutions because these solutions can also be acquired by other firms, and are less socially embedded and sustainable. Hence, firms in a highly eficient market favor the indirect path from BAC to BAA, which is more con tingent on a firm’s innovation capability and less likely to be imitated.

Second, from the national culture perspective, Western countries have a culture that embraces innovation more than do East Asian countries and, therefore, favor the indirect path of BAA. The national culture is normally studied under the Hofstede dimensions (i.e., power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, and long term orientation vs. pragmatism) [80,81], and these dimensions are often found related to corporate innovativeness. For example, Khan and Cox [82] have found that innovative societies are characterized by high individualism, low masculinity, and high pragmatism but that uncertainty avoidance is not related to corporate innovativeness. Chen, Podolski, and Veeraraghavan’s [83] research has verified that cultural norms of individualism and uncertainty avoidance afect corporate in novation. Taras, Kirkman, and Steel’s [84] meta review of the Hofstede dimensions has confirmed that individualism and uncertainty avoidance are prominent factors that afect organizational innovation, with individualism having the highest impact. Among the research we reviewed [82–84], individualism is consistently found to have the highest impact on organizational innovation, followed by uncertainty avoid ance. This is because individualism places a higher value on personal goals and is associated with a predisposition to accept novelty and be creative. Individualists are more likely to champion new ideas in the face of resistance. Because Western countries tend toward high in dividualism, we propose that Western countries rely more on the in direct path for BAA than do East Asian countries, especially because in this research context, innovation and BAA are assumed to be emergent from local users and the indirect path for BAA fits well in a high individualism-driven innovation environment.

Interestingly, prior research [81] suggests that when considering economic development, individualism is the only factor among all the Hofstede dimensions that is positively related to national wealth when a country is developed into a service economy because of the capacity of individualism to generate innovations. Therefore, combining the cultural and economic perspectives (i.e., individualism and market eficiency), we expect that BAC is more likely to be assimilated through AC in Western countries, while East Asian countries are more likely to favor the direct path from BAC to BAA.

Hypothesis 5 (H5). The mediating efect of AC is more prominent in Western than East Asian countries.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Measurement

Three major constructs, BAC, AC, and BAA, are conceptualized as reflective–formative higher order constructs. This is a type of aggregate construct in which the dimensions are algebraically combined to form the construct, while the indicators of each dimension are diferent manifestations (reflections) of their respective dimensions [85]. Table 1 provides the instruments of each construct, which are explained in the following.

BAC is measured as a higher order construct with three dimensions: BA objects (BACOB), BA operations (BACOP), and BA knowledge (BACKN). Five items are developed to measure BA objects, which refer to a firm’s extent of BA hardware, software, and support personnel (BACOB1-5). BA operation measures a firm’s implementation of BA processes, work practices, and routines (BACOP1-6). BA knowledge (BACKN) measures the extent to which a firm possesses a body of technical knowledge about BA applications and systems (BACKN1-4). For the measurement of BAC, a 7-point Likert scale adapted from Tippins and Sohi [66] is used, and it covers both tangible (i.e., BACOB) and intangible (i.e., BACOP and BACKN) BA assets that an organization owns. They are measured as reflective scales.

Thirteen items are developed to measure organizational AC, an organization’s ability to acquire, assimilate/transform, and exploit external knowledge. The measurement is developed based on the work of Pavlou and Sawy [40] and Cadiz et al. [86]. We use higher order constructs to organize and measure AC. In the literature, AC is measured with various numbers of dimensions. Some researchers measure it as a one-dimensional construct [19,40,53,62,65]; Setia and Patel [87] use two high dimensions (i.e., potential and realized AC); others measure AC with three dimensions: knowledge acquisition/assessment, assimilation/transformation, and application [43,86]; meanwhile, Liu et al. [88] use four dimensions. In our case, we adopt an exploratory approach and use two dimensions to measure organizational AC as both potential AC and realized AC. Potential AC includes items related to the firm’s ability to acquire and assimilate existing knowledge and realized AC includes items related to the firms’ ability to transform/create and apply new knowledge; both are measured as reflective scales. The two dimensions are theoretically grounded [47,87,89], and the psychometric properties of the construct are examined in Section 5.

BAA measures a firm’s strategic benefits achieved through BA in three major business functions—customer relations (5 items for BAACR), business operations (6 items for BAABO), and marketing and sales (3 items for BAAMS)—with each dimension combining both generic strategies and business activities related to a particular business function. The three-dimensional measurement of BAA is adapted from Elbashir, Collier, and Sutton [90] and covers all the primary activities in Porter’s value chain model: the front-end activities, such as customer relations and marketing and sales, and back-end activities, such as inbound logistics, outbound logistics, and production. The measurement uses an 11-point Likert scale following the original measurement of Elbashir et al. [90].

We conceptualize BAC, AC, and BAA as formative second-order constructs that are created by their lower order dimensions. Petter, Straub, and Rai [91] provide four rules of decisions on whether a construct should be formative or reflective: the direction of causality, interchangeability of the indicators, covariation among the dimensions, and nomological net of the construct dimensions. On the basis of these four rules, formative models are deemed appropriate because these first-order dimensions facilitate or “form” the second-order overall BAC, AC, and BAA. Table 2 provides detailed justification of the formative nature for each construct. The BACA is measured as a reflective construct with three items to measure the competitive advantage that an organization achieves through the use of BA. They are developed according to Barney [30] and Chen et al. [92], and measured using a 7- point Likert scale.

Two levels of control variables for BAA, our main research focus, are included in the model. First, at the industry level, an industry dummy variable measures whether the observation is in the service industry or goods industry. We measure 17 industries in the survey and classify them into service and goods industries according to the United Nation’s industry classification [93]. Service industries may be more advanced in BAA than are goods industries because service industries are more market driven and more likely to invest in BA technologies. Second, at the firm level, four variables are included: the BA tenure (i.e., the time since BA adoption), sales revenue, number of employees, and BA sophistication as measured by whether BA is implemented throughout the enterprise or not. It is believed that the longer BA tools are adopted, the higher the BAA level as well as the BACA level are. Meanwhile, the larger an organization’s size in terms of sales revenue and number of employees, the more the slack resources it owns and the more likely the organization devotes resources for BAA.

## 4.2. Data collection

This study used a structured survey method for data collection. The unit of analysis is the organization because the assimilation of BA into the organization is our major concern. Therefore, CIOs, BA managers and staf, and other informants who are knowledgeable about BA use in the entire organization were considered qualified respondents of the survey. BA involves close collaboration between BA personnel, IT staf, and business unit employees. In some companies, the BA department i separate from the IT department and involves employees from both business and IT departments [94]. Accordingly, the CIOs and BA managers, staf, and consultants have company-wide information to answer questions about organizational constructs, such as AC. A sample of 600 organizations was collected from six countries (i.e., Australia, Canada. China. Korea, Malaysia, and the United States). We collected data from diferent countries mainly to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the research results, and to investigate the impact of the national economic–cultural environment on BAA mechanisms.

## 5.1. The measurement model

We used partial least squares (PLS) to analyze the data because of the exploratory nature of the study. For some countries, such as Canada and Australia, we had small sample sizes and PLS can accommodate data analysis with small sample sizes. Data analysis was conducted mainly with SmartPLS 3.0 [99]. Because the study includes hierarchical reflective–formative constructs, the guidelines of Hair et al. [100], Hair et al. [101], and Ringle et al. [102] were adopted to validate the

measurement model.

First-order constructs reliability and validity analysi

The psychometric properties of the nine lower order constructs, including their reliability and validity, are discussed in this section. First, Table 4 shows the construct reliability index, including composite reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s alpha. The recommended threshold values of CR and Cronbach’s alpha are 0.8 [103] and 0.7 [104], respectively. As shown in Table 4, all the first-order constructs meet the above criteria and show strong evidence of high reliability.

Second, Fornell and Larcker [105] suggest that the average variance extracted from a construct should exceed 0.5 for the items to demonstrate convergent validity, and the correlations between any two constructs should be lower than the square root of the average variance shared by the items within a construct. As shown in Tables 4 and 5, all the first-order constructs satisfy these criteria for convergent and discriminant validity.

Third, the outer loadings of the indicators on their respective constructs, as well as the cross-loadings on other constructs (as shown in Table 6), were analyzed for convergent and discriminant validity. According to Hair et al. [100], an indicator’s outer loading on the associated construct should be above 0.708 and should be greater than all of its loadings on other constructs (i.e., the cross-loadings) to demonstrate convergent and discriminant validity. As shown in Table 6, all the first order constructs satisfy these criteria: all outer loadings on the associated constructs are above 0.708, statistically significant, and greater than the cross-loadings on other first-level constructs, demonstrating convergent and discriminant validity.

Fourth, the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT) suggested by Henseler. Ringle. and Sarstedt [106] was analyzed. The HTMT is derived from the multitrait–multimethod matrix by calculating the average of the heterotrait–heteromethod correlations relative to the average of the monotrait–heteromethod correlations. The HTMT has proved more sensitive to a lack of discriminant validity than the average variance extracted and cross-loading methods. Henseler, Ringle, and Sarstedt [106] recommend a criterion of 0.90 or 0.85 (more conservative) to assess the HTMT. The HTMT tests resulted in the following items being dropped from the measurement: ACAC3, ACTR2, ACEX3, BAACR5, BACOP1, and BAAMS1. After adjustment, the constructs demonstrated good discriminant validity. Table 7 shows the results of the HTMT analysis for all the first-order constructs. As shown in the table, most of the HTMT ratios are below 0.85. The exceptions are the ratios between BAABO and BAACR, between BACOP and BACOB, and between BACOP and BACKN, which are higher than 0.85 but lower than 0.9. Because the pairs represent dimensions of the same construct. which can be correlated, we kept these lower order con: structs to maintain the completeness of the construct domain.

## 5.1.1. Second-order constructs measurement model validation

The normal validation guideline for reflective scales does not apply to formative constructs because formative constructs are aggregate constructs that do not assume the covariation of indicators. Multicollinearity is a concern for formative constructs. Following the guidelines of Ringle et al. [102] and Hair et al. [100], we calculated the variance inflation factors (VIFs) at the second-order level. All VIF values, as shown in Table 8, are below 10 and thus do not indicate mul ticollinearity problems [91,107]. In formative constructs, weights measure the relative contribution of a dimension to its construct. We also examined whether the weights of dimensions were significant $[ 1 0 0 , 1 0 2 , 1 0 7 ]$ . As shown in Table $^ { 8 , }$ all the weights are significant at the 0.01 level. The validity of diferent dimensions as significant parts of the higher order constructs is therefore warranted.

## 5.1.2. Common method variance

As the survey research is self-reported in nature, which introduces common method bias, several approaches were adopted to prevent or check such potential biases. First, diferent response formats were used in the questionnaire design [108], including a 7-point Likert scale, 11- point Likert scale, and interval scale (for some control variables). Second, the common method bias would have resulted in extremely high correlations $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , r \geq 0 . 9 0 )$ [40]. The highest correlation among constructs in this study is 0.829 (between BACOP and BACKN, as shown in Table 5), and at the item level, no correlation exceeds 0.9. Third, the Harman one-factor test [108] was conducted by loading all of the variables into an exploratory factor analysis. The test results show a slight symptom of common method bias because the highest factor explained 45.9% of the variance among all measures. Fourth, a common method factor was created and incorporated into the models, following Liang et al. [25]. The coeficients for both measurement and structural models did not change much and remained significant after controlling for the common method factor. Hence, we conclude that there is a slight common method bias issue, but the model statistics and their significance are not afected.

## 5.2. The structural model

We used the two-stage approach to analyze the structural model. This approach is an estimation method suitable for models with higher order formative constructs in endogenous positions. It estimates the construct scores of the first-order constructs in a first-stage model without the second-order construct present, and subsequently uses these first-stage construct scores as indicators for the higher order latent variable in a separate second-stage analysis [102,109]. We ran the structural model using both the full sample and the country-level samples.

The test results for the structural model are shown in Fig. 2 and Table 9. For the full sample, hypotheses H1a about the relationship between BAC and BAA, H2 on the relationship between BAC and AC, H3 on the relationship between AC and $\mathsf { B A A } ,$ and H4 on the relationship between BAA and BACA are validated and supported. The coeficient is 0.360 for BAC and BAA, 0.807 for BAC and $\mathrm { A C } ,$ and 0.364 for AC and BAA, and all the path coeficients are significant at the 0.01 level. In terms of the control variables, none but BA sophistication is significantly and negatively related to BAA. This is probably because large-scale BA implementation can bring potentially high benefits but also risks.

Predictive accuracy of the model is assessed by the $R ^ { 2 }$ values as shown in Fig. 2. According to the efect sizes defined for $R ^ { 2 }$ by Cohen [110] (small: 0.2; medium: 0.5; large: 0.8), all the efect sizes in the present study can be classified as medium (0.650 for AC, 0.465 for BAA, and 0.538 for BACA).

## 5.2.1. Mediation tests

We also tested the mediation efect of AC on BAC-BAA relationships, as suggested by H1b. The significance of direct and indirect efects was obtained through bootstrapping. The Sobel test procedure was adopted to investigate whether the mediation is full or partial [111]. The results are shown in the Mediation test section of Table 9.

First. a direct model without AC was tested, and the path was found to be significant. The path coeficient of BAC on BAA is 0.661 and significant at the 0.01 level. Second, an indirect model with AC added was run. The path coeficient between AC and BAA is reduced to 0.360, and both the direct effect (the path coefficient is 0.360) and the indirect efect (the path coeficient is 0.293) are significant. This suggests that AC does partially mediate the relationship between BAC and BAA, and hence confirms H1b. The direct efect coeficient (0.350) is slightly higher than the indirect efect coeficient (0.293), indicating that the direct efect is slightly stronger than the indirect efect.

## 6. Discussion

This study empirically examined an important mediation efect of organizational AC on the relationship between BAC and BAA, which captures the uniqueness of BA technologies in comparison to traditional process-based IS. BA technologies cultivate organizational AC, which, in turn, contributes to BAA by providing assimilation motivation and capability.

BAC was found to have both a direct effect and an indirect effect on BAA, as hypothesized in H1. The results further indicate that the direct efect (the direct coeficient is 0.360) is slightly stronger than the indirect efect. The direct path coeficient is 0.360, and the indirect path efect is 0.294 (i.e., 0.807\*0.364). The diference in coeficient of 0.066 suggests that the prepackaged functionalities play a slightly stronger role in BAA than customized functionalities developed in organizations.

AC was found to mediate the relationship between BAC and BAA, as hypothesized in H2 and H3. The finding of H2 about the relationship between BAC and AC also supports the literature on IT business value, which argues that IT can improve organizational AC [20,66]. The research finding of H3 corroborates findings from the IT assimilation literature in which organizational AC plays a major role in providing motivation and ability of firms to facilitate absorption of the knowledge transferred from external vendors/consultants and from new application and insight generation internally [20,90]. The role of AC in med iating the relationships was confirmed in most country-specific sam ples, implying that the mediating role of AC may be a global phenomenon.

We found surprising results for the control variables. Specifically, the coeficients for the firm size, as measured by the number of employees and annual revenue, and for the length of time of adoption are not significant. The insignificant results may be because data analytics technologies are now undergoing significant changes, which enable entrepreneurial firms and small- and medium-sized enterprises to catch up by adopting the latest technology and to flexibly reconfigure their resources to achieve high BI assimilation.

Cross-country comparisons suggest that the cultural–economic environment may afect BAA mechanisms. First, the national development stage can afect BAA mechanisms as we observe that the two countries with the highest contrast in market eficiency (the US-CA and China) exhibit systematic diferences in BAA. This finding corroborates the current research on the diferences in innovations between the United States and China [113,114]. The US economy is well developed and has moved beyond pure physical asset advantage, while China is a new, fast follower participant in the open economy and the technology world [114]. Hence, eficient use of prepackaged BA can still bring organizations competitive advantage in China. Second, evidence of the impact of the national culture (especially individualism) is less strong as the two countries with the highest contrast in individualism $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ US-CA and Korea) are not systematically diferent in BAA paths. A possible explanation is that because IT has greatly enhanced cultural globali zation (i.e., the rapid transmission of ideas, meanings, and values around the world through the Internet) [115] and culture convergence [78], an organization’s attitude and behavior toward BA use converges regardless of the national culture and values.

This study advances the knowledge base in several ways. First, it contributes to the BA value research by focusing on the BAA process after firms build BAC. Past studies on BA success have focused mainly on discrete success factors but have not fully explored the BAA process [9–11]. This study proposes both direct and indirect paths of BAA and adopts the lens of the RBV to explain how these paths might generate sustainable competitive advantage. To our best knowledge, no prior research has studied the direct and indirect efects of BAC on BAA.

Second, this study examines the role of general organizational AC in BAA. In prior BAA studies, such as Elbashir et al. [29,90], only IT-related AC has been examined, and not the role of general AC. We extend the research and argue that the unique nature of BA technology as a generative technology demands the study of the more general organi zational AC, which can be cultivated by BA technologies and enhances BAA. Through this process, the outcome of BAA involves not only the implementation of the original functionalities but also the possible generation of new BA applications by employees.

Third, this study contributes to the IT assimilation literature in general. The literature has focused mainly on knowledge transfer [20,53] but has ignored the continuous knowledge creation requirements of technology difusion. This study argues that, depending on the nature of the technology, knowledge creation can be an even more important requirement for technology assimilation. Fourth, this study explores the role of the cultural–economic environment on BAA mechanisms. Although the research results are explorative, they provide a direction for future researchers to incorporate the micro-environmental factors. As far as we know, the current BA value research has not paid attention to these factors [13].

This study has implications for practice, especially for BA stakeholders who are involved in planning, reviewing, and implementing BA initiatives. First, organizations should not only implement the BA technologies and learn how to use the tools but also focus on using BA technologies to improve AC, which will bring more prolonged and generative BAA outcomes. The BAC must be deployed in a way that enhances information acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation capability of firms so that BAC can be best leveraged to enhance BAA. An organization should make easy-to-use, self-service data tools, and high-quality data accessible to employees and provide social media tools for employees to share insights and newly created applications. Given time, good locally fit BA applications will emerge from the self-organized activities of employees.

Second, the research results on the national diferences suggest the urgency of cultivating AC in diferent cultural and economic environments. Companies in the developed economies with eficient markets face a more urgent need to build organizational AC to achieve BAdriven competitive advantage than do those in the developing economies. Third, the multidimensional measurement of key constructs also provides BA stakeholders guidelines to evaluate an organization’s BAC, organizational AC, and BA value from multidimensional perspectives. For example, BAA should be gauged against value chain processes such as customer relations, business operations, and marketing and sales. BA investment should be used not only to purchase BA objects but also to acquire BA expertise. A comprehensive perspective is valuable as it covers the related elements, which should not be taken for granted.

## 7. Conclusion and future research

This research conceptualized and empirically examined the med iating role of organizational AC between BAC and BAA and the subsequent impact on competitive advantage. Using the PLS method with data collected from 600 organizations across six countries, this study concluded that organizational AC plays a pivotal role in mediating the efects of BAC on BAA. Further, BAA was shown to have a positive impact on competitive advantage.

As in any other research effort, this study has some limitations. which also ofer opportunities for further research. First, the BA-AC framework of this research only considered the BAC, BAA, and AC. Hence, future research can incorporate more industry and organiza tional contextual factors—for example, comparison of companies in the more turbulent markets with those in the stable markets. The direct path to BACA may be more important for companies in the turbulent industries than for those in the stable industries. Second, in the crosscountry comparison, although we found some diferences between US-CA and China and interpret them as the impact of the country development stage, the evidence needs to be further confirmed. The lack of evidence for the impact of national culture may also be because of the small sample size. Future research can further explore the impact of economic–cultural conditions by collecting data from more countries.
