# On the contingent value of IT-based capabilities for the competitive advantage of SMEs: Mechanisms and empirical evidence

Paolo Neirotti<sup>a,</sup>\*, Elisabetta Raguseo<sup>b</sup>

## A B S T R A C T

This article assesses the value of the capabilities developed through the use of Information Technology (IT) in Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs), by analysing whether these capabilities are a determinant of the intra-industry differential in their performance. Our work makes two principal contributions to the understanding of the value of IT. First, the article shows that externally oriented IT-based capabilities have a greater impact on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s intra-industry differential in revenue growth and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability than those capabilities focused on ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvement, namely internally oriented IT-based capabilities. Essentially, this result is consistent with two facts: i) the greater importance that market and supply chain relationships have for <sup>fi</sup>rms with a limited scale compared to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvement, and ii) the greater social and organizational complexity captured by IT use in <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external relationships. Second, through a contingent perspective, our article shows that the returns of externally oriented IT-based capabilities are more likely to provide performance advantages to SMEs in contexts of low muni<sup>fi</sup>cence and low dynamism. This evidence thus highlights that under these environmental conditions, IT is more likely to hinder than to facilitate SMEs’ ability to respond to market changes and new opportunities.

## 1. Introduction

There has long been debate on the impact that Information Technology (IT) has on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The consensus is that IT can lead certain <sup>fi</sup>rms to a competitive advantage and that value creation may manifest in different ways, strictly depending on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s organizational and environmental context. Nevertheless, given the increasing commoditization of IT resources [1], the mechanisms of value creation from IT have not been completely understood. This is particularly true in Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs), which face different conditions for IT use compared to their larger counterparts.

SMEs are not small versions of large enterprises, and they face higher costs for adopting and using IT compared to their larger counterparts. Because their business processes are highly idiosyncratic and less formalized, SMEs face signi<sup>fi</sup>cant adjustment costs [2] when introducing IT systems and undertaking related organizational changes in their business processes. Furthermore, because of their limited scale, SMEs may not be able to afford to develop internal resources and formalized managerial routines to govern IT assets, leading them to rely on consultants and vendors. This may reduce their opportunity to use IT as a strategic asset based on the use of rare, appropriable and non-imitable resources [3]. Finally, their limited vertical integration and low scale may lead SMEs to search for IT usage with an external orientation to improve their coordination and collaboration with external partners and their ability to enter market niches on an international scale.

An external orientation in IT use is a critical success factor for responding to market changes and new opportunities. However, this type of use requires a more complex array of managerial and organizational capabilities that is necessary when IT is used only to automate business processes and improve internal ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (e.g., [4,5]), and it is thus more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for SMEs to master. The complexity that SMEs face in developing this use for IT is corroborated by the limited diffusion of IT solutions supporting an external orientation – such as Customer Relationship Management (CRM) tools – among SMEs, as con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by European statistics. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the country-level diffusion rate for such technologies for SMEs range from 7% to 33%, whereas for large <sup>fi</sup>rms, the same systems have among enterprises an average diffusion rate of 77% in the top 15 European nations [6].

As a possible consequence of the limited diffusion of IT systems among SMEs, their market share is shrinking [7]. Differences in their ability to use IT help to explain the increasing performance dispersion across <sup>fi</sup>rms [8] in those industries where IT plays a more critical role in competition, with SMEs being able to capture only a limited part of the value generated by competitive actions enabled by IT. In some industry contexts, SMEs are thus losing ground because of poor and limited use of IT resources; this raises the issue that the industry environment in<sup>fl</sup>uences how resources are used and their role in enabling value creation. Thus, the industry environment in<sup>fl</sup>uences the value that <sup>fi</sup>rms can create through these resources [9] and it is a possible moderator in the relationship between IT use and performance. In this regard, past research conducted on large enterprises has shown that IT resources play a more important role in contexts of higher uncertainty and market growth. However, SMEs can demonstrate a profoundly different response to environmental conditions than their larger counterparts. For example, past research has shown that SMEs can react to environmental uncertainty by decreasing their information processing needs rather than by processing more information (as may occur in larger <sup>fi</sup>rms) or that they may be unable to pursue the growth opportunities available in the external environment due to the limited availability of slack resources.

These considerations highlight the need for a better understanding of how SMEs can use IT resources for value creation depending on their environmental conditions. In this article, we enrich this understanding by integrating contingency theory logic into a Resource-Based View (RBV) interpretation of how IT use affects <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. We operationalize the orientation of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT use (i.e., externally or internally oriented or both) in terms of capabilities. We identify the capabilities that <sup>fi</sup>rms develop through the use of IT resources by relying on the notion of IT-based capabilities [10]. Furthermore, we look at <sup>fi</sup>rm performance as the differentials in performance that <sup>fi</sup>rms achieve compared to their counterparts in the same industry. This allows us to explore whether SMEs are capable of building sustained competitive advantages based on IT. In so doing, we explore the role that the rate of turbulence (i.e., dynamism) and opportunities of growth (i.e., muni<sup>fi</sup>cence) available in the environment affects the capacity that SMEs have to build a competitive advantage from IT-based capabilities. Such environmental conditions require SMEs to develop second-order dynamic capabilities [11] and co-evolutionary adaptation processes [12] on their portfolio of resources and capabilities that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop given SMEs’ limited organizational slacks. In the paper we describe these processes from a theoretical standpoint and we discuss the reasons why they can limit SMEs’ returns from the development of IT-based capabilities.

We explore these issues empirically by assessing a multiindustry sample of 238 Italian SMEs to determine whether: 1) an external orientation in developing IT-based capabilities has a greater performance impact than an internal orientation; 2) the effectiveness of such external orientation on SMEs’ performance differentials depends on the industry environment.

## 2. Background literature

This section illustrates the nature of IT-based capabilities (Section 2.1) and shows how SMEs can build economic value from their use depending on the industry environment (Sections 2.2). In so doing, we illustrate the theoretical underpinnings for how SMEs – given their unique traits – can follow different capabilitiesbuilding processes to respond to their environment compared to their larger counterparts (Section 2.3).

## 2.1. IT-based capabilities

IT-based capabilities are “complex bundles of IT-related resources, skills and knowledge, exercised through business processes, which enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to coordinate activities and make use of the IT assets to provide desired results” ([10], p. 185). Following the RBV [3], IT-based capabilities constitute a meta-level construct that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the manner in which IT resources are used in support of business processes [13].

From an evolutionary perspective [14], capabilities re<sup>fl</sup>ect a high-level structure of intentions and objectives supporting <sup>fi</sup>rms competitive actions and a set of codi<sup>fi</sup>ed solutions for the execution of these actions. Firms develop intentions and the related codi<sup>fi</sup>ed solutions in response to certain environmental stimuli and to the availability of the resources necessary to put a certain set of routines into action. The development of IT-based capabilities re<sup>fl</sup>ects the outcome of the processes for assimilating IT systems. Through this process, an organization becomes capable of incorporating and routinizing these resources into its business processes to enhance operational performance [15].

In general, <sup>fi</sup>rms may develop two types of meta-capabilities from the use of IT resources (Table 1): 1) “internally oriented” or 2) “externally oriented” IT-based capabilities [10]. Internally oriented IT-based capabilities are based on the use of IT systems, such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) solutions, to process information on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal operations. IT systems may assist operations managers with decisions that pertain to short-term horizons, and they may thus facilitate ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvement and operational control through data integration across functions [16]. As such, IT implementation with an internal orientation may include the use of ERP technologies aimed at reducing operational costs by improving inventory and cycle times. Another use of IT with an internal orientation relates to general and administrative activities and is based on the automation of transactional processes such as ordering and payment.

Clearly, <sup>fi</sup>rms’ attempt to improve their internal operations re<sup>fl</sup>ects mechanisms through which they cope with their external environment. For example, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s program to improve the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of their production systems (machineries, routines in manufacturing) re<sup>fl</sup>ects the strategy to improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency while proposing a broad product variety to customers or reducing lead times in serving customers. According to our view of internal versus external orientation, this program has an internal orientation.

Externally oriented IT-based capabilities support <sup>fi</sup>rms in managing their external environment, as represented by customers and suppliers. As such, they are based on the use of IT systems to support New Product Development (NPD) activities, ecommerce initiatives, and CRM. They also enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to support external operations such as collaboration and coordination with supply chain partners, sales and customer relationships. Furthermore, externally oriented IT-based capabilities can also be one of the preconditions through which <sup>fi</sup>rms pursue more exploratory paths for responding to market changes and seizing new market opportunities. In this vein, they are a good match with the conceptualization of dynamic capabilities that is commonly adopted in the extant literature, i.e., routines that recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the organizational resource base [17,18]. Mithas et al. [19] identify two modes through which IT can support these change routines. First, IT can allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to create new value propositions through the development of new offerings for customers. This occurs thanks to the opportunity created by IT when it collects customer information at a low cost during their lifetime with the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and it may lead <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve their one-to-one marketing effectiveness and their capabilities in product differentiation market segmentation. In SMEs, this strategy type can take form in the ability to quickly, and at competitive cost, provide unique made-toorder products for customers. Second, ITcan allow SMEs to develop new marketing and sales channels that help to promote awareness within their established customers of their product/service offerings or to acquire new customers at a low acquisition and service cost. This is the case for e-commerce initiatives, especially when they can help SMEs avoid export intermediaries to sell abroad [20]. Online channels can especially help the disintermediation of SMEs from their distributors given the great potential of online channels for the distribution of niche products on a broad geographical scope. Online channels can also lead SMEs to a reintermediation in favour of large distributors because centralizing their distribution systems can enlarge their supply by including niche products supplied by other SMEs [8].

These two ways of creating value through IT-based capabilities with an external orientation are also founded on the role that ITcan play in NPD processes and in the product life cycle to support <sup>fi</sup>rms in designing products with improved quality and performance that thus produce greater economic value. In the NPD process and in the post-launch revision of products with high technical complexity, IT solutions support <sup>fi</sup>rms in managing their intellectual capital as embodied in product design and engineering activities [21].

These considerations imply that the concept of externally oriented IT-based capabilities has similarities to Zollo and Winter’s [17] de<sup>fi</sup>nition of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dynamic capabilities; examples of these are commonly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in new product development and alliance management capabilities, given their role in recon<sup>fi</sup>guring <sup>fi</sup>rms’ product portfolios and in supporting access to external knowledge. Externally oriented IT-based capabilities represent a good match with this de<sup>fi</sup>nition of dynamic capabilities because they re<sup>fl</sup>ect the capability to i) capture and respond to external signals, and ii) recon<sup>fi</sup>gure products and supply chain relationships. From this perspective, CRM systems are aimed at sustaining <sup>fi</sup>rms in market intelligence generation and IT systems supporting the NPD area can be seen as a crucial resource for market responsiveness, enabling better coordination with supply chain partners and a shorter time-to-market in product development [22]. Therefore, externally oriented IT-based capabilities can be considered to be the way IT resources are used to support dynamic capabilities and thus to identify and process external information and to support routines that recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the organizational resource base.

In sum, we can contend that internally oriented IT-based capabilities are mainly aimed at achieving greater ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and cost reduction, whereas the development of externally oriented ITbased capabilities supports market adaptation and product differentiation.

## 2.2. IT-based capabilities and the environment

The industry environment “explains” a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of the variance in SMEs’ strategic choices and performance [23]. In investigating how the environment may in<sup>fl</sup>uence strategy and performance, the strategy literature has focused prominent attention on dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence (e.g., [24]). Dynamic (i.e., turbulent) environments are characterized by instability and continuous change in market and technology conditions. Muni<sup>fi</sup>- cent environments create resource and growth opportunities for <sup>fi</sup>rms and identify less mature markets in which a product/service is more differentiable and is far from being a commodity, characterized by customers’ limited willingness to pay. In sum, dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence have a prominent role in strategic analysis since they depict the degree of growth in market demand and the rate of change in technology and market conditions in an industry.

The environment is associated with regularities in <sup>fi</sup>rms responses with regard to organizational structures and capabilities. According to the contingency view [25] and the evolutionary perspective [14], <sup>fi</sup>rms that deviate from such “optimal” responses have lower performance and are likely to exit their industry in the long term. In principle, as IT investments can enable easy adaptation to the volatility of market demand, changes in customer needs and new market opportunities [12,26], dynamic and resource-abundant environments would require greater IT deployment in support of externally oriented IT-based capabilities. In contrast, in mature and resource-scarce environments, <sup>fi</sup>rms should gain greater economic value from developing internally oriented IT-based capabilities, given their importance to improve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

The level of dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence in competition may also affect the value and appropriability of IT investments because they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the complexity of the IT assimilation process [15]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the environment may affect the “adjustment costs” sustained by <sup>fi</sup>rms when they adapt their organization to the deployment of IT resources in their business processes [2], or change modules within the implemented software to meet the requirements for running some processes [27]. From a RBV perspective, this implies that the development of capabilities from adoption of IT resources entails a certain level of organizational complexity as well as investments in human resources $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , IT specialists) and managerial routines (i.e., IT governance systems) that strictly depend on the environmental conditions. For example, in a turbulent environment, <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to need a greater and more frequent number of changes in the IT system to maintain its support to a business process even after its implementation. In muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments, <sup>fi</sup>rms might need to extend their IT systems to include new modules or new ways of running processes that are required to realize business expansion. In this case, IT systems should be easily recon<sup>fi</sup>gurable to allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to catch new

market opportunities that entail changes in business processes and routines.

These considerations imply that the environment affects the costs and the barriers to build capabilities from IT use and to shape their evolution. SMEs – given their limited scale – may thus <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT investments, and the related process of building and changing IT-based capabilities, are not affordable under certain environmental conditions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in environmental contexts in which governing IT is too complicated and requires continuous changes to the portfolio of IT resources and to business processes, SMEs may opt for simpler organizational solutions that do not adopt “too much” IT. This implies that although externally oriented IT-based capabilities are assumed to provide a competitive advantage to large <sup>fi</sup>rms under dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments, this may not hold true for SMEs given their unique structural traits and their dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in managing the coevolution of IT systems and business processes. We discuss the theoretical foundations of this position below.

## 2.3. Coevolution in capabilities-building processes

The importance of using IT to respond to external changes and new market opportunities in contexts of muni<sup>fi</sup>cence and dynamism draws attention to the process of co-evolutionary adaptation to the external environment [12]. Co-evolutionary adaptation refers to how <sup>fi</sup>rms learn over time and through experience as they invest in IT resources and as they leverage these IT initiatives to launch a variety of new competitive actions. Through coevolution, <sup>fi</sup>rms build their resources, capabilities and knowledge base in the attempt to respond to external changes and pursue new market opportunities.

The dynamics of coevolution in IT management imply an iterative learning loop between competences in IT investments and competitive actions (see Table 2). Competences in IT investments involve a vast array of domains including the governance of IT resources and an integrated partnership between IT specialists and those roles accountable for business process innovation. Such competences may lead <sup>fi</sup>rms to increase the number and scope of competitive actions in their repertoire. In turn, an expansion in the repertoire of competitive actions through IT initiatives may lead <sup>fi</sup>rms to reinforce their competences in managing IT investments because they can improve the cultures, routines and processes needed to manage IT initiatives. For the strategic management literature, this coevolution is particularly critical to performance in dynamic environments [28] where <sup>fi</sup>rms can hardly sustain competitive advantages over the long term and are required to engage in rapid and relentless innovations in products, operations, and IT systems.

Co-evolutionary processes also entail what previous studies have de<sup>fi</sup>ned as second-order dynamic capabilities, i.e., the routines that recon<sup>fi</sup>gure <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dynamic capabilities [17]. Second-order dynamic capabilities can be essentially thought of as “learning-to-learn” capabilities [29]. For SMEs, developing these second-order dynamic capabilities can be complicated given the limited availability of slacks that can be used for learning processes, the low diversity of experience and specialization within the top management team, and the more limited number of competitive actions that they can undertake in a given time horizon. ## 3. Hypotheses

IT-based capabilities that support SMEs’ external orientation are crucial for their competitiveness, and they are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to build under certain environmental situations. In this study, we develop and test three hypotheses addressing the effectiveness of these capabilities in supporting SMEs’ competitive advantages and how dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence in the environment affect this relationship.

## 3.1. The effect of IT-based capabilities on performance differential

There are multiple arguments as to why internally and externally oriented IT-based capabilities may have a different impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and may foster advantages with different levels of competitive sustainability. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rms – and especially SMEs – may be better able to appropriate the value created by externally oriented IT-based capabilities than the value stemming from IT initiatives with an internal orientation for three reasons.

First, when SMEs undertake adoption decisions and the subsequent process of assimilating those IT resources that enable a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external orientation, they address a higher organizational and social complexity [19] than when they introduce IT systems (such as ERP) to improve their internal operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. IT projects with an internal orientation are easier to deploy because they are based on the automation of business processes or on information integration among different functional areas. This integration in SMEs is relatively easy to achieve given the low complexity of their organizational structure [34]. Conversely, the adoption of IT solutions with an external orientation is often conditional on the previous adoption of IT systems supporting internal solutions and is thus subject to greater path dependencies.

Second, SMEs may be unable to appropriate the value of IT resources supporting an internal orientation because they may be forced to pass along the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT-enabled increased ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the form of lower prices, with their business customers being the ultimate bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary of such returns [35]. In the case of SMEs, this means that large business customers demanding the tighter inter-organizational integration of IT systems in their supply chains could appropriate the economic returns of any ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvements obtained by their small suppliers through IT implementation. This may particularly occur when SMEs are highly revenue dependent on a few large customers because it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to diversify and identify new market segments for their products.

A third explanation for a limited impact on performance from internally oriented IT-based capabilities suggests that because of a long history of <sup>fi</sup>rms viewing IT mainly as an automation-related investment, with a focus on cost reduction rather than revenue generation, <sup>fi</sup>rms may have “exhausted ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains from IT” ([22], p. 210). In other words, internally oriented IT-based capabilities may have been the object of a commoditization trend that has considerably reduced their potential impact on revenue and pro<sup>fi</sup>t growth, even among SMEs. Cloud computing and the rise in the market for simple versions of standardized ERP packages for SMEs are two elements of the commoditization trend that also involve SMEs.

Instead, with regard to the IT resources that support a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external orientation, the timing and cost of their deployment may still be very different across SMEs, which may foster the emergence of intra-industry differentials in <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [36]. Externally oriented IT initiatives are more costly to imitate because they entail higher social complexity, as they are based on the integration of information systems and managerial practices among partners in a supply chain [37]. For example, the adoption of IT systems supporting the NPD process such as Product Lifecycle Management systems helps SMEs improve the coordination, creation and exchange of knowledge and digital artefacts with their customers and partners. Among SMEs, the extensive use of externally oriented IT can be a rare condition that is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate and that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the supply chain relationships, knowledge and practices that these <sup>fi</sup>rms have developed over time with larger partners. In this regard, past research shows that large <sup>fi</sup>rms can have a prominent and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial role in triggering the adoption and assimilation processes of IT resources by their smaller partners [38].

These arguments highlight that the use of IT resources for an external orientation is co-specialized with other external resources, such as inter-organizational relationships, brand recognition, trust, and information repositories on customer transactions [39]. All of these resources are rare because they are the fruit of asset stock accumulation dynamics that are path dependent, dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate, and isolated by time compression diseconomies [40]. Thus, SMEs that started deploying IT resources and their related cospecialized assets earlier have an advantage over competitors in developing externally oriented IT-based capabilities, and their knowledge of their customers means that customers will bear a higher switching cost if they move to a supplier that lacks that knowledge [19].

In sum, all of these considerations are in favour of the concept that externally oriented IT-based capabilities are rarer and less imitable among SMEs and produce greater and more appropriable value for these <sup>fi</sup>rms. As such, they may be more effective in helping SMEs to achieve and sustain a competitive advantage. Thus, we formulate the following hypothesis:

H1. Externally oriented IT-based capabilities have a greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on SMEs’ competitive advantage than internally oriented IT-based capabilities.

## 3.2. The mediating role of environmental dynamism

Contingent studies have generally asserted that IT-based capabilities can produce more economic value in dynamic environments (e.g., [10]) because IT resources can speed up decision making, facilitate <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal and external communication and help them to adapt quickly to changing conditions [12]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT resources such as ERP systems can help <sup>fi</sup>rms to rapidly adapt to supply and operations changes in volume and product mix to meet market demand. In the same way, systems supporting CRM and NPD processes can help <sup>fi</sup>rms to detect new purchasing habits and to develop new product features that meet new customer needs.

However, past studies have also noted that IT may hinder a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capacity to adapt to radical changes in the environment, due to the rigidity of the <sup>fi</sup>xed physical and technological artefacts of IT systems [41]. Firms are often constrained by the limitations of in<sup>fl</sup>exible legacy IT systems, rigid IT architectures, or complex nests of disparate technology silos to an extent where IT may hinder a rapid adaptation to external changes [42]. Past studies have also shown that high IT spending in the face of change and uncertainty reinforces the current underlying patterns and logic in managerial and organizational routines when <sup>fi</sup>rms lack the competences to manage investments in IT. This leads to unintended <sup>fi</sup>rm rigidity in responding to radical environmental changes. For example, greater IT spending and reliance on business intelligence tools may reinforce the environmental scanning of current domains yet ignore weak signals in new domains and lead to an inaccurate perception of market changes and misdirected decision making [5].

The role of IT in generating rigidity rather than agility in addressing the external environment could be particularly prominent in those SMEs facing dynamic contexts. This may be driven by the fact that SMEs usually lack the preconditions (discussed above in Table 2) for the coevolution of their IT resources, capabilities and knowledge. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, SMEs rarely develop managerial routines and processes to govern IT investment decisions, invest in IT-business partnerships, and explore emerging technologies [43]. In this regard, exploration in dynamic environments requires a broad and <sup>fl</sup>exible scope to search for and absorb knowledge from the external environment [44], whereas SMEs are more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in searching for knowledge in local domains due to specialization and the limited availability of slack resources for exploration. This limits their market-sensing capacity [12].

These considerations echo the potential ineffectiveness of <sup>fi</sup>rstorder dynamic capabilities in highly dynamic environments (e.g., [18]): contexts where change is frequent, radical and unpredictable produce problems for <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dynamic capabilities. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the environment changes, <sup>fi</sup>rms reactivate organizational responses that proved successful under similar situations in the past. This implies that unfamiliar states in the external environment are ignored or are treated in a similar manner to some other type of event encountered and understood in the past. When an apparently proven response to an identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problem exists in organizational memory, experimentation with alternatives becomes less attractive. This is especially likely to occur in SMEs given their specialization and their attitude towards local search. However, this pattern is not effective in highly dynamic contexts.

In sum, highly dynamic environments may require secondorder dynamic capabilities and co-evolutionary adaptation processes that SMEs are usually not able to enact given the limited availability of resources for “learning to learn” and for mutually adapting their IT systems and business processes. We may thus expect the following:

H2. The impact of externally oriented IT-based capabilities on SMEs’ competitive advantage decreases as environmental dynamism increases.

## 3.3. The mediating role of environmental munificence

Environmental muni<sup>fi</sup>cence refers to the extent to which the environment can support <sup>fi</sup>rms in their efforts to achieve sustained market growth, and it re<sup>fl</sup>ects growth in market demand and the abundance of those critical resources needed by <sup>fi</sup>rms to compete. Such resources may be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in less price competition from customers and suppliers (leaving more economic value for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm) and a greater abundance of <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources for stakeholders to invest in the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, a muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environment presents opportunities for business expansion in existing and new markets, and it promotes slack resources that can support growth. As such, in muni<sup>fi</sup>cent contexts, SMEs potentially face a lower risk of dependence on a few customers and greater tolerance for experimentation. Consequently, these contexts may encourage SMEs to invest in the development of new resources and capabilities because they perceive greater opportunities for them to generate returns [45]. In muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments, SMEs may thus see more potential for returns from investments in IT-based capabilities, especially when they search for growth in new markets or in their established settings.

These considerations suggest that externally oriented IT-based capabilities are more needed in muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments, but they do not necessarily suggest that SMEs are better able to extract higher economic value from these capabilities under these conditions. Indeed, muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments require <sup>fi</sup>rms to enact co-evolutionary adaptation processes to extend their capability portfolio to capture new market opportunities. However, as discussed above, SMEs may be ineffective in spotting, interpreting and seizing new market opportunities and may insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently evolve their portfolio of IT resources, capabilities and processes to seize such opportunities.

Conversely, in contexts of low muni<sup>fi</sup>cence, <sup>fi</sup>rms can build and appropriate higher economic value by developing externally oriented IT-based capabilities for two types of reasons. First, in contexts with limited opportunities for market growth and more limited availability of resources for new investments, SMEs are usually reluctant to enrich their IT-based capabilities because they are <sup>fi</sup>nancially more vulnerable to the risk of IT project failure. As such, in minimally muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments, IT-based capabilities are more likely to be rare and non-imitable. Additionally, under these conditions, the market for IT resources can exhibit lower prices compared to more muni<sup>fi</sup>cent industries where the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ willingness to pay for IT can be higher and IT vendors can extract higher value from their clients. Second, in non-muni<sup>fi</sup>cent and mature market segments, there is no need to integrate and synchronize product development and CRM capabilities, which is crucial in a muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environment when <sup>fi</sup>rms are obliged to offer relentless product innovation to address the rapid appearance of market opportunities. Thus, in a non-muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environment even a “scattered” use of IT on only some of the functional domains supporting an external orientation can allow SMEs to use differentiation strategies that mitigate the pressure on price and pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins. For example, a CRM capability can allow a SME to develop new customized offerings that increase a client’s willingness to pay and can thus mitigate the commoditization in their product offering, which is a more likely condition in nonmuni<sup>fi</sup>cent markets. In the same way, a capability in managing ecommerce initiatives can reduce distribution costs and help SMEs retain more economic value in the face of high pressure on price or can allow an entry into geographical markets that are more muni<sup>fi</sup>cent than the domestic context.

In sum, under contexts of low muni<sup>fi</sup>cence, the capability building processes for an external IT orientation is more linear because of the infrequent need to adjust the IT portfolio to emerging market opportunities, less imitable, more appropriable and require a low degree of organizational complexity. The same extent of IT-based capabilities can thus achieve greater performance impact in non-muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments compared to muni<sup>fi</sup>cent settings. We thus expect that SMEs are able to extract

more value from externally oriented IT-based capabilities in a context of low muni<sup>fi</sup>cence than in benign contexts that are rich with opportunities for investing in resources where co-evolutionary adaptation processes are required. This leads to the formulation of the following hypothesis:

H3. The impact of externally oriented IT-based capabilities on SMEs’ competitive advantage decreases as environmental muni<sup>fi</sup>cence increases.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

The data used for this study are derived from a survey conducted on a population of 5000 SMEs between February and April 2010 in the Piedmont region in Italy. The survey is part of the output of a regional research observatory of the Information Society that has been conducted every year since 2005. These data were complemented by <sup>fi</sup>nancial data gathered from the AIDA database (Bureau Van Dijk), which includes <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures for all Italian companies.

Located in Northwest Italy, the Piedmont region has a population of 4.4 million people and an annual per capita GDP of s27,700 [46], which is among the highest in Europe [47]. According to the Regional Innovation Scoreboard, Piedmont is one of the top three regions in Italy with regard to innovation and technology adoption at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, and SMEs localized in Piedmont show an aggregate aptitude for innovation that is comparable to that of the most innovative regions in Germany, France, and the UK [48]. Furthermore, Piedmont exhibits an industrial structure and a set of institutional factors (i.e., the presence of large enterprises, broadband penetration, availability of public funds for IT adoption, openness to international trade, access to debt capital through the banking system, national IT vendors’ supply of IT solutions for SMEs, etc.) that are similar to the situation in other large regions in Europe, e.g., Lombardy, Emilia Romagna, (in Italy), Baden-Württemberg (Germany), Rhones Alpes (France), and Cataluña (Spain). This implies that SMEs in Piedmont do not follow business practices that are highly speci<sup>fi</sup>c to their local culture. Therefore, the results obtained in this study can be generalized to the level of SMEs in other advanced European nations such as Germany, France, and the United Kingdom. Thus, the results of this study are likely to be generalized to other national contexts.

We focused the survey on four industry groups: 1) manufacturing, 2) wholesale and retail trade, 3) logistics and transportation services, and 4) business services. We did not survey industries that use IT in highly speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways, such as multimedia, software, IT services, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. The industry population was de<sup>fi</sup>ned using Bureau Van Dijk’s AIDA database. The <sup>fi</sup>rms that were surveyed had between 10 and 249 employees. This range is consistent with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a “Small and Medium Enterprise” at the European Union level.

Two thousand companies were randomly selected and contacted by phone to identify the key respondents. We asked the respondents to indicate whether there was a position for the management of IT within their companies (a Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer or equivalent role). For companies that did not appoint a specialized IT management role, we asked for the owner or the CEO. To prevent the respondents from reporting their personal perspectives on the issues that were covered by the survey, we required them to express positions that were agreed upon by the entire upper management team. ## 4.2.1. Independent variables

4.2.1.1. IT-based capabilities. The nature of capabilities as an intangible asset constrained us to derive inferences on capabilities by examining a series of outputs associated with the existence of these capabilities and conditional on the use of IT resources [49]. This method of measuring capabilities implies that they are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as latent constructs. The fact that capabilities are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to observe implies that they are also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure directly. Accordingly, the measures of these capabilities were based on multi-item scales. Our approach was based on using existing validated measures of IT impact that have good psychometric properties [16,29,50,51] with some modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to suit the context of our research on SMEs (refer to the last column of Table 4). Our scales have also a correspondence in the measures used in more recent studies to analyse the role of IT in supporting an internal and external orientation [9,52–54]. References to these more recent studies are also reported in Table 4. To help respondents report objective evaluations, we asked them to base their assessment on the impacts observed over the previous three years (between 2006 and 2009).

We then calculated a single indicator for each of our two constructs of IT-based capabilities (i.e., internally and externally oriented) using the latent factor score approach. This latent factor score approach is recommended for calculating single measures for research variables having multiple indicators; in this approach, the multiple indicators are weighted to resolve the potential biasing effects of measurement error. Three stages were utilized to develop and evaluate the measurement model. We conducted validity tests for the original measures of the directly observed constructs (i.e., the items listed in Table 4) that are the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs of each IT-based capability. To do so, we conducted an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using principal component analysis. The purpose of this step was to assess whether the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dimensions had valid factor structures. Next, we developed new indicators for our principal research constructs by calculating the latent factor scores of the calculated <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs. Last, we repeated the latent score approach and calculated the single indicators of our principal constructs for our hypothesis tests using regression analysis. This process empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the validity of our distinction between internally and externally oriented IT-based capabilities.

4.2.1.2. Environment. To operationalize the environmental factors, we followed the approach used by previous authors [10,24], and we used industry data from the AIDA database (Bureau Van Dijk), which includes <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures for all Italian companies. To do so, we considered industries by using NACE classi<sup>fi</sup>cation at the three-digit level. We measured dynamism by considering turbulence in the distribution of revenues within each industry using <sup>fi</sup>rm-level data from AIDA. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the dynamism of an industry was operationalized as the variability in annual industry sales: for each sector, the industry-level total sales for 5 years (from 2005 to 2009) were regressed on the year variable, and dynamism was measured as the standard error of the regression slope coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of annual industry sales divided by the industry mean for the 5 year period. By using the same data for total industry sales revenues, muni<sup>fi</sup>cence was measured as the growth rate in annual industry sales for 5 years, measured as the regression slope coef<sup>fi</sup>cient divided by the average industry sales.

## 4.2.2. Dependent variables

4.2.2.1. Competitive advantage (firm performance differential). Firm performance differentials were considered referring to operating pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and revenue growth between 2006 and 2009. The choice of this time series is consistent with the time horizon given to respondents to express their evaluations of their development of IT-based capabilities. Thus, we did not lag any performance indicator as done in other studies on the value of IT (e.g., [2]) because our choice to measure capabilities as the result of the assimilation of IT in business processes postulates that we have already controlled for the delay between the adoption of an IT resource and the manifestation of its outcomes. Moreover, the use of performance data from annual <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports has the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of reducing the risk of common method variance that can arise in survey-based research using perceptual data from a single respondent.

Revenue growth was operationalized as the logarithmic form of the ratio between 2009 and 2006 revenues. The analysis of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s differential compared to the industry median values allowed assessing whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm had achieved a competitive edge (or disadvantage) or bridged (or increased) a competitive delay during the period under analysis. Analysing pro<sup>fi</sup>t differentials through a comparison between a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s performance and that of the median <sup>fi</sup>rm in the industry is common in research in strategic management (e.g., [36]) and Information Systems (e.g., [57]) because using the average industry performance as a control is equivalent to incorporating an industry level control and increases the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and external validity of the study [58].

## 4.2.3. Control variables

In testing the impact of IT-based capabilities on the performance differential, we controlled for <sup>fi</sup>rm size (measured as the logarithm value of total sales), for the dependent variable measured in 2006 (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the logarithmic form of the revenues in the models with the revenue growth differential as a dependent variable and the ROA in the models with the ROA growth differential as a dependent variable) and for industry types, which were de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on the <sup>fi</sup>rst digit level NACE codes, which were included in the models as dummy variables.

A <sup>fi</sup>rm may decide to adopt certain IT resources and to undertake a related organizational innovation based on factors that are unobservable and that also affect performance. In the case of IT use, such unobservable variables can be the managers’ attitude towards IT or some particular network relationships that have pushed <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt IT systems and develop capabilities from their use before competitors. This holds especially true for SMEs, whose business models are often based on trust and longstanding relationships with some preferred customers.

To account for the endogeneity of IT-based capabilities, the analyses used a two-stage least-squares (2SLS) regression approach. Thus, we used instruments that must be correlated with ITbased capabilities but not with dependent variables. For this purpose, we employed six instrumental variables. Appendix A illustrates the reasons for using these variables as instruments. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the geographical localization of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in a metropolitan area and the foreign ownership of the company were two of the instruments used.

The third instrument referred to SMEs’ management team involvement in the process of planning IT investments. This variable was operationalized through a multi-item measure based on a 5-level Likert scale that considered the extent of managers’ participation to the following tasks: i) the selection of IT solutions and the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of their business requirements; ii) the decisionmaking process for business strategy choices involving the domain of IT and e-commerce; and iii) change management endeavours induced by IT projects and affecting business processes and reporting systems. EFA con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that a unique latent factor encompassed each of these items.

The fourth instrument referred to the use of “outside-in” approaches for selecting appropriate IT solutions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we measured the extent of use of information channels such as professional associations, innovation brokers, industry experts, IT exhibitions, and benchmarking practices with competitors.

The <sup>fi</sup>fth instrument referred to the pressure for IT adoption from external stakeholders such as strategic customers, suppliers, or new equity investors. Finally, we used two industry dummies to differentiate <sup>fi</sup>rms according to the degree of information intensity in the industry’s typical operations. For this dichotomization, we referred to Porat and Rubin’s study [61] on information-based sectors.

## 5.1. Descriptive statistics

The descriptive statistics (Table 7) indicate that SMEs are more likely to develop internally oriented than externally oriented ITbased capabilities. This result is consistent with the greater organizational and social complexity that <sup>fi</sup>rms must address when they use IT to develop externally oriented IT-based capabilities. ## 6. Discussion and conclusions

With the increasing commoditization of IT solutions, this study is relevant because it examines the value that stems from SMEs’ attempts to develop capabilities based on IT use. For this examination, we used the RBV and a contingent approach to analyse how IT-based capabilities in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in SMEs and how environmental factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence this relationship. In this way, our empirical research can contribute to the debate on how and in which contexts IT investments provide more economic value. In so doing, we were better able to address those methodological problems of endogeneity and value creation that are typical of the strategic management research on the value of ITbased capabilities and of prior studies on SMEs [62]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our results highlight that externally oriented IT-based capabilities foster greater economic value than internally oriented IT-based capabilities because they have a greater impact on SMEs’ performance differential compared to competitors in terms of both revenue growth and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we found that a lower percentage of SMEs have developed a high level of externally oriented IT-based capabilities, which is consistent with the greater social and organizational complexity inherent in their development. In line with their rarity, externally oriented IT-based capabilities are, in general, more in<sup>fl</sup>uential in allowing <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve performance differentials in business growth and pro<sup>fi</sup>t compared to internally oriented IT-based capabilities. Additionally, internally oriented IT-based capabilities have a negative impact on pro<sup>fi</sup>t differentials. Following a RBV logic, this result may suggest an appropriability problem: SMEs that have only developed internally oriented IT-based capabilities have generated a limited portion of added economic value compared to their competitors. Such added value may be more than offset by an increase in the costs of governing the complexity of IT systems. The appropriability problem may re<sup>fl</sup>ect a commoditization trend in some

IT-based capabilities that relate internal operations. As such, SMEs may have eventually exhausted the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains from such capabilities, due to their isolated use (a capability may have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance effect when <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to develop it in a bundle of other capabilities) and due to the costs for investing and maintaining in the IT resources at their origin. Our second research question examined how the industry environment affects the importance of externally oriented IT-based capabilities to the achievement and sustainability of SMEs’ competitive advantages. In this regard, our results highlight that when revenue growth is taken into account, the impact of externally oriented IT-based capabilities on the performance differential is stronger in contexts of low dynamism and low muni<sup>fi</sup>cence. This result suggests that such capabilities allow SMEs to pursue and realize superior market growth opportunities compared to competitors when the industry environment is stable and poor of growth opportunities. In dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments SMEs with externally oriented capabilities are not able to achieve superior growth opportunities. A plausible interpretation is that due to their limited degree of second-order dynamic capabilities and their <sup>fi</sup>nancial constraints, SMEs with externally oriented IT-based capabilities may be able to pursue only “local optima” that imply a limited growth of their revenue in dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments. These weaknesses impede SMEs’ ability to adjust their portfolio of IT resources/capabilities and business processes to respond to radical market changes.

Contrarily to our expectation, instead, the positive impact of externally oriented IT-based capabilities on the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability differential does not depend on the level of dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence. Thus, the fact that externally oriented IT-based capabilities have similar positive impact on pro<sup>fi</sup>t differentials irrespective of the environmental conditions may suggest that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of co-evolutionary adaptation process on the performance impact of externally oriented IT-based capabilities is more complex for SMEs compared to what we hypothesized in this study. A speculative interpretation for explaining why the environment does not moderate the relationship between externally oriented IT-based capabilities and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability differentials may be that the pursue of “local optima” opportunities of business growth thanks to externally oriented IT-based capabilities (which equate to <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dynamic capabilities) may come in dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments at a limited cost in SMEs given the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of their operational model and this turns out in a pro<sup>fi</sup>t advantage even though the revenue growth opportunities are limited. For example, larger counterparts can catch higher market growth opportunities but their cost for the recon<sup>fi</sup>guration of resources and capabilities needed to pursue those opportunities are higher than for smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms. This possible interpretation calls for more attention in future studies on sorting out the difference between <sup>fi</sup>rst and second-order dynamic capabilities in SMEs. SMEs may be more <sup>fl</sup>exible in redeploying their resources and capabilities to new contexts, but they may be more rigid when they need to learn how to evolve them to technological discontinuities. In other words, more research is needed in understanding in what SMEs are inherently <sup>fl</sup>exible and in what are structurally rigid when it comes to the evolution of their IT-related resources and their digital strategies. In highlighting this evidence, our paper calls for more attention focused on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational and business conditions of IT use in SMEs. In this regard, future studies on SMEs should also consider that among SMEs, size can act as a moderator in the relationship between IT-based capabilities. performance and the environment. Size can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the coping mechanisms enacted by SMEs to respond to their environments [25] because the SMEs category, which usually includes <sup>fi</sup>rms in the range of 10–250 employees, covers a potentially wide diversity of resource endowments and behaviours in strategic positioning and organizational design [63–65]. In this logic, medium-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms are probably in a better condition to develop the secondorder dynamic capabilities that are needed to exploit IT in more dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent environments. In the same way, because of their larger scale, medium-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms may generate more economic value from the development of internally oriented ITbased capabilities. As such, the prominence of externally oriented over internally oriented IT-based capabilities can be less evident for larger SMEs.

Our evidence also offers managers some relevant implications. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that SMEs’ managers should invest more in developing externally oriented than internally oriented IT-based capabilities. Furthermore, in more dynamic environments, there are modes through which SMEs can address market uncertainty that are more frugal and less complex and that produce more sustainable returns than IT [66]. In contrast, in contexts that are low in dynamism and muni<sup>fi</sup>cence, a limited IT investment is also likely to put <sup>fi</sup>rms in a condition to achieve a competitive advantage. In this regard, our results echo the classical perspective provided by the RBV: in those industries characterized by <sup>fi</sup>rms investing fewer resources in innovation, innovators are generally in more favourable conditions to develop an advantage based on rare and costly-to-imitate resources. Instead, for SMEs in dynamic and muni<sup>fi</sup>cent context our study recalls the importance of building the conditions for the coevolutionary adaptation processes that are needed to evolve IT resources in alignment with business processes.

From an industry-level perspective, our results somehow complement the evidence provided by recent research showing an increasing divide in IT use and in performance in information intensive industries [8]. In this regard, our study shows that SMEs are less likely to gain value from IT in muni<sup>fi</sup>cent and dynamic environments, two conditions that are typical of informationintensive sectors. In these settings, SMEs are more likely to be “losers” rather than winners because they confront greater dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in adjusting their IT use to changes and new opportunities in the external environment. These considerations also raise important policy-making issues in European countries regarding the importance of and the modes for supporting SMEs in the more advanced and strategic use of IT.

The paper has also limitations, some of which are typical of empirical research on capabilities. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our measure of capabilities was collected from a single respondent at single point in time rather than observed directly through <sup>fi</sup>eld-based study. This is currently the standard methodology in Information Systems research, but it has certain drawbacks. We tried to correct for these drawbacks by selecting respondents who were suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently knowledgeable about the business. However, this approach may present fewer drawbacks in SMEs than it would in larger enterprises because in SMEs, CEOs and other managers usually play a more general role and may be thus more knowledgeable about IT-related issues. Second, our research should be extended to SMEs in other European regions to better assess the generalizability of our results and to control how much cultural and local institutional speci<sup>fi</sup>cities affect SMEs’ ability to draw business value from IT use. SMEs in the Piedmont region do not apparently exhibit any idiosyncratic element in their culture, institutional setting and managerial practices; however, extension to other regions or countries can be important to the empirical validation of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Future research should also explore what happens in the economic returns of IT investments when <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to develop more advanced levels of IT-based capabilities, especially those having an external orientation. In our study, SMEs exhibited a low level of development in these capabilities and thus lack a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect from externally oriented IT-based capabilities on performance differentials; we found that this could be because in dynamic environments, economic returns from these capabilities are visible only beyond a certain level of development. Future studies could thus assess whether a critical mass exists for the accumulation of IT-based capabilities. In this regard, higher levels of externally oriented IT-based capabilities could accompany the co-evolutionary adaptation process and second-order dynamic capabilities. These two factors have not been observed in this study. Disentangling the antecedents and the nature of these factors for the use of IT in SMEs represents an important avenue for future research.

## Instruments for IT-based capabilities

The geographical localization of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in a metropolitan area was included for instrumenting IT-based capabilities to study their effects on performance. The reason for this instrument is based on the fact that SMEs located in metropolitan areas usually have better access to the market for standardized IT solutions and to the complementary consulting services related to their implementation. Indeed, the supply of these technologies and services is usually concentrated in metropolitan areas rather than in rural areas. Economists studying innovation diffusion (e.g., [46]) have shown that localization in metropolitan areas favours knowledge spillovers between adopters and potential adopters that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision to adopt a certain technology. Spillover effects are particularly important for small businesses, which are more likely to be unable to develop the necessary technology transfer competences when isolated. Information exchange and direct observation of the adoption of new technologies by others allow small businesses to improve on others’ methods to manage IT and – along with more favourable access conditions to the market for IT resources – may lead SMEs to develop better IT-based capabilities with respect to their counterparts in rural areas. There are, however, no a priori arguments that SMEs with operational units in metropolitan areas systematically have superior performance, as localization in a metropolitan area is not a rare condition that allows privileged access to inputs that are critical for performance.

The other instruments capture conditions that the Information Systems literature describe as facilitators of the adoption and the assimilation process of IT and that thus have an expected positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the development of IT-based capabilities: the management team’s involvement in and commitment to the planning process for IT investments can lead to a more rational and effective decision-making process for IT investments. This may reduce the risk of strategic missteps and may lead <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop a portfolio of IT resources that is better aligned with business needs. Similarly, the use of information channels or the pressure from external stakeholders may lead SMEs to the early adoption of technologies that can play a critical role in the development of business capabilities. Despite the positive expected in<sup>fl</sup>uence from these factors on capability development and accumulation, there are no a priori arguments to contend that they have a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s performance differentials within its industry. This is why the presence of some factors (especially the use of certain information channels and pressure from stakeholders) can be quite common within some industries. Therefore, in these industries, these factors are not rare conditions that are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for <sup>fi</sup>rms to build a competitive advantage. Furthermore, these factors facilitate the adoption and the assimilation process of IT, but they are not themselves critical success factors for the generation of superior economic value.
