# The efects of relative and criticism-based performance appraisals on task-level escalation in an IT project: a laboratory experiment

Jong Seok Lee<sup>a</sup>, and Mark Keil<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Information & Technology. Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN , USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA

## ABSTRACT

While performance appraisals provide an opportunity for managers to review employee performance, prior research points to potential negative consequences associated with performance appraisals. Specifically, prior IS research suggests that relative and criticism-based appraisals can have an undesirable impact on employee behaviour. Motivated by this, in this study we theorised and empirically investigated how relative and criticism-based appraisals can influence an IT project member’s tendency to continue working on a task despite encountering serious setbacks (i.e., task-level escalation). To shed light on the underlying mechanisms through which appraisals influence task-level escalation, we proposed and tested two mediating pathways based on goal orientation theory and prospect theory. In a laboratory experiment, we found that a relative appraisal led to greater task-level escalation as compared to an absolute appraisal. Proving goal orientation and avoiding goal orientation mediated this relationship. In addition, we found that a criticism-based appraisal led to greater task-level escalation as compared to a praise-based appraisal. Risk propensity and self-esteem mediated this relationship. Lastly, we found that the efects of both a relative appraisal and a criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation were significantly diferent from a control group that did not involve any form of performance appraisal.

## 1. Introduction

To date, prior research in the IT human resource management literature has investigated a number of important topics that concern IT personnel, such as the skills required for IT professionals (Lee, Trauth, & Farwell, 1995; Leitheiser, 1992), the relationship between job involvement and job satisfaction (Igbaria & Parasuraman, 1994), the roles of gender (Igbaria & Baroudi, 1994, 1995) and race (Igbaria & Wormley, 1992) in IT work settings, and turnover among IT professionals (Mourmant, Gallivan, & Kalika, 2009; Niederman, Sumner, & Maertz, 2007). However, performance appraisal, which is an important human resource management practice, has received relatively little attention from IS researchers. While performance appraisals provide an opportunity for managers to review individual employees’ performance and provide feedback, management scholars have suggested that they can sometimes have negative consequences.

For example, in a paper based on a meta-analysis of performance appraisal studies, Kluger and DeNisi (1996) concluded that 38% of the efects found in prior studies were negative with respect to performance. Furthermore,

Scott and Einstein (2001) suggested that in team-based organisations evaluation of individual members’ performance can cause a shift of the team’s focus from teamwork to individual work. In the IT industry, concern over the potential negative efects of performance appraisals has led several computer software companies, such as Adobe Systems, to abandon their formal performance appraisal systems. One of the cited reasons for such actions is that performance appraisals can have a negative efect on collaboration and teamwork (Baer, 2014).

The findings and trends mentioned above should raise concerns for both IS researchers and practitioners. Unfortunately, there is a lack of empirical research aimed at understanding which types of performance appraisals tend to cause undesirable behaviours in IS settings. Prior IS research cautioned that a relative performance appraisal tends to ignore individual circumstances and can lead to an unfair assessment (Chilton & Hardgrave, 2004), and suggested that support may have a more positive impact on employees than criticism (Jiang & Klein, 2015). Against this background, we theorise and empirically investigate how relative and criticism-based appraisal approaches used in conducting performance appraisals can influence a project member’s tendency to continue working on a task despite encountering serious setbacks. Herein, we refer to this behaviour as task-level escalation. Escalation of commitment is an undesirable form of behaviour that has received much attention from management scholars (see Sleesman, Conlon, McNamara, and Miles (2012) for a meta analysis) and IS scholars (Keil, 1995; Keil, Mann, & Rai, 2000a). While prior IS escalation studies focused exclusively on project-level escalation (Keil, 1995; Keil et al., 2000a; Lee, Keil, & Kasi, 2012; Newman & Sabherwal, 1996; Pan, Pan, Newman, & Flynn, 2006), our study aims to shed light on task-level escalation in the IT project management context.

In sum, we suggest that further research is warranted to bring attention to the negative efects that relative and criticism-based appraisals may have in IS settings. In this study, we do this by focusing on the efects that these types of performance appraisals can have on the problem of task-level escalation in IT projects. To shed light on the underlying mechanisms through which appraisals influence task-level escalation, we propose and test two mediating pathways based on goal orientation theory and prospect theory. In this way, our study extends prior IS research on performance evaluation and IT project management by highlighting a negative consequence that can occur from relative and criticism-based performance appraisals in an IT project setting.

## 2. Performance appraisals

Performance appraisal is centred on evaluating a performance outcome of an employee against a standard (Miller & Cardy, 2000; Scott & Einstein, 2001). One of the most well-known performance appraisal approaches that has been used in multiple industries is the relative performance appraisal. The relative performance appraisal approach was popularised in the 1980s by the former General Electric CEO Jack Welch, and was subsequently adopted by other corporate giants, such as Microsoft, Ford, and Conoco (Olson, 2013). When this approach is used, the performance of an employee is evaluated in relation to the performance of other employees (Gofin, Jelley, Powell, & Johnston, 2009; Moore & Klein, 2008). In contrast, when the absolute approach is used, the performance of an employee is evaluated without any reference to the performance of other employees.

The relative appraisal approach is commonly used across a variety of diferent settings, including consulting, sales, and even some universities. An article published in the Wall Street Journal estimates that at least 30% of Fortune 500 companies continue to use the relative appraisal approach to evaluate their employees (Ovide & Feintzeig, 2013). This would be consistent with prior research suggesting that the ability to draw distinctions between employees can be important for making promotion and salary decisions (Cleveland,

Murphy, & Williams, 1989). Despite its popularity, the relative appraisal approach has often received criticism suggesting that it does more harm than good (Rogers, 2004). One commonly cited criticism is that it can negatively impact creativity (Allen, 2012). Further, relative performance appraisals can lead to destructive internal competition, which makes it hard to create a collaborative organisational environment (Pfefer & Sutton, 2006) and relative performance feedback can reduce the quality of low performers’ work (Eriksson, Poulsen, & Villeval, 2009). Not too long ago, the technology company Yahoo drew criticism for reportedly adopting the relative appraisal practice (Brustein, 2013). Against this backdrop, in this study we aim to draw attention to another potential negative efect of the relative performance appraisal approach in which a relative appraisal of an individual project member’s performance can lead to task-level escalation in an IT project.

In addition to whether the relative or absolute approach is used, in conducting performance appraisals supervisors may or may not attempt to ofer support to a subordinate. One way to ofer support is using praise (Cederblom, 1982). Prior research found that in general using praise during a performance appraisal is associated with positive appraisal outcomes (e.g., increased satisfaction among subordinates) (Burke, Weitzel, & Weir, 1978; Burke & Wilcox, 1969). However, using praise during appraisals may not be efective when praising doesn’t appear to be sincere or when the supervisor seems to “sandwich” praise in a “praise-criticism-praise-criticism” fashion (Kay, Meyer, & French, 1965). The opposite of giving praise is using criticism during a performance appraisal (i.e., criticism-based performance appraisal). While there are anecdotal reports suggesting that ofering criticism can help workers to correct their behaviour and improve their performance (Lindsay, 2012; Thomas, 2012), research indicates that employees can become unhappy when they receive criticism regarding their performance (Culbertson, Henning, & Payne, 2013). Furthermore, some suggest that employees do not appreciate any form of criticism, and that they tend to become hostile when they receive negative feedback regarding their performance (Schultz & Schultz, 2009). In the business community, more companies are now encouraging managers to avoid criticising their employees and to focus more on the positive aspects (i.e., praising) of their employees’ performance (Feintzeig, 2015). Against this backdrop, in this study we aim to bring attention to a potential negative efect of the criticism-based appraisal (as opposed to the praise-based appraisal), in which criticism can lead to task-level escalation in an IT project.

In sum, in this study we focus on whether appraisals are based on an absolute or relative approach, and whether appraisals are based on praise or criticism, and theorise that the appraisal approach will afect project members’ task-level escalation in an IT project.

## 3. Escalation of commitment in IT project management

Escalation of commitment is a phenomenon whereby individuals continue to invest valuable resources into a previously chosen course of action despite signs that the course of action is failing to produce the desired results (Brockner, 1992; Staw, 1976). Prior research has shown that escalation of commitment manifests itself across a wide variety of contexts, including corporate bidding wars (Bazerman, 1999), financial investment (Tan & Yates, 2002), plant construction (Ross & Staw, 1993), policy decisions (Fox & Staw, 1979; Staw & Ross, 1978), and IT projects (Keil, 1995; Keil, Mixon, Saarinen, & Tuunainen, 1995; Lee et al., 2012; Newman & Sabherwal, 1996) which is the context in which we examine the phenomenon. A high percentage of IT projects (30–40%) experience escalation of commitment (Keil et al., 2000a). Thus, it is common to witness troubled IT projects that appear to take on a life of their own, continuing to attract valuable organisational resources despite having little chance that they will ever be completed successfully.

Over the years, escalation of commitment has become recognised as an important management problem in IT projects, and researchers have invoked several theories to understand IT project escalation<sup>1</sup>; such theories include self-justification theory (Staw, 1976), agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976), approach avoidance theory (Rubin & Brockner, 1975), and prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) (see Appendix A). However, to date, prior research on IT project escala tion has largely focused on project-level escalation, with little or no attention given to task-level escalation. This represents an important gap in the literature on IT project management, because when a serious setback on a task is not adequately addressed through re-scaling or collaboration (i.e., a project member escalates their commitment to completing the original task on their own), it will ultimately have a negative efect on the overall project quality due to task interdependency (Andres & Zmud, 2001, p. 44). When there is task interdependency, the work of a team member is often held up because an input required from another member is not provided in a timely manner (Saavedra, Earley, & Van Dyne, 1993). This suggests that task-level escalation in an IT project can negatively afect the subsequent tasks, and can even cause a delay in the overall project schedule. Specifically, projects can be modelled as a network of activities each of which is comprised of one or more tasks. When the total slack associated with an activity is zero (i.e., the activity is on a critical path), any delay in the activity will adversely afect the overall project schedule. Thus, an aggregation of task-level events (e.g., escalation) that cause delays in certain activities can ultimately have a negative impact on project-level outcomes. Yet, the factors that can lead to task-level escalation in the IT project environment are unknown in the literature. Based on this, we believe that further research is warranted to investigate task-level escalation in an IT project.

## 4. Properties of IT project environment that can breed task-level escalation

In this section, we discuss two properties of the IT project environment that can breed task-level escalation and where our study is applicable: lack of visibility and goal misalignment. We explain these properties below.

First, unlike a construction project where progress is visible for all to see, it is inherently more dificult to observe the work being performed in IT projects (Keil et al., 2000a) due to the invisible nature of IT project work (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991; Brooks, 1987). Moreover, prior research on IT project management has shown that project members can easily hide the fact that they are behind schedule on a given task and may choose not to report critical status information (Park & Keil, 2009; Smith & Keil, 2003; Smith, Keil, & Depledge, 2001). This research suggests that it may be easier for project members to continue working on a task after encountering serious setbacks without necessarily having to share the true status of the task with their manager. Based on this, we suggest that the IT project environment (i.e., where work products are not visible and lack of progress can be easily concealed) is one that can promote task-level escalation.

Second, goal misalignment refers to a situation in which an employee’s goals may not be aligned with their manager’s goals. In IT project settings, the project team is often comprised of various specialists (e.g., analysts, programmers, systems architects, database administrator, etc.) and prior research on IT project management suggests that it is not uncommon for some team members to pursue their own personal agenda even if it is not aligned with the goal of the project manager (Kirsch, Sambamurthy, Ko, & Purvis, 2002). For example, computer programmers who are hired to work on a project on a contract basis may focus more on getting a good evaluation from the project manager and landing a fulltime job. Based on this, we suggest that the IT project environment (i.e., where goal misalignment can occur) is one that can promote task-level escalation.

## 5. Research model and hypotheses

In theorising and investigating the relationships between a relative vs. absolute performance appraisal and tasklevel escalation, and between a criticism- vs. praisebased appraisal and task-level escalation, we draw on two theories: (1) goal orientation theory (Elliott & Dweck, 1988), which explains diferent types of goals that people lean towards in achievement or organisational settings and (2) prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), which explains decision-making under risk and uncertainty. Specifically, we draw on goal orientation theory to theorise how a relative appraisal (as opposed to an absolute appraisal) promotes task-level escalation in an IT project, and on prospect theory to theorise how a criticism-based appraisal (as opposed to a praise-based appraisal) promotes task-level escalation in an IT project. ## 5.1. Goal orientation theory and the efect of a relative appraisal on task-level escalation in an IT project

Prior goal orientation research suggests that in achievement or organisational settings individuals may focus on proving their existing ability in comparison to others or avoiding having to reveal their lack of ability in comparison to others (Beck & Schmidt, 2013). In the goal orientation literature, these two diferent goal orientations are known as proving goal orientation and avoiding goal orientation<sup>2</sup> (Elliot & Church, 1997; Jagacinski, Kumar, Boe, Lam, & Miller, 2010; Middleton & Midgley, 1997; Vandewalle, 1997; VandeWalle, Cron, & Slocum, 2001). What lies at the heart of proving and avoiding goal orientations is one’s motivation to judge and show his/her ability in comparison to that of others (Farr, Hofmann, & Ringenbach, 1993). In other words, individuals with a proving goal orientation want to demonstrate that they are superior to their peers, whereas individuals with an avoiding goal orientation want to avoid being evaluated in relation to others as such a comparison may make them look bad (Jagacinski et al., 2010). These distinctive characteristics associated with proving and avoiding goal orientations provide us with a theoretical foundation to predict how a relative performance appraisal (which is centred on evaluating one’s performance in comparison to others) may influence task-level escalation in IT projects.

In the IS literature, prior research has suggested that measuring an employee’s performance relative to other employees can lead to an unfair assessment as each employee works under diferent circumstances and there may be external factors that are outside the control of employees that must be considered (Chilton & Hardgrave, 2004). In addition, when a relative appraisal approach is used to evaluate project members in an IT project, it creates an environment in which project members are motivated to compare each other’s performances and compete against each other. Such an environment makes it dificult for project members to avoid such comparisons. Based on this we suggest that when a relative performance appraisal approach is used to evaluate project members’ performance during an IT project, project members may become more motivated to look or do better than their peers (proving goal orientation) and less motivated to avoid being compared to their peers (avoiding goal orientation). In other words, we predict that a relative performance appraisal will positively influence IT project members’ proving goal orientation and negatively influence IT project members’ avoiding goal orientation.

In addition, prior goal orientation research suggests that proving goal orientation is associated with a sense of high competence, whereas avoiding goal orientation is associated with a sense of low competence (Jagacinski et al., 2010; Kumar & Jagacinski, 2011). For example, Elliot and Church (1997) measured individuals’ competence expectations for how well they can do in an achievement setting, and found that there was a positive correlation between competence expectation and proving goal orientation, and a negative correlation between competence expectation and avoiding goal orientation. Drawing on this, we suggest that a proving goal orientation will have a positive efect on task-level escalation in an IT project, whereas an avoiding goal orientation will have a negative efect on task-level escalation in an IT project.

In summary, we suggest that a relative performance appraisal approach used to evaluate a project member’s project performance during an IT project will increase the proving goal orientation and decrease the avoiding goal orientation of the project member. We further expect that the project member’s proving goal orientation will positively influence task-level escalation and avoiding goal orientation will negatively influence tasklevel escalation in an IT project. Based on this, we propose the following mediation hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. A relative appraisal leads to greater task-level escalation in an IT project than an absolute appraisal, and this efect is mediated by (a) proving goal orientation (H1a) and (b) avoiding goal orientation (H1b).

## 5.2. Prospect theory and the efect of a criticismbased appraisal on task-level escalation in an IT project

Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) explains human decision-making under conditions of risk and uncertainty. Specifically, prospect theory explains that individuals’ decisions are influenced by whether a potential outcome is framed as a loss or a gain against the reference point that they adopt. Individuals consider a lesser outcome compared to the reference point a loss, and a greater outcome compared to the reference point a gain. Individuals exhibit risk seeking behaviour when they are in the domain of losses, whereas they exhibit risk averse behaviour when they are in the domain of gains. In the context of escalation decisions, negative feedback concerning a previously chosen course of action (e.g., initiating an IT project) indicates a loss and this motivates individuals to return to the reference point (Whyte, 1986). Such behaviour is driven by people’s preference to avoid a sure loss (quitting a failing course of action results in a sure loss) over an uncertain larger loss with a chance to return to the reference point.

Prospect theory has been used to explain the sunk cost efect in which decision-makers escalate their commitment and “throw good money after bad” (Garland, 1990). Specifically, sunk costs place decision-makers in the domain of losses and this promotes escalation of commitment. In other words, individuals prefer to recoup a prior loss by recommitting to a failing course of action, hoping to recover the loss rather than accept the sure loss that has already occurred. We suggest that prospect theory can ofer insights into the mechanism underlying the relationship between a criticism-based appraisal and task-level escalation in IT projects. Specifically, criticism received during a performance appraisal may create a perception of a loss with respect to job performance, and this may promote task-level escalation in IT projects, as we theorise below.

In order to induce desired behaviour from individuals, a manager may ofer praise or criticism (Cederblom, 1982). In the IS literature, prior research found that IS professionals have greater satisfaction with their career when their supervisor’s support is prominent (Jiang & Klein, 2015). When a criticism-based (as opposed to a praise-based) performance appraisal is given regarding a project member’s performance on previous tasks we expect that the project member will perceive this as negative feedback concerning his or her overall performance on the project, thus creating the conditions for escalation of commitment. In other words, when a criticism-based appraisal is used to evaluate prior performance this provides negative feedback concerning a project member’s overall performance, and may create perception of a loss of reputation or standing with respect to his or her overall performance. We expect that the perception of a loss will increase the project member’s risk propensity<sup>3</sup> (an individual’s willingness to take risk (Sitkin & Weingart, 1995)) and decrease the project member’s self-esteem (an individual’s own evaluation of self-worth<sup>4</sup> (Heatherton & Polivy, 1991; Judge & Bono, 2001)). In turn, we expect that the willingness to take risk will positively influence task-level escalation (Wong, 2005). Furthermore, the lowered self-esteem will motivate project members to continue working on a task despite encountering serious setbacks in order to recoup the loss in perceived self-worth. Based on this, we propose the following mediation hypotheses as follow:

Hypothesis 2. A criticism-based appraisal leads to greater task-level escalation in an IT project than a praise-based appraisal, and this efect is mediated by (a) risk propensity (H2a) and (b) self-esteem (H2b).

## 5.3. The efect of self-eficacy and fear of failure on task-level escalation in an IT project

In our research model, we included self-eficacy and fear of failure as control variables as they have been found to have significant influences on escalation behaviour. First, prior escalation research found that individuals’ positive perception of their own eficacy concerning a particular decision task (e.g., stock market investments, petroleum exploration, etc.) promotes the decision to escalate commitment to a failing course of action within the same decision context (Whyte & Saks, 2007; Whyte, Saks, & Hook, 1997). These findings suggest that self-eficacy concerning a project task (e.g., developing database queries) may have a significant impact on one’s decision of whether or not to continue the task when one is faced with significant challenges. Second, fear of failure (one’s concern over potential consequences that may result from failing to successfully complete a previously chosen course of action) has also been linked to escalation behaviour in prior research. Specifically, prior research suggests that escalation of commitment is more likely to occur when decision-makers’ fear of failure is high (Staw & Ross, 1987a, 1987b). In the context of task-level escalation on IT projects, we suggest that a team member’s fear of failure (one’s concern over potential consequences that may result from failing to successfully complete a task) may promote task-level escalation of commitment.

Given that the efects of self-eficacy and fear of failure on escalation behaviour are well known in the literature, we do not formally theorise their efects but instead include them in the model as control variables and simply test their efects on escalation behaviour in our research context. In other words, our goal is to test the efects of self-eficacy and fear of failure on escalation in our research context (task-level escalation) and to control for their impact when testing the efects of diferent approaches of performance appraisals on tasklevel escalation.

## 6. Method

In order to test the hypothesised relationships in our model, we conducted a laboratory experiment employing a scenario-based approach (i.e., vignette studies) that has been widely used in prior studies of escalation (Conlon & Garland, 1993; Garland, 1990; Keil et al., 2000b; Moon, 2001). We chose to use a laboratory experiment based on a consideration of the type of external validity we were interested in, that is “theory application” and not “efects application” (Calder, Phillips, & Tybout, 1981). Theory application research aims to advance a general understanding of the world by focusing on scientific theory, whereas efects application research aims to generate insights that can be directly applicable to the real work situation of interest. Laboratory experiments are known to be particularly strong in theory application. Further, in testing causal relationships between variables, internal validity is of paramount concern and the experimental method is universally acknowledged to be strong in this area. By examining the hypothesised relationships in a controlled experimental setting, we were able to achieve high internal validity.

In addition, vignettes have a long history of being used in the social, behavioural, and health sciences (Alexander & Becker, 1978; Bachmann et al., 2008; Wallander, 2009) as well as in IS research (Keil et al., 2000b; Lee et al., 2012). Vignette studies allow researchers to manipulate specific variables of interest through a written stimulus while controlling other factors, ofering “a glimpse into how individuals’ thoughts, feelings, behaviours, and decisions are afected by factors that may not be easily accessible in real-life situations because of confounding sources of variability that cannot be controlled” (Evans et al., 2015, p. 161). Furthermore, Thaler (2015) suggests that the use of vignettes in decision-making research can be valid when there is no strong reason to believe that people would behave diferently from what they say they would do. In their seminal paper for which they received the Nobel Prize – “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk” – Kahneman and Tversky (1979, p. 265) state:

By default, the method of hypothetical choices emerges as the simplest procedure by which a large number of theoretical questions can be investigated. The use of the method relies on the assumption that people often know how they would behave in actual situations of choice, and on the further assumption that the subjects have no special reason to disguise their true preferences.

## 6.1. Experimental design, participants, and procedure

The experiment involved a $2 \times 2$ factorial design in which absolute vs. relative appraisal and praise- vs. criticism-based appraisal were manipulated independently. Further, we included a control group in which participants did not receive any information regarding performance appraisal. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five experimental conditions.

We recruited 219 computer programmers using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Prior research has shown that Amazon’s Mechanical Turk is a high-quality source of data for psychology and social science research (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling, 2011). Further, MTurk samples tend to be more demographically diverse than other Internet samples as well as student samples, thus reducing potential sampling bias and generating more generalisable findings (Buhrmester et al., 2011). In the IS literature, MTurk has been recognised as an innovative alternative to student samples in IS research and MTurk samples have been shown to provide reliable data that are similar to student and consumer panels (Steelman, Hammer, & Limayem, 2014). MTurk subjects tend to come from many diferent countries (Buhrmester et al., 2011) and Steelman et al. (2014) cautioned that findings obtained from US samples vs. non-US samples may be diferent and suggested that studies aiming generalise to the US population focus on US-based participants. Since the goal of our study was not to generalise to the US population, we deemed MTurk to be appropriate for our study.

In our recruitment message on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, we explained that one must be a computer programmer working in an organisation to be qualified to take part in this study. We provided a link to our web-based survey (where our experimental materials were hosted) in the recruitment message on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Upon visiting the web-based survey, we asked participants to read the informed consent form and click “next” if they choose to participate in this study. On the next page, we provided general instructions about our study without revealing the true research objectives and told participants that this was a scenario-based decision task in which they would be asked to read a scenario and answer a set of questions assuming the role described in the scenario. Next, we asked participants to read the decision scenario (see Appendix B; we used a randomiser to randomly display one of the five decision scenarios to each participant). Following this, we asked participants to answer a set of questions pertaining to task-level escalation as well as other variables included in our research model. Further, we asked participants to answer a set of questions pertaining to their familiarity with agile or waterfall development, primary programming language, the number of years worked as a programmer, age, and gender. We also asked participants to respond to a multiple-choice question in which they were asked to select a statement that correctly describes what a “loop” is in computer programming. Lastly, we thanked the participants and provided a random code that they could use to receive credit for their participation through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Each respondent was paid 2 US dollars for their participation in our study.

We used the questions about primary programming language, the number of years worked as a programmer, and the “loop” statement to filter out unqualified respondents. Specifically, we excluded participants who did not provide a programming language or did not have work experience as a programmer. Further, we excluded participants who did not choose the correct statement that explains what a “loop” is. Through this process, we obtained a total of 176 qualified responses (see Table 1). Further, we asked participants to indicate their primary programming language, and we found that Java was the most popular programming language (approximately 40% of the participants), followed by C++ (20%) and Python (10%).

## 6.2. Decision task and measures

We constructed the decision scenario to be consistent with the typical escalation situation in which negative feedback is introduced regarding a previously chosen course of action, and individuals must decide whether or not to continue the same course of action (Brockner, 1992). Given our focus on task-level escalation in IT projects, the escalation decision involved the willingness of a computer programmer (working as a project member) to continue working on a database-related task associated with an IT project despite having encountered serious setbacks on the task. Specifically, participants were told that they were one of six computer programmers hired by a company to work on a new IT project. Further they were told that they had already completed several assignments related to the project, and for their current assignment their boss (the project manager) asked them to develop a number of database queries that would allow users to search for information within the system.

## 7.5. Hypotheses testing

Having examined the main efects of our independent variables on participants’ task-level escalation, we proceeded to test our mediation hypotheses (H1–H2). H1 involved the efect of a relative performance appraisal on task-level escalation, and H2 involved the efect of a criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation (Figure 1). We conducted two separate regression-based bootstrapping analyses to test these two sets of hypotheses<sup>5</sup>. Bootstrapping is a statistical method based on random re-sampling with replacement from the data-set (see Shrout and Bolger (2002) for a more detailed discussion). We chose the bootstrapping approach for testing of indirect efects over the Sobel (1982) test because of the unrealistic assumption of the Sobel test regarding the normality of the sampling distribution of the indirect efect. For our analysis, we followed the guidelines suggested by Hayes (2013) and used the PROCESS SPSS macro provided at Hayes’ webpage (http://www.processmacro.org).

First, we conducted a bootstrapping analysis with 5000 resamples (independent variable: relative appraisal, mediators: proving goal orientation and avoiding goal orientation, dependent variable: task-level escalation, control variables: self-eficacy and fear of failure). The results indicated that the indirect efect of each mediation path (αβ) (H1a: relative–proving–task-level escalation and H1b: relative–avoiding–task-level escalation) was significant; the confidence intervals did not include zero, indicating the indirect efects were significantly different from zero (see Table 7). These results provided support for H1. Further, the confidence interval for the direct efect of the relative appraisal on task-level escalation included zero after controlling for the two mediators, indicating that performance goal orientation (proving and avoiding) fully mediated the efect. Overall, the mediation model yielded an adjusted R<sup>2</sup> of .47, meaning that it explained 47% of the observed variance in task-level escalation.

Second, we conducted another bootstrapping analysis with 5000 resamples (independent variable: criticism-based appraisal, mediator: risk propensity and self-esteem, dependent variable: task-level escalation, control variables: self-eficacy and fear of failure). The results indicated that the indirect efect of each mediation path (αβ) (H2a: criticism–risk propensity–tasklevel escalation and H2b: criticism–esteem–task-level escalation) was significant; the confidence intervals did not include zero, indicating the indirect efects were significantly diferent from zero (see Table 7). These results provided support for H2. Further, the confidence interval for the direct efect of the criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation included zero after controlling for the two mediators, indicating full mediation. Overall, the mediation model yielded an adjusted R<sup>2</sup> of .31, thus explaining 31% of the observed variance in task-level escalation.

## 8. Discussion and implications

In this study, we focused on the problem of task-level escalation (i.e., a project members’ tendency to continue to work on a task despite encountering serious setbacks) and investigated how two commonly used performance appraisals can impact task-level escalation in an IT project. Specifically, we identified the relative performance appraisal- and criticism-based appraisal as having the potential to cause task-level escalation in an IT project. In investigating these relationships, we advanced and tested two mediating mechanisms based on goal ori entation theory and prospect theory. In a laboratory experiment with computer programmers, we found that a relative appraisal leads to greater task-level escalation in an IT project than an absolute appraisal, and that this relationship is fully mediated by proving and avoiding goal orientations. Further, we found that a criticism-based appraisal leads to greater task-level escalation in an IT project than a praise-based appraisal, and that this relationship is fully mediated by risk propensity and self-esteem. The efects of both relative appraisaland criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation were found to be significantly diferently from a control group that did not involve any form of performance appraisal. We now discuss empirical and theoretical contributions of our study, followed by practical impli cations, limitations, and directions for future research.

## 8.1. Empirical contributions

While performance appraisal is a very important and relevant management topic and has received much attention in the management literature (Banks & Murphy, 1985; Feldman, 1981; Korsgaard, 1995; Mayer, Davis, & James, 1999; Murphy & Cleveland, 1995), in this study we were motivated by a line of research and news reports that highlighted potential negative efects of performance appraisals on collaboration and teamwork in organisations (Baer, 2014; Scott & Einstein, 2001). Further, prior IS research suggested that relative and criticism-based appraisal approaches used in conducting performance appraisals can have undesirable impacts on employee behaviour (Chilton & Hardgrave, 2004; Jiang & Klein, 2015). In this regard, our study ofers valuable empirical evidence showing that certain types of performance appraisals (i.e., those employing a relative appraisal approach or a criticism-based appraisal) can promote undesirable behaviour in IT project settings (i.e., task-level escalation).

In addition, our study is the first one to empirically investigate task-level escalation in IT project settings. While escalation of commitment has become an important issue in IT project management, prior research has focused exclusively on project-level escalation (a tendency to continue a troubled IT project) (Keil, 1995; Keil et al., 2000a; Lee et al., 2012; Newman & Sabherwal,

1996; Pan et al., 2006), while ignoring task-level escalation. In this paper, we argue that task-level escalation is an important problem that negatively afects IT projects, and can result in significant schedule delays, budget overruns, and even project failure, due to task interdependencies. In this regard, our study conceptualises task-level escalation in the IT project management context, and ofers empirical evidence that brings attention to task-level escalation in an IT project.

## 8.2. Theoretical contributions

In probing the efects of a relative performance appraisaland a criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation in an IT project, we leveraged two theoretical perspectives: goal orientation theory (Elliott & Dweck, 1988) and prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Specifically, we ofered theoretical arguments for how a relative performance appraisal influences task-level escalation through proving and avoiding goal orientations, and for how a criticism-based appraisal influences tasklevel escalation through risk propensity and self-esteem. We believe that these findings represent an important theoretical contribution for two reasons. First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to use goal orientation theory to investigate escalation of commitment, and thus ofers a new theoretical perspective on escalation behaviour. Second, as noted earlier, prior IT escalation research focused exclusively on project-level escalation. Third, our study extends prior work using prospect theory to suggest the mediating mechanism for the efect of a criticism-based appraisal on task-level escalation. We believe our study has the potential to initiate a discourse on the distinction between task-level and project-level escalation, and to encourage further research that ofers additional theoretical insights into escalation of commitment at the task-level.

## 8.3. Practical implications

Given that our aim was to generalise to theory and not to a particular population, our implications for practice are necessarily limited and somewhat speculative. While previous research has examined escalation at the overall project level, our study examines escalation at the task level, which is a much more granular approach. We believe that this distinction is important for the very practical reason that as project managers know, projects do not become problematic all at once – they get there one day and one task at a time. By focusing on escalation at the task level, we highlight that project delay can be traced back to task-level decisions that project members make. An individual may decide, for example, to continue working on a task after encountering serious setbacks rather than bringing the matter to the attention of his/her supervisor and looking for an alternative course of action to successfully complete the task.

Such behaviour is likely to be particularly prevalent in IT project settings due to the invisible nature of work in IT projects. Such circumstances create the opportunity for individuals to conceal lack of task progress in the hope that they can successfully rectify the problem before their supervisor discovers the situation. When this occurs, and the employee is unable to complete the task successfully according to the project schedule, it can bring significant delay or harm to the overall project. Our study suggests that project managers should look for signs of escalation at the task level.

While further work is needed to determine if our findings can be generalised, our study suggests that project managers should be mindful when it comes to conducting performance appraisals with project members, as the approach used may have unintended consequences on individuals’ commitment to a task. We are not prepared to suggest that performance appraisals should be eliminated entirely (Lawler, 2014; Ryan, 2015) in IT project settings, as we believe performance appraisals are needed to adequately assess the performance of individual project members, and to motivate them to perform better. What we suggest instead is that practitioners become more aware of the potential negative efects of the rel ative performance appraisal- and criticism-based performance appraisal in IT project management. While the relative appraisal- and criticism-based appraisal may have their place, managers should be aware that these approaches could afect project members’ goal orientations, willingness to take risk, self-esteem, and task-level escalation. This is concerning because task level escalation could ultimately be detrimental to project success.

In addition, in this study we found that project members are more likely to escalate their commitment to a task when their performance is evaluated in relation to other project members as opposed to being evaluated based on their own absolute performance. This finding suggests important implications for practice and for future research concerning IT project control. Specifically, while formal controls (behaviour and outcome controls) are commonly implemented in the context of IT projects (Kirsch, 1996), prior research has not specified whether such controls should be extended to the individual level and if so, whether they should be configured to evaluate project members’ absolute performance or relative performance. Our findings suggest that it may be fruitful to conceptualise formal controls (both behaviour and outcome) as relative vs. absolute controls. This might lead to a richer understanding of how various control modes influence the behaviour of project members.

## 8.4. Limitations and directions for future research

As with any research, our study is not without limitations. First, to probe the connection between performance appraisal and escalation of commitment at the task level, we conducted a laboratory experiment. While laboratory experiments have distinct strengths, their external validity is often seen as a limitation. In our case, we were able to justify this approach because we were primarily interested in generalising to theory and not to a particular population or set of organisations. Still, while the experimental approach allowed us to achieve high internal validity, it obviously does not allow us to model all of the complexities of actual work settings. Achieving the high level of internal validity aforded by laboratory experiments requires that researchers focus on a small number of variables while tightly controlling possible confounding factors. By necessity, this means that it is neither practical nor desirable to attempt the level of realism normally associated with a workplace setting. This trade-of of higher internal validity and lower external validity is commonplace in laboratory experiments and should not necessarily be construed as a weakness. As noted by Cook and Campbell (1979, p. 84), “jeopardising internal validity for the sake of increasing external validity usually entails a minimal gain for a considerable loss.” Experimental designs should therefore be evaluated on whether they are likely to increase our understanding of human behaviour, not on whether they mimic organisational settings (Dobbins, Lane, & Steiner, 1988).

Second, while we theorised that performance appraisal is an important managerial practice that is used by project managers to influence IT project members, this is certainly not the only form of managerial practices used by project managers. Indeed, there may be other managerial practices that can shape escalation behaviour of project members in IT projects. Thus, while our study is the first to probe the impact of performance appraisal on the escalation behaviour of project members, further research is needed to understand how other managerial choices made by a project manager may afect the escalation behaviour of subordinates.

Third, in this study we focused on a performance appraisal that is either praise-based or criticism-based, but this may not always be a binary choice as there may be other types of performance feedback (e.g., developmental feedback) (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). In fact, the negative efects associated with criticism can be attenuated when criticism is ofered on a specific behaviour along with possible remedies for improvement, or when criticism is ofered in a form of writing (to make it appear more professional and less personal) (Miner, 1975). While our study ofers initial evidence that a criticism-based appraisal causes task-level escalation, we believe further research is warranted to investigate whether the efect of a criticism-based appraisal on tasklevel escalation can be attenuated when the criticism is ofered in a diferent form or manner. Furthermore, prior research suggests that relative performance feedback can be beneficial when it is perceived to be relevant to one’s job or when the feedback recipient is a superior performer (Mahlendorf, Kleinschmit, & Perego, 2014). Thus, we encourage future research to investigate factors (e.g., perceived relevance of relative feedback and the characteristics of feedback recipient) that can attenuate or change the negative efect of a relative performance appraisal on task-level escalation.

Fourth, in investigating task-level escalation we focused on two properties of the IT project environment that can breed task-level escalation: (1) lack of visibility and (2) goal misalignment. However, tasklevel escalation may be less likely to occur in IT project environments that involve increased visibility and less goal alignment. For example, in SCRUM a daily stand up could increase visibility, and in some open source projects goal misalignment may be less likely to occur. In addition, the consequences of task-level escalation (or its impact on the overall project) may not be as severe when the degree of modularity of work is low (i.e., tasks tend to be independent from each other). Based on this, we suggest that further research is warranted to identify contextual factors that may reduce the incidence of tasklevel escalation or minimise its consequences when it does occur.

Fifth, our experiment was not designed in a way that allowed us to fully explore whether projects run with diferent development methodologies would be more or less prone to task-level escalation. We did ask par ticipants whether they were more familiar with agile or waterfall development (i.e., a binary choice), and used this measure to explore if people familiar with the agile method would be more or less prone to task-level escalation in an IT project. We found that 72.7% of the participants (N = 128) were more familiar with agile and 27.3% of the participants (N = 48) were more familiar with waterfall. Further, we conducted an ANOVA to investigate whether there were diferences in task-level escalation among those who were familiar with agile (N = 128, M = 5.00, SD = 1.47) vs. waterfall (N = 48, M = 5.20, SD = 1.59), but we did not find a significant diference. Besides this measure, we did not explicitly consider the development method (agile vs. waterfall development) in designing the experiment and defining the research context, and this can be viewed as a limi tation. For example, in a waterfall environment project members are often assigned to specific tasks, whereas in an agile environment team members often get to choose their own tasks. Thus, the findings of our study (in which we told participants that they were assigned to a task) may not be generalisable to agile development environments in which team members have the freedom to choose their own tasks. We believe further research is warranted to investigate how task-level escalation in an IT project manifests itself across diferent develop ment environments (e.g., agile vs. waterfall). On the one hand, certain characteristics associated with agile (e.g., an emphasis on communication and collaboration) may reduce the incidence or severity of task-level escalation in an IT project. On the other hand, in the escalation literature it is well known that individuals who initiated a course of action are more likely to escalate their commitment to the chosen course of action despite negative feedback due to the personal responsibility associated with having initiated it (Staw, 1976). This indicates that being able to freely choose one’s own tasks, which is often the case in agile development environments, may actually promote task-level escalation in an IT project. We believe that additional empirical work is needed to more definitively explore how various practices that are commonly associated with agile development may influence task-level escalation. Additional research in this area can shed further light on possible distinctions that may exist between agile and other development environments with respect to task level escalation.

Lastly, we suggest that further research is warranted to shed further insights into the role of IT personnel’s demographics (age and gender) and their culture or nationality in understanding the efects of performance appraisals. Specifically, in our experiment the majority of participants were 40 or under (84%) and there were more males (70%) than females (30%). While this is quite consistent with the typical demographics of computer programmers (i.e., a large number of young male programmers) (Eadicicco, 2014, 2015; Henn, 2014; Igbaria & Baroudi, 1995; Kozlowski, 2012), this may not be representative of the entire IT personnel population and further research is warranted to investigate task-level escalation with more diverse age and gender groups. In addition, in our study we did not consider nationality of participants. Prior research found that there are some diferences between US-based vs. non US-based samples on online crowdsourcing markets (e.g., Amazon mechanical turk) (Steelman et al., 2014). Based on this, we suggest that future research incorporate the role of culture or nationality in how performance appraisals influence IS workers.

## 9. Conclusion

This study represents the first attempt to understand escalation of commitment at the task level in an IT project. Through a laboratory experiment with high internal validity, we found strong evidence for how performance appraisal can influence an individual’s decision of whether or not to continue a task despite encountering serious setbacks. Our findings suggest that individuals are more likely to escalate their commitment to a task when performance appraisals are: (1) criticism-based as opposed to praise-based, and (2) based on a relative approach to convey performance feedback as opposed to an absolute approach. We further found that proving goal orientation and avoiding goal orientation, as well as risk propensity and self-esteem, play important mediating roles in the relationships between performance appraisal and task-level escalation.

## Appendix B. Scenario and manipulations

Three months ago, you were hired as a computer programmer on a contract basis in the information technology (IT) department at Electro Lions, a large retailer of consumer electronics. You were one of six programmers hired to work on a new IT project at Electro Lions. The project, if successfully complet ed, is expected to deliver a lucrative e-commerce system. You have already completed several assignments related to the project, and for your current assignment your boss (the project manager) asked you to develop a number of database queries that would allow users to search for information within the system

(performance appraisal manipulation begins: control group did not receive the following information)

## [Absolute]

At the beginning of your employment, you were told that each programmer assigned to this project would be evaluated based on an absolute rating that is independent of the performance of other programmers. Further, you were told that there would be quarterly performance appraisal meetings with your boss. Your hope is to land a full-time job with this company following your current engagement as a contract employee

[Relative]

At the beginning of your employment, you were told that each programmer assigned to this project would be evaluated based on a relative ranking of his or her performance in comparison to the performance of other programmers. Further, you were told that there would be quarterly performance appraisal meetings with your boss. Your hope is to land a full-time job with this company following your current engagement as a contract employee

A week ago, you had your first performance appraisal meeting

[Absolute/Praise] Your boss was very pleased with your performance on the previous assignments related to the project. During the appraisal, he went out of his way to praise your performance without any reference to how your performance compared to that of the other programmers

[Absolute/Criticism] Your boss was very critical of your performance on the previous assignments related to the project. During the appraisal, he went out of his way to criticise your performance without any reference to how your performance compared to that of the other programmers

[Relative/Praise] Your boss was very pleased with your performance on the previous assignments related to the project. During the appraisal, he went out of his way to praise your performance showing how your performance compared to the other programmers

[Relative/Criticism] Your boss was very critical of your performance on the previous assignments related to the project. During the appraisal, he went ou of his way to criticise your performance showing how your performance compared to the other programmers

(performance appraisal manipulation ends)

Today, in reviewing your queries for the current assignment you discovered several errors and suddenly realised that you had completely misunderstood what needed to be done. Given the tight timeline for your deliverable, if you attempt to fix the problem on your own there is a 75% chance that you will be the cause of a delay in the overall project and that this will jeopardise any chance you have for converting the current employment into a permanent job ofer

Now you have come up with three courses of action to choose from: (1) continue working on the task with the goal of fixing your queries on your own and in a timely manner, (2) rescale the task in order to deliver functional, but limited queries in a timely manner, and (3) give up and ask for help to your boss
