# The competitive impact of information technology: can commodity IT contribute to competitive performance?

Gabriele Piccoli<sup>1</sup> and Tsz-Wai Lui<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Pavia, Pavia, Italy; <sup>2</sup>School of Hotel and Tourism Management, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China

Correspondence: Gabriele Piccoli, University of Pavia, Via San Felice, 5, 27100, Pavia, Italy. Tel.: +39 340 0571712;

## Abstract

Despite polarizing arguments on the strategic potential of information technology (IT), academic research has yet to demonstrate clearly that information systems initiatives can lead to sustained competitive performance (CP). We investigate this question using data from 165 hotels affiliated with two brands of an international lodging chain. We study the effect of successful use and unreliability of an incremental IT-enabled self-service channel on overall CP. We find that the effect of the incremental service channel depends on the firm’s organizational resources. We also show that different organizations experience significantly different use and unreliability rates. Further, we find that the positive association between the use of an IT-enabled self-service channel and CP endures over a 2-year period, despite competitors’ widespread adoption of the technology enabling the incremental service channel (self-service kiosks). Our findings corroborate research on the strategic role of IT resources when appropriately coupled with complementary resources. They lead us to question the notion that IT is a strategic commodity. Indeed, the findings suggest that IT-dependent strategic initiatives have the potential to generate sustained CP, even when the technology that enables them appears ‘simple’. These findings suggest the need for a theoretical explanation of the complementarities and interaction among the elements of IT-dependent strategic initiatives.

## Introduction

Research on the business value of information technology (IT) has accumulated over two decades of theoretical development and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Melville et al, 2004). Conversely, the questions of sustainable competitive advantage and the role of IT in fostering it have received much less attention (Nevo &Wade, 2010). With limited <sup>fi</sup>rm-level (Santhanam & Hartono, 2003) and application-level evidence (Nevo & Wade, 2011) to demonstrate that ITenabled competitive performance (CP) persists, academic research has yet to prove that IT is a key resource in the continued search for strategic advantage. More importantly, academic research has yet to explain how and under what circumstances IT is instrumental to sustained CP. This lack of conclusive evidence allows in<sup>fl</sup>uential misconceptions to recur in the press (Carr, 2003), despite their lack of methodological and theoretical rigor (McFarlan & Nolan, 2003). Information systems (IS) scholars have started to rectify this shortcoming by conceptualizing IT-dependent strategic initiatives (Piccoli & Ives, 2005) and IT-enabled resources (Nevo & Wade, 2010) as the basis for understanding the role of IT in the quest for sustained competitive advantages. This theorizing, however, awaits systematic empirical validation.

In recent years, the resource-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm has dominated the strategic IS literature (Bharadwaj, 2000; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003; Wade & Hulland, 2004; Fink, 2011). Within this perspective, however, IT is often explicitly or implicitly treated as fungible commodity (Mata et al, 1995) that must be coupled with complementary resources to enable sustained CP (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997; Banker et al, 2006). In this study, we build on this tradition and seek to advance the theoretical debate on the competitive value of IS by testing the proposition that IT is a commodity and that IT-enabled resources engender sustained competitive advantage (Nevo & Wade, 2010). We do so within the paradigm of application level research (Piccoli & Ives, 2005; Setia et al, 2008) by studying the effect of an IT-enabled customer service (CS) initiative on competitive-level outcomes. The initiative utilizes the same CS technology (self-service kiosks) and leverages the same technology infrastructure across all organizations in the sample. Moreover, we constrain our analysis to focus on a homogeneous set of organizations, thus controlling for the presence of complementary resources. Following recent theory, we propose that even ‘simple’ commodity-like IT assets, when coupled with organizational resources, create an IT-enabled resource characterized by emergent properties that enable sustained superior CP.

This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, we investigate the differential effect of the same technology-enabled initiative in different organizations, testing the interaction between IT resources and complementary resources empirically. Second, we explore the emergence of IT-enabled resources – formed through the purposeful combination of IT assets and organizational resources – by examining the variability in the performance of an IT-dependent initiative (in terms of use and unreliability). Finally, we challenge the notion that IT is a fungible commodity and that replicating IT-dependent strategic initiatives quickly erodes the leader’s competitive advantage. As such, we conduct the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical test of strategic combinatorial complexity (Rivkin, 2000, 2001) in the context of IT-enabled initiatives. Focusing on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c initiative enables us to measure actual adoption and usage through transactional data rather than selfreports and to link actual adoption of the initiative to CP over time.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present the theoretical frame for our investigation and introduce our hypotheses. We then discuss the context of our study, the available data and measures, and the methodology employed. Next, we present and discuss the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclude by drawing implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical framework

IS theorists have recently called for complementing the literature’s traditional focus on IT investments with a more precise measurement of IT resources, concluding that ‘investments in speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT assets explain performance differences along dimensions consistent with their strategic purpose’ (Aral & Weill, 2007, p. 763). Using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT-dependent strategic initiative as the unit of analysis enables precise measurement of customers’ adoption and use of the initiative (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003) while limiting confounding factors (e.g., different foci of IT investments). For this reason, IT-dependent strategic initiatives, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘identi<sup>fi</sup>able competitive moves that depend on the use of IT to be enacted and are designed to lead to sustained improvements in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitive position’ (Piccoli & Ives, 2005, p. 748), represent the unit of analysis in study. The IT-dependent strategic initiative perspective holds that a <sup>fi</sup>rm integrates IT assets and complementary organizational resources to design a value proposition (Vargo & Lusch, 2004) by building synergistic and integrated IT-enabled resources (Nevo & Wade, 2010) that produce sustained CP. Thus, no single component of the initiative (e.g., IT employees) enables value creation. Rather, all components work together to deliver the value proposition for customers (Milgrom & Roberts, 1995). For example, to differentiate from its competitors, an airline introduces a smartphone app enabling customers to check in for their <sup>fl</sup>ight. This initiative creates a new value propositions for customers (e.g., more convenient and fast check-in process). It does so by relying on a number of IT assets, including some that the <sup>fi</sup>rm must introduce (e.g., the app, the gate QR code readers), as well as on appropriate complementary resources. The IT-enabled strategic initiative perspective emerges from the tradition of research on complementarities in value creation (Setia et al, 2008), extending this research to the question of sustainable advantage. Thus, it is consistent with previous work on the creation of competitive advantage (Bharadwaj, 2000; Duliba et al, 2001; Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003), although it awaits validation with respect to the differential, long-term effect of the IT asset in different contexts and the sustainability of the IT-enabled competitive advantage.

## IT-enabled CS systems

The context of our study is IT-enabled CS initiatives, an area that has a long tradition in IS (see Ives & Learmonth, 1984; El Sawy & Bowles, 1997; Karimi et al, 2001; Theotokis et al, 2008; Rowe et al, 2011). The increasing importance of CS as a means to competitive differentiation and the co-evolution of CS and IT (Day et al, 2004) have renewed the impetus to advance this area (El Sawy & Bowles, 1997; Brohman et al, 2003; Ray et al, 2005; Chesbrough & Spohrer, 2006; Brohman et al, 2009). Many examples of IT-enabled strategic initiatives that address CS exist, ranging from personalized Websites that greet returning customers and call center software that allows service agents to engage in personal interactions to selfservice kiosks and mobile alerts that keep customers informed about their bank accounts balances. When working properly, self-service can increase service speed (Chase & Tansik, 1983), provide customers with more control (Lee & Allaway, 2002), and it can have a positive effect on customer satisfaction when it provides users with access to services at a convenient time and location (Meuter et al, 2000; Bitner, 2001; Yen, 2005). This type of IT-enabled service, representing an important tool for competitive differentiation and superior performance (Rayport & Jaworski, 2004; Sheehan, 2006; Brohman et al, 2009), can allow organizations to sustain price premiums over time (Nault & Dexter, 1995). Much of the early work on selfservice has focused on a single service channel considered in isolation (Galletta et al, 2004; Otim & Grover, 2006; Cenfetelli et al, 2008). However, it is critical to evaluate the effect of an incremental service delivery channel (e.g., the new self-service channel added to the current set of channels) as a part of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s CS system on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overall CS process and CP in order to gain a systemic view of how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s service system performs and to account for cross-channel synergies (Falk et al, 2007).

## System usage

Firms introduce IT-enabled CS systems to generate customer value. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, self-service systems enable technology-mediated customer contact by delivering fully automated service without employees’ involvement (Froehle & Roth, 2004). However, customer value only accrues if the customer successfully utilizes the service system. Consider the example of airline check-in kiosks. The kiosks provide value for customers who are comfortable with computerized service and believe the self-service channel enables them to access transportation more quickly. For these customers, the kiosks reduce the stress of waiting in line and allow customers to choose a preferred seat.

Despite being an important construct in IS research, system usage does not have a standard agreed upon de<sup>fi</sup>nition (Venkatesh et al, 2008). Some researchers (e.g., Devaraj & Kohli 2003; Venkatesh et al, 2008) conceptualize system usage in terms of duration, frequency, and intensity of utilization. Others theorize it as the level of cognitive absorption users have when interacting with the system (e.g., Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006) while some other authors treat system usage as an affect (e.g., Webster, 1998). Given the lack of an accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we construct one by specifying its three elements: a user, a system, and a task (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). We focus on ‘a user’s employment of a system to perform a task’ (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007, p. 659) and therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ne successful use of an IT-enabled CS system as the interaction between users and an IT-enabled CS channel that leads to the completion of the CS transaction at hand. Therefore, in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of the hotel check-in process, the actual receipt of the room assignment and key by the customer who elected to use the self-service kiosk instead of visiting the front desk re<sup>fl</sup>ect successful use of IT-enabled CS channel.

## System reliability/unreliability

In IS research, reliability has been theorized to be one of the components of system quality (Delone & McLean, 1992, 2003). From the service quality perspective, reliability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately (Parasuraman et al, 1985). That is, the <sup>fi</sup>rm honors its promise and performs the service correctly the <sup>fi</sup>rst time (Berkley & Gupta, 1994). The major factors that affect the reliable performance of IS include imperfect technology, uncontrollable user behaviors, and dynamic environments (Butler & Gray, 2006). Self-service channels are prone to failure because customers are not only the service recipients, but also service agents and technology users (Cenfetelli et al, 2008). The variability of customer behavior, combined with imperfect technology and the <sup>fl</sup>uidity of business operations, contribute to the unreliability of CS systems. We therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ne unreliability as the incidence of unsuccessful attempted transaction (e.g., the hotel customer is unable to obtain a room assignment and key using the kiosk). Note that this unreliability is not a technology property but a property of the CS system as a whole. The unreliability of self-service channels is particularly penalizing because breakdowns have the highest effect when they occur through IT-enabled self-service channels (Meuter et al, 2000; Parasuraman et al, 2005). Customers experience the failure directly with no service personnel available to engage in pre-emptive service recovery (Bitner et al, 2002).

## The differential strategic effect of IT initiatives

In addition to the IT asset itself (i.e., the self-service kiosk), IS research recognizes the role of complementary resources in enabling IT-dependent strategic initiatives to deliver bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Banker et al, 2006; Aral & Weill, 2007; Chari et al, 2008). Central to this theorizing is the notion that <sup>fi</sup>rms compete based on value propositions. Therefore, successful competitive replication must enable competitors to produce a comparable value proposition. This is not equivalent to creating a comparable ‘IT system’ because the IT-dependent strategic initiatives that underlie value propositions depend on the characteristics of the technology itself, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s characteristics, and the value network in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm operates (Piccoli & Ives, 2005). It follows that the same technology will have different effects in different organizations, not only with respect to implementation outcomes and system acceptance (Orlikowski & Iacono. 2001: Vitari et al. 2007). but also with respect to CF (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT-enabled resources – formed through the purposeful combination of IT assets and organizational resources – display emergent properties. These emergent properties held independently neither by the technology nor by the organization, allow

IT-enabled resources to contribute to value creation and sustained competitive advantage (Nevo & Wade, 2010).

Given this study’s focus on CS systems and CP, we use the notion of brand to characterize the organizations in which the initiative is introduced. Brands ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ect the complete experience that customers have with products’ (Keller & Lehmann, 2006, p. 740). As such, a brand is a source of know-how and standardized operations, particularly for multi-unit service operations (e.g., retail or hospitality chains). It is developed by codifying, applying, and monitoring brand standards. Brand standards represent a set of speci<sup>fi</sup>cations that encompass both <sup>fi</sup>rm assets (e.g., the physical layout of the store) and operating procedures (e.g., staf<sup>fi</sup>ng levels and business processes). Brand parents seek to ensure compliance with these standards through various enforcement mechanisms, such as quality assurance, customer evaluation scores, and mystery shopping.

From the customer’s perspective, a brand represents a bundle of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that determines their preference for one provider over another and their willingness to pay for the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s offering (Keller & Lehmann 2006). The brand, therefore, represents a suitable aggregated proxy for the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s complementary resources that contribute to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c instantiation of an IT-dependent strategic initiative when coupled with IT resources. In such initiatives, complementary resources that are more synergistic and integrated with an IT asset will strengthen the effect of those assets on the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ CP (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Therefore, we posit:

H1.1: Brand is a significant moderator of the relationship between successful use of the incremental IT-enabled service channel and competitive performance.

H1.2: Brand is a significant moderator of the relationship between unreliability of the incremental IT-enabled service channel and competitive performance.

IT as a commodity and sustained competitive advantage The IT-dependent strategic initiative framework focuses on whether IT-enabled competitive advantage is sustainable based on the premise that IT ‘does not contribute to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in isolation, but instead contributes as a part of an activity system that fosters the creation and appropriation of economic value’ (Piccoli & Ives 2005, p. 766). This intuition has been recently formalized around the notion of IT-enabled resources by explaining the chain of causality that leads to value creation and sustained CP (Nevo & Wade, 2010). The IT-dependent strategic initiative perspective also provides an embryonic challenge to the notion that IT is a commodity by claiming that ‘at a minimum, the documented high failure rate of IT projects casts some doubts on the accuracy of the “easily replicable” hypothesis’ (Piccoli & Ives, 2005, p. 767). Both the press (Knowledge@Wharton, 2004) and academic literature (Mata et al, 1995; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997) have espoused the idea that IT is a fungible commodity input. Moreover, this notion seems to implicitly underlie studies that treat IT investments as interchangeable by focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s aggregated IT investments as the unit of analysis (Aral & Weill, 2007). Nevertheless, the notion that easily-replicating IT leads to its inability to contribute to sustained CP (i.e., IT is a ‘strategic’ commodity) overlooks the fundamental insight that complex socio-technical systems emerge from the relationships among subsystems and components that do not need to be intrinsically complex when considered individually (Anderson, 1999).

Webster’s Dictionary de<sup>fi</sup>nes a commodity as ‘a massproduced unspecialized product’. As commonly used in the IS literature, the term refers to a product, tangible or intangible, that is the same regardless of the producer (e.g., a 20-Megabyte RAM chip, music streaming software). Thus, a CPU delivering two GFLOPS<sup>1</sup> of computational power or a hard disk holding one Terabyte of data may indeed be a commodity, regardless of who manufactures the hardware components that deliver the functionality. However, from this parallel between a commodity and hardware devices, some authors have expanded the ‘IT as a strategic commodity’ metaphor to include software, software applications, and even ‘embedded best practices’ (Carr, 2003). The strategic IS literature has begun to challenge this extension by recognizing that IT has strong ‘evolution-in-use’ dynamics that shape the relationship between IT assets and organizational resources over time according to contextual in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Therefore, once contextualized, the same technology produces combinatorial effects that depend on how its components behave in relation to one another and to the environment (Corning, 2002). Early research validated this idea by reporting outcome differences after the implementation of identical geographic IS in similar departments of two distinct organizations (Robey & Sahay, 1996).

The strategic IS literature now fully embraces this line of reasoning, recognizing that ‘as IT systems assimilate throughout organizational processes, the applications themselves change as they are contextualized and reinvented into the changed organizational context’ (Setia et al, 2008, p. 9, emphasis added). In other words, the evolution of IT-enabled strategic initiatives may diverge signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly even when the same IT assets are at their core. More formally, IT-enabled resources can be rare and inimitable even when the IT asset at their core is not (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Complexity of a system does not require the complexity of its component parts (Simon, 1962, p. 468). The complexity of an IT-enabled strategic initiative is a function of the number of elements that comprise it and the nature of the internal relationships among them. For example, in the context of strategy imitation, when strategic replication is characterized by search in a multidimensional decision space, imitating the leader’s strategy successfully depends on the complexity of the said strategy (Rivkin, 2000). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of elements make it more likely that outside observation by competitors will lead to an imperfect understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s initiative. Moreover, a high degree of interrelation among elements magni<sup>fi</sup>es the negative performance implications of any judgment errors competitors may make. This work calls into question the ‘algorithmic solutions’ approach to competitive imitation (Rivkin, 2000, p. 824) that is typical of the ‘IT as a strategic commodity’ argument. Small differences in the initial or intervening conditions of a complex system can lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences among entities even when they share similar initial conditions (Anderson, 1999). Similarly, a unique IT-enabled resource emerges, as IT assets are coupled with organizational resources, even when the same IT project is implemented in organizations that share the same set of IT resources (e.g., infrastructure, data repositories, IT skills, IT management skills, technological training) and complementary resources (e.g., processes and procedures, human resource practices, strategic positioning, customer base). Recent work demonstrates that IT resources are ‘components of a bundle of resources that form a complex and interactive system’ ( Jeffers et al, 2008, p. 725). These elements work together in a highly dependent fashion that determines the initiative’s ability to deliver the expected customer value. IT-enabled CS systems, for example, include many design and implementation elements, including interface con<sup>fi</sup>guration, infrastructure elements, data sources and databases, physical location of the technology, process design, roaming agents, and agents and/or customer training. Moreover, these components interact in non-trivial ways. The emergence of different IT-enabled resources from the interactions of these many components will then produce variations in successful use and unreliability of an ITenabled service initiative that leverages them (Nevo & Wade, 2010). We posit:

H2.1: Successful use of an incremental IT-enabled service channel varies significantly among firms that employ the same technology and complementary resources.

H2.2: Unreliability of an incremental IT-enabled service channel varies significantly among firms that employ the same technology and complementary resources.

The IT-dependent strategic initiative perspective indicates that an initiative can enable sustained competitive advantage when the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitors face high barriers to erosion in replicating the leader’s initiative (Piccoli & Ives, 2005). Barriers to erosion represent impediments that make replicating the initiative time consuming and costly (Wernerfelt, 1984; Reed & DeFillippi, 1990), as in the presence of IT resources, complementary resources, and IT project barriers, or prevent competitors from being able to offer the same value proposition as the leader, as in the case of the preemption barrier. Thus, the competitors’ intent to replicate the leader’s initiative must produce a comparable value proposition that, as an interlocking set of activities and components (Rivkin, 2000), is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to replicate compared with the IT core taken in isolation (Piccoli et al, 2003). The four barriers ‘contribute independently or in combination with one another’ (Piccoli & Ives, 2005, p. 751), although little is known about how they combine to hinder imitation.

Recent work on strategic combinatorial complexity provides theoretical guidance to improve the precision of the IT-enabled strategic initiatives framework by uncovering the mechanisms by which the barriers to erosion work together (Ryall, 2009). Under conditions of combinatorial complexity, the analysis of individual components of an IT-dependent strategic initiative in isolation (Mata et al, 1995; Carr, 2003) becomes misleading because it treats the effect of each component separately instead of drawing attention to their interrelationships. We posit that IT-dependent strategic initiatives, which are often considered ‘simple’ because they rely on a replicable IT core and complementary resources open to competitive scrutiny, may display combinatorial complexity due to the interrelated nature of its socio-technical components. As such, they offer signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunities for sustained advantage (Ryall, 2009), as imitators must rely on imperfect heuristics to guide their replication efforts (Rivkin, 2001). To our knowledge, previous studies in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld have not tested this proposition explicitly. However, some evidence on the resilience of competitive advantage in environments characterized by apparent easy imitation exists in the mutual funds industry (Makadok, 1998). We posit:

H2.3: The positive relationship between successful use of the incremental IT-enabled service channel and competitive performance holds over time.

## Methodology

In this study, we utilize an archival research methodology. The context is the lodging segment of the hospitality industry where we examine the check-in process occurring through the CS system, comprising the traditional personal service channel (i.e., the front desk) and the IT-enabled self-service channel (i.e., hotel lobby kiosks). The kiosk is a dedicated computer that enables customers to register, receive a room assignment, and a room key. The machine cost about 10,000€ at the time of the study. We obtained monthly check-in transactions for a 2-year period (from 1 January 2006 to 31 December 2007) for 165 U.S. properties of a large international lodging chain (3465 records). The properties are af<sup>fi</sup>liated with two of the chain’s brands, and they compete in the same upscale segments of the lodging industry. The brands differ in their product characteristics, with Brand A being an all-suite product and Brand B being a transient full-service hotel product. The corporate IT and marketing functions managed the initiative’s roll out, including implementation, training, and support, centrally. After testing, the brand-wide roll out began formally in 2005. The kiosk application was standard across the brands, as was the IT infrastructure and the databases the application uses. Finally, operating procedures, staf<sup>fi</sup>ng decisions, quality assurance, and all other business processes were controlled uniformly and tightly through the chain’s brand standards. Thus, in our data set, the characteristics of the IT application, the IT resources, and complementary resources that comprise the IT-enabled strategic initiative were standardized.

The lodging context is particularly well suited to the operationalization of the brand construct. A lodging brand identi<sup>fi</sup>es and speci<sup>fi</sup>es most characteristics of a hotel product while delivering the promised service to customers through standardized operating procedures (Dev et al, 1995). Consequently, large hotel chains consciously develop clearly positioned brands to segment their global customer base. For example, Marriot International manages 8 different brands, Hilton Hotels manages 10, Accor has a portfolio of 15 brands, and Intercontinental Hospitality Group administers 7. Hotel brands create clear ‘swimming lanes’ that set customer expectations, amenities, level of service offered, price point, and the like (Weinstein, 2008). In essence, a brand de<sup>fi</sup>nes the hotel’s overall customer value proposition and provides clear directives and tools for management to enact it.

## Measures

The literature has measured system usage in different ways: objectively as actual utilization based on system logs, or subjectively as reported usage. However, subjective measures tend to provide a biased estimate of actual usage (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). We therefore adopted an objective measure of successful use of the incremental ITenabled service channel (S-Use) and computed the monthly ratio of the number of completed check-in transactions performed through the kiosk by the total number of arrivals at each property. Doing so enabled us to standardize volume differences in properties of different size. We measured the unreliability of the incremental ITenabled service channel (Unr) by computing the ratio between failed check-in transactions through the kiosk and the total number of attempted check-in transactions through the kiosk, thus standardizing it by usage volume. We coded Brand A as 0 and Brand B as 1. Finally, we measured CP using an independently collected standard industry measure of competitive pricing called RevPAR Index. Revenue per Available Room (RevPAR) is the standard metric for measuring daily room revenue per available room in the hotel industry. RevPAR Index, computed by Smith Travel Research, an independent research <sup>fi</sup>rm, compares an individual property’s RevPAR with its competitive set. A RevPAR Index of 100 points indicates that the brand has achieved a RevPAR that falls within the average of its competitive set. Numbers higher than 100 points indicate the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to drive superior room revenue while numbers below 100 indicate a negative gap in room revenue performance. RevPAR Index is an optimal measure of CP because it automatically controls for the potential confounding effect of the economic cycle while enabling direct comparison with a relevant set of competitors. We lagged CP by 3 months because we expected that the positive effect of any CS initiative will surface in <sup>fi</sup>nancial (RevPAR) and competitive measurers (RevPAR Index) only after customers repurchase the hotel service. The 3-month lag time was based on the average repurchase cycle of the clients in the data set (79.64 days).

## Discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings

As expected, our results indicate that the brand construct moderates the relationship between successful usage of an incremental IT-enabled service channel and CP. That is, one brand displays a direct positive effect of the incre mental service channel on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to outpace it competitors in terms of revenue while the other does not – despite the fact that both brands employed the same corporate-supplied standard technology and received training and support from the same corporate team. This result corroborates the growing body of evidence tha points to the role of synergistic interaction between orga nizational resources and IT assets in shaping the competi tive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IT-dependent strategic initiatives. This result is particularly reliable because we conducted this study at the application level (Setia et al. 2008). Thus, we standardize the characteristics of the initiative, measure actual successful usage rather than aggregate IT investment, and estimate the effect of successful usage on a direct measure of comparative CP. The results show that compared with Brand A, Brand B experiences a stronger competitive response positively associated with successful usage. In other words, given the same degree of use by customers, the same initiative engenders a more polarized outcome in one organization compared with the other. While our design does not allow us to explain the speci<sup>fi</sup>c reasons for this brand difference, the results call into question the notion that IT is a fungible commodity input and validate empirically the proposition that IT-enabled initiatives must be consistent with complementary organizational resources (Milgrom & Roberts, 1990). An important aspect of our study is that we evaluate the IT-enabled multi-channel CS systems as a whole. Therefore, the successful usage of kiosks will have an effect on the entire customer base, not just those customers who checked in via kiosk, even when usage rates may appear low. To comprehend this point, consider that in our study, the average usage rate of kiosks was 2.1%, the average number of arrivals per month was 3564.24, and the standard check-in time was 5 min. Therefore, a hotel can free up 6.27 h of front desk agents’ time per month. As a result, the front desk agents can use these 6.27 h to serve other customers better and may be able to even generate more revenue through relationship building and up-selling cross-selling instead of attending the routine check-in tasks. Moreover, during slow periods, the usage rate can be zero and front desk agents will be able to attend to every guest without creating a line. During the busy period, the usage rate will be higher than the average (i.e., 2.1%) and

The moderating effect of brand does not appear to extend to the relationship between unreliability of the incremental IT-enabled service channel and CP. This result challenges the notion that failure of a self-service channel has a negative effect. The discrepancy with previous research may be due to the fact that earlier work studied a single service channel (Meuter et al, 2000). Conversely, our unit of analysis is the CS system as a whole. In a multichannel environment, service recovery is easier, as front desk agents are easily reachable and can rectify problems more promptly. Moreover, customers in our study selfselected into the self-service or personal service channel. Consequently, enjoying freedom of choice, the average customer may be more likely to take responsibility for the failure of the check-in transaction (Arkin et al, 1976).

The results of H2.1 and H2.2 further support the claim that the same commodity IT asset, the self-service kiosk, produces different results even in similar settings. We <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variability in successful usage (62.02%) and unreliability (68.48%) of the IT-enabled CS channel because of individual <sup>fi</sup>rm-level variables after controlling for brand and time differences. Our results show no systematic effect of brand on the extent of successful usage and unreliability of the incremental service channel. The property (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rm) level accounted for the bulk of the variability in successful usage. This result supports the notion that despite having the same brand resources (e.g., brand standards, training, infrastructure, standard operating procedures) and the same technology assets, the properties in the portfolio of each brand experienced substantial differences in terms of success and failure of the incremental service channel. Our results directly challenge the ‘IT as a strategic commodity’ perspective, which hypothesizes that organizations can rapidly and precisely replicate a given value proposition by implementing the same technology and best practices, as ‘competing <sup>fi</sup>rms are usually not disadvantaged in imitating by history, causal ambiguity, or social complexity’ (Mata et al, 1995, p. 497). More broadly, our results for H2.3 empirically show that even apparently simple IT-enabled strategic initiatives, simple in terms of technology and complementary resources, enable sustained superior CP for organizations that successfully foster adoption of the initiative. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect of successful usage of the incremental IT-enabled service channel on CP holds over 2 years, even when similar initiatives proliferate in the marketplace, leading industry observers to contend that the technology has moved ‘from competitive advantage to competitive parity’ (Gartner, 2007, p. 4).<sup>4</sup> In other words, despite evidence of the technology being adopted pervasively and claims of ‘competitive parity by business observers, the positive relationship between successful usage and CP remained signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for more than 2 years, even after controlling for variations in the quality of these complementary resources. These results are consistent with a combinatorial view of IT-enabled strategic initiatives, whereby the source of sustained competitive advantage is not found in any one single component of the initiative, but instead, it is found in the non-trivial interactions among them. Moreover, our work casts doubt on the simplistic assumption implicit in much of the strategic IS literature, which suggests that the imitation of IT-enabled strategic initiative requires merely observation – to identify the source of the advantage – and a step-by-step ‘algorithmic’ replication of the leader’s behaviors.

## Implications for future research

Our results have profound implications for future research. First, rather than seeking to isolate the components that contribute to sustained competitive advantage, research should investigate the varied complementarities of the many elements that comprise IT-dependent strategic initiatives and produce the value proposition. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings call attention to the need to better understand the nature of IT-enabled resource complexity. The IS literature, however, has yet to provide a clear test to measure IT complexity in the context of organizational IT assets, which is a sorely needed addition if we are to move the discourse beyond the simplistic view that ‘IT is easily imitable’. Others have recognized the importance of investigating complementarities as the next frontier for strategic IS scholars (Aral & Weill, 2007; Jeffers et al, 2008). Understanding them is also critical for research on sustainability of IT-enabled competitive advantage because the presence of interactions helps explain the permanence of different results associated with strategies that are subject to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant public scrutiny (Rivkin, 2001). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT-dependent strategic initiatives may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate because locally optimizing a subset of the system’s elements does not lead to successful reproduction of the overall value proposition (Rivkin, 2000). Recent IS literature has theorized the characteristics of IT-dependent strategic initiatives that create rare and inimitable ITenabled resources (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this work identi<sup>fi</sup>es the central role of synergy, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the potential for positive outcomes stemming from melding IT assets and organizational resources. The likelihood of a <sup>fi</sup>rm realizing synergy depends on the degree of complementarity among its IT assets and organizational resources, as well as the effort exerted to integrate them (Nevo & Wade, 2010).

Therefore, with respect to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s CP stemming from an IT-dependent strategy initiative, it appears that successfully eroding the leader’s advantage is predicated on two conditions. First, competitors must identify the resources at the heart of the initiative and their complementarities. Second, they must be able to mount a coordinated response designed to assemble the needed elements appropriately. In other words, for the innovator, the ability to stave off successful imitation is predicated on internal conditions (e.g., complementarities within the initiative’s components) and the competitor’s characteristics (e.g., ability to produce an appropriately coordinated response). Our results are consistent with this explanation, exemplifying that an apparently ‘simple’ IT-enabled strategic initiative leads to competitive advantage that is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to erode. IS scholars, with their understanding of new technology design, appropriation processes, organizational structures and strategies, are best positioned to further this line of inquiry.

Second, we need to extend the notion of complexity and dependencies to the relationship between IT and complementary resources while considering both the nature and number of these interactions. Recent work has mapped dependencies among architectural elements of a software application, thus enabling the estimation of propagation cost associated with individual design changes (MacCormack et al, 2006). While the focus of the research is on changes to existing software code, the intuition behind it suggests an approach that could be used to measure the complexity of IT-enabled strategic initiatives as a function of the number of non-trivial socio-technical interactions it features. Along these lines, the relationships among components of an IT-dependent strategic initiative have recently been theorized to be additive, synergistic, or suppressive when their effects combine independently, magnify, or reduce each other’s effect (Jeffers et al, 2008). Our results lend empirical support to recent theoretical advances, positing that the most fruitful direction for strategic IS scholars is to study these complex relationships as opposed to adopting a reductionist approach seeking to identify and single out the most important component. The interaction between IT assets and complementary resources gives rise to emergent properties that are unique to the IT-dependent strategic initiatives. Thus, ‘attempting to trace their source by examining the components in isolation may be unproductive’ (Nevo & Wade, 2010, p. 166). By understanding the manner in which these properties come to be, we can contribute to explain the contribution of IS to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s sustained superior performance. Beyond the additive, synergistic, or suppressive interaction effects among the components of an IT-dependent strategic initiative, we envision scenarios where resources act as prerequisites or constraints for one another and thus create path dependencies (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Piccoli & Ives, 2005). In other words, interactions can occur not only among resources that are co-present, but also over time. For example, a data repository cannot be populated until the appropriate IT infrastructure to generate data is deployed and operational. A forecasting and optimization mathe: matical model requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cant training before it can provide satisfactory results (Piccoli & Watson, 2008). For example, Farecast (later acquired by Microsoft to be included in its Bing Travel product) pioneered a new approach to airfare <sup>fl</sup>uctuation predictions based on statistically evaluating the probability of future ticket price changes with the aim to inform travelers about whether to book now or wait for prices to drop. This approach is only possible for organizations that have developed their forecasting models based on millions of historical ticket shopping transactions and fare changes. Little is known about the types, characteristics, and effects of the complementarities and interactions among elements of ITenabled strategic initiatives; hence, further research in this direction is needed

## Limitations and conclusions

As with any study of this scope and complexity that uses actual organizational data, this study has limitations we must acknowledge in order to accurately interpret the results. We believe that using an archival methodology and a data set of actual transactions represent strengths of this study; however, we are constrained by the data available in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s records. Consequently, our model explains less than 10% of the variance in the CP of the <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample $( R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 0 9 7 )$ ). This result should be interpreted in the context of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT-enabled strategic initiative (i.e., adding an incremental service channel to the portfolio of CS delivery systems) and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational process (i.e., hotel check-in). Such focus is important for reducing confounding factors and variability even though it limits the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Given that this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical test that explicitly challenges the notion of IT as a fungible strategic commodity, it is important for future studies to replicate our study before we can provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant managerial implications and prescriptive guidelines. Another limitation of the study concerns the use of a highly aggregated construct, brand, to measure a wide variety of complementary resources. While this measure enables us to contribute to the literature on strategic IS and to the important debate on IT as a commodity, it limits our ability to provide solid guidance to practicing managers. We are unable to discern the speci<sup>fi</sup>c in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the complementary resources
