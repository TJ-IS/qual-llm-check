# Municipal credit rating modelling by neural networks

Petr Hájek

Institute of System Engineering and Informatics, Faculty of Economics and Administration, University of Pardubice, Studentská 84, 532 10 Pardubice, Czech Republic

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 10 November 2009 Received in revised form 4 November 2010 Accepted 28 November 2010 Available online 8 December 2010

Keywords: Credit rating analysis Municipal credit rating Neural networks Support vector machines

## a b s t r a c t

The paper presents the modelling possibilities of neural networks on a complex real-world problem, i.e. municipal credit rating modelling. First, current approaches in credit rating modelling are introduced. Second, previous studies on municipal credit rating modelling are analyzed. Based on this analysis, the model is designed to classify US municipalities (located in the State of Connecticut) into rating classes. The model includes data pre-processing, the selection process of input variables, and the design of various neural networks' structures for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The selection of input variables is realized using genetic algorithms. The input variables are extracted from <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements and statistical reports in line with previous studies. These variables represent the inputs of neural networks, while the rating classes from Moody's rating agency stand for the outputs. In addition to exact rating classes, data are also labelled by four basic rating classes. As a result, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracies and the contributions of input variables are studied for the different number of classes. The results show that the rating classes assigned to bond issuers can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed with a high accuracy rate using a limited subset of input variables.

## 1. Introduction

Credit rating process is an independent evaluation whose aim is to <sup>fi</sup>nd out how an object is capable and willing to meet its payable obligations, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally based on complex analysis of all the known risk factors of the assessed object. It is realized by a rating agency. A higher credit rating shows a low credit risk. According to the assessed object, credit ratings of the state, company, municipality, <sup>fi</sup>nancial institution, single bond, etc. exist. Credit rating is a result of a credit rating process. It is represented by a rating class de<sup>fi</sup>ned on a rating scale. Rating classes are assigned to assessed objects.

Credit ratings are used by bond investors, debt issuers, and governmental of<sup>fi</sup>cers as a measure of the risk of a company. They provide a means of determining risk premiums and marketability of bonds, allowing <sup>fi</sup>rms issuing debt to estimate the likely return investors require. Bankers and companies considering providing credit rely on credit ratings to make important investment decisions, many regulatory requirements for <sup>fi</sup>nancial decisions are based on credit ratings, etc.

Credit ratings are costly to obtain because rating agencies invest large amount of time and human resources to perform the credit rating process. Therefore, there has a large much effort made in order to simulate the credit rating process of rating agencies through statistical (e.g. [23], [34], [35]), and soft-computing methods (e.g. [7], [32], [51]). The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in designing such models lies in the subjectivity of the credit rating process. Complex relations between <sup>fi</sup>nancial and other variables are evaluated. Such a complex process makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to classify rating classes through statistical methods. However, soft-computing methods (neural networks [7], [38], [36], fuzzy systems [3], [51], evolutionary algorithms [6], arti<sup>fi</sup>cial immune systems [14], [40], and hybrid systems [37]) can be applied for the modelling of such complex relations. High classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy has been achieved especially by neural networks (NNs) [7], [50] and support vector machines (SVMs) [32], [41] in corporate credit rating modelling. In economics and <sup>fi</sup>nance, NNs (including SVMs) are usually applied in such cases where variables are in non-linear relations. This is reported to be typical for economic and <sup>fi</sup>nancial data [24]. NNs make it possible to model these relations as they learn the dependencies in training data. As a result, gained knowledge is stored in synapse weights. Moreover, the knowledge can also be applied for unknown input data which were not used in the training process. This is also known as generalization ability of NNs. The disadvantage of NNs lies in the fact that NNs are usually designed as so-called “black boxes”, i.e. it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to extract understandable knowledge from them. Therefore, prior studies in modelling credit rating are aimed at quantifying the effect of input variables for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, i.e. to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which input variables are crucial for credit rating process. Mostly, sensitivity analysis has been employed for this purpose. Based on the mentioned facts, it is possible to state that the methods capable of processing and learning the expert knowledge, enabling their user to generalize and properly interpret, have proved to be most suitable for credit rating modelling.

The paper is structured as follows. First, basic components of credit rating process will be introduced. Further, current methods used for municipal credit rating modelling will be reviewed. Then, basic notions of NNs will be presented with an emphasis on NNs' models suitable for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem realization. Using a number of NNs for credit rating modelling has so far not been reported in prior studies. Using probabilistic NNs, GMDH NNs, or cascade correlation NNs is novel in credit rating modelling. Moreover, the modelling of municipal credit rating process has only been realized through statistical methods previously. The input variables for the modelling are designed based on all the aspects of economic and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of municipalities. Most input variables used in this study have also been used in previous works. However, compared to previous studies, a more complex set of input variables is proposed in this study. It involves economic, debt, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and administrative categories. In order to get an optimum set of input variables, a novel two-step approach is proposed. First, the original set of input variables is optimized by a combination of a correlation-based approach and genetic algorithms (GAs). Then the contribution of selected input variables is studied using sensitivity analysis, with the intention to compare the relative importance of the input variables. The contribution analysis of input variables for different numbers of rating classes has also not been previously realized. Finally, the gained results will be compared across selected models of NNs and benchmark methods.

## 2. Credit rating modelling

Credit rating modelling is regarded as a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem, i.e. as supervised learning. For its realization, rating classes assigned by a rating agency should be known in advance. The rating classes represent a result of credit rating process. Therefore, credit rating process and credit rating modelling is introduced in this part of the paper.

## 2.1. Credit rating process

As presented above, credit rating process represents a subjective assessment of both quantitative and qualitative factors of a particular object (company, municipality, etc.) as well as industry and market factors.

The credit rating process begins with an application to the rating agencies by the issuer (object). The object contacts a rating agency and requests an issue of credit rating to the new debt or to the object. Documentations like <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements, the preliminary of<sup>fi</sup>cial statement, a prospectus for the debt issue, other non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial information, etc. are provided to the rating agencies. Discussions take place between the rating agency and management of the object, and a rating report is prepared by the credit analysts examining the object. Key analytical factors are discussed in the rating report. The credit analyst provides a recommendation for credit rating to a rating committee. The committee decides the credit rating to be assigned to the object. The result of this process is represented by an assignment of a rating class to the assessed object. The assignment is based on an evaluation of relevant economic, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and management factors. Finally, the rating is released to the issuer, followed by a publication of a full credit report. The assessed objects pay a fee to the rating agencies for the credit rating.

Rating agencies emphasise that the credit rating process involves consideration of <sup>fi</sup>nancial, as well as non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial, information about the object, and also considers industry and market-level factors. The precise factors, and related weights of these factors used in determining a credit rating, are not publicly disclosed by the rating agencies. As a result, credit ratings issued by different rating agencies need not to be the same. In a comparison of the ratings of Standard & Poor's and Moody's [39], it was found that in a sample of 1607 US bond ratings of investment grade, the two rating agencies differed in 836 credit ratings. In the cases where the rating classes differed, the difference was more than one rating class in 111 cases. Rating agencies should preserve independency and objectivity in their evaluation. Their assessment is widely respected by <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets.

All public credit ratings are monitored on an ongoing basis. It is common to schedule an annual review with management. Subsequent to the initial credit rating, an object may be re-rated upwards (upgrade) or downwards (downgrade) if the object or environmental circumstances change. The effect of an object being assigned a lower rather than a higher credit rating is that its riskiness is considered to be higher, and consequently the required interest yield of the bonds rises.

Credit rating can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the process of a rating class assignment to an object according to expected default probability. Rating class indicates the probability that the given object will be capable to meet its payable obligations. It is de<sup>fi</sup>ned on the rating scale. Rating classes are ranked in the rating scale according to credit risk rate. Although the precise notation used by individual rating agencies to denote the credit rating of an object varies, in each case, the credit rating is primarily denoted by a ‘letter’. Rating agencies such as Standard & Poor's (S&P's), Moody's, and Fitch belong to the most important rating agencies. Taking the rating scale of Moody's as an example, the credit ratings are broken down into 9 broad rating classes. The strongest rating class is denoted as Aaa, and the rating classes then decrease in the following order, Aa, A, Baa, Ba, B, Caa, Ca, and C. Therefore, credit ratings are typically conveyed to investors by means of a discrete, mutually exclusive, letter grade. Rating classes between Aaa and Baa (inclusive) are considered by investors to represent ‘investment grade’, with lower quality rating classes considered to represent debt issues with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant speculative (risky) characteristics (non-investment grade or ‘junk’ bonds). A ‘C’ grade represents a case where a bankruptcy petition has been <sup>fi</sup>led, or a case where the borrower is currently in default on its <sup>fi</sup>nancial obligations. Moody's applies numerical modi<sup>fi</sup>ers 1, 2, and 3 in each generic rating classi<sup>fi</sup>cation from Aa through Caa. The modi<sup>fi</sup>er 1 indicates that the obligation ranks in the higher end of its generic rating class; the modi<sup>fi</sup>er 2 indicates a midrange ranking; and the modi<sup>fi</sup>er 3 indicates a ranking in the lower end of that generic rating class.

Credit rating cuts the costs of assessed object, investors and banks. For the assessed object, good rating classes are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in lower interest rates [9], and they can represent an impetus to new business contracts. A good credit rating secures favourable credit terms too. As rating agency compares key features of the accounts, budget, etc. with benchmarks derived from assessing similar objects' operations in the country or internationally, the rating process helps the management to identify areas that need improvement. In similar structures, a stronger credit rating may often mean better <sup>fi</sup>nancial management. Credit ratings also serve as an external validation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial health.

For investors and banks, credit rating makes decision-making more effective and faster, mainly due to lower transaction and personal costs necessary for information acquisition about the business partners. Commercial banks may have the skills and willingness to assess the credit quality of an object. However, investors rarely have the specialist skills to investigate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial health and management competence of a borrower, so they tend to rely on experts (rating agencies) to do this for them.

Based on the given facts it is desirable to design such models which make it possible to simulate the credit rating process using publicly available data.

## 2.2. Municipal credit rating modelling

The speci<sup>fi</sup>c position of municipalities associated with their <sup>fi</sup>nancial management, requires the use of different input variables than for companies. Municipalities dispose, same as companies, of internal and external <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources. Internal <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources come mostly from municipal taxes, while loans and bonds represent external resources. Moreover, the share in central government taxes and grants rank among key <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources of municipalities. It is obvious that the mentioned facts in<sup>fl</sup>uence the credit rating process of municipalities. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>nancial indicators are being monitored by rating agencies, as well as other socio-economic and management information. Small data sets have been used in previous studies. This makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to get consistent results. Therefore, only convention statistical methods have been used for the modelling so far.

The use of multiple discriminant analysis (MDA) was reported by Carleton and Lerner [10] to study those variables affecting municipal credit rating. A set of four key input variables was used from which a discriminant function was generated. Although signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were obtained, only 50% of testing sample was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed correctly. The same database as in Carleton and Lerner [10] was used by Horton [31] in an attempt to improve the results. Regression analysis was employed instead of MDA. A sample of 150 municipalities, nationwide, was used as 75 of them had investment grade ratings. The study was superior to that one of [10] as 80% of testing data was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed correctly.

Regression analysis was also used by Rubin<sup>fi</sup>eld [54] in order to derive a rating index based on Moody's four highest rating classes for New England municipalities. Unlike previous studies, the issue it addressed was to determine the input variables that led to an Aaa credit rating. The Aaa rating class was the function of eight input variables. The model correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed 67% of training data. However, testing data were not used to support the conclusions.

The aim of the study by Raman [53] consisted in trying to replicate changes in either direction from Moody's A rating class (1975–1979) nationwide for thirty cities with population of 50 000 or more. MDA was applied to twelve input variables. Upgraded and downgraded rating classes were examined with several stages used to test the stability of the results. However, the results could be hardly validated due to small set of training data.

The study by Farnham and Cluff [18] questioned the results obtained in previous studies in three ways. First, some data sets were too small or were con<sup>fi</sup>ned to a single region. Thus, general inferences drawn from these studies were held to be suspect. Second, classi-<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy has varied according to the number of rating classes used. Third, the input variables used in previous studies were criticized. Attempting to overcome these weaknesses, a sample of 680 municipalities was used by Farnham and Cluff [18]. Four groups of input variables were created, namely <sup>fi</sup>nancial, debt, economic base, and administrative. Out of 680 observations, 205 served as testing data set. Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy for the testing data ranged from 62% to 68%.

In Farnham and Cluff [19] Moody's credit ratings of 976 municipalities and S&P's credit ratings of 271 municipalities were used in order to compare the credit rating criteria between the two agencies. A special form of regression analysis was designed to more accurately measure the importance of input variables than MDA and ordinal logistic regression (OLR). Only four of the total 23 input variables were represented by <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables. The remaining input variables were either socio-economic or geographical.

So far only a small effort has been done to employ arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence methods for municipal credit rating modelling. A model based on fuzzy logic was used in Ammar et al. [3]. In this case a fuzzy set with three triangle membership functions was created for each input variable. Membership functions and a rule base were designed by experts. The rule base was being modi<sup>fi</sup>ed during the learning process so that maximum classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy was obtained. However, only 30 municipalities were used as training sample to develop the model. Thus, doubts of the generalization ability of such a model rise.

As a result, only about two thirds of testing data were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed correctly by Serve [55] using three rating classes as output.

There have been several attempts made to overcome the problem concerning small data sets in the literature. One of the possibilities consists in the design of an expert system based on the knowledge acquired from the rating agencies' experts [48]. Further, it is possible to extend the training and testing set using unsupervised methods. In this case only low proportion of municipalities is labelled with rating classes a priori. Three variables (the indicator for sovereign default history, GDP per capita and a debt ratio) explained more than 80% of Moody's sub-sovereign credit ratings.

## 3. Neural networks for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation

Credit rating modelling represents a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem. The model of credit rating should simulate the decision-making process of rating agencies in order to correctly classify the objects into rating classes. The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation process is based on data matrix X that includes the values of selected input variables ${ \bf X } _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } , . . . , { \bf X } _ { \mathrm { I n } }$ and rating classes $\mathbf { \omega } _ { \mathrm { { t , j } } } { \in } \Omega$ , where $\mathrm { j } = 1 , 2 , . . . , \mathrm { q } , \Omega = \{ \mathsf { A a a } , \mathsf { A a } 1 , . . . , \mathrm { C } \}$ , for assessed objects $\mathfrak { o } _ { \mathrm { i } } \in 0 , 0 = \{ 0 _ { 1 } , 0 _ { 2 } , . . . , 0 _ { \mathrm { i } } , . . . , 0 _ { \mathrm { n } } \}$ . From a mathematical point of view, a classi<sup>fi</sup>er (machine realizing the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem) is represented as a projection F of each object $_ { 0 \mathrm { i } } \in \mathrm { O }$ into a set of rating classes $\mathbf { \omega } _ { \mathbf { { i } , j } } { \in } \Omega$ so that F:O→Ω, where Ω is a rating scale. The quality of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is usually measured with classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy CA [%] given as a proportion of correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed objects.

Neural networks (NNs) [29] are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as massively parallel processors, which tend to preserve the experimental knowledge and enable their further use. They simulate the human brain with the intent to collect the empirical evidence during the learning process, and inter-neural connections (synapses) are used to store the knowledge. An important feature of NNs, in addition to the ability of learning, is the ability to generalize the learned knowledge. Currently, there exist many structures and learning algorithms of NNs, including a number of their applications. In the economic <sup>fi</sup>eld, NNs are used primarily in problems where the variables are in non-linear relationships. An extensive overview of NNs' applications in the economic <sup>fi</sup>eld can be found in Herbrich et al. [30]. In addition to credit rating modelling, NNs have been also applied for other classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems such as corporate <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis [15], bankruptcy prediction [2], credit applicants classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [22], new products development [59], etc. Previous comparative studies have shown that NNs perform better than common statistical methods (like ordered probit models) in classi<sup>fi</sup>cation tasks. For example, in Bennell et al.[5] it is demonstrated that NNs represent a superior method for classifying sovereign credit ratings relative to ordered probit model in spite of this has been considered to be the most successful econometric approach by the previous literature. The NNs used for the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of municipal credit ratings will be introduced in this part of the paper.

## 3.1. Feed-forward neural networks

A feed-forward NN (FFNN) [29] uses neurons connected among themselves in layers. The neurons of adjoining layers are connected so that the output of one neuron is distributed into the inputs of the neurons in the following layer. As a result, the input values only move from input to hidden layers and, at the same time, from hidden to output layers. A vector $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ of input values is presented to the input layer. This pattern is then expanded (transformed) through the FFNN using synapse weights $\mathrm { w _ { p q } }$ between the p-th and the q-th neuron and activation functions f up to the outputs of the FFNN. The resulting weighted values are added together producing a weighted sum given to a transfer function. The outputs of the transfer function are distributed to the output layer. They are multiplied by a weight $\mathsf { W } _ { \mathtt { q r } } ,$ and based on the resulting weighted values, the weighted sum is put to a transfer function, which output is a value ${ \bf { \omega } } _ { \Theta , \mathrm { { j } } }$ representing a class. The objective of the learning lies in obtaining such a setting of synapse weights that the deviation (error) E between actual and target outputs of the FFNN is minimum for the given training patterns. The partial derivative of the error E, with respect to the synapse weights, represents the minimization of the error E by gradient method. Backpropagation algorithm is a standard learning algorithm of the FFNNs [29]. Since the FFNN uses the gradient method in the learning process, it is possible that the learning algorithm gets stuck in local minimum within the error function. This can be solved, for example, by adding noise to the equation for synapse weights adaptation, adding neurons, setting the learning rate, or adding momentum.

## 3.2. Radial basis function neural networks

Radial basis function NNs (RBFNNs) [29] are a popular alternative to the FFNNs. They are also good at modelling non-linear data and can be trained in one stage, rather than using an iterative process as in FFNNs, and also learn the given application quickly. The RBFNN means any kind of FFNN that uses RBF as a activation function. Using RBFNN for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is suitable, since in most cases a speci<sup>fi</sup>c group of input vectors x belongs to one of classes $\mathbf { \omega } _ { \mathbf { { i } , { j } } } \in \Omega ,$ , which are sought by RBFNN. It is therefore possible to pick a group representative, and consider the set within output of required class $\mathbf { \omega } _ { \mathrm { { t , j } } } { \in } \Omega$ its surroundings. In RBFNNs, approximation is done by functions, which in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>nal function only in the center surroundings ${ \mathsf { c } } _ { \mathrm { p } }$ of the RBF neuron and not in the whole function range. In RBFNN's learning process, it is required to set a number of centres ${ \mathsf { c } } _ { \mathrm { p } }$ of activation function $\mathtt { h } _ { \mathtt { p } } ( \mathbf { x } )$ of RBF neurons, and to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most suitable positions for RBF centres ${ \mathsf { C } } _ { \mathrm { p } } .$ Other parameters are radiuses of centres ${ \mathsf { C } } _ { \mathrm { p } } ,$ rate of activation functions $\mathrm { h } _ { \mathrm { p } } ( \mathbf { x } )$ of RBFs, and synapse weights $\mathsf { W } _ { \mathbb { q r } }$ setup between hidden and output layer.

## 3.3. Probabilistic neural networks

Probabilistic neural networks (PNNs) [56] are based on Bayes classi<sup>fi</sup>ers. They learn to approximate the probability density function of the training objects (i.e. underlying objects' distribution). The PNN consists of neurons allocated in three layers. The hidden layer has one neuron for each case in the training data set. The neuron stores the values of the input variables for the case along with the target value. When presented with the x vector of input values from the input layer, a hidden neuron computes the Euclidean distance of the test case from the neuron's center, and then applies the RBF kernel function using the sigma value. There is one neuron for each class in the class layer. The actual target class of each training object is stored with each hidden neuron. The neurons add the values for the class they represent (hence, it is a weighted vote for that class). The decision layer compares the weighted votes for each target classs and uses the largest vote to predict the target class.

## 3.4. Cascade correlation neural networks

Cascade correlation NNs (CCNNs) [16] combines two key ideas. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the cascade architecture, in which hidden units are added to the network one at a time and do not change after they have been added. The second is the learning algorithm for each new hidden neuron. The magnitude of the correlation between the new neuron's output and the residual error is maximized. Every input neuron is connected to every output neuron by a synapse with an adjustable weight $\mathrm { w } _ { \mathrm { i q } } .$ The learning algorithm begins with no hidden neurons. The direct input–output synapses are trained over the entire training set. Then any of the learning algorithms for single-layer NNs can be applied. The CCNN has three layers (input, hidden, and output layer). Each output neuron representing class ${ \bf { \omega } } _ { \Theta , \mathrm { { j } } }$ receives values from all of the input neurons (including the bias) and all of the hidden layer neurons. Each value presented to an output neuron is multiplied by a weight $\mathbf { W _ { q r } } ,$ and the resulting weighted values are added together producing a combined value.

## 3.5. GMDH polynomial neural networks

Group method of data handling (GMDH) polynomial NNs [17] are self-organizing networks, which means that the synapses between neurons in the network are not <sup>fi</sup>xed but rather are selected during training to optimize the network. The network begins with only input neurons. During the training process, neurons are selected from a pool of candidates and added to the hidden layers in the similar matter as in CCNNs. The number of layers in the network is selected automatically to produce maximum accuracy without over<sup>fi</sup>tting. Neurons in the hidden layers are selected based on their contribution to the mean squared error during the process of learning. Traditional GMDH NNs use complete quadratic polynomials of two variables as transfer functions in the neurons.

## 3.6. Support Vector Machines

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) [1], are af<sup>fi</sup>liated to NNs and create the category of so-called kernel machines. Using kernel functions SVMs are an alternative learning method for the RBFNNs and FFNNs. During the SVMs learning process, the decision boundaries

are derived directly from the data. Thus, separating margins are maximized in multidimensional feature space. This learning strategy minimizes the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors in training (testing) data $0 _ { \mathrm { t r a i n } }$ $\left( 0 _ { \mathrm { t e s t } } \right)$ . Hence, the generalization ability of SVMs differs from other methods signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, especially, when the training set $0 _ { \mathrm { t r a i n } }$ is small [33]. The main principle of SVMs consists in the creation of the decision hyperplane between classes ${ \bf { \omega } } _ { \omega }$ so that the margin between positive and negative patterns is maximized. The central point in the construction of SVMs algorithm is the inner product kernel between support vector $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { j } }$ and vector $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ from input space. Support vectors $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { j } }$ consist of a small subset of training data $0 _ { \mathrm { t r a i n } }$ extracted by the algorithm. The design of SVMs depends on the non-linear projection of the input space $\Xi$ into multidimensional space Λ, and on the construction of an optimal hyperplane. This operation is dependent on the estimation of inner product kernel referred to as kernel function k(x<sub>i</sub>,x<sub>j</sub>).

Based on the given facts, it is possible to <sup>fi</sup>nd linear separators in the q-dimensional space Λ, so that $\left( \mathbf { X } _ { \mathrm { i } } , \mathbf { X } _ { \mathrm { j } } \right)$ is replaced by kernel function k(x ,x ). Accordingly, the process of learning can be realized so that only kernel functions ${ \bf k } ( { \bf x } _ { \mathrm { i } } , { \bf x } _ { \mathrm { j } } )$ can be computed instead of full list of attributes for each data point. Evidently, the found linear separators can be transformed back into the original space Ξ. This way any non-linear boundaries between the positive and negative patterns can be obtained. Various kernel functions k(x ,x ) representing different spaces can be used for modelling, e.g. linear, polynomial, RBF and sigmoid kernel function [1].

## 4. Municipal credit rating data

In this study US municipalities (located in the State of Connecticut) will be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into rating classes $\mathbf { \omega } _ { \mathrm { { t , j } } } \in \Omega .$ . First the input variables have to be designed which are relevant for credit rating assignment. Municipal credit rating is based on the analysis of four categories of variables, namely: economic, debt, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and administrative [43], [45].

The economy, while probably the least controllable of the four credit factors, remains critical to credit analysis because the economic base is what ultimately generates the resources to repay municipal debt [43]. Economic variables include socio-economic conditions such as population, unemployment, and local economy concentration. These variables affect the revenue and expenditure of a municipal budget. Higher population entails higher municipal tax revenue. At the same time, more populated municipalities have higher spending for infrastructure and other public goods. The change in the number of inhabitants is reported as good criterion for the economic vitality of a municipality [18], [43]. Economic growth of the municipality leads to the growing number of its inhabitants. The income of population has an impact on the demand for public services. Populations with higher incomes require better public goods and services. A municipality may receive additional funds resulting from taxes and higher fees for services provided. One of the most useful statistics for determining a municipality's economic well-being is per capita and median family income. Municipalities with more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed economy and more favourable social and economic conditions are better prepared for the economic recession. Credit rating analysts also consider the credit quality and market position of a region's largest employers, and the strength and diversity of its largest taxpayers.

Debt variables include the size and structure of the debt. With every new issuance of debt, the issuer's debt position is reevaluated in order to determine the impact of the increased debt on credit quality [43]. Overlapping debt is considered to determine the overall debt burden to the taxpayers. Ratios are often used to measure both the debt of the municipality and its ability to pay off a debt service. Debt service (especially related to total revenue) is a crucial debt factor measuring the ability of the municipality to pay off the debt service from budget revenues. The debt service includes yearly interest and annuity payment. The value of debt service/total revenue above a certain level can be considered a signal of the imminent debt trap. Total debt represents a gross measure of the municipality indebted ness, i.e. the extent of the accrued debt per inhabitant.

US municipalities dispose of a high <sup>fi</sup>scal autonomy. This allows municipalities to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their revenues through local taxation and fees for municipal services. Municipal management chooses a combination of own revenue and the debt on public goods <sup>fi</sup>nancing. ## 5. Experimental results

As we know the rating classes ${ \mathfrak { o } } _ { \mathrm { i } , \mathrm { j } } \in \Omega , \Omega = \{ \operatorname { A a a } , \operatorname { A a } 1 , . . . , \operatorname { B a a } 2 \}$ , it is possible to realize the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem by supervised methods. Therefore, the supervised NNs presented previously can be applied for municipal credit rating modelling. Based on the presented facts, the model is designed for the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of objects (municipalities) $\textstyle \mathbf { 0 } _ { \mathrm { i } } \in \mathbf { 0 }$ into rating classes $\mathbf { \omega _ { \mathrm { { i , j } } } } \in \Omega .$ The modelling is realized on municipal credit rating data set with four, Ω={Aaa, Aa, A, Baa}, and nine rating classes, $\Omega = \{ \mathrm { A a a } , \mathrm { A a } 1 , . . . , \mathrm { B a a } 2 \}$

For all the models of NNs the data are pre-processed <sup>fi</sup>rst. Data preprocessing is carried out by means of data standardization. Thereby, the dependency on units is eliminated. The pre-processed data represent the inputs of the proposed structures of NNs.

NNs work more effectively if the input variables in<sup>fl</sup>uence their output [30]. Therefore, it is suitable to optimize the set of input variables before they are used as inputs of NNs. An easy method used previously [32] lies in selecting optimal sets of input variables following a step-wise procedure. One starts from a simple model, and then each remaining variable is added in the model. During this process, the model is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed if any classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy improvement is observed. The process is iterated with the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed model until no improvement is observed. Besides, there are several other more sophisticated methods addressed to realize this optimization process, e.g. principal component analysis, gain ratio, information gain, SVMs, genetic algorithms (GAs), etc. GAs have worked well recently [33]. In this way, the features of the data are removed that are irrelevant or redundant with respect to the task to be learned (feature selection process). A combination of correlation based approach [28] and GAs will be used for the feature selection in this study. The correlation based feature selection results from the hypothesis that good feature sets contain features that are highly correlated with the class, yet uncorrelated with each other (for more information see [28]). As for m initial input variables (features) there exist 2<sup>m</sup> possible subsets we usually use an exhaustive or heuristic search strategy to <sup>fi</sup>nd an optimal subset of input variables. It is more effective to use heuristic search strategies in this case as there is $2 ^ { 5 2 } = 4 . 5 \mathrm { E } + 1 5$ possible subsets. GAs represent a heuristic search strategy generating solutions to optimization problems using techniques inspired by natural evolution. The GA optimizes the set of input variables so that it evaluates the worth of a subset of variables by considering the individual predictive ability of each input variable along with the degree of redundancy between them. The parameters of the GA are set as follows: crossover probability=0.6, mutation probability=0.03, population ${ \mathsf { s i z e } } = 2 0 .$ , maximum number of generations=20. As a result only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant input variables remain in the data sets. The selected input variables for both data sets are PO, FI, and TAXR/TR.

So only three input variables are left in the data set, i.e. the size of municipality (PO), wealth of municipality (FI), and the <sup>fi</sup>scal autonomy (TAXR/TR). According to the mean values for each rating class (Table 4, Table 5), a better credit rating is linked with the wealth and <sup>fi</sup>scal autonomy of the municipality.

The experiments were realized for different settings of NNs' parameters. A pattern search algorithm [58] was used for <sup>fi</sup>nding the optimal values of these parameters. The pattern search starts at the center of the search range and makes trial steps in each direction for each parameter. As a result, the optimum values of parameters were found. In this study, all four categories of municipal credit rating are used as input variables, i.e. economic, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, debt and administrative. However, after the optimization by GA only three input variables are left in the data set, i.e. the size of municipality (PO), wealth of municipality (FI), and the <sup>fi</sup>scal autonomy (TAXR/TR). Neither the debt variables nor the balance of budget played an important role for the used data set although these variables have been held for signi<sup>fi</sup>cant by many researchers so far (e.g. [19], [53], [54], [55]). The municipal indebtedness and balance of budget are supposed to be assessed with caution as they change over time depending on election cycle, interest rates, political factors, etc. [25]. On the other hand, size and wealth of municipality, together with its <sup>fi</sup>scal autonomy represent long-term factors being stable over time.

Concerning methods, regression analysis and MDA prevail in studies on municipal credit rating modelling. As stated by Huang et al. [32], the major difference between traditional statistical methods and NNs is that statistical methods usually require researchers to impose structures to different models, such as the linearity in the multiple regression analysis, and to construct the model by estimating parameters to <sup>fi</sup>t the data or observation, while NNs allow learning the particular structure of the model from the data. As a result, the structures of the models used in statistical methods are relatively simple and easy to interpret, while models obtained by NNs are usually very complicated and hard to explain. For an example of credit scoring modelling, used model size was used by Galindo and Tamayo [22] to differentiate statistical methods from NNs. For a given training sample size, there is an optimal model size. The models used in statistical methods are usually too simple and tend to under-<sup>fi</sup>t the data while NNs generate complex models and tend to over-<sup>fi</sup>t the data. Thus, a trade-off between classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy and generalizability and interpretability of the model was observed.

The vast majority of the corporate credit rating models have used FFNNs trained with the back-propagation learning algorithm (e.g. [7]). However, numerous researchers document the disadvantages of this approach. For example, authors in Ref. [8] mention that standard FFNNs are subject to problems of local minima, and can be tedious and extremely time-consuming to build. Results can also be very sensitive to speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of learning rates, momentum and other processing elements, and there is no clear guidance on the selection of those parameters. Therefore, also alternative models of NNs are used in this study with the aim of <sup>fi</sup>nding such a structure that would be the most suitable for municipal credit rating modelling.

The comparison to prior studies is possible to realize only with the MDA method, as it was mostly used for municipal credit rating modelling. For a three-class problem, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy on testing data $\mathsf { C A } _ { \mathrm { t e s t } }$ ranges from 55% [13] to 66% [55], for four-class problem 62%-71% [18], [57], and for <sup>fi</sup>ve-class problem 46%-63% [10], [47]. In this study the results obtained for four-class problem using statistical methods (78.6% for MDA, 74.4% for LR) are slightly better than previous results, while especially PNNs (98.8%) and SVMs (96.0%) achieved signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better classi<sup>fi</sup>cation quality. For the nine-class municipal credit rating, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracies on testing data obtained by NNs are also high. The best results are achieved by PNNs (96.3%). In previous studies only in Ammar et al. [3] more rating classes were used but the obtained results were poor (53% on training data). Moreover, the PNN models classify also bad classes (Baa1, Baa2) with a high accuracy as this has been observed a problem e.g. by Huang et al. [32]. Municipal issuers are sharply skewed toward investment grade. Highly skewed distribution of municipal credit ratings shows on unbalanced data sets. The ability of a model to classify the outer classes (e.g. Aaa, Baa2) correctly shows that it is possible to handle this problem using PNNs.

As a result, PNNs proved to be a suitable tool as they outperformed other methods in classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy. According to Specht [56], the most obvious advantage of the PNN is that training is trivial and instantaneous. It can be used in real time because as soon as one pattern (municipality) representing each class has been observed, the PNN can begin to generalize to new patterns. As additional patterns are observed and stored into the network, the generalization will improve and the decision boundary can get more complex. One of the disadvantages of the PNN compared to the FFNNs is that PNN models are large due to the fact that there is one neuron for each pattern. This causes the model to run slower than FFNNs when using it to predict classes for new municipality. Therefore, unnecessary neurons were removed from the model after the model has been constructed in this study. As a result, the size of the stored model was reduced, the time required to apply the model for new patterns was reduced, and the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy of the model was improved.

So far PNNs have been applied in only a few studies in <sup>fi</sup>nance such as short-term liquidity modelling [42], stock index forecasting [11], audit reports quali<sup>fi</sup>cations [20], and corporate credit rating modelling [50]. The results obtained for corporate data in [50] have shown that PNNs are also suitable for such cases where rating classes are more numerous, i.e. $\Omega = \{ \mathrm { A a a } , \mathrm { A a } 1 , . . . , \mathrm { D } \}$

In this study the Moody's rating classes are used as the true class variable. So the question arises if the results can be generalized also for other notable rating agencies. In the used data set, 70 municipalities are labelled with a rating class from another rating agency, 52 with Standard & Poor's and 18 with Fitch rating agency. Of these, 77% belong to the same rating class (in the 4-class rating scale) and the rest of them are one-class away. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study would not be altered much by using the rating classes of another rating agencies.

The model's interpretation consists in the evaluation of input variables' effects on the results of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. However, the possibilities of the interpretation of NNs are bounded. The knowledge stored in synapse weights are represented by <sup>fi</sup>gures ranging from 0 to 1. Thus, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to interpret them in a way understandable to a user. In previous studies on credit rating modelling, this issue was addressed only by Huang et al. [32]. The simplest way to solve this problem is a step-wise procedure consisting in successive extraction of input variables and, at the same time, in monitoring its effect on classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy. There are however more sophisticated methods for this problem realization. For example, Huang et al. [32] tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the relative importance of selected <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables to the credit rating problem using Garson's measure of relative strength of inputs to the NNs. It is also possible to extract the IF-THEN rules from the NNs [26]. Interpretation capability of NNs can be improved in this way. In this study the calculation of variables' importance is performed using sensitivity analysis where the values of each variable are randomized and the effect on the quality of the model is measured. This method of calculating variable importance often is more accurate than calculating the importance from step-wise procedure, but it takes longer to compute.

## 7. Conclusion

The paper introduces the problem of municipal credit rating process. This process is presented as a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem. Prior studies are reported in order to get an overview of obtained results and input variables used. The results showed that there are several crucial problems in the credit rating modelling. First, data availability was a critical point of concern in prior studies. Suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of objects assessed by rating agencies and, at the same time, values of important input variables must be at hand when modelling credit rating. Without a large data set, the use of input variables is also limited. Next point lies in the selection of input variables as rating agencies do not publish the details on their credit rating process, and they stress out the subjectivity of the evaluation process. Further, the appropriate method has to be applied in order to model the complex relations among the input variables.

In the paper different models of NNs are proposed in order to address the presented issues. Data were collected for US municipalities, and the assessed objects were labelled by rating classes from Moody's rating agency. The selection of input variables was realized as a two-step procedure. First, the original set of input variables was proposed based on previous studies. Then a correlation-based approach with GAs was employed with the aim of reducing the original set. The input variables together with rating classes were used for training and testing NNs' models. The goal of the modelling consisted in achieving the highest classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy on testing data. The PNNs showed best results for both the four-class and the nine-class municipal credit rating problem. Moreover, the contribution of input variables was studied for each model and, at the same time, for different numbers of rating classes. This way, the gained results can be compared across all the models of NNs as the contributions of input variables show their relative importance in the models.

The results conform to prior research results [7], [32] indicating that the models of NNs based on publicly available <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial information could provide accurate classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of credit ratings. Although the rating agencies and many institutional writers emphasize the importance of subjective judgment of the analyst in determining the ratings, it appeared that a small list of input variables largely determined the rating results. This also validates that the set of variables identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study captured the most relevant information for the credit rating decision. The proposed models present an easier conception of the municipal credit rating evaluation for public administration managers, banks, investors, or rating agencies.

In future, such models should be developed which make it possible to predict future credit rating. This can be realized by the prediction of input variables signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for credit rating process and, consequently, by the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of municipalities into rating classes.
