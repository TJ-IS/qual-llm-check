# Visualizing institutional logics in sociomaterial practices

Lotta Hultin ⁎, Magnus Mähring

Stockholm School of Economics, Saltmätargatan 13-17, 113 83 Stockholm, Sweden

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 17 November 2013 Received in revised form 25 May 2014 Accepted 25 May 2014 Available online xxxx

Keywords: Sociomateriality Institutional logics Visualization artifacts Organizational change Affordances Focus of attention

## a b s t r a c t

This paper aims to deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the mutual constitution of competing institutional logics and sociomaterial entanglements by combining a sociomaterial lens with the institutional logics perspective. We present <sup>fi</sup>ndings from an interpretive, longitudinal case study at the emergency general surgery ward of a Nordic university hospital. By focusing our analysis on how sociomaterial affordances emerge through the implementation, use and continued development of digital and physical visualization boards, we show how these artifacts constitute an integral part of the operational staff's sensemaking and enactment of a new institutional logic. We make two contributions. First, we show how the perceived affordances of a technology are created from the experience of using several different technologies and how the rejection of one technology can simultaneously constitute another. Second, we show how visualization artifacts, entangled in sociomaterial practices, can shape individual focus of attention and thus facilitate the integration of a new institutional logic in operational practice.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring question in the information systems (IS) literature is how to conceptualize the relationship between the social and the material in a way that encompasses not only interactions between people and material things, but also the mutually constitutive relationship between the social and the material (Kautz & Jensen, 2013; Leonardi, 2011; Leonardi, 2013; Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Orlikowski, 2007, 2010; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013). The sociomaterial lens has proven useful in explaining how organizational routines and technologies become interwoven (Leonardi, 2011) and how the social and the material are entangled in practice (Orlikowski, 2007). However, it has not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently accounted for and linked institutional macro-structures with underlying interpretive processes through which these practices are enacted. Nor has previous research fully explored how sociomaterial practices at the micro-level emerge and develop in complex institutional contexts in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by multiple and partly con<sup>fl</sup>icting norms, beliefs and guiding rules (Battilana & D'Aunno, 2009; Lawrence & Suddaby, 2006; Meyer, Höllerer, Jancsary, & Van Leeuwen, 2013; Mignerat & Rivard, 2009). The perspective of institutional logics, emphasizing how institutional contradictions provide actors with both opportunities and constraints for agency and institutional change (Thornton, Ocasio, & Lounsbury, 2012) has the potential to address this gap in the literature.

In this paper, we present <sup>fi</sup>ndings from a longitudinal case study of the emergency general surgery ward of a Nordic university hospital. In the case, we observe how the operational staff members at the ward use visualization boards to make sense of and gradually adopt and develop work routines consistent with a new institutional logic. The new logic is manifested in the production philosophy called Lean management practices (Lean practices), in turn part of a larger institutional trend in the service and healthcare industry often described under the rubric of New Public Management (NPM) (Pollitt & Bouckaert, 2004). The Lean philosophy originates from the automotive industry and has recently become a popular method to increase productivity and quality also in the service industry and in healthcare (Radnor, Holweg, & Waring, 2012). A central work method in Lean practices, aimed to support everyday continuous improvement, is work<sup>fl</sup>ow visualization (Liker, 2004). Taking inspiration from the manufacturing sector, the general surgery ward in our study has since 2011 implemented and continuously developed two visualization boards, one physical and one digital. By studying the continuous development and enactment of these visualization boards, our overarching aim is to unravel the mechanisms underlying the mutual constitution of competing institutional logics and sociomaterial entanglements. Our main research question is: How can visualization artifacts afford and constrain organizational change in institutionally complex contexts? More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we are interested in two sub-questions:

• How do visualization artifacts become a part of the enactment of institutional logics?

• How do multiple institutional logics, enacted in sociomaterial practices, shape affordances and individual focus of attention?

In addition to shedding light on the mechanisms underlying the mutual constitution of competing institutional logics and sociomaterial entanglements, our focus on visualization artifacts responds to a recent call for research dedicated to questions of how visuality in organizations impacts individual, managerial, and, consequently, organizational attention and sensemaking (Kaplan, 2011; Locke & Lowe, 2012: Mever et al, 2013: Yakura. 2013).

Our case shows how the implementation, use and development of the digital and physical visualization boards direct the staff's attention on particular features of the operations and provide occasions for the staff to make sense of the new Lean work practices. By focusing our analysis on how affordances emerge in sociomaterial practices, we show how visual representations construct the operational reality in a way that makes the principles of the Lean logic central to the staff's efforts to develop effective work practices.

Our study makes two contributions. First, we show how the perceived affordances of a technology are created from the experience of using several different technologies and how the rejection of one technology can simultaneously constitute another. Second, we show how visualization artifacts, entangled in sociomaterial practices, can shape individual focus of attention and thus facilitate the integration of a new institutional logic in operational practice.

In addition, our study suggests that there is potential in mutually informing the research streams on institutional logics and sociomateriality. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the practice turn in IS and Organization research has inspired scholars within the sociomaterial as well as the micro-institutional foundations of organizing to go beyond traditional dualisms of subject and object, social and structure (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Kellogg, 2009; Orlikowski, 2007; Zilber, 2002). Based on the results from this study, we believe that cross-pollination between the conversation within institutional theory on the role of embedded agency at the micro-level of organizing, and the sociomateriality discourse within IS research, can lead to important new insights on the mutual constitution of technologies and human action.

In the next section, we review and discuss recent research developments within the institutional logics and sociomaterial perspectives, followed by an argument for how an increased focus on affordances, emerging in sociomaterial practices embedded in competing institutional logics, has potential to provide valuable insights both into the micro-foundations of institutional change and into sociomaterial practices. We then account for our research approach and process and for the context and setting of our case. Thereafter, we present our case of the emergency ward at the Hospital, which is followed by discussion and conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. From technological determinism to sociomateriality

The question of how technologies shape organizations has been studied by organizational theorists for more than half a century. (Barley, 1986; Leonardi & Barley, 2010; Markus & Robey, 1988; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Scott, 1990). One of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theoretical perspectives in the last 30 years is the practice perspective, which argues that as people form interpretations about technologies in their everyday work practices, certain patterns of technology use are accumulated into particular “technologies-in-practice” (Leonardi, 2013; Orlikowski, 2000). Inspired by the practice perspective, scholars have explored how the interpretive processes through which people use technologies form speci<sup>fi</sup>c patterns of technology use that become institutionalized within and across organizations (e.g. Baptista, 2009; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Davidson, 2002; Labatut, Aggeri, & Girard, 2012; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004; Vaast & Walsham, 2005; Wang & Swanson, 2007). However, critique has been voiced against the practice lens for offering a too socialized view of technology. Critics argue that the focus on social actors' interpretations and actions leave technologies as marginal actors, subject to the impulses of their users (Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Orlikowski, 2007). To steer away from an overly emphasized focus on social mechanisms in the construction of practices, a new trend has emerged in the last years discussing how every organizational practice is always bound up with materiality (Orlikowski, 2007). In an effort to reclaim the important role that technologies play in organizational life without resorting to antiquated claims of determinism, scholars have advocated a view of the social and material as entangled (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). According to this view, no practices are only social or only material but always and everywhere sociomaterial, constituting and shaping the contours and possibilities of everyday organizing (Orlikowski, 2007).

The sociomaterial lens employed in this paper is based on a relational ontology which rejects a view of the world in which individuals and objects are composed of separately attributable properties that exist in and of themselves (Orlikowski, 2010; Scott & Orlikowski, in press). This ontology privileges neither humans nor technologies (Barad, 2003; Knorr Cetina, 1997; Latour, 2005; Pickering, 1995; Schatzki, 2002), nor does it treat them as separate and distinct realities (Orlikowski, 2010: Scott & Orlikowski. in press). The notion of constitutive entanglement (Orlikowski. 2007) presumes that the distinction of humans and artifacts is analytical only and that these entities relationally entail or enact each other in practice. Continuously ongoing and constitutive entanglement results in sociomaterial assemblages, temporarily binding together a heterogeneous assembly of distributed agencies (Orlikowski, 2007).

We adhere to the view that the sociomaterial perspective is not meant to be built on one philosophical foundation only. As recently argued (Leonardi, 2013; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013), different foundations each have their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and disadvantages and the choice of which foundation to use depends on which bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the researcher <sup>fi</sup>nds most important and which drawbacks are least bothersome (Leonardi, 2013). “They are simply different and each one may be better suited for particular contextual circumstances” (Leonardi, 2013, p. 73). In our exploration of a combined theoretical approach we employ ideas that work within a relational approach to challenge the ontology of independent agents, technologies and institutions.

## 2.2. From macro to micro via institutional logics

Over the years, research within the institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld has shifted attention from the stabilizing effects of institutions, portraying human agency as primarily shaped by macro-institutional forces (see, Ocasio, 1997; Thornton, 2004), to the role of agency and everyday practices in both the reproduction and transformation of institutions (Lawrence, Suddaby, & Leca, 2011; McPherson & Sauder, 2013; Zilber,

2002). Inspired by the practice perspective in which the purpose is to go beyond this deterministic approach to collective action to understand the mutually constitutive ways in which agency is shaped by but also produces, reinforces, and changes its structural conditions (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Jarzabkowski & Seidl, 2008; Lounsbury & Crumley, 2007; Whittington, 2006), researchers have increasingly been attending to the micro-foundations of institutions (Powell & Colyvas, 2008; Zilber, 2008).

The institutional logics perspective has been at the forefront in the efforts of bridging the macro–micro divide in institutional theory (Thornton & Ocasio, 2008; Thornton et al., 2012). While the institutional logic lens traditionally has been viewed and applied as a macro theory, explaining how logics at the <sup>fi</sup>eld level in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dynamic between organizational and societal actors, there is currently a growing interest among scholars in examining the complex and mutually in<sup>fl</sup>uential relationship between local actors and institutions (McPherson & Sauder, 2013). Emphasizing how institutional contradictions provide actors with both opportunities and constraints for agency and institutional change, the work of institutional logic scholars has generated important insights concerning the micro-level building blocks of institutional theory (Dacin, Munir, & Tracey, 2010; McPherson & Sauder, 2013; Powell & Colyvas, 2008).

Institutional logic is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ‘socially constructed, historical patterns of cultural symbols and material practices, including assumptions, values, and beliefs, by which individuals and organizations provide meaning to their daily activity, organize time and space, and reproduce their lives and experiences’ (Thornton et al., 2012, p. 2). Friedland and Alford (1991) suggest two distinctive views about the relationship between individuals and institutional logics. The first view emphasizes how “institutional contradictions provide individuals and organizations with opportunities for agency and institutional change by exploring these contradictions” (Thornton et al., 2012, p. 77). The second view emphasizes constraints, positing that institutional logics shape individual focus of attention, preferences, organizational interests, and the categories and repertoires of actions to attain those interested preferences. Unlike earlier neoinstitutionalist accounts that view cognition primarily as a source of institutional constraints (e.g., DiMaggio & Powell, 1991; Zucker, 1977) institutional logics provide the cognitive and symbolic building blocks for how social actors through their social interactions transform and reproduce social and cultural structures (Thornton et al., 2012).

Our choice to apply the institutional logics perspective is motivated by three bene<sup>fi</sup>ts detected in our tentative application of the perspective and subsequently throughout the iterative process of analysis and data collection. First, our choice was motivated by our data, in which expressed opinions of informants about the newly implemented work practices and technologies revealed taken-for-granted beliefs and assumptions, seemingly rooted in a highly professionalized context potentially threatened by the logic of Lean practices. Viewed in light of the literature applying the institutional logics perspective to explain the dynamics in organizations inhabiting diverse and potentially con<sup>fl</sup>icting norms and belief systems (Battilana & Dorado, 2010; Binder, 2007; Lounsbury, 2007; Pache & Santos, 2010), we saw the opportunity of explaining the adoption of new work practices as a constitutive part of the dynamic between multiple and coexisting institutional logics. Second, we saw the opportunity of using the concepts of affordances and constraints, central to both the institutional logics and a sociomaterial perspective, to create an integrated framework enabling a deeper and more elaborate understanding on how and why affordances emerge in practice. Finally, we realized that although our study is not intended to capture long-term changes in institutionalized work practices, our application of the institutional logics perspective enabled us to create an understanding of how the global practice of Lean management is locally adopted, interpreted and translated through the enactment of visualization artifacts.

Although, institutional logics are frequently seen as encompassing both symbolic systems of meaning and related “material practices” (Friedland & Alford, 1991; Thornton & Ocasio, 2008), much of the research in this area referring to material aspects do so only in terms of structures and practices (Cloutier & Langley, 2013; Thornton et al., 2012) and not more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in terms of objects and technologies through which logics might become instantiated (Cloutier & Langley, 2013; Zilber, 2013). With a few recent exceptions (Baptista, 2009; Labatut et al., 2012; Mangan & Kelly, 2009; Raviola & Norbäck, 2013) the role of information technology, and in a broader sense materiality, has been largely ignored in the discussion of the micro-foundations of institutional change. Moreover, scholars have suggested that a greater awareness of the imbrication (Leonardi, 2011) and entanglement (Orlikowski, 2007) of materiality within institutional forms “could contribute to a richer understanding of the constitution, constraints, and affordances of institutional logics” (Cloutier & Langley, 2013, p. 364). Here, we see an opportunity for a cross-pollination between the current conversation within institutional theory and the IS discourse on sociomateriality. Next, we will elaborate on how the potential in exploring these micro-foundations from a sociomaterial perspective can be realized by the employment of the concept of affordances.

## 2.3. A relational view of affordances

The concept of affordances, initially developed in ecological psychology and originally conceptualized by Gibson (1977), has become adopted within the Information Systems literature to serve different theoretical purposes (DeSanctis, Fayard, Roach, & Jiang, 2003; Leonardi, 2011; Lindberg & Lyytinen, 2013; Markus, 2005). Several researchers have advocated this concept as a promising approach to overcome the subject–object and agency–structure oppositions that have restrained much of the research at the intersection of technology and organizations (Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Markus & Silver, 2008; Zammuto, Grif<sup>fi</sup>th, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007).

We adhere to the view of affordances as rooted in a relational ontology. In line with Stoffregen (2003), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne affordances as emergent “properties of the actor–environment system that determine what can be done (Stoffregen, 2003, p. 124).” An affordance, in this view is not a single attribute, property or functionality of the technology artifact or the actor. Rather it is a multi-faceted relational structure, realized through the enactment of several mutuality relations between the technologies and the actor (Farai & Azad. 2012: Lindberg & Lyytinen. 2013). Affordances emerge out of the constant entanglement of social and material aspects and cannot be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the dynamics of the actor or the environment. Correspondingly, constraints are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as relations between the actor and the environment, constituting emergent properties of the actor–environment system that constrain behavior (Stoffregen, 2003).

We view affordances as grounded in institutional logics present at the micro-level of organizing and, while not mechanistically determined by the dynamic of these logics, affordances are constrained, enabled and aligned by them. It is at the collusion between institutional logics and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations of speci<sup>fi</sup>c actors that affordances emerge. In other words, institutional logics specify the drivers that manifest themselves as behavior in practice and help us to understand how affordances are enacted. An actor who perceives that a given goal or intention inscribed in an institutional logic cannot be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed can become motivated to seek out conditions under which the intention can be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. This in turn, can motivate exploration of ways to develop either existing or new routines and technologies (Stoffregen, 2003).

## 2.4. Summary of theoretical framework: an integration of perspectives

In order to explore the mechanisms underlying the mutual constitution of competing institutional logics and sociomaterial entanglements, we have developed a theoretical framework combining the sociomaterial with the institutional logics perspective. The framework outlined in Fig. 1 builds on Thornton et al.'s (2012) cross-functional model and is extended to account for the process of sociomaterial entanglements. The shadowed area indicates the focus of this study. In the model, organizational actors are culturally embedded in dominant institutional logics which focus their attention on particular features of the organization and their environment, as well as shape the availability of the repertoire of organizational solutions and initiatives (Thornton et al., 2012). Availability accounts for the knowledge and information that can be used by individuals in their cognitive processes, while accessibility refers to the knowledge and information that comes to mind and is shaped by both culture and the situational context (Thornton et al., 2012). Given the availability of multiple logics, individuals have the potential for agency in choosing which of the multiple logics to rely on for social action and interaction (Friedland & Alford, 1991).

As shown in Fig. 1, individual focus of attention is shaped not only by top–down in<sup>fl</sup>uences of logics present at the <sup>fi</sup>eld level, but by bottom–up environmental stimuli (Ocasio, 2011). In Thornton's original model, the bottom–up attentional process is shaped by social actors' embeddedness within institutional logics and prior commitments and experiences which in<sup>fl</sup>uence which identities, goals and schemas will be readily accessible to attend to salient environmental stimuli (Thornton et al., 2012). From our sociomaterial perspective, embeddedness not only includes social actors but also materiality and, consequently, sociomaterial agency emerging through everyday work practices. Hence, the identities, goals and schemas available to attend to environmental stimuli are shaped in these sociomaterial practices. Besides recognizing the performative role of materiality, our modi<sup>fi</sup>ed model zooms in on the constitutive parts, i.e. intentions, affordances and constraints of the process in which agency emerges. This enables a more detailed analysis of how logics are enacted and of how a new logic gradually becomes integrated in sociomaterial practices. Affordances and constraints are central in our modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the model as they are recognized as main vehicles for driving and inhibiting change in both the institutional logics and sociomaterial perspectives; they thus serve as common denominators around which a dialogue between the two perspectives can develop, potentially contributing to a deeper understanding of how and why affordances emerge in practice. These processes at the micro-level are selected and retained through processes of cultural evolution from which institutional logics emerge at the levels of society, institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld, and organization (Thornton et al., 2012).

To summarize, our review of the literature shows that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the practice turn in IS and Organization research has shed light on the mutually constitutive ways in which agency is shaped by, but also produces, reinforces, and changes its structural conditions. Sociomateriality scholars and scholars studving the micro-institutional foundations of organizing share the interest of going bevond traditional dualisms of subject and object, social and structure. This suggests an opportunity for a closer exploration of a combined theoretical approach that taps into both the conversation within institutional theory, focusing on the role of embedded agency at the micro-level of organizing, and the sociomateriality discourse within IS research. focusing on the mutual constitution of technologies and human action. Our combined theoretical approach may also be seen as addressing calls for a fusion of institutional theory and theories based on or developed out of structuration theory (Barley & Tolbert, 1997; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001; Pinch, 2008).

## 3. Methodological approach

This study employs an interpretive, single case design. It started as an explorative study within a larger research project aimed at investigating the implementation of Lean practices in institutionally complex environments. In the initial phase of our <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at the Nordic University Hospital (a pseudonym) we noted how two visualization artifacts played central roles in the way the staff discussed, seemed to make sense of, and developed new work practices. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the unfolding developments at the emergency general surgery ward as a suitable case to study how sociomaterial entanglements emerge in continuous, dynamic intra-actions between competing institutional logics, and the research questions in this paper emerged during <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. In this section, we outline the institutional context in which the hospital operates and in which the new logic of Lean practices has recently strengthened its in<sup>fl</sup>uence. We then move on to describe the research site, our data collection and analysis process.

## 3.1. Empirical context: institutional logics in the healthcare field

The healthcare industry in developed economies and nations has for the most part of the century been managed through professional dominance (Scott, Ruef, Mendel, & Caronna, 2000). The primary institutional logics have stressed professional authority and the quality of care as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the physician (Bird, Conrad, Fremont, & Timmermans, 2010). However, the biggest trend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence professional work in the past 30 vears has been the call for greater accountability and efficiency in service delivery (Leicht Walter, Sainsaulieu, & Davies, 2009). In Western Europe, the trend has developed under the name of new public management, signi<sup>fi</sup>ed by attempts to introduce market-based decision-making practices from industry into central and local government activities (Leicht et al., 2009; O'Reilly & Reed, 2011).

For more than a decade this marketization trend has been in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the introduction of methods used in operations management, such as Lean Production, Six Sigma, and TQM. These quality models stress customer focus, continuous improvement, decentralization and teamwork, emphasizing the importance of contributions by the full range of participants in the healthcare delivery system (George, 2003; Proudlove, Moxham, & Boaden, 2008). Lean management practices have increasingly been adopted by actors in the healthcare sector and have in many cases improved patient <sup>fl</sup>ow and reduced patient queues (Lodge & Bamford, 2008; Suárez-Barraza & Ramis-Pujol, 2010). The fundamental principles of Lean practices emphasize the development of process capability with a focus on maximizing <sup>fl</sup>ow ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency rather than resource utilization (Liker, 2004; Modig & Åhlström, 2012). Lean also involves the development of learning capability through established routines for problem identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, problem solving, and solution retention which are all necessary ingredients for continual improvement (Fujimoto, 1999).

While often leading to improved quality and shorter lead times, the application of Lean practices and similar managerial approaches have often been troubled and hindered by several of the unique features of healthcare (Nembhard, Alexander, Hoff, & Ramanujam, 2009). These features include the uncertain and risky nature of the work, which inhibits experimentation required to implement innovations, and the highly hierarchical and segmented professional structure of the workforce, which creates strong professional and weak organizational identities and can hinder the collaborative learning required for mastering increasingly interdisciplinary innovations in care (Nembhard et al., 2009). These problems are aggravated by the divided authority structure in healthcare organizations and persisting views of medical care as a craft profession, aspects of the organization that traditionally have created con<sup>fl</sup>icts between organizational management and the professional workforce (Nembhard et al., 2009). Perceived con<sup>fl</sup>icts concerning, e.g., the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of quality and the purposes of improvement initiatives, have also partly been fueled by underdeveloped performance measurement and control systems.

In order to more clearly describe how a new institutional logic becomes integrated into the sociomaterial practices of the ward, we make an analytical distinction between the principles underlying the enactment of each identi<sup>fi</sup>ed logic. However, as the notion of constitutive entanglement (Orlikowski, 2007) presumes, this distinction is analytical only. In practice, logics are not <sup>fi</sup>xed entities but relationally entail or enact each other.

We base the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and depiction of institutional logics (the professional and the Lean logic) in this study on interviews with and observations of operational staff, members of the improvement team and representatives from the Strategic Services Development Unit (SSDU), corroborated with theoretical descriptions of the logics (Currie & Guah, 2007; Kitchener, 2002; Korica & Molloy, 2010; Leicht et al., 2009; Nembhard et al., 2009; Reay & Hinings, 2009; Scott et al., 2000). In Table 1, we outline characteristics associated with each logic in order to show major differences and similarities between them. We do not claim that these are the only logics of practice at the ward; they were, however the most salient at the time of our study.

## 3.2. The research site

The research site, here referred to as “The Hospital,” is a publicly owned and funded tertiary academic medical center which, after a merger in 2004, operates at two main sites in a Nordic capital. It has a budget of approx. 1 billion Euros, staff of 15,000, and an uptake area covering about 2 million inhabitants. In 2007, the hospital management initiated a long-term strategic improvement program building on Lean management principles and practices, with the aim of improving quality and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency throughout the care processes. The implementation started at the emergency (ER) department, which generates approximately 60% of all hospital admissions. The strategy was guided by goals to reduce average patient waiting time, increase patient throughput, and improve quality and safety through standardization and continuous improvement of work routines. In the daily operational work, the Lean program translates into six principles (see Table 2).

The implementation process is designed and facilitated by the hospital's Strategic Services Development Unit (SSDU). The SSDU consists of eight so-called “<sup>fl</sup>ow coaches”, responsible for training and coaching line managers and the operational “improvement teams” in the development of Lean practices. Two system developers in the SSDU bridge operations and the IT organization, with the primary purpose of facilitating development of the operational IT systems to better support adoption of Lean practices. An important part of their responsibility is the support and development of the digital visualization boards, introduced to support visualization and coordination of patient <sup>fl</sup>ow within and across wards.

We choose to focus our study on the general surgery ward, one of the three emergency surgery wards at the hospital. An overview of the emergency department and its patient <sup>fl</sup>ow is displayed in Fig. 2. We choose to focus on the general surgery ward because it is the ward that most recently started to implement Lean practices, in October 2011.

## 3.3. Study design and data collection

It has recently been argued that research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of institutional theory has largely failed to employ methodologies consistent with the need to attend to meaning systems, symbols, myths and processes by which organizations interpret their institutional environments (Suddaby, 2010; Suddaby, Elsbach, Greenwood, Meyer, & Zilber, 2010). This study responds to this call by accepting the notion that organizations engage in structured patterns of collective interpretation which involves the attachment of meaning to events and the infusion of value into organizational processes and outcomes (Daft & Weick, 1984; Suddaby, 2010). We therefore attend less to the organizational products of institutional pressures and more to the processes by which institutional pressures are understood. Focusing on perception, use and development of digital and material artifacts, our interpretive approach is in line with the interpretive tradition of artifacts and information technology studies (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1993; Yanow, 2006).

Data collection was initiated in November 2012. Retrospective data were collected for the period October 2011–November 2012 and real time developments were studied from November 2012 to June 2013. From June to September 2013 we conducted supplementary interviews and observations to deepen our understanding of certain operational routines. The advantage of retrospective data is being able to trace events over time, but there are unavoidable concerns about reliability of recall and subjective bias among informants (Golden, 1997). We therefore triangulated data by tapping into different sources of data and by complementing primary data with supplementary data (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Also, we focused in the interviews on concrete events, rather than framing our questions around general development of more abstract concepts (Miller, Cardinal, & Glick, 1997).

We adopted a combination of data collection methods: observations of daily work, interviews, participation in meetings and seminars, studies of archival materials, and continuous informal discussions with medical professionals. To create an overview of the operations and of how the implementation of Lean practices was perceived and affected the daily work routines at the emergency clinic, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author initially interviewed people from all wards at the emergency clinic as well as <sup>fl</sup>ow coaches and system developers at SSDU. When we decided to focus our study on the developments at the general surgery ward, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author initially interviewed people who had worked at the ward since before the implementation, who were members of the improvement team and could provide rich information on initiatives and challenges since the start of the implementation. To reduce the risk of capturing only a narrow set of interpretations, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author also interviewed more recently employed staff and people who were not members of the improvement team. These interviews involved representatives of all the relevant professional categories: Surgeons, Anesthesiologist, Nurse Anesthetists, Surgical Nurses and Assistant Nurses. We conducted 40 interviews, of which 22 were conducted with staff at the emergency general surgery ward. Interviews averaged one and a half hours, were open-ended, and followed a protocol that evolved with the research project (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). To begin with, the interviewee was asked to narrate his/her personal experience with respect to the implementation and continuous development of Lean practices and the development and use of the visualization boards. The interviewees were especially encouraged to elaborate on practices or functionalities of the visualization boards, including how they had worked or might still work to try to solve problems related to the boards. Throughout the iterative process of data collection and analyses, more focused questions were introduced to capture different aspects of Lean practices, certain functionalities of the visualization boards and internal problem-solving and decision-making structures. All interviews were recorded and detailed interview notes were produced.

In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author spent several days (approx. 65 h) at the ward between December 2012 and September 2013, initially participating in the operational morning meetings and observing the work at the emergency of<sup>fi</sup>ce where the operational coordinator planned the work of the teams and were staff members used to come by to check the status of the operations or chitchat with the colleagues. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author also participated in meetings with the improvement team held for 2 h every second week. These observations provided a better understanding of the structures and dynamics of daily work routines as well as common challenges faced by staff in their daily work and by the improvement team trying to drive and engages the staff in change efforts. As our understanding of historical developments, current challenges and critical aspects of the operational work routines grew, we started to focus our observations on certain routines. We used shadowing as a method to gain deeper insights into how different professional groups used the digital visualization board. Through our interviews we had identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the operational coordinator and anesthetic nurses as two of the professions who used the visualization board most frequently and thus decided to shadow these groups. The first author followed each person for half a day at two different occasions. Due to stringent patient privacy constraints, it was not possible to tape-record these observations, but copious notes were taken on a daily basis. We complemented the shadowing with observations inside the surgery rooms, studying the interactions in the work teams and particularly focused on how the “sign-out”-routine (deviation reporting routine) was performed.

Finally, we consulted all the relevant documents regarding the implementation of Lean practices and the development of visualization boards. The materials reviewed included protocols from meetings of the improvement team since the start of the implementation (225 PowerPoint slides), speci<sup>fi</sup>ed standards and role descriptions for new work routines, action plan for implementation (in total 50 written pages) and prototypes and user manuals of the digital visualization board (15 written pages). This set assisted us in mapping the implementation of Lean practices retrospectively as well as the evolution of the system in regard to features and functionality. We also used statistics of the daily operations to study how the patient <sup>fl</sup>ow and the deviation reporting routine had changed over time. Our preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ndings were shared with IT representatives and <sup>fl</sup>ow coaches at SSDU, who provided helpful comments that strengthened and elaborated on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 3.4. Data analysis

Our overall analytic approach was iterative and abductive, with the goal of building and re<sup>fi</sup>ning theory from rich case study data (Cavaye, 1996; Myers, 1997). We transferred all the interview transcripts to a spreadsheet application, which enabled us to code passages within transcripts and to sort the segments by codes. In addition to serving as a repository for regular memos as we analyzed the data, we could also log a timeline of key events and organizational changes in the excel sheets. A simple form of thematic coding was used to identify similarities and differences in actors' perspectives and to identify representative statements, recurring themes, and common terms (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). We analyzed the data as it was gathered, and returned to it repeatedly as the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork progressed. Our data analysis was an emergent process involving the interplay between data interpretation and theory (Putnam & Pacanowsky, 1983; Walsham, 2006). Our choice of the entanglement lens is a result of this iterative process in which we found the view of sociomaterial entanglement the most helpful in making sense of our data. In contrast to earlier observations of and arguments about the entanglement lens being dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply in practice (Mutch, 2013), we found that it, by allowing an analytical distinction between the social and the material (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), enabled us to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about technology and account for the development, implementation and enactment of visualization artifacts, while also allowing us to explore the ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c material-discursive practices’ (Barad, 2007: 146) that formed matter and meaning and were a productive part of the phenomena, in our case, Lean practices. In this way, the data analysis was an emergent process involving interplay between data interpretation and theory (Putnam & Pacanowsky, 1983; Walsham, 2006). We also iteratively developed a narrative description and a timeline of key events that together enabled development and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of theoretical ideas in subsequent analytical steps (Langley, 1999). The text encompassed a chronological description of events and activities, as well as an explanation of how and why the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed sequence of events and activities resulted in the outcomes observed.

To cross-check accounts we used three different means (Miles & Huberman. 1994). First, we compared interviews, underlining inconsistencies that were investigated in follow-up or successive interviews. Second, we conducted a detailed examination of the visualization artifacts, their functionality and features but also their user manuals and development logs. As showed by previous research in this area (Bechky 2003; Star & Griesemer, 1989), the stories told by the structure and characteristics of the objects themselves are important counterpoints to interviewees' stated motivations and aims. Third, the extensive documentation of discussions, decisions and activities of the improvement team since the start of the implementation enabled us to map the process of events and triangulate with the interview material.

4.3. Entanglements 1 and 2: digitally visualized work flow affords professional autonomy and facilitates work coordination

The initiative to develop a digital visualization board (see Fig. 4) was inspired by the Lean principle of work<sup>fl</sup>ow visualization and adopted from the manufacturing sector where digital boards are commonly used to support the overview of work<sup>fl</sup>ow. Boards are also installed at the orthopedic ward, pre- and post-general surgery ward, X-ray and laboratory units as well as at most wards delivering patients to the general surgery ward. Each board is designed to only show the information necessary for the staff at each ward.

The digital visualization board was installed at the general surgery ward in March 2012. The board is displayed on monitors inside and outside the surgery rooms, as well as on the intranet, enabling the staff to keep an overview of the patient <sup>fl</sup>ow and the work teams' activities from any location at the hospital. Data for all acute-noti<sup>fi</sup>ed patients in the surgical operations (including the Surgical, Orthopedic and the Oral and Maxillofacial wards) are registered in a database. This database was previously the only tool supporting the medical staff in planning the operational routines. According to the new routine, the surgeon who registers a patient for surgery decides, in consultation with the Anesthesiologist in the operational management team, the time interval (12, 24 or 72 h) within which the patient needs surgery. The visualization board then automatically presents the surgical patients, separated from the other wards' patients, in prioritized order. This has given the work teams a clearer and more focused overview of the patient <sup>fl</sup>ow. Before the implementation of the visualization board and the new routine of prioritizing the patients, the work to coordinate the work teams and the patient <sup>fl</sup>ow required more phone calls and negotiations between surgeons who claimed that their patient needed <sup>fi</sup>rst priority.

We see how the board not only mirrors the operational reality by visualizing an overview of the work<sup>fl</sup>ow, but also how it constructs the operational reality by visualizing information that was previously hidden or hard to comprehend because of its unstructured and fragmented nature. The digital board's visualization of work<sup>fl</sup>ow allows the Lean principle of <sup>fl</sup>ow ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency to acquire a visual form that shapes and informs its meaning into something concrete, understandable and possible to achieve. In other words, the visualization of work<sup>fl</sup>ow constructs the operational staff's reality in a way that broadens their' attention from resource ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (i.e., utilizing their own time in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way) to <sup>fl</sup>ow ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (i.e., utilizing their time to achieve an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fl</sup>ow of the patient) and thus helps introducing the Lean logic as a reference point for interpretation, discussions and actions in the operations.

4.4. Manual deviation reporting constrains professional autonomy and authority to define qualitative healthcare

The improvement board (see Fig. 5 for an example) was developed by SSDU and the improvement team and introduced in March 2012, at the same time as the digital visualization board. Its primary purpose was to support the routine of deviation reporting and facilitate continuous improvement of work routines. The board was developed out of a standardized template created by SSDU and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the improvement team. The board included checklists and short manuals of key work processes, post-its with noted deviations and proposed measures. According to the new standard, the staff should note down all deviations occurring during the day, bring to the afternoon meeting and put them on post-its on the whiteboard where they would serve as a basis for discussions and improvements

The daily afternoon meetings were only held for a couple of weeks and were then replaced by a weekly meeting. Most of the staff did not have time to jointly gather around the board every afternoon and argued that one weekly meeting enabled them to better see and analyze trends in the reported deviations. The improvement board was used during the weekly meetings for three months but was then discarded. There were several reasons for this. First, the staff did not experience the discussions about reported deviations as constructive or leading to quality improvement and professional learning. They felt uncomfortable if they had to report a mistake involving a colleague (since this would go against professional loyalty, respect and camaraderie) and accused if they were reported as responsible for causing a deviation. Furthermore, the whiteboard's limited functionality in storing, structuring and analyzing operational deviations and improvements over longer periods of time was not perceived to give a robust basis for problem solving related to medical tasks or improvement of related practice. Finally, the whiteboard did not afford tracking of cases, nor did it provide feedback on results of proposed measures, thus not supporting improvement of medical skills and performance. This de<sup>fi</sup>ciency was perceived to be a demotivating factor in the pursuance and development of standardized work routines.

“Once in a while a couple of post-it notes came up on the whiteboard, but no one never knew what happened to them. It was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to see what people had written and deviations were saved in thick binders that nobody knew what to do with.”/Anesthesiologist

Falling into disuse, the improvement board instead came to serve as a general noticeboard, placed on the wall in the local of<sup>fi</sup>ce behind the computers on which the operational management team prioritizes patients and displays the patient <sup>fl</sup>ow on the digital visualization board.

To understand the process of implementation, enactment and dismissal of the improvement board, we need to adopt a dynamic view on institutional logics, accepting them as temporal in character and with the ability to coexist with other con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional logics at a micro-level (Thornton et al., 2012). We also need to account for the role of social actors exposed to alternative institutional logics in the transformation of organizational and institutional structures. While SSDU and the improvement team have incorporated the Lean logic, it is not yet part of most of the staff's norms and belief systems and hence, they cannot use it to make sense of the board. Instead they draw on the professional logic, which lead them to perceive the board as useless and hence, dismiss it. The staff is able to perform the tasks and the routines prescribed and initiated by the board, but not in a way that simultaneously affords goals inscribed in the professional logic and hence, their interpretation and enactment of the board, guided by the professional logic, makes the staff perceive the board as useless and dismiss it.

4.5. Entanglements 3 and 4: digital deviation reporting affords robust data analysis and facilitates team based problem solving

According to the principles of Lean practices and the new work routines developed by the improvement teams, deviations from standardized procedures should be reported and serve as the basis for discussions, learning and improvement in the work teams. Minor deviations from work practices should be discussed and solved within the work teams, while more serious problems affecting patients or work environment should be escalated to senior managers. For the latter type of deviations, all departments at the hospital use an IT system to report and escalate deviations. The former kind of deviations has not previously been reported at the general surgery ward. Among all new work routines so far introduced to the ward, this routine turned out to be the most challenging to pursue. The <sup>fi</sup>rst challenge was related to the structural conditions of the work at the ward. Large staff groups, rotating workforces, high employee turnover and the use of temporary workers did not create the necessary continuity to establish and develop standards.

“To be able to evaluate yesterday's work, the employees who worked yesterday need to be here today.”/First Line Nurse Manager

The second challenge was the medical staff's perception of standards as undermining their professional role and intellectual challenge of their work. Some interviewees explain that following standards creates the feeling that the ultimate goal of the work is quantity. They perceive standards as a mean to measure time and that quality was secondary.

“Standards create the feeling of working in a factory, it is all about how many patients we operate and how much behind schedule we operate. I know it's not the intention of the principle but it's still the feeling it creates.”/Anesthesiologist

In addition to the challenge of making the staff follow standards, came the challenge of making them report deviations from the standardized processes. First, because the afternoon meetings had been replaced by one weekly meeting it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to remember the deviations that had occurred during the week. Second, it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to properly discuss and <sup>fi</sup>nd root causes for the reported deviations since the staff members who were present when the deviation occurred were often off duty when the meetings were held. As a consequence, the deviations were perceived as subjective descriptions and sometimes lead to irritation because people felt accused.

“It worked like a shame and blame system aimed at identifying scapegoats. It didn't feel good. You don't want to be the person picking on others for their mistakes, nor being the one accused for committing one.”/Anesthesiologist

It also made the medical staff feel that their authority to de<sup>fi</sup>ne qualitative healthcare and decide how it should be achieved was undermined, countering the values of professional expertise and autonomy:

“The reporting of deviation adds to the feeling that you, as an individual are not trusted, that someone always has to watch you and tell you what to do.”/Anesthetic nurse

The idea to develop a digital system for reporting of deviations came out of discussions within the improvement team who saw potential in using the digital display board as a reporting tool since it would allow for the accumulation of deviations over time and hence a more accurate analysis and formulation of measures. A system developer at SSDU started to develop the new feature in September 2012 and it was implemented in October 2012. According to the new standardized procedure, the teams should complete every surgery with a “sign-out”, a quick meeting in which the team reports possible deviations on a digital checklist on a computer inside the surgery room. If no deviation occurred during the surgery, this is also reported. During the weekly meeting, statistics of the most commonly reported deviations of the week are displayed and possible measures for improvements are discussed.

The digital reporting routine increased the staff's motivation to report deviations, as they perceived it as a more effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way to identify and analyze problems. If for example the team ended the surgery 1 h after schedule they had to agree on the actual underlying reason for this. This provided a better basis for constructive discussions about possible measures for improvement.

“I don't see the system as a deviation reporting system but as an implementation tool. A way to keep track of ourselves and make sure we follow the standardized work routines we have agreed upon. It is a way to <sup>fi</sup>nd the problem without having to <sup>fi</sup>nd a scapegoat. It is just a way to discuss how the operations work.”/Anesthetic nurse

Before the implementation of the digital reporting system the staff could not know if an operation was performed without deviations or if deviations had occurred but simply not had been reported. The clarifying statistics and the visualization of current state and progress over time work as encouraging feedback to keep up the work. Fig. 6 shows the number of reported deviations and the surgeries reported without deviations from the time of the implementation of the digital deviation reporting system. Apart from the summer vacation month of August and the Easter period during April, when there are many temporary staff members at the ward and Lean work standards are less consistently observed, Fig. 6 shows that the number of reported deviations as well as the number of surgeries reported without deviations have increased, indicating an understanding for and willingness among the staff to perform the new reporting routine.

The accumulated statistics of deviations also helped the staff to realize that the same deviations tend to happen over and over again and hence, are often not caused by individual mistakes, but by underlying structural reasons. Discussions became more focused on why deviations occurred. The statistics also made visible smaller deviations that previously, when there were no ways to immediately report them, were usually ignored or forgotten about.

Summarizing the development and adoption of the new reporting routine with an institutional logics lens, we see that although the medical staff primarily adopts the visualization board because it affords goals inscribed in the professional logic, they soon <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves enacting routines that support the Lean logic. It is important to note that the enactment of the Lean logic resulting from the entanglement with the visualization boards does not represent a shift from one logic to another. Rather, it exempli<sup>fi</sup>es how different institutional logics can coexist within the same organization, department, team and individual and be both mediated and gradually changed through the enactment of sociomaterial practices. As our description of the implementation of the improvement board and the digital deviation reporting routine shows, the coexistence of multiple institutional logics may lead actors to more easily form intentions to explore and develop new routines and technologies but may also hinder them from adopting routines and technologies which are not perceived to afford goals inscribed in any of the dominant logics guiding behavior.

## 4.6. Entanglements 5 and 6: visualized operational results afford feedback and continuous improvement

The most commonly reported deviation brought to the staff's attention through the new reporting system was that patients arrived for surgery insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently prepared by another ward. For example, patients were sometimes not showered, were too cold, or did not have a urinary catheter inserted. This resulted in delays, sometimes up to several hours. Several interviewees explained the importance in <sup>fi</sup>nding suf<sup>fi</sup>cient systems and routines to report deviations across wards on a daily basis:

“It makes a big difference if the catheter is already in place when the patient arrives. Every minute of delay means a lot because everyone's work is interlinked.”/Surgical nurse

“It's really important that the responsible unit is informed. Currently, we report deviations and are happy that we have established this as a practice, but we have no routines to ensure that it actually reaches the person responsible and that it results in improved work standards.”/Anesthetic nurse

The latter quote highlights the challenge of <sup>fi</sup>nding ef<sup>fi</sup>cient communication paths throughout the organization and to ensure that the problem, not only reaches the person with the resources and mandate to <sup>fi</sup>x it, but also that the staff concerned with the problem receives the relevant feedback. According to the members of the improvement team, the challenge to keep the large and rotating staff group engaged and motivated to pursue the change efforts, in particular the routine of deviation reporting, is one of their largest challenges. Once again the improvement team sees opportunities in developing the digital visualization board to give feedback on performances and thus enhance the motivation to report deviations among the staff.

In December 2012. the improvement team together with the system developer from SSDU. started to develop a digital result board as an attempt to try to increase the commitment and motivation among the staff (see Fig. 7). The board is a large touch screen showing a menu with links to key <sup>fi</sup>gures, the last week's results and deviations, and an overview of the standardized process. One link in the menu lists suggestions raised by the staff to be discussed by higher management and ongoing improvement projects led by the operational improvement team.

The screen, installed in March 2013, was placed in the coffee room where the staff gathers for the daily morning meetings. An Anesthesiologist and a member of the improvement team explained that the screen works as a reminder and an encouragement to “keep up the good work” and as a way to introduce new initiatives to the staff. For example, they have not yet started to work according to a Lean work method called PDSA (Plan-Do-Study-Act). a method that builds on the principle of continuous improvement and is intended to guide the work teams through cycles of problem-solving. However, the Anesthesiologist argues that by including it in the menu on the screen, it becomes an explicit ambition and in<sup>fl</sup>uences the staff.

“We can only work with what is visible. It's like, if it's not on the screen, it doesn't exist at all.”/ Anesthesiologist

The intention was to more deeply involve the operational staff in the continuous improvement of work routines and to encourage their effort by acknowledging good results and performances. This board was a simple kind of paper-board containing lists on which the staff can write down deviations that they might have forgotten to register during the week, suggestions for improvement or acknowledge someone who might have done an extra good job with a “golden star” (see Fig. 8).

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. The emergence of affordances in institutionally complex sociomaterial practices

Although the notion of affordances enabling new actions and triggering peoples' intentions to develop new technologies and routines has been discussed before (Leonardi. 2011). to the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to adopt an institutional logics perspective to understand the mechanisms underlying this process. The concept of affordances is key to connecting the two perspectives of institutional logics and sociomateriality since it allows for an analysis of how opportunities for action emerge in institutional complexity in which the presence of diverse intentions drives the exploration for new ways of performing routines and developing technologies which in turn, when enacted in practice, create new affordances and constraints and trigger new ideas and intentions. Our application of a relational view of affordances allows us to understand emerging sociomaterial assemblages as the continuous mutual constitution of materia artifacts, social actors and multiple institutional logics, continuously providing both opportunities and constraints for action. Hence, this study contributes to the stream of research advocating affordances as a promising approach to overcome the subject–object and agency–structure oppositions by deepening our understanding of how and why affordances emerge in institutionally complex sociomaterial practices.

Our empirical description explained how the staff's primary motivations for using the digital visualization board and the digital deviation reporting system are related to goals inscribed in the professional logic such as professional autonomy, high quality as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the physician and the use of validated work processes and methods. The staff members perceive and enact affordances because it allows them to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll goals inscribed in the professional logic. Hence, it is in the complementary fit between the opportunities for action provided by the visualization board and intentions emerging in the dynamic between multiple institutional logics which affordances are actualized. Furthermore, perceiving that a given intention cannot be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed can motivate an actor to seek out conditions under which the intention can be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (Stoffregen, 2003). This can motivate exploration of ways to either develop existing or new routines and technologies. In the case of the implementation of the digital reporting routine for example, members of the improvement team draw on their experiences of using both the improvement board and the digital visualization board. While the improvement board introduces deviation reporting as an idea and a method to improve operational work practices, the digital board, because of the staff's perception of it as affording goals inscribed in the professional logic, becomes the focus of the staff's intentions to develop a new routine. The negative experience of using the improvement board works as a common reference point for discussions about the actual purpose of deviation reporting and how the routine, and possible technological artifacts supporting the routine, can be customized and used to support the goals of the ward's operations. These needs and goals are formulated and discussed in the improvement team under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of both the Lean and the professional logic and the outcome is a digital reporting routine, affording goals inscribed in both logics.

In this regard, the rejection of the improvement board is not to be seen only as a failed implementation. Since the operational staff members draw on their experiences of using the improvement board when developing the idea of the new deviation reporting system, the constraints experienced when using the improvement board contributes to the successful development and implementation of the digita reporting feature. In other words, the rejected technology shapes the attention and intentions of the staff in ways that affect the parallel, successful implementation of the digital reporting system. While previous IS literature has largely been silent on the mutual in<sup>fl</sup>uence of parallel implementation processes, this study advances our understanding of how implementations of technological artifacts cannot be seen as separate processes; instead they are interlinked in such a way that affordances and constraints perceived in one process in<sup>fl</sup>uences the affordances, constraints and intentions formed in another. Hence, an artifact that does not become embedded in practice and thus part of the organizational infrastructure long-term, may still in<sup>fl</sup>uence actions and lead to development and use of other technologies and work routines.

We can see the same pattern emerging when the improvement team discusses the purpose and potential developments of the digital result board and draw on their experiences of using both the improvement board and the digital deviation reporting system. Thus, a contribution in this paper is to show how the perceived affordances of a technology are created from the experience of using several different technologies and how the rejection of one technology simultaneously can constitute another. While the unpredictability of organizational consequences arising from introduction of new technology has been previously highlighted (Orlikowski, 1996; Robey & Boudreau, 1999), our study shows how agency emerges in complex and non-deterministic sociomaterial entanglements in which social actors draw on previous or parallel attempts to introduce new practices and technologies.

This contribution highlights how opportunities for agency and change, provided to individuals by institutional contradictions, emerge in sociomaterial practices in which social and material agency are mutually constitutive. Although the improvement board is currently standing in a corner of the ward, not used by anyone, it is still part of the sociomaterial practice, shaping human cognitions and actions through the staffs' stored experiences of using it. This <sup>fi</sup>nding adds to the literature on sociomateriality by showing how the improvement board is not only sociomaterial while imbricated in the organizational infrastructure (Leonardi, 2013), but also after it has been rejected by the staff it is part of the sociomaterial practice (Orlikowski, 2007), shaping the staff's understanding of the Lean logic and what it means and should mean in practice. While the staff constitutes the whiteboard through their interpretation of it as useless and inadequate, the whiteboard also constitutes the staff by forming their intentions and understanding of what Lean practices means (Introna & Hayes, 2011). Only if we view materiality as integral to human experience — i.e. making sense with technology and not of technology, we can understand exactly how and why the implementation and adoption of new routines and technologies and the introduction of a new institutional logic emerge at the ward.

## 5.2. The sociomaterial integration of institutional logics

Our analysis of affordances emerging throughout the process of sociomaterial entanglements, shows how visual representations not only express or represent reality. but also assist in constructing it (Mever et al. 2013). In the sociomaterial enactment of routines, the digital visualization board and the digital deviation reporting system construct the Lean principles of <sup>fl</sup>ow ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, team-based problem-solving and continuous improvement as core principles in the operational practice. While the Lean logic has not previously challenged the professional logic as a reference point to understand and act in the operations, the visualization artifacts construct the operational reality in a way which reconstitutes the Lean principles, from alternative ways of organizing, to the most effective ways to organize operational activities to achieve the staff's professional goals. This <sup>fi</sup>nding adds to the literature on micro-level responses to and enactment of institutional complexity (Greenwood. Raynard. Kodeih. Micelotta. & Lounsbury. 2011: Johnson. Smith. & Codling, 2000; Meyer & Hammerschmid, 2006; Powell & Colyvas, 2008) by showing how a technology which is adopted based one logic and which affords goals inscribed in multiple logics, can shape focus of attention toward a second logic and facilitate the enactment of new work routines.

From a relational view, sociomateriality represents the enactment of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of activities that meld materiality with institutions, norms, discourses, and all other phenomena we typically de<sup>fi</sup>ne as “social” (Leonardi, 2013). Similar to the description of sociomateriality as a melding process, Lee (1999) uses the word “compound” to describe how technology and its organizational context blend together, having mutually transformational effects and forming a new whole that has properties different from its constituent parts. We <sup>fi</sup>nd the images of melding and compounds useful to understand how the visualization boards are entangled with Lean practices in a way that makes them inseparable. However, in order to better capture the process by which partly con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional logics are implicated in these entanglements, we would like to introduce the process of emulsi<sup>fi</sup>cation as a metaphor. An emulsi<sup>fi</sup>cation occurs when two or more liquids that are normally immiscible, such as oil and water, blend together. Usually, when we add a few drops of oil to water, it forms little circles. However much we try to mix them together, they won't bind together. Nonetheless there are substances, known as emulsi<sup>fi</sup>ers, which can perform this miracle. A typical example of an emulsion is mayonnaise. The ingredients of mayonnaise include oil, and an aqueous component, which can be either vinegar or lemon. To bind the two, egg yolk is used as an emulsi<sup>fi</sup>er, as it is rich in surfactants: molecules with two ends that attach to the water and oil molecules like glue. By adding them to the mixture and whipping vigorously, a mayonnaise is created, in which the previously separate ingredients are mixed and bound together.

In the same way that oil does not naturally mix with the vinegar, the operational practices at the general surgery ward, permeated by the dominant logic of medical professionalism do not easily mix with Lean practices, embedded in the partly contradictive NPM logic. The staff does not completely understand the value of and do not want to conform to the new standardized work routines. As a result, the Lean principles do not initially get integrated into everyday operational practices, or to use our mayonnaise metaphor, the sauce is initially curdled. However, with the implementation of the visualization boards, the Lean logic gets introduced to the practice in a way that makes a mix of the two logics possible. The visualization boards work as the emulsi<sup>fi</sup>er that attaches to the Lean and the professional logics like glue, enabling an emulsi<sup>fi</sup>cation to take place. In the same way we cannot separate the yolk once it has been added to the mavonnaise, we cannot separate the material from the social in the operational practices of the ward. The staff's perception of Lean is bound to their use of the visualization artifacts, which in turn keep reconstructing the operational reality. Of course, unlike in the case of mayonnaise, the naked eye of an observer can discern between visualization artifacts and the medical staff of the ward. However, the focus of our metaphor is not the physical world, but sociomaterial practice. In other words, we focus the process of emulsi<sup>fi</sup>cation – the binding together of immiscible phenomena through the addition of an additional “ingredient” – and this is what is achieved in our case. While the artifacts are discernible, the practice at the ward consists of an emulsi<sup>fi</sup>ed enactment of two institutional logics, bound together by the visualization artifact.

The entanglement perspective has received critique from scholars arguing that it appears to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply in practice (Battilana & Dorado, 2010; Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Pache & Santos, 2010), that it is ignoring time and temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow and hence, reduces considerations of structure and institution (Mutch, 2013), and that it neglects the broader context in which practices are situated (Mutch, 2013). Through the analysis of our data, we have found the entanglement lens valuable to understand and explain the process of development, implementation and use of the various visualization artifacts in a way that answers to this critique. Similar to the way that the imbrication metaphor (Leonardi, 2011) allows us to explain the interweaving of human and material agency over time and to recognize how the products of prior imbrications (e.g., a routine or a technology) lay the groundwork for continued organizing (Leonardi, 2011), our process of entanglements helps us to understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of new technologies over time with an emphasis put on the mutual constitution of the material and its multifaceted institutional context. However, instead of, as in the imbrication view, maintaining the distinction between human and material agencies and studying Lean as a set of interacting principles, routines and artifacts, the entanglement view allows us to describe how the phenomena of Lean practices emerges as a sociomaterial practice in which the meaning of Lean cannot be ascribed to any separate routine or technological feature but is continuously formed in the mutual constitution of the social and the material. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t of this is that it enables us to view the interpretation and enactment of new work practices as a distributed sensemaking process in which human agency does not just respond to material agency but in which human and material agency mutually constitute action and responses through intra-action (Barad, 2003). In response to critique that an entanglement lens leads research to “overlook how and why phenomena get put into relationship with each other, and, consequently, how their relationship might change phenomena other than themselves” (Leonardi, 2013, p. 67), our study shows how the entanglement lens can be valuable in explaining how the adoption of new technologies over time create and continuously change the way a phenomena, in this case Lean practices, emerge and develop.

While the institutional logics perspective acknowledges individual agency as a driving force in micro-institutional change, scholars applying the perspective have not yet considered how opportunities for agency and change are distributed and emerge in sociomaterial practices in which social and material agencies are inseparable and mutually constitutive. The application of a sociomaterial perspective enables us to see how the agency manifested in the staff's actions is not separable from the technology capable of shaping how they understand and act in the operations. In other words, the technology is acting with the staff and meanings and materialities are enacted together in everyday practices (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Introna & Hayes, 2011). Hence, this study adds to the literature on the microfoundations of institutional logics by showing how an integration of a sociomaterial view on agency advances our understanding of how capacities for action are relational, distributed and enacted through ongoing sociomaterial practices.

## 6. Conclusions

This article contributes to the cross-pollination between the current conversation within institutional theory, focusing on the role of agency in driving institutional change at the micro-level of organizing, with the sociomateriality discourse within IS research, focusing on the mutual constitution of technologies and human action. Our theoretical approach allows us to explore the intra-relations between institutional logics, technologies and human action, and their dynamics over time, as well as attend to the micro-foundations of institutional logics, allowing for the exploration of meanings and practices as dynamic and contested sociomaterial phenomena. Whether the banner is New Public Management, Lean or some other management concept, our study shows how sociomateriality is inseparable from these constructions, their implementation, and their ability to co-exist with other, partly con<sup>fl</sup>icting ideas. While previous studies have used an analytical language of separateness and focused on processes of translation between global ideas and context, this article emphasizes how these management concepts are always sociomaterial enactments and how the exploration of them requires concepts capable of acknowledging the fusion of the conceptual and the material that constitute these concepts (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). By demonstrating how logics enter into the sociomaterial activities of actors, our work opens the “black box” of the workings of logics on the ground, shedding light on the underlying mechanisms of the mutual constitution of competing institutional logics and sociomaterial entanglements.
