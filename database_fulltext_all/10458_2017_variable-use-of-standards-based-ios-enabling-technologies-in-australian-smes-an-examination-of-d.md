# Variable use of standards-based IOS enabling technologies in Australian SMEs: an examination of deliberate and emergent decision making processes

Damien Power<sup>1</sup> and Richard L. Gruner<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management and Marketing, The University of Melbourne, Parkville, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Department of Marketing, UWA Business School, 35 Stirling Highway, Crawley, WA 6009, Australia

Correspondence: Richard L. Gruner, Department of Marketing, UWA Business School, 35 Stirling Highway, Crawley, WA 6009, Australia. Tel: +61 6488 5876; Fax: +61 6488 1004 E-mail: richard.gruner@uwa.edu.au

## Abstract

Use of inter-organizational systems (IOS) is widely recognized as pivotal to organizational success. However, the nature of decision making processes regarding the adoption and use of IOS-enabling technologies has received little research attention. The authors explore approaches to decision making relating to SMEs’ use of these technologies and outline the drivers and implications of such decision making. Data were collected through two crosssectional surveys and multiple case studies. The surveys allowed for the identification of two groups of three SMEs each—the first group increased and the second group decreased the use of IOS-enabling technologies over time. This identification provided the context of strategic change or transition in the use of these technologies over time. The study’s results challenge widely held assumptions about the performance benefits of higher-level, deliberate planning over functional-level, emergent decision making regarding SMEs’ technological choices. Results also show that SME managers mainly use flexible IOS technology adoption and implementation strategies to promote organizational performance. Further, two main factors drive flexible decision making: a lack of managerial power and a lack of financial resources. Overall, the study offers insights into the link between IOSenabling technology use and organizational strategy, and advances research concerning the contingencies influencing SMEs’ decision making in this context. ## Introduction

The decision making processes of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) with respect to the adoption and implementation of technologies for inter-organizational systems (IOS enabling technologies) are central to the success of inter-firm collaborations (Levy & Powell, 2000; Gaur et al, 2011). Conventional wisdom dictates that failing to engage in thoughtful, deliberate decision making – and then sticking to decisions – inevitably compromises organizational performance. Or does it? Research provides little empirical evidence as to how decisions are made within SMEs regarding the adoption of IOS enabling technologies, including standardsbased technologies, and offers divergent perspectives on the performance benefits of opposing approaches.

On the one hand, findings point toward SMEs’ need to overcome limitations associated with smaller businesses by developing and following well-defined and carefully planned IOS strategies (Hagmann & McCahon, 1993; Levy et al, 2001). Researchers note that ‘‘the current literature [emphasizes] a deterministic or deliberate approach to managing the supply network…where a firm is expected to control supply network activities by planning and control ling them through inter-organizational measurements facilitated by information technology’’ (Choi et al, 2001, p. 352). We refer to this approach as deliberate, higher-leve strategic decision making, which is characterized by ‘‘a rational process of deliberate calculation and analysis, designed to maximize long-term advantage’’ (Whittington, 1993, p. 3). This perspective is supported by the information systems (IS) alignment literature, which sees the deliberate and planned development of strategy as a precursor to a tightly coupled alignment between organizations’ strategies and IS investment decisions (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1999; Hussin et al, 2002).

On the other hand, some studies laud SMEs’ ability to react swiftly and adjust to changing circumstances instead of rigidly following predetermined plans (Benbasat & Dexter, 1995; Kuan & Chau, 2001). We refer to this reactive approach as emergent, functional-level strategic decision making because it is characterized by adaptation to changing circumstances, where plans are typically regarded as adjustable in light of unpredictable business environments (Williamson, 1991; Overby et al, 2006). This approach is also supported by the IS alignment literature, which regards the ad hoc and emergent development of strategy – in other words, functional-level decision making – to be a precursor to a loosely coupled alignment between organizations’ strategies and IS investment decisions (Chan & Reich, 2007). Many similar studies promote the ability of such investments to increase organizational flexibility (e.g., Overby et al, 2006; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). However, while these works highlight flexible operations as a critical foundation for performance, most do not specifically focus on managers’ flexibility in their decision making processes.

There is theoretical support for the notion that insights into decision making processes are fundamental to understanding the types of technology investments that support organizational objectives (Boonstra, 2003). Practical examples of successful adoption abound too, even in an inter-organizational context and for SMEs. For instance, when Shun Sang (an authorized distributor for Procter and Gamble) upgraded its IOS, it resulted in myriad benefits including shorter lead-time, improved customer satisfaction, and more efficient inventory operations for the SME (Yen, 2003). Further, many industry segments such as trucking consist of small companies that would, without IOS enabling technologies, fail to operate efficiently and achieve economies of scale (Wise & Morrison, 2000). Nevertheless, the issue of how these decisions are made within firms remains largely under-researched, particularly in an interorganizational context and for SMEs. Research is particularly lacking for firms that have changed strategic direction – toward the adoption of more or less IOS enabling technologies over time. Ultimately, the way SMEs decide whether to adopt or even discard these technologies over time has implications for their strategic technology alignment and performance. To investigate this important unresolved issue, we examine the following research questions: How are decisions made within SMEs regarding the adoption and use of standards-based IOS enabling technology over time? What are the implications of SME managers’ decision making process for their (a) IOS technology alignment and (b) organizational performance?

We explore data that support deliberate higher-level or emergent functional-level approaches to decision making concerning the use of standards-based IOS enabling technologies. Against this background, we also investigate whether any patterns suggest that SMEs use an atypical and previously unexamined decision process in this context. The first stage of this research involved a quantitative study. Using the same measures, we collected survey data in 2001 and again in 2011 within the same firms. The results allowed us to draw a trajectory between two data points and form two groups of SMEs: (a) those that evidenced more implementation of standards-based IOS enabling technologies within the firm and across the supply chain in 2011 compared to 2001, and (b) those that evidenced less implementation in 2011 compared to 2001. The exploration of firms that adopted more or less of these technologies over time provided a context of strategic change or transition for our qualitative exploratory study. This context was important, as our intention was to investigate how these firms approached this transition strategically – whether in a deliberate and structured way or as an unstructured and emergent process. Further, this sampling technique led to a more diverse group of firms for our subsequent in-depth qualitative study and allowed us to avoid a pro-adopter bias, which refers to studies’ tendency to neglect firms that have stopped using specific technologies over time (Jeyaraj et al, 2006; Power & Gruner, 2015). To allow for triangulation and cross-verification of findings, we conducted seven case studies – three within each group of SMEs – and one within a large IT solution provider to these firms.

Our case studies show that rather than following longerterm, higher-level strategic plans, SME managers often make decisions regarding technology standards by focusing on short-term performance benefits for their own firm rather than the supply chain as a whole. In response to environmental demands, the SMEs in our study were uniformly dynamic and flexible in their use over time of these technologies. This reaction is in line with an emergent, functional-level decision process, which implies ‘‘learning what works – taking one action at a time in search for that viable pattern or consistency’’ (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985, p. 271). The Australian SMEs comprising our sample often indicated that broader organizational performance goals were guiding their transition to altered levels of investment in these technologies (which is indicative of some level of deliberate decision making). However, our observations showed that when these firms increased or decreased investment in these technologies, the main pattern of decision making was emergent at a functional level, resulting in a typically more loosely coupled IOS alignment (Chan, 2002; Peak et al, 2005).

A key contribution of this study is further insight into the nature and implications of decision processes within SMEs during a strategic transition toward the use of more or less standards-based IOS enabling technologies over time. Our study contributes to a more nuanced view of decision making in this context and supports the notion that such decisions are fundamental in connecting firms and supporting inter-organizational processes (De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013; Grant & Tan, 2013). Importantly, the findings challenge widely held assumptions about the performance benefits of higher-level, deliberate planning over functional-level, emergent decision making and point toward the pervasiveness of loosely coupled IOS alignments. These results broadly support and extend previous research that found implementation of such technologies to be guided by managers’ perception of their ability to achieve shortterm organizational performance for their own firm rather than benefitting the supply chain as a whole (e.g., Power & Gruner, 2016). Other studies found a link between the use of similar technologies and organizational flexibility (e.g., Overby et al, 2006). This study also advances research concerned with the contingencies that determine standards-based IOS enabling technology adoption and implementation within SMEs and could therefore facilitate organizational learning in other contexts and in relation to the use of similar technologies (Iacovou et al, 1995). Managers can benefit from the study’s insights into the implications of how they decide about the extent of use of these and other related technologies over time. Results suggest that these decision making processes shape a firm’s strategy, which in turn can influence the organizationa performance managers typically hope to generate from investments of this nature.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we present the research background in terms of definitions, a literature review, and a description of what motivated this study. We then describe our research design for both the qualitative and quantitative studies, after which we present our empirical findings. Subsequently, we discuss our findings in light of our research questions and provide implications for theory and practice. We conclude with limitations and suggestions for future research.

## Background

## Definitions

SMEs We define SMEs as businesses that are independently owned and operated and that have no more than 200 employees. SMEs constitute the backbone of developed economies and are an integral part of almost every supply chain (Kuan & Chau, 2001). As a result, IOS-related decision making strategies within SMEs have potentially important consequences for trading partners. We refer to trading partners as supply chain collaborators of all sizes. These collaborators include suppliers for upstream, but also customers for downstream activities. Suppliers and customers are highly interdependent, and information flows between them can be crucial in determining a supply chain’s effectiveness (Spekman et al, 1998). We focus on interrather than intra-firm systems because SMEs typically have flatter, simpler structures with close proximity of all staff, and they usually rely less on investment in internal information systems than bigger firms (Cragg et al, 2011).

IOS enabling technology decision making We define IOS enabling technology decision making as a course of action (process or strategy) in implementing standards-based IOS enabling technologies for the purpose of interacting with trading partners. Modifications of existing IOS (e.g., as a result of use of these enabling technologies) fall within this definition and the scope of our research. We broadly define IOS as an interconnection between sending and receiving systems aimed at creating, storing, and transmitting information between trading partners (Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2011; De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013). The IOS enabling technologies of interest are those based on the GS1 standards and in this study administered by GS1 Australia. We measure and explore how GS1 standardsbased IOS enabling technologies have been used over time. These technologies include GS1 standard barcodes, GS1 standard product numbers, electronic data interchange (EDI), extensible markup language (XML), radio-frequency identification (RFID), and data synchronization (GS1 net). GS1 Australia is a nonprofit association that is part of GS1, a global federation of organizations with over 1 million member firms in 102 countries that is dedicated to developing, implementing, and promoting technologies to improve supply chain management worldwide. Firms of all sizes use the GS1 standards-based technologies explored in this study.

The main purpose of the GS1 standards is to allow trading partners to exchange data electronically and to strengthen the alignment between a firm’s strategy and its IOS investment decisions. These IOS are typically dependent on technologies based on stable global standards. Stability of the basic characteristics of these technologies is particularly important to this study given that we assess change in technology use over a ten-year period. This constancy helped us to control for rapid technological changes, which present a hurdle for the successful alignment of strategy with IS investment decisions (Van Der Zee & De Jong, 1999).

IOS alignment Conventional definitions of IS alignment emphasize ‘‘fit’’ between IS and business strategy (Hussin et al, 2002) or ‘‘harmony’’ and ‘‘congruence’’ between goals and IS use (Chan & Reich, 2007). Descriptions of IS alignment commonly hold that decisions on hardware and software have long-term consequences for an organization and that tying them to current and/or future plans is paramount for their success, particularly in an interorganizational context. Throughout the paper, we employ the terms IS alignment and IOS alignment to denote different phenomena. IS alignment refers to the alignment of IS and strategy from an internal firm perspective, whereas IOS alignment refers to firms’ alignment between their strategy and their decisions concerning the adoption and implementation of technologies that enable collaboration between trading partners.

Organizational performance The definition we use for organizational performance is the business value derived from IOS enabling technology decision making. Investment in IS has become associated with supply chain success as trading partners increasingly use IS to become more interconnected (Zhou & Benton, 2007; Huo et al, 2015). Researchers have identified a relationship between IS use and business value, which is typically captured through financial performance measures such as revenue growth and return on equity (e.g., Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012; Mithas et al, 2012; Quaadgras et al, 2014). Research has also identified that the value of IS adoption and use can manifest itself in other ways including productivity improvements, support of business growth, and innovation performance (Kohli & Grover, 2008; Chen et al, 2015). The technologies of interest to this study are fundamental to the connection and integration of IS between trading partners and are therefore seen to further promote these potential benefits. By extension, the process by which decisions are made to increase or decrease use of these technologies can influence performance outcomes. Thus, throughout the paper, we refer to organizational performance as a broad conceptualization of general business value and strategic advantage that result from decision making processes within SMEs related to the use of these technologies.

## Literature review and study motivation

Trading partners’ adoption and implementation of IOS has evoked widespread interest in the literature, as these are crucial in building inter-firm connections and efficient supply chains (Rai et al, 2012; De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013; Tafti et al, 2013). Both research and anecdota evidence suggest that use of IS within firms can be central in creating boundary-spanning processes and optimizing the efficiency and accuracy of supply chain transactions (Tafti et al, 2013). To obtain the maximum overall performance benefits from a supply chain, trading partners small and large can leverage their available internal IS capabilities (Archer et al, 2008) by connecting systems and integrating processes by deploying IOS. However, the literature offers little grounded insight into the nature of IOS decision making processes in relation to specific enabling technologies such as those based on GS1 standards, or in relation to integrating IS between firms, particularly for SMEs. Because the relationship between IS and a firm’s strategy is less complex and is relatively easy to study, the unit of analysis for most IS studies is single firms and inter-firm systems integration rather than supply chains (e.g., Boonstra, 2003). As a result, few scholars have studied how decision processes differ between an intra- and inter-organizational context, and how these processes relate to IOS use. IOS decisions relate to coordinating channel partners and business processes and require a commitment from involved partners (Seggie et al, 2006; Quaadgras et al, 2014). These IOS decisions need to consider the extent to which systems are compatible with those of channel partners, and SMEs often rely on third parties such as GS1 Australia or an IT solution provider to coordinate this process (Wu et al, 2006). Consequently, we acknowledge that IOS decision processes are likely to differ from IS decision making processes. Further, most research fails to explore firms that have discontinued their use of various IS or IOS – a phenomenon referred to as the pro-adopter bias (Jeyaraj et al, 2006). Having said this, there is a strong body of literature in the IS area that covers decision making, strategic choice options, and alignment that we will use to provide context, motivation, and precedent for this study.

Importantly, a review of the IS literature also reveals no consensus as to what IS decision making strategy organizations should follow, as a wide range of possible recommendations surfaces. Although we are aware that a simple dichotomy may not adequately capture the nuances of a complex organizational phenomenon such as IS adoption decisions, the literature falls broadly into two perspectives: (1) studies that focus on the performance benefits of deliberate, higher-level IS decision making that leads to tightly coupled IS alignment, and (2) studies that focus on the performance benefits of emergent, functional-level IS decision making that leads to loosely coupled IS alignment. In Appendix C, we provide an overview of selected empirical works on both deliberate and emergent IS decision making. In the following section, we describe the two perspectives and follow with an overview of the related literature on IS and organizational flexibility.

Deliberate IS decision making The first research stream comprises works that focus on the organizational performance benefits of deliberate IS decision making. According to this perspective, profitability is a business’s ultimate goal, with rational long-term planning and management commitment supplying the means to achieve this goal (Stoel & Muhanna, 2009; Quaadgras et al, 2014). This approach to strategy-making is largely top-down, planned, long-term, and rational. A widely cited definition of strategy incorporates the characteristics of this perspective: ‘‘strategy is the determination of the basic, long-term goals and objectives of an enterprise and the adoption of courses of action and the allocation of resources necessary to those goals’’ (Chandler, 1962, p. 13). Strategies are deliberate in that they represent ‘‘a pattern [in a stream of decisions] intended before being realized’’ (Mintzberg, 1979, p. 1). According to this view, decision making is a controlled, conscious thought process. Managers who follow this approach typically ‘‘rigorously evaluate their firm’s current strengths and weaknesses, internal resources and capabilities, and external opportunities and threats’’ (Collins, 2016, p. 66) to increase the performance benefits resulting from their IS decision making process.

Many arguments support SMEs’ adoption of this process in an IOS adoption and use context. Given the less hierarchical structure of SMEs, fewer decision makers would be involved in conceiving an IOS strategy. Further, scholars have argued that to overcome the risks and demands of running their businesses, managers of SMEs must develop and follow a well-defined, planned strategy to reap the potential benefits of these systems (Hagmann & McCahon, 1993). ‘‘SMEs that plan ahead and manage change are [perceived as] more likely to be successful in managing growth’’ (Levy & Powell, 2000, p. 64). This view is supported by the IS alignment literature, which considers the deliberate and planned development of strategy to be a precursor to a tightly coupled alignment between strategies and IS investment decisions (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1999; Cragg et al, 2002; Hussin et al, 2002).

Emergent IS decision making The second research stream comprises works that focus on the organizational performance benefits of emergent IS decision processes. This view takes firms’ vagaries into account and places less confidence in managers’ ability to act rationally and devise long-term plans to ensure a competitive advantage (Whittington, 1993). Instead, managers are merely optimizers in constantly evolving environments, where ad hoc decisions aimed at environmental fit over the long term take precedence over intentional managerial decision making processes. Indeed, some research has found deliberate strategic processes to be counterproductive. The ‘‘construction of grand long-term strategies may be so much vain distraction; managers would do much better to get down to the modest business of making sure that what they do now is done as efficiently as possible’’ (Whittington, 1993, p. 22). Thus, strategy is ‘‘emergent,’’ which implies learning what works and acting sequentially. Particularly for SMEs, the assumptions of a higher-level approach to the adoption and implementation of IS have been challenged, as SMEs tend to lack not only the financial and temporal resources but also the skills and experience they need to adopt and exploit various IS (Iacovou et al, 1995; Levy et al, 2001). It could be expected that a similar argument could be made for decisions related to IOS enabling technology use.

IS and organizational flexibility According to research focused on emergent decision making, IS decision making is not a matter of detached and deliberate planning, but is rather an agile tool that helps firms to achieve organizational performance, especially in increasingly competitive business environments (Chen et al, 2014). A significant related body of literature emphasizes the importance of a firm’s ability to cope with often rapid and uncertain changes (van Oosterhout et al, 2006). These works typically highlight endogenous features of IS standards as enablers of organizational flexibility and ultimately performance (for an overview, see Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). For example, studies have found that IS infrastructure capability enables strategic agility (Weill et al, 2002; Overby et al, 2006; Chen et al, 2014). In this context, flexibility (or agility) is usually defined as a firm’s ‘‘capability to deal with changes that often arise unexpectedly in business environments via rapid and innovative responses’’ (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011, p. 933). While this type of agility highlights flexible operations as a critical foundation for performance, it does not specifically focus on managers’ continuous sense-making processes that drive their decisions (Weick, 1995). In the context of IOS decisions, such agility might be promoted if there is a high degree of collaboration between trading partners; however, this agility could be mitigated by the uncertainty associated with many individual supply chain actors that tend to make short-term decisions based on an emergent paradigm.

Our study seeks a deeper understanding of decision making processes within SMEs concerning their use of standards-based IOS enabling technologies and their impact on IOS alignment and organizational performance, specifically for SMEs that have changed their use of these technologies over time. In addition, we explore the effect of organizational flexibility on these relationships. To achieve this goal, we adopted a mixed methods research design, the details of which we outline in the following section.

## Research design

## Rationale for a mixed methods approach

Social Sciences research has consistently extolled the use of multiple methods of data collection (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), and IS and related research is no exception (e.g., Gable, 1994). Different kinds of data, particularly emerging from qualitative and quantitative methods, provide a broader coverage that may result in a fuller picture of the research problem and offer insights into phenomena that cannot be fully understood using only one type of method (Venkatesh et al, 2013). Further, a mixed methods approach can have a synergistic effect, whereby the results from one method help develop the form the other method takes. We therefore adopted a mixed methods approach to address our research questions (Creswell, 2003). As a first step, a quantitative survey study allowed us to identify SMEs that increased and decreased their use of GS1 standards-based IOS enabling technologies over time.

The decision to increase or decrease use of these technologies is an important strategic choice for managers (Furneaux & Wade, 2011; Power & Gruner, 2015). We explored our research questions by identifying a diverse sample of SMEs that either increased or decreased their use of GS1 standards-based technologies over time. In so doing, we not only provided the context of change/transition for our qualitative, exploratory study, but we also avoided pro-adopter bias, which refers to the failure to study firms that have discontinued their use of various IS (Jeyaraj et al, 2006; van Donk, 2008). We conducted seven case studies, three within each group of SMEs and one of a large IT solution provider. This path enabled us to sample case studies from two opposing types, which in turn facilitated our observations of contrasting patterns in the data and increased the validity of our findings (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). We refer to analogous previous study designs in this context (see Power & Gruner, 2015, 2016).

Accordingly, in 2012 and 2013, we inductively collected data and conducted theory-building case studies of six SMEs (Bluhm et al, 2011). To provide triangulation and obtain a more complete picture of IOS enabling technology decision processes, we carried out an additional case study at an IT solution provider – an appropriate step since to compensate for their lack of IS expertise and experience many SMEs use external providers to assist with advice and implementation services (Bloomfield & Danieli, 1995). The case study method allowed us to draw from the world of practice and the experiences of managers, ‘‘rather than from abstract derivations of hypothetical formulations’’ (Corley & Gioia, 2011, p. 22).

## Quantitative phase: surveys

In the quantitative phase, we collected and analyzed data from two cross-sectional surveys, conducted in 2001 and 2011, within the same 62 Australian firms. By comparing responses to the same set of questions in both time periods, we were able to identify the firms where measurable changes in GS1 standards-based IOS enabling technologies use occurred over time. We explain the procedure in greater detail below.

Data collection From a survey conducted in 2001 of 3356 members of GS1 Australia, we received 553 responses for a response rate of 16.5%. After exclusion of sole traders and other inappropriate members, the sample comprised a randomly selected subset representing approximately one-third of the full membership of GS1 Australia (at that time known as EAN Australia and having a membership of approximately 11,000). The survey was distributed by mail as an enclosure in GS1’s quarterly newsletter, and members were notified regarding the content, aims, and objectives of the survey by e-mail. Non-respondents were surveyed to test for significant differences between respondents and non-respondents based on two variables: primary business activity (industry) and firm size (number of employees). 1707 randomly selected non-respondent firms were surveyed by phone over a two week period. Objective data relevant to these two variables supplied by GS1 were used to supplement responses. Chi-square tests indicated that the two groups did not differ significantly on the two variables, giving confidence that the 553 respondent members were representative of the total membership.

In 2011, we asked 136 respondents to the 2001 survey to answer a subset of the same questions they had responded to in 2001. These 136 firms were a subset of 281 respondents to the earlier survey who had indicated their willingness to be included in any future research (i.e., they identified themselves and signed a consent form when the original survey was conducted). By the time of the second survey in 2011, only 136 of the original 281 firms were still in business and/or still members of GS1 and using these technologies. Of these, 62 firms (46%) responded, providing 62 matching responses in both time periods (see Appendix B for a demographic breakdown of respondents). The database used to identify appropriate respondents within GS1 member firms directed the questionnaire to the ‘‘GS1 coordinator’’ within each firm. Owing to the 10-year interval between the two surveys, we did not expect that in all cases the same person would be holding this position at both T1 and T2. Further, owing to the ethics guidelines under which the surveys were conducted, knowing whether the same person responded in T1 and T2 was not possible. To assess whether the data were biased owing to respondents’ different understanding of survey questions and their individual characteristics (i.e., if the respondent was not the same in T1 and T2), we performed a factor analysis on both the T1 and T2 data (Table 1). In both data sets, the items converged to form a construct at acceptable levels of validity and reliability.

Extent of IOS enabling technology implementation We measured the extent of implementation of the technologies directly through nine questions, as shown in Table 1. As a factor analysis established the content validity and reliability of these items in both 2001 and 2011, we used the same questions to classify groups in both time periods. These questions were derived from the definitions (see Appendix A) for generic levels of implementation developed by the GS1 organization (EAN, 1998) and were drafted in consultation with managers of GS1 Australia and tested within the membership of the organization for face validity. Each question was drafted such that high scores would reflect activities consistent with high (strategic) use of these technologies.

Respondent firms were classified as having low (reactive), medium (tactical), or high (strategic) implementation, depending on the averages of the scores they recorded for each question in both 2001 (T1) and 2011 (T2). For the summated scale comprising all nine items, a respondent with a mean score of less than 2 was classified as low. To be classified as high, a respondent had to have a mean score C3. The medium group comprised those who did not fit into either category. Table 2 provides a cross-tabulation of the groups for T1 and T2 and the movement between the groups. Firms that have moved off the diagonal (as indicated by the right/left arrows in the table) represent those who over time either increased (16 firms) or decreased implementation (9 firms). Firms located on the diagonal are firms for whom the extent of implementation had not changed. From these two groups, we drew our case companies for SMEs using the technologies. The case study for the IT solution provider was drawn from a sample of firms providing these services to the GS1 Australia membership to allow some triangulation and cross-verification of the findings (i.e., the solution provider did not participate in these surveys).

## Qualitative phase: multiple case studies

Following our two cross-sectional quantitative studies, we conducted an interpretive investigation through multiple case studies to address our research questions. Scholars increasingly advocate case studies to pursue management knowledge, including complex operations and IS-related phenomena (e.g., Walsham, 1995).

We collected most data in 2012 and 2013 through semi-structured interviews that included open-ended questions. We kept the interview protocol as general as possible without specific reference to the IOS enabling technology decision making process or its impact on IOS alignment. The role of the interviewer and the protocol was to facilitate rather than guide the interview, and the conversation was allowed to proceed at its own pace to ensure a natural flow of narration (Lockstroem et $^ { a l , }$

2010). This approach was critical to let themes emerge and to minimize any potential interviewer bias, although for triangulation purposes we asked respondents more or less the same questions. The interviews also offered the opportunity to inquire about, and thus control for, the intervening years between the two crosssectional studies. We chose a five-year interval to control for events between the two surveys. Five years is the typical strategic planning horizon for many organizations, and an assessment of GS1 standardsbased technology implementation at these intervals facilitated our analysis of the changes between T1 and T2 (Barringer & Bluedorn, 1999). We therefore asked managers whether the use of specific standards-based IOS enabling technologies had changed over both the past five and ten years.

We subsequently conducted an additional case study at a large Australian IT solution provider. Again, to reduce the probability that variables other than those reported by the first respondents explained the phenomena under study, we allowed these participants sufficient freedom in their responses for themes to emerge. However, as we used this case study to more explicitly probe some of the findings that resulted from the first round of case studies, we asked respondents more directly about their clients’ approach to IOS enabling technology decision making and its implications.

Sampling From each of the two groups of SMEs, we selected three organizations: three that indicated they had increased their use of GS1 standards-based IOS enabling technologies and three that indicated they had decreased use over the specified period. This approach allowed us to collect data from SMEs that mainly increased (or decreased) their use of these technologies over time and thus avoid a biased sample that consists of mainly (or even exclusively) one of the two groups (Eisenhardt, 1989). We also selected a large IT solution provider that works as a consultant for SMEs across both types of firms (Bloomfield & Danieli, 1995). Considering an IT solution provider’s perspective increased variety in the data and offered a more complete picture of decision processes and IOS alignment, especially as most SMEs turn to external IT solution providers ‘‘to make up for the lack of internal expertise’’ (Cragg et al, 2011, p. 353). In our sample, no firm had an IS department and staff with formal IS training, and all firms but one relied on the services of an IT solution provider. We decided to add this case study only after we had collected and partially analyzed the data from the SMEs. During this iterative data collection and analysis, we also decided to expand our interview protocol to include more specific questions regarding technology-related IOS strategies. Overall, this multiplecase design allowed us to ‘‘both augment external validity, and help guard against observer bias’’ (Voss et al, 2002, p. 202).

Data collection and analysis Case study data were collected mainly through face-to-face interviews with managers. In some cases, telephone interviews supplemented the information gathered during the personal interviews. Interviews lasted between 45 min and 2 h. We conducted the interviews in English, and we used an interview protocol based on the questionnaire from the quantitative phase and the literature on the adoption and implementation of IS, IOS, and related technologies in the supply chain (see Appendix D). GS1 Australia assisted in refining some of the interview questions. We discussed three broad subjects during our interviews with informants: (1) the types of IOS enabling technologies and the way firms use them, (2) the promises and benefits of IOS enabling technologies, and (3) the changes and challenges pertaining to IOS enabling technology adoption and implementation over time.

We kept the interviews flexible and continuously modified the interview protocol to capture themes and concepts that emerged spontaneously. We also gathered observational data during plant tours to glean more insights into operational processes and ensure internal consistency. During face-to-face interactions, all interviewees showed us various IOS enabling technologies in use and explained how they were being applied. To capture contextual information, we took field notes and converted them into full notes shortly after our observations. These methods provided us with in-depth understanding of the SMEs, and their products and allowed us to explore decision making processes concerning these technologies from different angles. To improve the robustness of our case studies, we also triangulated our findings by means of multiple respondents, several evaluators (i.e., researchers), interpretations from different theoretical frameworks (i.e., an emergent and deliberate perspective on strategy), and different sources (i.e., interviews, observation) (McCutcheon & Meredith, 1993). We stopped collecting data once we agreed that we had reached theoretical saturation and were able to offer detailed descriptions of decision processes and their implications.

We transcribed the interviews, which resulted in 116 pages (39,500 words) of data. We imported the data into QSR NVivo10 to manage and search the transcribed texts and identify emerging themes – an approach that resulted in a more transparent account of our data analysis. Additionally, we filtered the data mainly for insights relating to firms’ decision making processes to further our understanding of this phenomenon and its impact on IOS alignment and performance. Our analysis began with a focus on the deliberate and emergent approaches to decision making obtained from the literature. Rather than starting with a ‘‘clean slate,’’ the analysis inductively searched for evidence from SMEs that fit or contradicted, for instance, the benefits of deliberate or emergent decision making processes as identified in the two research streams. Our research questions and objectives thus provided guidance during the data analysis, but not rigid, predetermined expectations about what our data would reveal.

We read the transcripts repeatedly and identified concepts from distinct events in the data. We used open coding, where the text is scrutinized and broken apart to make sense of what the data show. NVivo facilitated this process through nodes (i.e., storage areas for references to coded text). We also used diagrams to depict what was emerging and to help establish relationships between various nodes and their link to the study’s research questions and objectives. In addition, we used coding stripes in NVivo to reflect how nodes related to one another. Concepts that emerged included determinants of the nature of firms’ IOS enabling technology decision processes, such as ‘‘hurdles’’ and ‘‘facilitators’’ of emergent and deliberate IOS enabling technology adoption and implementation processes. We broke these down into more specific categories (resulting in a node structure), including ‘‘IOS costs’’ and ‘‘IOS strategic benefits,’ and explored the extent to which these affected SME managers’ technology-related IOS decision making pro cesses and their implications. Overall, these coding procedures allowed us to make sense of each case and ultimately perform a cross-group analysis by comparing and contrasting our findings across the firms that increased or decreased their use of GS1 standards-based IOS enabling technologies over time. We applied the same procedure to our field notes.

We also hired a research assistant to code our data, and we discussed any differences in interpretation until we achieved agreement. Coding was determined to be complete when consensus was achieved on each construct (effectively forcing 100% inter-rater reliability between the co-authors and the research assistant). However, disagreements were minimal and mainly concerned with whether additional subcategories (i.e., subnodes) would add value to the analysis, such as ‘‘hybrid IOS adoption’’ and ‘‘hybrid human interference and IOS adoption.’’

Throughout this process, we moved back and forth between the literature and our within-case and cross-case data. This approach ensured confidence that our data would allow us to clearly discern IOS decision making processes concerning these technologies within SMEs, determine how decisions are made, and understand the implications of these processes. Surprisingly, while we expected differences in how the two groups (those that increased and those that decreased use over time) engaged in IOS enabling technology decision processes, the generated data indicated a consistent pattern independent of whether firms had increased or decreased their use over time. The same was true for insights drawn from the within-case and cross-case analyses. In the following section, we present the findings of our crossgroup analysis.

## Empirical findings

## SME managers’ IOS enabling technology decision making processes

Across all cases (i.e., both types of SMEs), our data yielded overwhelming evidence that IOS enabling technology decision making processes are predominantly emergent at a functional level – largely in line with the benefits of functional, emergent IS decision making identified in the literature review (see also Appendix C). We also found some evidence of greater organizational flexibility being promoted by emergent IOS decision making processes rather than endogenous IS features and capabilities (cf. Goodhue et al, 2009). The cross-case analysis revealed that in an effort to avoid jeopardizing their organizational performance, SME managers often make flexible IOS-related decisions leading to ad hoc solutions. Participants offered strong evidence that responding to their environments and maintaining their autonomy and independence was more important than formulating and following deliberate, higher-level strategic decision making processes.

Although the technologies under examination are based on stable and centrally administered global standards (i.e., IOS that are not inherently flexible), we found that in line with theories of sense- and decision making in organizations, situational factors greatly influenced the application of these standards. Indeed, Weick (1995) and others have repeatedly pointed out the contextual nature of managers sense- and decision making processes (Maitlis, 2005). For instance, the firms in our sample operated mixed communication systems, using IOS enabling technologies with their large suppliers and customers and more traditional media (mainly phone and e-mail) with their smaller trading partners. Many managers stated that more traditional media were essential for responding to different trading partner needs. As an operations manager of Firm B explained:

With Retailer Y we do [use IOS enabling technologies], their orders are EDI, they account for about 12% of our business, but apart from that, other orders are just emailed through or faxed through.

Several managers referred to the hybrid nature of their IOS enabling technologies, consisting of a mix of various traditional media along with those based on GS1 standards. Managers admitted that they sometimes needed to modify their use of standards-based technologies to solve basic supply chain-related problems. The use of IOS enabling technologies between trading partners was characterized more by differences in application than by similarities. Similarly, we found that SME managers emergent decision making processes for coping with IOSrelated problems entailed human intervention with technology in the supply chain, as in many cases SMEs IOS would not function without human intervention. A supply chain manager from Firm A said:

[Following an order from a trading partner] SAP will generate the requested orders but then we’ll actually have to take that figure and translate it back into action. And it all comes back down to us.

IOS enabling technologies should facilitate information visibility and accuracy of orders, production planning, and inventory. However, our data show that in SMEs human interaction must complement technology, even if the accuracy of information is compromised. The director of Firm D explained:

[IOS enabling technologies] probably should increase the accuracy of orders, but because it slows the order process down by so much, I have found that in most warehouses that I go into, including ours, they find shortcuts which get around the building of accuracy… So every product isn’t scanned [even though] it should be … the product gets scanned, it gets put into a box, the box is full, you tell the system the box is full, it prints out a label. They don’t do that, they will pack one box to figure out how much fits in that box, do it that way, and then they’ll cheat the system to get the order through quickly.

Managers act in response to incentives and specific situational factors – a reality that demonstrates one of the many ways in which ‘‘standards’’ are subject to an individual firm’s circumstances and are developed inde pendently by each business partner (De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013). A manager from Firm A stated:

We took the barcodes off deliberately for that product to make sure it never gets sold retail and it’s … just sold through the minibars in some of the major hotels around the world … this was not part of our original plan [strategy] it just sort of happened this way…it’s the only thing that makes sense for us in this case.

The patterns that emerged from our data suggest that the carefully orchestrated, formal IOS enabling technology decision processes that produce tightly aligned business and IOS configurations are largely irrelevant to SMEs’ dayto-day operations. Instead, managers typically regard their decisions as emerging to meet the individual firm’s (often short-term) goals, which depend on the firm’s circumstances at a particular point in time. These findings support the role of hybrid forms of IOS enabling technology use, such as that of interconnected systems with asymmetric characteristics synchronized by different trading partners with different needs (De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013).

The literature on emergent IS decision making shows that managers in SMEs often sideline the formal procedures for deploying various technologies; instead, managers typically use these to solve individual problems with little regard for a coherent plan that might enable greater exploitation of existing information among trading partners (Levy et al, 1999). As a result, the strategic alignment process also has an unplanned, emergent, or ‘‘loose’’ character that easily leads to strategic misalignment.

Our data indicate that SMEs view higher-level IOS enabling technology strategy as a set of choices that determines how (and with whom) the firm implements technology to compete. While taking second position most of the time, we found some evidence that managers in SMEs recognized the value of being driven by a corporate (higher-level) strategy. The owner of Firm C explained that although ‘‘most of the time it is just about getting things done, some choices must be about the broader picture and developing new business models [like shifting toward organic production methods].’’ A supply chain manager from Firm A said, ‘‘We cannot get bogged down by plans, but that’s not to say that we do not have and sometimes need one.’’ Despite this recognition, many managers indicated they continually evaluate their options and value propositions for their trading partners, making changes when they need to and when in doubt sticking to what has worked in the past, which in many cases has directly influenced their company policies and higher-level strategies. A manager of Firm D showed us his objectives specifically related to his firm’s IS, and when asked how he would fare without those objectives, he stated that they are important so long as they do not stifle everyday creativity when it is needed.

Main factors leading to the adoption of emergent strategies The way IOS enabling technology decision making processes played out varied with each organization and its environment. In fact, we found IOS decision making within SMEs to be highly contextdependent, and difficult to imitate because ‘‘locally optimizing a subset of the system’s elements does not [necessarily] lead to successful reproduction of the overall value proposition’’ (Piccoli & Lui, 2014, p. 624). Nonetheless, and in line with previous research (e.g., Hussin et al, 2002), we found consistency in the factors that influence these decision processes, especially toward an emergent functional-level approach. In particular, two main factors – lack of power and lack of financial resources – stood out in leading to the adoption of emergent IOS decision making and loosely coupled alignment. Respondents indicated that managers in SMEs are often talked at rather than with by larger, more powerful trading partners in relation to IOS adoption. To illustrate this power imbalance, the director of Firm D drew a diagram showing two circles, a very small one (representing Firm D) next to a big one (representing one or more big customers). He added,

The point I’m trying to make, with a business of our size, we don’t get to make the rules. We just follow the rules…we’re told what we have to do and we just do it, and if we want to deal with [large retailers], that’s how it is.

As a result of power imbalances and institutional pressure to adopt IOS enabling technologies (such as those based on GS1 standards), collaboration is often dysfunctional and SMEs act through a variety of ad hoc, emergent responses rather than deliberate, planned processes. SMEs often use more emergent decision making processes to compensate for a lack of financial resources, or for poor IS capability and power asymmetries between them and larger trading partners (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2015). This finding is in line with resource-based theory as applied in the IS domain, which states that firms should acquire different competencies – and thus also strategies – to cope with resource scarcity and to successfully employ various IS (Bharadwaj, 2000; Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012). SMEs are often resource poor (Cragg et al, 2011), and the high costs of setting up and conforming to an integrated suite of standards-based IOS enabling technologies can present problems. A technical manager from Firm A, for instance, noted that despite possible cost savings down the track, ‘‘the biggest barrier is just the cost of setting up EDI.’’ Such costs often leave SMEs unable to afford more than basic technologies such as barcodes, a circumstance that explains their need to intervene and complement their systems to make things work. We also found evidence that cost factors can compromise firms’ conformance to even the most basic standards such as those relating to barcodes. For example, the chief information officer at Firm F pointed out:

The style of packaging [necessary for barcodes] that they insisted on was impossible because… a lot of our products are 20-cent items, 40-cent items, and they want it in secure packaging and so the packaging all of a sudden is 300% of the cost of the item.

These cost factors also indicated the need for managers in SMEs to be able to adapt their use of these technologies, at times with little concern for the long-term implications of these decisions for IOS alignment.

Organizational performance implications of IOS enabling technology decision making processes Our data suggest that adaptation and flexibility lead to loosely coupled IOS alignment, which is crucial in generating successful IOS enabling technology decision making processes. In line with the literature, study participants reported that emergent decision processes produced easier access to information, quicker response to customer information requests, and faster inventory planning (Devaraj et al, 2007). However, most SME managers mentioned general benefits resulting from the adoption and implementation of various IOS enabling technologies. For example, the director of Firm B mentioned:

[T]here is no doing the old manual way versus doing it the ‘‘scanpack’’ way, everything has got to be done correctly, and it’s quick and it’s efficient, and the guys embrace it.

Similarly, an IS manager at Firm F showed us around the firm’s warehouse and mentioned that without IOS enabling technologies that allowed managers to make ‘‘quick decisions’’ the firm would run into ‘‘massive’’ problems. He explained, ‘‘[Only by keeping our systems going] and responding whenever we need to, did we manage to keep error rates low and have no problems… we rarely send out wrong stock, or over-supply or under-supply our customers.’

Although we found little objective evidence of performance implications, such as a percentage increase in profits following the adoption of EDI, the benefits we did observe came from avoidance of operational problems rather than from improved sharing of accurate and timely information. Thus, managers typically reported benefits such as overcoming a performance gap or exploiting a business opportunity (Premkumar & Roberts, 1999).

Importantly, in line with our evidence that maintaining autonomy and independence was more important than formulating and following deliberate, higher-level strategic IOS decision making processes, we found that managers stated that their focus was on maximizing profits for their own firm (Cohen & Cyert, 1965). No manager mentioned broader supply chain implications that were not directly related to their SME’s immediate operational reality. Our data contained little evidence that increased IOS enabling technology adoption promoted supply chain integration and collaborative improvement of processes and practices between firms. Most managers, for instance, mentioned that they would adopt more IOS enabling technologies (even RFID technology) – and more tightly align their SMEs’ IOS strategy with their decision making – if this approach guaranteed a significant increase in their number of orders. This likely reflects the imperative for most SMEs to generate revenue.

## IT solution provider perspective

The findings from the case study with the IT solution provider largely confirmed that emergent decision making processes were dominant among their SME clients. For instance, SMEs often adopt what the IT solution provider called ‘‘bastardized IS standards.’’ A senior account manager explained that a combination of various traditional media and more fully integrated IOS enabling technologies is necessary because of different trading partner requirements and revenue concerns:

Firms may have implemented full integration for one retail customer that is delivering 60% of their revenue (or more) and then when a second trading partner comes along which might only provide them with 20% of the revenue, they’ll go: the justification for buying a fully integrated solution for that say additional 20% is not there so they get a second manual solution to the side.

However, the IT solution provider acknowledged that emergent decision making can have negative performance implications down the line. A business development manager explained:

For most small suppliers they only have a handful of orders so [strategic] integration is not at the top of the list, but we try to explain to them that this is highly problematic because even if they only make a handful of mistakes, say in keying in orders, this can incur potentially big penalties.

This statement hints at the potential importance of combining emergent and deliberate processes to lead to a more tightly coupled IOS alignment, which can help firms to mitigate risks and avoid penalties. Our data also indicate that IT solution providers both resist and perpetuate the use of emergent strategies among SMEs. On the one hand, they strive for deliberate decision making and adoption of standards-based technologies: ‘‘true integration through one language with no more differences in data requirements,’’ as a senior account manager put it. The use of a deliberate IOS enabling technology strategy resulting in one language serves the greater community, as it should allow trading partners to efficiently communicate with one another both upstream and downstream. On the other hand, IT solution providers are prepared to compromise on an IT solution if it allows them to gain new business. In that sense, they pursue immediate performance benefits for their businesses (as a result of compromising on standards-related implementations). As the senior account manager admitted, ‘‘We prefer to sell a solution rather than nothing.’’ The IT solution provider not only confirmed a preference within SMEs for emergent decision making when implementing these technologies, but also stated that solution providers encourage firms to take this approach initially but then to adopt a more deliberate approach focused on understanding and adhering to IOS enabling technology standards.

## Discussion

This empirically based research explored IOS enabling technology decision making processes within SMEs. The key questions motivating this study were: How are decisions made within SMEs regarding the adoption and use of standards-based IOS enabling technology over time? What are the implications of SME managers’ decision making process for their (a) IOS technology alignment and (b) organizational performance?

To guide our exploration of the first question, we used two dominant and divergent perspectives: firms’ deliberate and emergent IOS enabling technology decision making processes. Decision making regarding the adoption and implementation of standards-based IOS is an important issue for SMEs, because imposing too much intentional control ‘‘could detract from innovation and flexibility; conversely, allowing too much emergence can undermine managerial predictability and work routines’’ (Choi et al, 2001, p. 351). Further, the exploration of this first question provides the basis for answering the second question. We explored our research questions in the context of strategic IOS enabling technology usage changes over time by identifying two samples of SMEs: a group that increased and a group that decreased use of these technologies over time. Our findings clearly reveal that across both groups, SME managers’ main pattern of decision processes is emergent at a functional level. Next, we discuss the implications of our findings for theory and practice. We also identify limitations of our study and how these present future research opportunities.

## Implications for research

The adoption and implementation of IOS among trading partners within a supply chain has received widespread interest in the literature (e.g., Rai et al, 2012; Huo et al,

2015). Our research broadly supports the notion that IOS enabling technologies are fundamental for connecting firms to a wider network of trading partners and maintaining collaborative processes (De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013; Grant & Tan, 2013; Tafti et al, 2013; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2015). However, prior investigations have provided little grounded insight into how SME managers make everyday IS-related decisions and strategically approach transitions toward the adoption of more or less IOS enabling technologies over time. In this study, we address this issue and contribute to the literature by exploring how SMEs managers’ IOS decision processes work and relate to organizational performance.

The study’s findings contribute importantly to theory cognate to strategic IS alignment. Our findings specifically support the notion that SMEs prefer, and to some degree benefit from a fluid, loosely coupled alignment between their IOS strategy and their decisions concerning the use of IOS enabling technologies. The form IOS alignment takes appears to be highly situational and even configurable, supporting some contentions that ‘‘strategic alignment is a process of continuous adaptation and change’’ (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1999, p. 473) and that decision makers are heavily influenced by, and should adapt to, environmental cues (Maitlis, 2005). Thus, theoretical implications follow from our finding that, in SMEs, adopting deliberate IOS decision making processes does not appear to be necessarily consistent with greater use of IOS enabling technologies that are more tightly coupled with a firm’s IOS decision making strategy. Further, it is unclear if, for SMEs, such strategies are necessarily beneficial and associated with performance improvements (Frohlich & Westbrook, 2001; Saeed et al, 2011).

Power and Gruner (2015, 2016) have previously used a study design analogous to the present study to identify firms that exhibited dynamic (i.e., increased, static, and decreased) IOS enabling technology adoption behaviors over time. The main purpose of this prior work was to empirically explore why shifts toward less IOS enabling technologies occur (Power & Gruner, 2015), and to compare three different IOS implementation models (Power & Gruner, 2016). In contrast, this study focuses on how these shifts occur in SMEs. Understanding how this happens is necessary if managers are to take full advantage of IOS enabling technologies and bring about truly integrated supply chains (Fawcett & Magnan, 2002). Unlike previous related studies, we aim to achieve this understanding by shedding light on the nature of SME managers’ decision making processes as well as their IOS alignment and organizational performance implications. This is complementary to findings in related domains. In particular, the relationship between IS and organizational performance has been a persistent question in IS research (e.g., Quaadgras et al, 2014; Iyengar et al, 2015).

Findings from Power and Gruner (2015) illustrate that a number of circumstances (including operational disadvantages and compliance costs) can lead to decreased IOS enabling technology implementations. In 2016, the same authors show support for a calculative model of IOS enabling technology implementation (Power & Gruner, 2016). This calculative model captures supply chain managers’ often prevailing concern for short-term organizational benefits for their own firm rather than the supply chain as a whole, which explains divergences from a linear evolution of well-defined IOS implementation phases (Power & Gruner, 2016). Building on these works as well as others’ (Weick, 1995; Maitlis 2005), the present study’s results highlight the need to better understand the nature and antecedents of managers’ decision (and organizational sense-making) processes, particularly their process of scanning and interpreting the environment during IOS transition phases.

Moreover, this study provides support for prior work that found an association between the use and implementation of IS and organizational flexibility (e.g., Overby et al, 2006). However, while research typically links IS to increases in business flexibility – with flexibility being regarded as a performance benefit derived from a technological implementation (such as an investment in IS) (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011) – our data indicate that flexible decision making is important (Goodhue et al, 2009), particularly in understanding why SME managers vary the use of IOS enabling technologies over time. Our findings also challenge the belief that organizational flexibility depends on the inherent flexibility of different IS standards, such as modular design or open communication standards (Gosain et al, 2005; Tafti et al, 2013). Therefore, our paper makes a contribution to the literature concerned with how firms can capitalize on organizational flexibility (Sambamurthy et al, 2003).

Despite findings that emphasize the importance of flexible, emergent decision making processes applied to the use of IOS enabling technologies in SMEs, our data also show that, at times, managers in SMEs use higherlevel strategies and are aware of the performance benefits of more tightly coupled IOS alignment. Our findings thus indicate that the largely opposing camps of literature about the value of deliberate versus emergent functionallevel strategies are not necessarily irreconcilable. This contribution is valuable since most IS theory focuses on the importance and benefits of deliberate, higher-level or emergent, functional-level strategies. Our results provide evidence that higher-level, deliberate decision making remains necessary to set overall business objectives, even when emergent and often ad hoc decisions seem to be the most efficient way for managers to make the most of technology investments. When SMEs face volatility, uncertainty, and complexity requiring a change in investment in IOS enabling technologies, most of our respondents remain influenced to some extent by a deliberate higher-level strategy in some shape or form.

This study also advances research that explores the contingencies that determine IOS enabling technology adoption and implementation behaviors among SMEs (Iacovou et al, 1995). Our data indicate that managers constantly need to make sense of various often unforeseen circumstances that inform their IOS-related actions (Weick et al, 2005). Our study therefore supports some of Weick’s (1995) contentions, such as the notion that decision making is often ongoing and contextual, and involves more intuition than careful analysis and omniscient projection of future states (O’Connell, 1998). Mainly cost- and power-based factors emerged as influential in shaping intuitive sense-making, resulting in unpredictable IOS enabling technology decisions that can lead to the use of hybrid forms of IOS in practice. In line with de Corbiere and Rowe (2013), we found that these hybrid forms are often necessary for managers to keep their businesses afloat. Notably, despite these hybrid systems informal nature and potentially compromising role in establishing accurate information transferal and communication between trading partners, managers often employ these in an effort to meet their firm’s specific performance objectives (De Corbiere & Rowe, 2013; Power & Gruner, 2016).

## Implications for practice

In light of managers’ awareness of their decision making processes’ fundamental role in determining organizational performance (e.g., Huo et al, 2015), we aimed to generate insights into decision making that help managers to organize and improve these processes. Given increasingly dynamic and globally connected networks of trading partners, shedding light on the nature of the relationship between decision making, technology use, and organizational performance remains timely and relevant for man agers who seek to improve their strategic decision making and maximize the benefit of their IOS enabling technology investments (Andersen & Segars, 2001).

We found that, among SMEs that made upward or downward changes in the use of their IOS enabling technologies, IOS decision making processes regarding these technologies were mainly emergent at a functional level. This finding indicates that flexible and dynamic decision making processes within SMEs are consistent with their business needs. For instance, SME managers frequently deal with bigger companies that have different competitive dynamics and IS capability asymmetries. These circumstances emphasize the need for managers to recognize that standards-based IOS enabling technologies do not easily transfer between trading partners (i.e., are not scale-free), requiring managers to remain agile (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2015). However, the data also show a potential detrimental effect of too much focus on agility and short-term goals in everyday IOS-related decisions. Instead, IOS alignment that is loose enough to accommodate changing circumstances, but also deliberate enough to avoid being purely reactive and lacking in direction, seems to be more appropriate. Thus, although the main pattern in our data suggests that SMEs can remain responsive and use ad hoc strategies when necessary, failure to adopt any elements of deliberate strategic processes can lead to an egocentric approach that ‘‘may result in an inefficient combination of laborintensive and error-prone manual processes, including paper-based record keeping, phone calls, emails, and faxes’’ (Archer et al, 2008, p. 81).

Our study also provides managers with an opportunity to develop a broader perspective and to better understand the motives underlying their own sense-making and decision making processes as well as those of their trading partners. Our study confirms the importance of SMEspecific contingencies, namely the lack of financial resources and power that drive IOS decision making processes regarding their technological choices and the resulting IOS alignment. Although these contingencies are neither new (see, for example, Premkumar & Ramamurthy, 1995) nor exhaustive, researchers have previously given them little attention. Through our study’s results, we aim to increase managers’ chances to overcome some of the barriers to more deliberate IOS strategies and the resulting tightly coupled IOS alignments. If SME managers recognize the idiosyncrasies of their businesses and the factors that determine their IOS decision making, they will be more likely to recognize the potential benefits of using standards-based IOS enabling technologies, align objectives across supply chain partners, and share risks and rewards (Levy & Powell, 2000). IT solution providers and IOS standards bodies like GS1 should assist SMEs in understanding underlying IOS decision processes and the factors that drive these, and ultimately help develop solutions that assist managers in maximizing the return of their technology investments. Overall, our findings can aid SMEs in making IOS investment decisions that lead to successful firm-internal IOS alignment and firm-external inter-firm collaboration (Gaur et al, 2011).

## Limitations and future research

Our findings should be viewed with the limitations of the study in mind. First, as we focused exclusively on SMEs that increased or decreased their implementation of IOS enabling technologies, future studies should explore related research questions among firms that maintained their level of technology use over time. For example, do SMEs that have not changed their use over time typically also make emergent decisions at a functional level? If so, how do these decision making processes differ from those identified in this study? Examination of the proposed relationships from a broader representation of firms should include research on decision making processes in relation to firm size. For example, do our findings also apply to exclusively medium-sized firms? In addition, the relationships should be explored in the context of large, multinational firms and across both upstream and downstream trading partners of various sizes. Future research needs to test our findings across multiple partners of a supply chain to explore the aggregated effects. The study’s findings should also be viewed in light of the fact that different firms have different requirements and that IOS technology use requires alignment with those requirements (da Silveira & Cagliano, 2006; Saeed et al, 2011).

Second, the development and success of strategies depend on the social and cultural systems in which those strategies are embedded. While our study was conducted across a number of industries and with diverse participants, it was limited to a relatively small sample of Australian SMEs; conclusions should thus be drawn carefully. Future studies should probe our findings in global markets and across various cultures.

Third, our focus on inter-organizational systems allowed us to study firms’ leveraging of technology in a supply chain context in relation to firm-external trading partners. ## Appendix A

GS1 definitions for generic levels of Implementation (EAN Australia, 1998)

Three generic levels of implementation of these technologies are promoted by GS1 as being characteristic of how companies approach their application to supply chain management. The definitions used by GS1 are outlined below:

• Reactive Purely satisfying a request from a trading partner. This level is viewed as the lowest level of implementation and is generally restricted to applying barcodes to finished goods, with perhaps some EDI transactions included. Such implementations can be considered as merely adding cost to the business.

• Tactical This approach seeks to extend implementation to specific processes within the business to improve efficiencies in, for example, production or inventory control. At this level, adequate planning, costing, and definition of the project are recommended, and real cost savings are a primary objective.

• Strategic Implementations of this type seek to introduce integrated supply chain management techniques across the entire supply chain in a planned, staged manner. The first step is usually the introduction of the elements of the GS1 system within the initiator’s business, as the beginning of a long-term project expected to deliver significant savings and other business benefits.

## Overarching Questions

1. Obtain a description of the technologies like barcodes, RFID tags, EDI/XML business document exchange systems, Global Data Synchronization (GDS) systems that (company name) uses to facilitate communication and transactions with:

(a) suppliers (i.e., for upstream activities) and/or

(b) customers (i.e., for downstream activities) and/or

(c) internally (i.e., for internal functions and business processes)

2. Where and how are these technologies used?

3. How do different technologies interact with one another?

Questions on the effect of technology:

1. What technologies provide which benefit(s) (if any at all)?

(a) suppliers (i.e., for upstream activities) and/or

(b) customers (i.e., for downstream activities) and/or

(c) internally (i.e., for internal functions and business processes)

Questions on changes in the technology adoption behavior of firms:

1. Have any of the technologies in use changed over the past five/ten years (or over the period that the respondent has been with the firm)?

(a) suppliers (i.e., for upstream activities) and/or

4. Is there a story around the role these technologies play in the firm’s operations, and how this role may have developed and changed over the past five/ten years?
