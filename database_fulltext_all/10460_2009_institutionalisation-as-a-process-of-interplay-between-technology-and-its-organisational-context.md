## Abstract

This study contributes to the IS literature with a distinct explanation of the process of institutionalisation of technology in organisations. The research analyses the role of microlevel processes of interplay in embedding an intranet in the formal functioning of an organisation and in the habits and routines of its employees. The findings identify two types of processes of interplay underpinning this process of institutionalisation. The first operates at the level of constitutive expectations and refers to mutual changes to the governance, policy and control mechanisms which foster the perception that the intranet is part of the expected formal functioning of the organisation. The second operates at the level of background expectations and refers to mutual changes that make the intranet look more familiar, functional, easier to use, fostering its embedding in routines and habits of the employees. The study aims to motivate more research on institutionalisation as a bottom-up micro process of gradual development of institutionalised behaviour. ## The importance of intranets to organisations

oday, almost every large organisation relies on an intranet system to support internal communication, collaboration and as the backbone for many internal business processes. Intranets are also, in many cases, part of the business critical infrastructure and disaster recovery planning. Intranets are also often used to support other important strategic business initiatives, such as mergers and acquisitions, knowledge management and redundancy programmes, among other important programmes of organisational transformation. Despite this important role in organisations, the processes by which intranets become embedded in work practices and employee routines have hardly been analysed. The aim of this study is therefore to investigate the longitudinal processes of interplay that contribute to the institutionalisation of the technology in the functioning of organisations.

Compared to other types of ICT ‘intranets are poised to make the largest impact’ in organisations (Jacko et al., 2002). Bansler et al. (2000) go further to say that intranets ‘dramatically alter how people in organisations interact and communicate, how managers think about IT, and how organisations design and manage their information systems’. Intranets are built using internet technology to support services inside organisations (Bansler et al., 2000) and are implemented to ‘deliver the right information to the right people at the right time’ (Curry and Stancich, 2000). They evolve to become a single point of entry to most organisational resources, becoming so pervasive that it is difficult to establish a clear boundary between intranets and other types of technologies. Intranets may include emails (Holtz, 2000), static content, workflow management systems, collaborative groupware tools and databases (Lamb and Davidson, 2005). It may also include today’s social computing functionality such as wikis and blogs (Fichter, 2005). However, some authors exclude applications such as e-mail and discussion groups, unless they are accessible through the web-based interface of the intranet (Stenmark, 2002). Intranets may also be integrated with transactional systems (Watson and Fenner, 2000; Dias, 2001; McFarland, 2001), forming what is often conceived, as ‘portals’ or gateways to organisational resources and personalised information (Dias, 2001). The boundaries with external websites and extranets are also controversial because they are based on the same technology but target a different audience (Jacko et al., 2002).

However, intranets, according to Newell et al. (2000), are much more than just the web technology used to support the publishing of information (infrastructure). They also include the formal rules that regulate its use (infostructure) and the values and beliefs underpinning the text, images and other content (infoculture). Currently the literature predominantly focuses on the technical aspects of intranet systems providing a myopic view of the whole intranet environment. This excessive focus on the technical capabilities of intranets may actually lead to loss of personal interaction and unnecessary automation of organisational processes (Telleen, 1997). It may also create unrealistic expectations about how the technology will be used in practice. For example, it may create false expectations that implementing an intranet will automatically lead to more collaboration and sharing of information across the organisation. This is, however, an unrealistic expectation because this technology is not inherently collaborative and the sharing of information will only occur if the culture of the organisation promotes and supports such behaviour (Ruppel and Harrington, 2001). Intranets should therefore, be conceived, instead, as inherently social and embedded in the social fabric of their hosting organisations (Avgerou, 2000, 2001).

## Intranets interplay with their context of use

The concept of interplay and ongoing mutual change between technology and its hosting organisation is an underlying theme in many strands of the IS published research. Lee (1999) says that ‘an information system and its organisational context each have transformation effects on the other’ and conceptualises this interplay as ‘more like the reagents that react to and change each other’s properties in a chemical compound than the inert elements that retain their respective properties in a chemical mixture’. From this perspective, the two elements penetrate into and radically transform each other but nevertheless continue to preserve a distinct character and separate identities. Orlikowski, on the other hand, argues that this interplay forms a new identity where the two elements become more like one – what she calls sociomateriality (Orlikowski, 2007). From this perspective, technology is already social when it is first introduced in the organisation because it cannot avoid reflecting pre-existing socio-cultural interests and politically influenced distribution of resources, as argued by Orlikowski ‘technology embodies and hence is an instantiation of some of the rules and resources constituting the structure of an organisation’ (Orlikowski, 1992).

This type of interplay also underpins the evolution of intranet systems in organisations leading to ongoing mutual changes to the technology and the working practices associated with its use. This is because intranets emerge and evolve without a strong or preconceived plan of development. Changes are usually improvised small additions, rather than part of a ‘grand plan’ (Ciborra and Hanseth, 1998). This ongoing interplay has been conceived as drifting (Ciborra and Jelassi, 1994; Ciborra, 2000) and also as interpretive flexibility (Bjiker et al., 1987; Orlikowski, 1992). These two conceptual properties are considered important for the technology to be appropriated and accepted in the organisation. Orlikowski et al. (1995) say that ‘this openendedness offers benefits of flexibility but also creates the possibility that – without adaptation of the technology to the context and vice versa – the technology will not reflect local conditions or communication norms and hence be underutilized or inappropriately utilized’.

A corollary of this, is that this dynamic interplay is not manageable and leads to both intended and unintended outcomes to the technology and the organisation (Newell et al., 2001). The effects are mostly unpredictable and visible only on hindsight as noted by Newell et al. (2001) who find that intranets can sometimes reinforce preexisting cultural differences and ‘have the ironic effect of further reinforcing geographical and functional barriers to knowledge exchange’.

Two ongoing processes of mutual transformation stimulate this interplay. Process 1 refers to organisational processes that shape the intranet; Process 2 refers to changes to the culture and working practices of the organisation shaped by the use of the intranet. The follow two sub-sections describe these two processes in separate.

## Process 1: organisations shaping intranets

Organisations implement intranets and shape them to achieve specific business purposes. For example, Bansler et al. (2000) show how senior management implemented an intranet to promote a culture of transparency. Stakeholders involved in implementing the technology will shape it according to their business needs and objectives, as explained by Clarke and Preece, ‘certain actors and groups may have more opportunities than others to shape the technology (y) thus, it is important to know who/whom has this opportunity, what their aims are, what sources of influence they have and what means they can and do deploy to shape its configuration’ (2005).

Over time, the intranet will continuously absorb the properties of its environment. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) use the term ‘evolution-in-use’ to describe the continuous adaptation of the technology to the ongoing changing needs of its organisational context. They propose ‘Adaptive Structuration Theory’ as a framework for studying this ongoing dialectic interplay. Ciborra and Lanzara (1994) also argue that the organisational context is formative, because it shapes the way people make sense, perform and get organised in a specific situation. They describe formative contexts as ‘the set of institutional arrangements and cognitive imageries that inform the actors’ practical and reasoning routines in organisations’. Bansler et al. (2000) use this concept to compare the development of two different intranets and find that ‘these differences in formative context explain why the dynamics of intranet implementation in the two companies differ so markedly and why the two intranets evolve in different directions, following radically different trajectories’ (Bansler et al., 2000).

Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. (1999) investigate the role of organisational context in shaping an intranet and find that ‘the way various groups and the organisation adopted the OSS was deeply situated in their context and was shaped by their specific culture’. In some cases, the power of pre-existing organisational culture is so strong that implementing intranets may have only a limited impact in terms of organisational change. Paradoxically, it has been found that intranets, might actually, reinforce pre-existing cultural values, ‘despite efforts to encourage knowledge sharing on a global scale, conflicting reward structures encouraged localized knowledge hoarding’ (Newell et al., 2001).

## Process 2: intranets shaping organisations

Intranets are often implemented to drive organisational change. They are, for example, expected to increase organisational performance, sharing of information between organisational units, and improve decision-making. However, their impact goes much deeper because they penetrate the social and behavioural fabric of organisations. For example, Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. (1999) find that the use of the intranet ‘affected the way many individuals perceived, thought and felt in relation to their local problems. We observed the changing patterns of social interactions, increasing openness of the process, equalising effects, reduction of informational and emotional distance, that in turn affected the social context’ (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 1999).

Intranets also impact on the wider socio-political context of organisations because intranets systems ‘are not merely an intellectual commodity but a political resource, whose redistribution through new information systems affects the interests of particular groups’ (Keen, 1981) and they shift the balance of power in organisations (Dickey and Ives, 2000). This is shown through some of Clarke and Preece’s findings such as this quote showing effects in the expectations of employees about the use of the intranet by senior managers, ‘my director might surf around and ask simple questions, that you think ‘Oh God, what’s he found, what’s he uncovered this time?’ (Clarke and Preece, 2005).

These effects mirror Zuboff’s (1988) finding that the ‘informating’ capability of computer technologies may threaten traditional sources of managerial authority, which depend in part upon control over the organisation’s knowledge base. Faced with losing control, managers will react and seek ways to protect their power base. They will try to structure the use and configuration of the technology to defend and reproduce the legitimacy of their managerial authority – ‘the hierarchy will use the technology to reproduce itself ’. However, interestingly, employees may also exploit the technology to their advantage; for example, when using the intranet to show their superiors that they are ‘doing something’ as found by Hayes and Walsham (2000). They study the use of intranet technologies in a medical organisation where they suspected that ‘young careerists’ were trying to draw attention to themselves through their use of the technology, while the ‘old stagers’ and non-careerists were much less interested in using the system. They also noticed that the intranet changed the way facts emerged and became recognised as the ‘truth’ in the organisation. Whereas before the intranet, senior executives were able to establish ‘factuality’ because of their power to manage information flows in the organisation; with the intranet, information travelled freely across the organisation. Knowledge workers were then able to share information directly between each other, radically changing the way information was disseminated in the organisation.

## Institutionalisation as a process of interplay

These two processes of interplay underpin the embedding and institutionalisation of the intranet in the working practices and social fabric of the organisation. Damsgaard and Scheepers (2000) say that the intranet at this stage ‘corresponds to Heidegger’s term ‘‘ready-at-hand’’, in which the technology disappears and becomes a natural ‘‘extension’’ of the users. Users do not think about the technology itself anymore’. Gradually, the intranet becomes so deeply embedded in business processes and work practices that the technology becomes ‘forgotten’ or invisible to organisational stakeholders. At this stage, the technology simply becomes ‘part of the furniture’ in organisational life (Currie, 2004). Silva and Backhouse (1997) say that information systems that have become institutionalised ‘are no longer considered as innovations but as unnoticed and unremarkable tools that people take for granted in doing their work. Institutionalised information systems are noticed only when they break down’. Later, the same authors expand this notion, ‘an information system is institutionalized when associated practices and procedures have become routines that can be regarded as organisational habits’ (Silva and Backhouse, 2003). Other IS scholars have explored ways in which IT become legitimated and routinised in organisations (Currie and Guah, 2007).

The ‘invisibility’ of technology to organisational actors is seen as a sign of the level of institutionalisation. However, the literature is short in identifying the processes contributing to the institutionalisation of technology in organisations. Bansler et al. (2000) point up to the lack of knowledge in this area. This study addresses this gap in the literature by investigating the processes that drive the embedding of the technology in the working practices, habits, routines and perceptions of employees.

## Research question

The following research question addresses this gap and drives this research:

\- How do intranets become institutionalised in organisations?

This question directly addresses the gap in the literature identified above – it aims to improve understanding of the specific micro processes of interplay between the technology and its hosting organisation, which over time embed the intranet in the working practices of the organisation. The question focuses on the micro processes of mutual transformation underpinning the interplay between the technology and its hosting organisation. This focus aims to improve knowledge on how technology is shaped and shapes its organisational context of use, and how this interplay then underpins the institutionalisation of the technology in the working practices of the organisation.

## Theoretical framework

Scott (2001) reviews the origins of institutional thinking and suggests three roots in developing and sustaining institutionalised behaviour: regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive. In Scott’s own words these pillars or processes are ‘the building blocks of institutional structures, providing the elastic fiber that resist change’ (p. 49). This study follows Scott’s third pillar as the root to understand the process of institutionalisation; that is, it emphasises the role of cognitive processes in the development and transmission of institutionalised behaviour. This role of cognition and culture as vehicles of institutionalisation prevails because people ‘treat cultural categories as the cognitive containers in which social interests are defined and classified, argued, negotiated, and fought out’ (Douglas, 1982 in Scott, 2001).

This branch of institutional thinking emerges initially from Berger and Luckmann’s (1967) seminal study and their view of institutionalisation as shared knowledge and common belief systems. Institutions emerge through culture and shared value systems because these systems preserve dominant rewards and sanction schemas. This view contrasts to ideas of institutionalisation centred on the enforcement of rules and norms as proclaimed by the other two pillars in Scott’s (2001) framework. According to the cognitive-cultural perspective, institutionalised behaviour is determined by the relative stability, legitimacy and power of ‘common understandings that are seldom explicitly articulated’ (Zucker, 1983). These common understandings are engrained in the culture of the organisations and become the lenses through which actors see themselves as part of that social group – shaping their views of the world by forming socially accepted categories of structure, action and thought (Zucker, 1991). From this perspective, behaviour is driven not so much by the enforcement of norms and values (a more regulative and normative approach) but by unconscious and un-reflected behaviour based on classifications, routines, scripts and schemas in the minds of individuals.

The literature is also split in two main theoretical streams of institutional thinking (Zucker, 1987; Clegg, 1990). One focuses at the macro level and sees the environment as the main conductor of institutionalised behaviour – the context reproduces common practices and social facts. Meyer and Rowan (1977) seminal article uses this approach to study inter-firm behaviour and introduces isomorphism as a key process for explaining the diffusion of practices across organisations. Later, DiMaggio and Powell (1983, 1991) further develop this approach and defines isomorphism as a process that ‘forces one unit in a population to resemble other units that face the same set of environmental conditions (y) organisational characteristics are modified in the direction of increased compatibility with environmental characteristics’ (DiMaggio and Powell, 1991). According to this view, institutionalised practices travel across organisations and social groups fostering the convergence of practices through coercive, mimetic and normative forces (DiMaggio and Powell, 1991).

The second theoretical stream focuses at the more micro level of social behaviour in organisations and is more concerned with the emergence of shared meanings and patterns of behaviour – this process of generation sees the reproduction of institutionalised behaviour as a consequence of institutionalisation, not a cause. From this perspective, institutions or institutionalised-behaviour are the end result of the institutionalisation process and can be defined as the development of ‘social structures that have attained a high degree of resilience (y) by definition connote stability but are subject to change processes both incremental and discontinuous’ (Scott, 2001). Behaviour is initially socially constructed but over time the historical process that led to its emergence is forgotten (Schutz, 1962). Berger and Luckmann (1967) say that at this stage institutionalised behaviour has a more symbolic meaning to individuals because they are ‘experienced as possessing a reality of their own, a reality that confronts the individual as an external and coercive fact’. The same authors argue that institutionalised acts have two key characteristics: they are perceived as being both objective and exterior. Acts are objective when they can be repeated by other actors with no change in their perceived meaning and interpretation. Acts are exterior when their meaning is intersubjective and seen as independent to any personal interest (Berger and Luckmann, 1967, on reification and objectivation). Objectivity and exteriority are interrelated and to a certain extent correlated; an increase in objectivity may lead to an increase in exteriority (because social acts become better understood, which then disassociates it from any particular entity).

Berger and Luckmann (1967) describe three stages in the institutionalisation process: (1) externalisation, the production of shared meanings in social settings; (2) objectivation, the process by which facts become independent and perceived as something ‘out there’ and as a reality experienced in common with others; and (3) internalisation, the process by which objectified facts become part of routine behaviour, or as explained by the authors, the way in which ‘objectivated world is retroprojected into consciousness in the course of socialization’ (Berger and Luckmann, 1967).

The seminal work of Berger and Luckmann explains institutionalisation in general social settings. Its application to the specific context of organisations does not come until later when Lynne Zucker (1977) adopts their thinking to study micro-level processes of institutionalisation in organisational settings. She defines three distinctive stages in the process of institutionalisation (Tolbert and Zucker, 1996) in organisations. The first refers to a process of habitualisation, the second to a process of objectivation and the third to a process of sedimentation. In this research, Lynn Zucker’s institutional thinking was used to capture the longitudinal processes of interplay and the phenomenology inherent to the gradual familiarisation and embedding of the technology in working practices of the organisation. More specifically, Institutional-Based Trust Theory (Zucker, 1986) was a source of key concepts to interpret the findings of this research. In this paper, Zucker uses the work of Schutz (1962) to develop the concept of background expectations and the work of Garfinkel (1967) to develop the concept of constitutive expectations. The next two sub-sections further develop these two concepts by reviewing the original work of Schutz and Garfinkle.

## Background expectations

Schutz work is concerned with the ‘original constitution and meaningful structure of the world of daily life which humans take for granted as part of their natural attitude (1962). He uses phenomenology as an instrument to investigate the reality of common-sense life and the fundamental principles underpinning human behaviour in a social setting. The object of Schutz’s study is the takenfor-granted attitude of everyday life. For him, our world – the ‘everyday world’, the ‘common-sense world’ or the ‘world of daily life’ – is essentially intersubjective and we ‘simply take for granted that the reciprocity of perspectives holds’.

From this perspective, human perception of objects and events is shared and similar for all individuals in the same social setting. Thus, our perception of the world is that of a world that has ‘existed before our birth, has its history, and is given to us in an organized fashion’ (Schutz, 1962: xxvii). We accept the world as it is presented to us, with all its inherent typifications of behaviour. These typifications are however not formally recognised; instead they are entrenched in our ‘common sense world’ and in our ‘natural attitude’ of daily life.

Schutz posits that these typifications are the result of a ‘sedimented structure of the individual’s experience’ and the lens through which people interpret new events. He says that ‘no one has to teach us that the ordinary is the ordinary and that familiar is familiar’. Typifications are learned through our normal experience which we then use as recipes to understand the world and our new experiences in it. In this world of everyday life, reality is taken for granted. As long as the established scheme of reference appears to be in order – and the actions and operations performed under its guidance yield the desired results – we trust these experiences. It needs a special motivation, such as a strange experience or one that is inconsistent with expected frames of reference, to make us question our established beliefs. Our natural attitude is to operate unreflectively in the world using our established frames of reference; we only suspend this mode of functioning when what is perceived as an abnormal event becomes apparent.

Schutz’s ideas are used by Zucker to define the concept of background expectations as ‘the common understandings that are taken for granted as part of a world known in common’ (Zucker, 1986: 57). In the context of intranets in organisations, background expectations underpin the institutionalisation of technology because they foster the collective perception that the intranet is familiar and part of the world known in common by employees.

## Constitutive expectations

The concept of constitutive expectations is developed based on the work of Garfinkel (1963) and more generally Garfinkel’s ethnomethodology programme (Garfinkel, 1967). Like Schutz, Garfinkel was concerned with the mundane reality of daily life, the ‘natural facts of life in society’. However, his phenomenological approach focuses on the role of agreed norms and the specificity of particular contexts, exchanges and interactions. Garfinkel studies the existence of preconceived rules of engagement in social interaction which constrain action and enable the prediction of behaviour.

Garfinkel posits that rules in games, like in social settings, serve for each ‘player’ to interpret the adversary’s behaviour as well as his own. Rules establish normality because they become accepted and shared by all parties.

Perception of normality is a key concept in Garfinkel’s work. He suggests that we constantly create the ‘taken-forgranted’ world which we feel we ‘know’ and can be ‘at home’ with. We perceive our social world through a series of patterns we have created to making sense of and coping with the variety of situations that we encounter every day. This is necessary to avoid the confusion we would experience if we saw everything in the world as if for the first time.

For Garfinkel, norms and procedural rules are important for establishing cognitive guidance systems which are used reflexively to assure individuals and those around them that their behaviour is acceptable and reasonable. Deviation from these general rules may lead to emotional distress but, according to Garfinkel, sometimes this is necessary in order to make apparent the underlying rules of behaviour. Garfinkel developed ethnomethodology as a technique to identify these accepted rules of behaviour by creating a temporal disruption to the world which people take for granted. For example, in one of his experiments, Garfinkel asked students to behave as visitors in their own homes, and record the bemused reactions of their parents as they struggled to comprehend the sudden disruption of the informal relationship built up over many years with their children.

Constitutive expectations institutionalise the intranet in the organisation through the gradual embedding of the technology in the expected formal functioning of the organisation by establishing rules and governance that formalise its use inside the organisation.

## Methodology

This study follows other intranet studies adopting an interpretive research approach (Newell et al., 1999; Newell et al., 2000, 2001; Newell and Swan, 2000; Lamb, 2002). The study uses a single longitudinal in-depth case study research strategy as described by Yin (1994). The empirical setting chosen is a bank in the UK, Alliance & Leicester.

The strategy for collecting data and reconstructing the historical evolution of the intranet over the 5-year period emulates the seminal study of Markus (1983). This research adopts Markus’ technique to examine the historical evolution of the intranet in the bank in the UK from late 1999 to 2005. Three types of data were analysed: (1) documents, policies, meeting notes and other reports; (2) interviews, focus groups and observations; and (3) electronic copies of the intranet at three different points in time and statistics of use of the intranet for the 5 years of analysis. The researchers had unlimited access to all types of data, including management documentation, interviews and back-up copies of the intranet. All the data, including 93 documents, were collected, categorised and analysed. The volume and quality of these data is consistent across the 5 years covered by the study and allows for the longitudinal reconstruction of the intranet’s historical evolution. Figure 1 shows the data collected to cover the 5-year period.

Documentation analysed included notes for meetings of the IMG (Intranet Management Group) and IOG (Intranet Operations Group), other management reports such as the initial business case for the intranet, consultancy reports and various internal reports addressing ad hoc management issues. Other documents included year-on-year usability testing reports, user satisfaction surveys and expert reviews, reports from employee focus-groups conducted in 2000, 2001, 2002 and 2003 and results from the annual employee survey. Several company policies were also analysed including standards, procedures and other formal documentation directly or indirectly related to the intranet. Two full back up copies of the intranet were analysed, one in 2004 and the second in 2005, to capture changes in the design and structure of the intranet. In addition, free navigation of the intranet including capturing screenshots was conducted at several points in time. Log files were also analysed from 2000 to 2005 and included full information on hits per page and usage of sites across the intranet.

Thirty employees were interviewed and participated in focus groups. There were also a number of other informal interviews during meetings and social events attended by the researcher. Participants fell into three categories: the first were employees in the management team, intranet stakeholders and publishers; the second were intranet users at different levels and functions of the organisation; and the third were call centre employees and admin staff who were observed and interviewed at their workplace during normal work activities. There were also 20 in-depth interviews with the management staff of the intranet and publishers, including employees from the central management team and stakeholders from across the organisation. These interviews took place between July 2004 and February 2005. In-depth interviews were conducted with members of the Group Intranet Team (including intranet manager, intranet coordinators and IT strategy manager) and several content publishers (from marketing, HR, legal and compliance, retail network, charities and social news). Some further interviews with the intranet team were conducted during the period February 2005 to September 2005.

All interviews lasted just over 1 h and were recorded and transcribed soon afterwards. Interviews with publishers followed a more structured approach designed to identify procedures for publishing content, roles and responsibilities and compliance with policy across the organisation. Interviews with the users were semi-structured and mainly based on the themes from the literature review and theoretical framework. Two focus groups were also conducted, involving a total of 11 intranet users. The first focus group comprised six users from call centres while the second involved five users from across the organisation who had participated in a similar exercise in 2002. Focus groups were held in February 2005. Further interviews with intranet users, mainly admin and call centre staff, included observations about how the intranet was used for everyday activities. Call centre staff use the intranet to provide customer service and represent a large proportion of the total workforce (5500 staff out of 8500 working in frontline roles). These sessions were more informal than the focus groups and not all were recorded. In such instances, notes were taken of employee responses and observations about the use of the intranet.

The interpretation and analysis of data for this study evolved over several stages, following closely the hermeneutic process used by Markus (1994). The use of hermeneutics in IS studies is well established (Lee, 1994; Myers, 1997). The hermeneutic process adopted by this study allowed the researchers to reflect and improve the interpretation of findings over three main stages of data collection based on the theoretical backdrop described in the previous section.

## Background to the case

Alliance and Leicester was the ninth largest UK bank according to assets in 2005. Its products range from cash handling, retail and commercial banking to personal loans. The business strategy was to be a ‘Direct bank with high street presence’ using technology to migrate customer support from local branches to centralised direct channels such as the telephone and the internet. The Group employed 8500 full time staff of whom approximately 66% are front-line supporting customers and the rest are head office employees responsible for the infrastructure and new business development. The appointment in 2001 of a new CEO marks a change in management style and culture in the organisation towards greater integration and transparency.

The intranet was fairly young then. The first version took six weeks to build and launch and was commissioned by the new Chairman in 1999 to mark the launch of his new business strategy ‘Strategy 2000’. The intranet at the time was seen as a way to communicate this strategy across the organisation. Its high profile role in these early stages led to the quick recognition of its key role as a communication channel inside the organisation. The new strategy to use technology to serve customers and drive cost savings in office space, personnel and paperwork meant that the intranet also became quickly recognised as a key element in delivering the business objectives set by the executive team. The vision for the intranet was to be a self-service platform and single point of reference for all employees. The objective was to offer content for all organisational functions through key applications and services and social content, such as an online staff magazine. Some of the services offered included room and travel booking, HR calculators, document warehouse, all HR processes including pay slips, holiday booking, and employee appraisals, among others.

The central intranet team reported to the head of corporate communications, who was a member of A&L’s board and reports to the CEO. The three members of the intranet team managed the work of 11 staff from other departments and coordinated a community of 80 publishers. The organisation adopted a decentralised publishing model where local teams publish content ‘under license’ and observe standards set by a central intranet team. All roles and responsibilities are defined and made clear to stakeholders. Content publishing is regulated by a number of policies. A site license specifies the terms and conditions under which local teams can publish. Other documents explain the roles and responsibilities of everyone involved in creating content, from authors and content owners to senior sponsors. Important decisions are taken at one of a number of pre-established committees.

## Evidence institutionalisation in 2005

Zucker (1987) describes institutionalised behaviour as behaviour that have attained ‘(1) a rule-like, social fact quality of an organized pattern of action (exterior) and (2) an embedding in formal structures, such as formal aspects of organisations that are not tied to particular actors or situations (non-personal/objective)’ (p. 444). The use of technology in organisations may develop similar properties. From this perspective, intranets become institutionalised when its use becomes unnoticed by employees and the technology appears to be ‘invisible’ to the users (Silva and Backhouse, 1997; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2000). Case data show that in 2005 the use of the intranet at A&L exhibited these characteristics. It was (a) widely recognised as part of the normal functioning of the bank and (b) part of the formal policies governing the operating procedures of the bank.

The intranet had evolved from being just another application on the desktop of employees in 1999, to become in 2005 the primary communications channel and a gateway for key business resources. By policy, all employees had been given access to the intranet (either through their own desktop computers or through shared computers or kiosks in public places around the buildings). The intranet was officially considered to be the main communication channel in the bank and for that reason employees were expected to read the intranet on a regular basis to keep up to date with company developments (senior managers were asked to encourage the use of the intranet when speaking to members of their teams). The culture of the organisation also encouraged employees to use the intranet as a first resource for finding information. Employees knew that the intranet should take precedent over telephone or email for finding information. Case data show for example that it was a common practice for employees to redirect colleagues to the intranet to find information in order to avoid unnecessary meetings or time spent on the telephone.

These expectations and agreed conventions about how to use the intranet to ‘do things’ had become more deeply engraved in employee behaviour. In 2005, observations showed that employees used the intranet in routine tasks without much conscious awareness of its use. As the intranet became more embedded in routine work, employees became more familiar with it, taking its use for granted as a normal feature of their organisational life. Over time, the use of the intranet became unnoticed by employees as illustrated by one employee saying that ‘the intranet is now like the carpet and we no longer think about it’. This notion of the intranet as an ‘invisible’ feature of organisational life was a recurrent theme in the interviews and focus groups in data collected in 2005. It became more noticeable when employees were asked to ‘imagine’ working at A&L without the support of the intranet. Some employees felt strongly that they would not be able to be efficient without the intranet as illustrated by the following quote from an employee ‘if I went to another organisation that didn’t have all the information I need on the intranet, it would be quite daunting to know where to find it’. For this employee, the use of the intranet had become scripted in her daily routine and habitualised actions, making it difficult to ‘imagine normal work without this tool ‘ready-at-hand’. This perception of the intranet, shared by several employees at A&L, reflects what Schutz (1962) and Garfinkel (1963) refer to as a ‘natural fact of everyday life’. For these employees, the intranet had become just another feature of normal organisational life which became over time familiar and unnoticed.

At the management level, the intranet had also become deeply engraved in the functioning of the organisation. The Board Director responsible for the intranet said ‘I think the intranet has become fundamental to our business. It has grown to become the principle communication channel in the Group. But it is grown beyond that and it now has a number of business critical activities run from it. I couldn’t imagine and certainly wouldn’t want to be running A&L without the intranet’. Furthermore, the intranet was in 2005 formally rated as ‘business critical’ by the Board of the bank and became part of its disaster recovery planning in case of a major emergency. The intranet manager said in 2005 that this rating ‘is a very crude but objective measure of its importance’. The level of embeddedness of the intranet in the functioning of the organisation is also demonstrated by several key business applications operating through the intranet, examples include all the HR functions (pay slips, appraisals, holiday booking, etc.), performance indicators for branches and updates on mortgage interest rates used in call centres.

## Processes of institutionalisation

The section above shows the level of institutionalisation of the intranet in 2005; however, the main purpose of this study is to investigate the processes underpinning the institutionalisation of the intranet in the bank. The longitudinal study of the evolution of the intranet at A&L over a period of 5 years made visible the ongoing mutual changes to the technology and the organisation. This process of interplay and mutual changes allowed the intranet to better reflect the needs of the employees, but at the same time, it also allowed existing work practices to adapt to the possibilities of the new technology. It was also clear that the intranet increasingly reflected the values and interests of the organisation, which over time also changed through the way in which the intranet was used. This interplay shaped both the intranet and the organisation in a way that each better reflected each other. Similarities were visible in terms of the values, language, tone, content, functions, design, etc. Over time, the two became more closely coupled; making it difficult to distinguish processes inside and outside the intranet environment, both existed as part of the normal functioning of the organisation.

The research question asked how intranets become institutionalised in organisations and this section aims to answer that question. The theoretical background used in this study explains institutionalisation as a process driven by the development of constitutive expectations (perception that the intranet is part of the expected formal functioning of the organisation) and background expectations (intranet becoming taken for granted by employees and part of the world known in common). As described through the analysis of the data below, the development of these expectations is driven by the interplay between the technology and its organisational context of use. It is this interplay and the subsequent ongoing mutual changes that allow the intranet to better reflect the organisation and the organisation to better accommodate the inherent properties of the technology.

## Background expectations: becoming taken for granted

Background expectations are produced through mutual changes to content and design which increase the resemblance between the organisation and its electronic representation on the intranet. This mirroring effect is fostered by the use of similar images, colours and logos which make the intranet more familiar to users. As more services and applications were made available on the intranet, employees used it more frequently, ultimately becoming part of their everyday routines. Gradually employees learned more about how to use the intranet and adopted it as their preferred channel for ‘doing things’ in the organisation. These continuous process of convergence in the look and feel of the intranet and organisational imagery fosters the perception that the intranet is a normal feature of organisational life, as evident by a quote from an employee ‘the fact that we don’t think about the intranet possibly shows how far it has come, rather than being that new thing’.

Analysis of the data identified five specific processes of interplay underpinning the development of background expectations. Figure 2 shows these five processes of interplay and key micro changes underpinning the development of background expectations.

## Familiarity and normality

The intranet was initially designed to represent the values of the bank; however, over time its widespread use also affected the perception of these values. This ongoing interplay increased the sense of familiarity with the system, and its perception as a normal feature of organisational life. At A&L, the design and structure of the intranet changed several times between 2000 and 2005. During this period the structure of content on the homepage changed three times. Each change was aimed to better reflect the needs of the business and the interests of the organisation. Changes included different use of colours (especially to increase the use of orange, because this was the corporate colour), the use of logos (new logo and use of other corporate imagery) and the information architecture (changed from a nine box grid to a navigation bar at the top and side to reflect the various areas of the business). Several other changes took place on an ongoing basis to adapt the intranet to changes in the business. The language and tone used on the intranet also changed over time, to more closely reflect the language and tone of face-to-face and offline communication.

## Routine and habitus

As more content and functionality were added to the intranet in response to the changing needs of the employees, its usage increased and became more integral to the daily work and routines of employees. During the intranet’s first two years, many functions and services migrated online including small applications to support routine activities, such as ‘room bookings’ and ‘taxi bookings’. More critical applications, such as self-service HR functions, were also made available, along with ‘Forms Warehouse’ to help employees find all types of forms and documentation. These services shaped the use of intranet, but simultaneously the internal processes also changed to meet the requirements and new possibilities of the intranet.

The increasing number of services on the intranet also made its use more necessary on a daily basis by the employees; contributing over time to the development of routines which eventually made the use of those services taken for granted. The use of these services progressively became more automatic and unreflective, as illustrated by an employee who, when asked about her use of the intranet, said: ‘I don’t use the intranet that much’. However, after some reflection she realised that ‘actually, now that I think about it, I use the intranet quite a lot more than I thought’. She further acknowledged many of the services used on a daily basis ‘I use branch details at least three times every day. I check ‘‘Competitions’’ and new products and services, what is happening at A&L. I also use ‘‘Credit checks’’ in Forms Warehouse quite a lot’.

## Usefulness and ease-of-use

As the intranet became more useful and easy to use, employees suggested more changes and proposed new functions according to emerging new needs. The intranet team placed a great deal of attention on collecting feedback from users and made regular changes to the intranet in response to this. Usability testing was performed annually, based on focus groups and card sorting exercises, and expert reviews were conducted on an ongoing basis by an in-house consultant to improve navigation and information architecture.

This effort to collect and respond to user feedback led to a complete restructuring of the homepage at the end of 2001 and further ongoing changes. New content and services were introduced regularly in response to user demand, making the intranet more useful for a larger number of employees. Initially users would request that existing processes and information be placed online, but over time these requests became more sophisticated and specific to the new medium. In 2004 a new major restructuring and review of branding was required to deal with the growth and improve the identity alignment between on and off line channels. As the understanding of the new medium grew, employees were able to request changes that would make the intranet easier to use and more aligned with their interests.

## Preference and persistence

The intranet became over time the preferred channel of communication and gateway to organisational resources for most employees. It was initially seen as a new channel to support internal communication; however, over time, employees started to prefer its use, instead of other alternative medium for communicating and finding out about changes in the business. Over time, the intranet stopped being perceived as ‘new’ but rather as a preferred medium for ‘doing things’. This view became more persistent through training of employees, especially new employees who were presented with the intranet as a fact and were not aware of alternative methods of ‘doing things’.

## Reflects reality and fairness

As the intranet evolved more areas of the business became represented, improving the balance between all areas of the organisation. However, initially the intranet reflected primarily the worldview of senior management and the corporate communications team. Various areas of the organisation felt that they were not fairly represented. For example, still in 2004 there was the perception among some employees that content from departments closer to headquarters had greater exposure on the homepage, as evident in the following quote ‘although I use the intranet a lot I feel it is more Carlton Park [headquarters] biased and it would be nice to have some more news from outside Leicester!’ This perception changed over time as more areas of the organisation dedicated resources to publish content and use the intranet as part of their ongoing activities.

## Constitutive expectations: part of the formal functioning

Constitutive expectations developed through the embedding of the intranet in the formal functioning of the organisation. Roles and responsibilities became increasingly formalised with more policies and governance controls being enforced and entangled with other policies in the organisation. Over time, the role and purpose of the intranet in the business became more formally recognised by senior management. Data show that in 2005 the intranet was recognised as the central repository of information, as one employee said ‘for me, it is like a bible. We direct people to the intranet. They need to check the information there first; I don’t have time to go round and repeat everything’. This perception is very distant from the views in 2000 when the intranet was far from being recognised as a ‘bible’ or a legitimate source of reliable information. Five processes of interplay were identified to underpin the embedding of the intranet in the formal functioning of the organisation and the development of constitutive expectations. ## Governance, roles and responsibilities

The intranet initially mirrored the governance model of the organisation but as it evolved, new roles and responsibilities had to be created specifically for managing the intranet environment. The new roles for the intranet then also impacted on roles outside the intranet environment, because responsibility for the intranet became widespread in the organisation.

Content was initially published on the intranet by the internal communications team. This team contributed with content as an extension of their normal jobs, but these responsibilities were not formally recognised as part of their job descriptions. The contributions from employees from other departments were also only informally recognised. However, as the number of contributors expanded, a clearer and more specific governance model for the intranet had to be developed. In 2000, a new governance model was implemented based on the recommendation from an external consultant. His report recommended a decentralised publishing environment with strong control from the centre to enforce common standards. This model was implemented and new roles – such as ‘Site sponsor’, ‘Site owner’, ‘Publisher’ and ‘Author’ – became formally accepted and recognised in the organisation. These roles were also included in the job descriptions and annual objectives of the employees.

In 2004 and 2005, as the intranet expanded, the roles associated with the management of the intranet started to permeate other areas of the organisation. Some nonintranet related roles, such as supervisors in call centres, had to include the use of the intranet as part of their job descriptions. Senior managers also became responsible for training new staff on how to use the intranet. The roles for publishers and authors also became more regulated. This initially consisted of high-level guidelines contained within the existing IT policy. However, in 2000, a new ‘Web Policy’ formalised and regulated the publishing of content on the intranet. These intranet-specific policies became linked with wider business policies in 2005. One example is the Operational Risk Group which decided to include the intranet as part of their risk framework.

## Controls, policy and standards

Policies and controls for the intranet become more and more intertwined with wider business policies. A&L initially developed specific policies to regulate the intranet, but over time the intranet became also subject to business policies from other areas of the organisation, which in turn also had to change to reflect the intranet’s intrinsic role in supporting the business. There were several policies to regulate many aspects of the intranet, including the use of imagery and logos, compliance with external regulations such as the Data Protection Act, Writing for the Web and technical requirements, among others. Employees were aware of this high level of control through the enforcement of policies and monitoring; one said ‘although we don<sup>0</sup>t think about the mechanics of the intranet, we know that there is red tape. We know someone is writing the brief, their manager is probably checking in, along with legal and compliance if necessary. Although we don’t necessarily have to sit down and think about it, we always take it as correct. At A&L we are just stringent with the checks to make sure everything is correct. That is just our culture – everything is checked and double checked and triple checked’. As illustrated by this quote, the existence of standards and other policies to regulate the publishing of content contributed to the general perception that the intranet was part of the expected functioning of the bank.

## Senior support/regulatory enforcement

As senior leaders became more aware of the intranet as a key channel to communicate and manage organisational change, many set up their own intranet pages. CEO Richard Pym’s blog, ‘Pym’s page’, was always in the top five mostseen sites. Richard Pym used his blog to convey corporate messages but also to promote a new organisational culture that was more open, straightforward and transparent. The intranet played a role in changing the perception about senior leaders and the culture of the organisation. Case data show that the perception of strong senior support for the intranet and enforcement of standards fostered a general sense that the intranet had become part of the way of working in the organisation.

## Purpose/aligning with business

A&L’s intranet was initially very closely aligned with business objectives because it was set up in 2000 to promote the new business strategy in force at the time. Early in 1999, the initial purpose of the intranet was to promote the new business strategy, ‘Strategy 2000’. The intranet was designed to respond to this particular objective but over time it changed to respond to other business needs and objectives. The intranet became, for example, essential to the HR function by supporting the critical business issue of harmonising terms of employment across various parts of the organisation. The HR department actually used the intranet more extensively and developed it into a full selfservice function to enable employees to deal with HR processes themselves through the intranet. The site was very popular and the Executive Board saw it as an example of cost savings, to be followed by other parts of the business. As the intranet evolved, other organisational functions adopted the intranet and, in 2002, the intranet became formally part of A&L’s business strategy to be a ‘direct bank with high street presence’. This focus on direct channels forced the intranet into becoming more intrinsically related to the business strategy. Data show that establishing a close link between the purpose of the intranet and wider business objectives contributed to the development of an overall perception that the intranet was part of the expected formal functioning of the organisation.

## Importance and benefits

When the intranet was first launched in 2000 its benefits and importance were defined purely in terms of its role as a communication channel. The intranet gained more importance when in 2001 it played a central role in dealing with a large redundancy programme and the harmonisation of the terms of employment across the organisation. Despite its high profile in supporting these two major projects, the intranet only became recognised as a business tool later in 2002 when savings and efficiency gains were demonstrated as a result of the new HR.online site. The perception of the intranet as a business tool grew when considerable savings were realised from stopping the print of the staff magazine (SpectrumLive) and the print of internal forms, which had become electronically available through ‘FormsWarehouse’. As the intranet evolved, the intangible effects of the intranet to the organisation became more apparent and recognised by senior management, developing a clearer perception of the possible value of the intranet to stakeholders.

## Summary, conclusion and contributions

This study has found two types of processes of institutionalisation of technology in organisations: (1) processes that operate at the level of constitutive expectations by embedding the technology in the expected formal functioning of the organisation; and (2) processes that operate at the level of background expectations by embedding the technology in the routines and habits of employees. Case data reveal five processes of interplay between an intranet and its organisational context of use, responsible for the development of constitutive expectations and five processes responsible for the development of background expectations. Figure 4 represents these processes of interplay. Table 1 explains each process of interplay in more detail.

These findings offer a distinct view of institutionalisation as a process of generation of institutionalised behaviour through the gradual embedding of technology in the habits and routines of employees in an organisation. This generative view of institutionalisation is distinctive because it explains institutionalisation as a process emerging bottom-up, building on changes in behaviour at the microlevel. Just as Scott (2005) concentrates at the ‘macro-level’ in his institutional analysis and ‘contribution to a theoretical research program’, this paper aims to make a contribution to the growing body of research on the ‘micro foundations’ of the institutionalisation process (Zucker and Darby, 2005; Powell and Colyvas, 2008).

Calls for attention to micro-level approaches to institutions and to the process of institutionalisation have been made for some time (Zucker, 1977, 1983) but only recently has this level of analysis become more mainstream in academic research. A recent and inspiring review of this approach by Powell and Colyvas (2008) acknowledges that ‘In our view these macro lines of analysis could also profit from a micro motor’. They further attain that ‘Such a motor would involve theories that attend to enaction, interpretation, translation, and meaning. Institutions are sustained, altered, and extinguished as they are enacted by individuals in concrete social situations’.

Although the macro and the micro levels of analysis are inherently related, in the sense that institutionalised practices at the macro level require continuous replication of processes at the micro level or risks being replaced by new practices (Zucker, 1988), studies at the micro level offer distinct insights. Micro-level research tends to focus and adopt individual and organisational routines as their unit of analysis (Pentland and Feldman, 2005). On the other hand, studies at the macro level tend to focus for example on transnational transfers of organisational practices (Kostova, 1999).

Micro-level studies also tend to follow changes in individual behaviour over time to capture the longitudinal development of institutionalised practices. This effort to capture the processes by which institutions are created and maintained through ‘institutional work’ (Lawrence and Suddaby, 2006) has, for example, shed light in explaining institutionalising practices in e-Government (Azad and Faraj, 2009). There is a growing interest in looking at micro-level processes of institutionalisation; for example the body of work on ‘institutional entrepreneurs’ studies the role of individual agency in changing established status quo and institutionalised practices (Maguire et al., 2004; Wang and Swanson, 2007).

This focus on the individual, as the epicentre of institutional activity is implicit in more studies on institutionalisation. Green Jr (2004) and Green et al. (2009) for example find that managers play an active role in enacting and diffusing institutionalised practices through the rhetoric used to communicate ideas and negotiate their interactions with other organisational actors. In studies more close to the IS field, this central role of language in forming organising visions has been found to drive technology diffusion (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Ramiller and Swanson, 2003; Currie, 2004). Studies of power have also highlighted the central role of individuals in the institutionalisation process (Lawrence et al., 2005; Silva, 2007).

Therefore, a major contribution of this study is to strengthen this view of institutionalisation as a phenomenological and cognitive process primarily centred in the minds and behaviour of individuals, rather than in the social or organisational arrangements as conduits for disseminating practices across social groups. Towards this end, the study builds and expands two concepts originally developed by Zucker (1986). The concept of background expectations is enhanced using the work of Schutz (1962), and the concept of constitutive expectations is enhanced using the work of Garfinkel (1967). Case data are interpreted against this theoretical backdrop revealing ongoing changes to the intranet as a technology (as it increasingly reflects employee expectations and interests) and to the working practices associated with its use (as they evolve to accommodate the inherent properties of the technology). This ongoing interplay between the technology and its organisational context of use is found to underpin the development of familiarity with the technology by employees (background expectations) and to underpin its embedding in the expected formal functioning of the organisation (constitutive expectations). Although generalisation is not claimed based on this single case study, it is expected that these two concepts will offer a solid basis for further studies exploring the embedding of technology in work practices and employee behaviour.

From this perspective, this study contributes to the growing body of work explaining processes, rather than outcomes of institutionalisation. As indicated by Zucker (1991), institutional research has tended to focus on the effects rather than the process of institutionalisation, which largely remains a ‘black box’. More studies are needed to explain the longitudinal ‘life-course’ of institutions (Farjoun, 2002) and the mechanisms that support the development and maintenance of these institutions (Lawrence et al., 2001). This is rather important in the IS field since some the technologies only now have become more mainstream in organisations. Technologies such as intranets have been deployed in organisations since the end of the 1990s but it has only been in the last 5 years that these technologies have been adopted more widely and taken more seriously by organisations. It is therefore vital to trace the impact of these technologies in the functioning of organisations.

Its account of the evolution of an intranet in a bank over a period of 5 years offers a substantive contribution to the IS literature on intranets. This body of research grew significantly around the year 2000 when these technologies first appeared in organisations, but attention to the study of intranets decreased in the last few years. Recently, the rise of social media and Web 2.0 has revived interest in this area of research with some studies highlighting the growing importance of intranets to the functioning of organisations (Martini et al., 2009).

From a methodological point of view, this research provides a basis for more studies taking a longitudinal view of technology in organisations. It provides suggestions for data collection and analysis as well as an approach to the use of theory to interpret empirical data over time. It is informed and follows the suggested research approach by Markus (1983) to support the historical reconstruction of an intranet in a bank in the UK over a period of 5 years. However, this study follows events more closely by unravelling changes to the intranet and its organisational context of use. It builds on the interpretive research tradition and follows the single longitudinal qualitative case study research strategy.

## About the author

Joa˜o Baptista (John) is Assistant Professor of Information Systems at Warwick Business School. His research focuses on the use of technology to support communication and collaboration in organisation. He has specific expertise in the adoption and management of intranets in organisations gained from his long-term involvement with the Intranet Benchmarking Forum. John has a Ph.D. and M.Sc. in Information Systems from the London School of Economics. Before his Ph.D., John worked as Director of New Technologies at the UK Conservative Party, as IT manager in an insurance company and the World Expo 98.
