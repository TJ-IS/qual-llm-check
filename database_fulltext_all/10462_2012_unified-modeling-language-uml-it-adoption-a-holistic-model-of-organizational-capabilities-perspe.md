# Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Modeling Language (UML) IT adoption — A holistic model of organizational capabilities perspective

## a b s t r a c t

This study develops an integrated research model to examine various factors affecting the IT adoption in the context of the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Modeling Language (UML). UML is one type of business process modeling techniques, which in turn is a key aspect of the business process reengineering. The proposed research model is based on IT adoption framework and organizational culture theory. The model identi<sup>fi</sup>es fourteen variables, covering seven broad categories (IT characteristics, organization technology, environment, organization structure, organization process, organization culture, and project culture) that could potentially impact UML adoption in organizations. This comprehensive conceptual model is further validated by survey data collected from 251 North American organizations across <sup>fi</sup>ve different industries. Our results support the proposed conceptualization and shed new light on the key factors associated with <sup>fi</sup>rms' adoption of UML technologies. Theoretical and managerial implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of understanding Information Technology (IT) adoption is well documented [1]. Considerable scholarly research has focused on investigating the impact of one or several of these factors (e.g., IT characteristics, organization technology, environment, organization structure, organization process, and organization culture) in different environmental settings [32,57,58,76]. Extant research has recognized the importance of technological, organizational, and environmental factors (TOE framework) in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing IT adoption [8,14,80,83]. In spite of prior research that has found strong empirical support for the TOE framework, much fruitful theoretical work remains to be conducted. For instance, previous studies have proposed that IT adoption is affected by organization structure [14,17,60], organization process [12,14,56,76] and organization culture as well as project culture [52,60,80,84].

In spite of extensive prior studies, there is a lack of comprehensive and integrative understanding, from the organizational culture perspective, on the IT adoption process, which is crucial for both practitioners and researchers in terms of generating deeper understanding of IT adoption. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this void, we extend the TOE framework in our research to incorporate organizational culture theory such as organization structure, organization process, organization culture, and project culture. Thus, practitioners may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the holistic analysis of the determinants of IT adoption, and managers interested in introducing new technologies may be able to understand and act more effectively in terms of how to better facilitate IT adoption.

The objective of this paper is therefore to identify a holistic IT adoption model to investigate three research questions on how various organizational factors will impact the IT adoption process. The purpose of this study is threefold. First, it seeks to investigate whether the technological, organizational, and environmental antecedents tailored for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context affect IT adoption. Second, it aims to explore whether various organizational idiosyncratic factors (organization structure, organization process and organization culture) determine their IT adoption. Third, it attempts to show the superiority of the holistic IT adoption model as compared to a traditional TOE framework.

These research questions are examined in the context of Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Modeling Language (UML) adoption using survey data collected in the United States across <sup>fi</sup>ve industries. UML is a visual and graphical modeling language and has been increasingly used in the past decade in software engineering and e-commerce [1], enterprise modeling, business engineering, process analysis and system con<sup>fi</sup>guration [81]. The adoption of UML in organizational computing represents a major change in information systems development and implementation [27,72]. Despite the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and its promotion by many industry leaders and the Object Management Group (OMG), the adoption of UML has progressed slowly [71]. High level of complexity of UML makes learning and adopting UML problematic, especially when IT people were lacking of the prerequisite skills [24]. In practice, IT professionals often draw diagrams with the symbols provided by the UML tool, but without the meanings those symbols are intended to provide. Dzidek et al. [27] argued that there is little reported evaluation of the adoption of UML. Moreover, to date, most of the studies on UML are technically oriented [6], and there is little empirical research on UML adoption reported. Using UML adoption as a vehicle to study technology adoption in organizations will shed light on better understanding the adoption of this important technology in an organizational setting.

Our results suggest that technology characteristics, organization technology, and organization environment strongly affect UML adoption. In addition, larger organizations and organizations with a higher level of process maturity and strong presence of process champion are more likely to adopt UML. However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that some of the dimensions of both organizational and project cultures positively affect UML adoption while other dimensions of cultures have no direct impact on UML adoption.

This study makes an important theoretical contribution to IT adoption literature by being the <sup>fi</sup>rst to construct and test a comprehensive and integrated model integrating both organizational culture theory and the TOE framework. Move over, we argue that the proposed research framework for UML adoption is developed based on matching type of innovation for UML (hybrid innovation type) with the viable diffusion approach. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that our holistic UML adoption model is superior to the traditional TOE framework in predictive power. In addition, this study also adds to the literature on UML adoption across varied company sizes and in different industries. Despite its numerous perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, UML has met with relatively slow acceptance [71]. This paper, therefore, provides important managerial implications for both developers and managers in better understanding the driving forces of adopting UML, and thus implementing such applications more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently.

The paper will proceed as follows. First, we review relevant literature. We then develop our hypotheses. After developing our hypotheses, we describe our survey methodology, our multi-sector sample (N=251), and our regression analyses. We then present our results and discuss the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for both researchers and practitioners. Finally, we restate and summarize our contributions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Business process reengineering and UML

When the computer was <sup>fi</sup>rst applied to business in the 1960s, it was predicted that information technology would bring organizations great bene<sup>fi</sup>t. This prediction didn't happen until the 1990s largely due to the limited applications of computer information systems to merely simply and directly simulate business process instead of bringing in quantum improvement [67]. Business process re-engineering (BPR) concept was then proposed to face this challenge. It was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the fundamental re-thinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical contemporary measures of performance such as quality, speed, costs and service. BPR is regarded as a revolution of enterprise management [32].

Many factors can affect the success of BPR and one of the key factors is business process modeling (BPM). BPM is a set of technologies and standards for the design, execution, administration, and monitoring of business processes and it also provides the ability to model and analyze speci<sup>fi</sup>c business processes [30]. One of the major BPM tools is the UML, which is used to analyze and design the object-oriented systems. UML was created in 1997 by three modeling advocates and the Object Management Group (OMG) adopted UML as a standard modeling language for object-oriented applications [10–25]. Since then, UML has been seen as the dominant set of techniques and standardized general-purpose modeling language for modeling and building complex IT systems. It offers a variety of techniques for analyzing, designing, and implementing <sup>fl</sup>exible and robust information system, which can be applied and integrated in all business processes, throughout the systems analysis and development life cycle, and across different implementation technologies and platforms. It includes a large set of graphical notation techniques to create abstract models for speci<sup>fi</sup>c systems.

According to the research conducted by Chau and Tam [14], a technology innovation's perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and perceived barriers will affect the technology adoption. UML has some signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in comparison to other modeling languages based on its distinctive characteristics [24]. The perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of UML are summarized in the Appendix A.

Batra [6] claimed that, since the introduction of the UML, there was a certain degree of agreement on a number of the important issues revolving around the building of information systems, and real progress toward creating truly better systems seemed equally possible.

Barriers to UML adoption stem from its nature of high complexity; it is not easy to understand and implement, and it is recognized that learning how to properly construct high-quality UML diagrams is a challenging task [70]. Indeed, even the OMG warns users about the complexity of implementing UML, especially for building large and complex information systems. Complexity is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Tornatzky and Klein [80] as one of the innovation characteristics of the attitude towards the use of IT innovation. Hence, UML's complexity issue will ultimately have an effect on company's adoption of UML.

Over the years, substantial research on innovation adoption has been conducted based on innovation diffusion theory; however, it has been recognized that innovation diffusion theory does not provide a complete explanation for technology diffusion in organizations [41]. According to Zmud [86], much of the inconclusiveness of prior research can be attributed to a failure to recognize the innovation attributes that can be perceived very differently according to the specif ic organizational context involved. Drawing upon literature from both organization innovation and technology adoption (see Table 1), we propose a comprehensive IT adoption research model to study UML adoption.

## 2.2. UML and type of innovations

A variety of innovations have been studied in IS research. We suggest that IS innovations can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being administrative, business process, and/or technical innovations. Administrative innovations in IS facilitate the planning, control, and coordination of the organization, or serve the same purposes within some subunit of the organization [20,86]. Examples of administrative innovations in IS include the creation of the CIO position and database administrator (DBA) function within organizations, the departmentalization of the software maintenance function, and the choice to outsource the IS function of a <sup>fi</sup>rm [3,75]. These examples are clearly not innovations in the computer and data communications technology of the organization, and thus a meaningful distinction can be drawn between administrative innovations and others that may be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as technical innovations.

Business process innovations are changes to the methods and procedures that a <sup>fi</sup>rm uses to produce a speci<sup>fi</sup>c output such as a product or service [22]. Business processes may include operational processes such as manufacturing products and coding software, or may include supporting processes such as purchasing, accounting, and technical support [63]. Examples of business process innovations include the adoption of application prototyping methods [75] and the use of MRP and ERP systems to computerize business processes [86]. Though administrative innovation may occur concurrently with a process innovation, it is not mandatory and therefore can be independent.

Technical innovations are changes to the computer hardware, software systems, and data communications technology that the organization uses. The adoption of e-business represents one example of a technical innovation (e.g., [85]). Other similar examples of technical innovations include the use of inter-organizational systems such as electronic data interchange (EDI) [58], and the adoption of open systems [14]. Innovations of this type are distinct from changes to administrative structure, and to changes in business processes. Here, the central aspect of the innovation is the technical change, not the administrative or business process change.

Some innovations could be considered as a hybrid type of innovation, which possesses the characteristics of more than one type of innovation [26].

UML, the focus of this study, can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a hybrid of both business process and technical innovations, one that represents not only a change to the business processes but also a change to the soft ware system and data communications technology of the organization.

UML, an OOSAD method, uses a use-case driven, architecture centric, iterative and incremental information systems development approach rather than employs either data or process-centered and waterfall information systems development approach embodied in the traditional SAD methods. In a word, UML changes how IT professionals analyze and design the information systems. As such, UML adoption will create major changes in the business processes at both project and organizational levels.

Technically, UML is an innovative modeling tool in systems analysis and design, which can be used to model just about any type of application, running on any type and combination of hardware, operating system, programming language, and network. Built upon fundamental OO concepts including class and operation, UML is a natural <sup>fi</sup>t for object-oriented languages and environments such as C++, Java, and the recent C#, but you can use it to model non-OO applications as well in, for example, Fortran, VB, or COBOL. In this sense, UML can also be regarded as a technical innovation. Thus UML can be de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as a hybrid of both business process and technical innovations.

## 2.3. Matching adoption models to innovation types (UML)

With different types of innovations to study, a variety of approaches are needed to more fully understand their adoption. Indeed, many scholars have noted that it is unlikely that a single theoretical explanation for the organizational adoption and diffusion of innovations can be developed [44,85]. Existing theories must be re<sup>fi</sup>ned to match the speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation and the characteristics of that innovation [14,26].

We observe that the organizational adoption of administrative innovations has been investigated using theories that explain organizational forms and practices. Such theories are drawn from the <sup>fi</sup>elds of management and organizational psychology and focus on the planning, control, and coordination of the organization. Examples of such research include the use of institutional theory to study the outsourcing of the IS function [2] and the use of Galbraith's theory of organizational design to study the departmentalization of software development and maintenance [75].

When researching the organizational adoption of business process innovations, theories that account for the type of projects that are being performed in the business processes of the organization have been considered. Previous studies have examined the impact of organizational factors (organization culture theory) such as organization structure including <sup>fi</sup>rm size and scope, IS department size, and management structure [14,17,28,58], organization process including <sup>fi</sup>rm project maturity level, and championing of innovation [12,14,76], organizational culture comprising of positive work environment, top management leadership, and results oriented [21–28,52–82,84,85], and project culture on IT adoption [16,78].

Finally, the study of technical innovations has been undertaken using theories that explicitly take into account the characteristics of the particular technology [14]. A study of the adoption of open systems uses the technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework [14], with the authors noting that “the innovation is primarily of a technical nature” (p. 2). Elsewhere, an extensive research program to study the adoption of e-business, a technical innovation de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the use of the Internet with its associated standards and protocols, has also applied the TOE framework [83–85]. TOE framework is generally regarded as a viable approach in technical innovation diffusion research and hence can be applied as a part of the UML adoption framework.

In the current research, we propose and test a hybrid research model drawing upon both organizational culture theory and TOE framework for UML adoption.

## 2.4. Organizational culture theory

A review of prior research shows that organizational culture fac tors for IT adoption have not been suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently addressed [17,72]. Chau and Tam [14] argued culture factors of an organization can sub stantially impact the adoption or implementation of innovations. According to the organizational culture theory [66], organizational culture factors comprise of organizational structure, organizational process, and organizational culture. Since UML adoption decisions are often made at the project level, project culture (part of organizational culture) is also included in the current study.

## 2.4.1. Organization structure

The organizational structural context is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of structural resources available to support the acceptance of the innovation. These criteria include <sup>fi</sup>rm size and scope, the complexity of the managerial structure, and the quality and availability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's human resources. Bruque-Cámara et al. [12] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>rm size and scope as one of the organizational factors for facilitating the IT adoption. Lippert and Govindarajulu [45] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that <sup>fi</sup>rm size and scope, the complexity of the managerial structure, and the quality and availability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's human resources are a <sup>fi</sup>rm's resources available to support the acceptance of the innovation. Organizational structure can constrain or facilitate the adoption or implementation of innovations [14]. Teo and Pian [76] proposed organizational structural <sup>fl</sup>exibility as a facilitator for the innovation. In addition, Rogers [61] pointed out that organization size, which is used in many innovation studies as a surrogate measure for organization structure or resources, has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on adoption behavior.

## 2.4.2. Organizational processes

One of the most prominent models pertaining to organizational processes is the Capability Maturity Model (CMM). Humphrey [36] stated that the process capability maturity of organizations can greatly affect the technology change in a given organization whether in the software development area or areas like software engineering, project management, software maintenance and risk management. Depending on which capability maturity level the given organization is ranked, the likelihood that the given company adopts new IT technology will be different. This model explains that as organizations pursue process capability, it moves from being (1) an initial or adhoc process, where it is the starting point for a company to use of a new or undocumented repeat process; to (2) a managed process, where the process is at least documented suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently such that repeating the same steps may be attempted; to (3) a de<sup>fi</sup>ned process, where the process is de<sup>fi</sup>ned/con<sup>fi</sup>rmed as a standard business process; to (4) a managed process, where the process is quantitatively managed in accordance with agreed-upon metrics; and <sup>fi</sup>nally to (5) an optimized process, where the process management includes deliberate process optimization/improvement. Researchers studying innovation have also found that the presence of a process champion de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a management-level person who recognizes the usefulness of an idea to the organization and leads authority and resources for innovation throughout its development and implementation by Meyer (2000), is critical in innovation adoption [58,60]. A process champion creates the awareness and favorable impression of the innovation during the development process, which is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variable to impact successful adoption and implementation [58].

## 2.4.3. Organization culture

Schein [66] de<sup>fi</sup>ned organizational culture as a pattern of basic assumptions that are invented, discovered, or developed by a given group as it learns to cope with problems of external adaptation and internal integration and that have worked well enough to be considered valid and, therefore, taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel in reference to those problems. Various studies also suggest that organizational culture plays an important role in technology and innovation adoption. For instance, the diffusion literature asserts that organizational culture is an important organizational factor for technology adoption [60,80]. IS literature also echoes this assertion [41,52,84]. Belassi et al. [7] conducted a survey of organizations listed in the Mergent Online database organizations randomly selected from Fortune magazine's Fortune 500 list to identify the dimensions of organizational culture within the context of new product development project success. Based on their study, three dimensions were developed. These dimensions were adapted in the present study and include positive work environment, top management leadership, and results oriented.

## 2.4.4. Project culture

Shore [69] de<sup>fi</sup>ned project culture as the shared perceptions of project work practices, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by both the project leader and organizational culture. Project culture is characterized by the way in which project planning, execution, and controls are exercised; in other words, it is in alignment with the project management culture or work culture de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Baba et al. [5]. Recent studies show that project culture has a direct link with the IT adoption decisions [16,78]. Kendra and Taplin [39] suggested that project culture is represented by a set of values that form linkages among the dimensions of project success. These dimensions are common project management language, collaborative teams, and competent project managers within an organization which de<sup>fi</sup>ne the individual value links that construct a project management culture.

## 2.5. TOE framework

Researchers have proposed several models explaining various factors that impact <sup>fi</sup>rms' decisions to adopt new technologies. Many of these models address different aspects of organizational factors. For instance, the TOE framework [52,58,76,80,83] explains that three different contexts in<sup>fl</sup>uence adoption decisions. First, the technological context includes the internal and external technologies to be adopted that are relevant to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Second, the organizational context refers to the characteristics and resources, including <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources, human resources, IT staff capabilities, and existing technology. Third, the environmental context includes the size and structure of the industry, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitors, the regulatory environment, the macroeconomic context, and the regulatory environment.

## 2.5.1. Technological characteristics

The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of characteristics of innovations on the technology adoption has been studied quite extensively [43,80]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst characteristic considers the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of available technologies to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, both internal and external, which might be helpful in improving organizational productivity [45]. The adoption decision can be largely driven by the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of adopting an innovation in a specific organizational setting [14,57,77]. The second characteristic is the complexity of the new technology. The cost of migration, the technical expertise of existing IT staff members, and the degree of entrenchment with the new technology can act as barriers to the adoption process when the new technology is complex [4]. Firms delay inhouse adoption of complex technology until they have obtained the technical know-how to implement and operate it successfully [4].

## 2.5.2. Organization technology

Whether the existing organization technology infrastructure is adequate for adopting an innovative technology is also critical to its adoption [56]. Since not all innovations are relevant to an organization, the degree of relevance depends on the relative advantages and abilities to adopt [56,63]. As innovation can bring different types of changes to the existing technology, for instance, incremental changes that provide additional features to the existing product or process, or discontinuous changes that involve the development of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant new products or processes [34]. The satisfaction level towards the existing available technology, and the perception of whether the change that the new technology can bring being positively substantial are important factors affecting the adoption.

The degree of compatibility between the innovation's characteristics and the current practices of the organization also impacts new technology adoption [56,58]. Bruque-Cámara et al. [12] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that an organization's experience and knowledge in the use of basic technologies is one of the organizational factors affecting IT adoption. Most recently, Ramamurthy et al. [59] investigated key determinants of data warehouse adoption, and suggested that the existing technology structure and technical scope are among the key determinants affecting data warehouse adoption. It is evident that an innovation that induces discontinuous changes may impose large knowledge barriers that may potentially outstrip the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [7], thus negatively affecting the adoption. On the other hand, Ramamurthy et al. [59] found that when the new technology is suitable for the existing organizational technical scope, sustainable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be obtained by new technology adoption.

## 2.5.3. Environmental context

Competitive pressure has been widely recognized and empirically supported in IT adoption literature as one of the major factors in the environmental context of the TOE model [7,34]. Competitive pressure refers to the degree of stress that the company feels from competitors within the industry [55]. Adoption of a new innovation might help a <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain and sustain competitive advantages over its competitors [40,55]. Similarly, it has been explained that innovations allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve an edge in competitive environments [14]. We echo this earlier work and suggest that IT innovations are often adopted in situations of competitive pressure because IT is seen as one way that organizations can become more agile and better-positioned to respond to emerging competitive threats [35].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

In our research model, we build a hybrid research model drawing upon both TOE framework and the organizational culture theory for UML adoption based on the notion that UML adoption embraces characteristics of both business process and technical innovations. We tailor our technological, organizational, and environmental antecedents to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation, as suggested in prior research [5,14,60]. We then go beyond existing research based on the TOE framework by combining it with organization structure, organization process, and organization culture as contributing factors to IT adoption. By integrating these factors with the TOE framework, we present a more viable holistic view of IT adoption in organizations. Our research model is illustrated in Fig. 1. Detailed rationales for each of our hypotheses follow.

## 3.1. Research hypotheses

## 3.1.1. Technology characteristics

Based on the innovation diffusion theory, Cheng et al. [15] developed a research framework to identify factors in Internet banking adoption. The results of the study revealed that perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (relative advantage) and complexity are major factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the intention to adopt Internet banking services, and their <sup>fi</sup>ndings were supported by Thong [79]. An innovation possesses the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when it is superior on one or more dimensions to the product it replaces [61]. Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are also similar to the term “relative advantage”, whose impact has been included and supported in other adoption studies [15,32,44].

Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of UML include better communication between user and software developer, reusable objects, less redundancy, simpler programming of events, better understanding, gathering, and modeling of business requirements, as well as meeting real needs of users [24]. UML is a viable tool in software and systems development [60], which is a widely known process model that claims to improve development process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [1]. Given these stated potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1a. Higher levels of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of UML are positively associated with adoption of UML in organizations.

Systems have become increasingly complex, and as a result development methods have become more complex as well. The UML has been criticized for the often cited and sometimes overwhelming complexity it presents to its users and those seeking to learn to use it.

Using Rossi and Brinkkemper's [62] complexity metrics, Siau and Cao [70] completed a complexity analysis of UML and 36 other modeling techniques, <sup>fi</sup>nding that UML is indeed more complex than other techniques. According to Cooper and Zmud [19], complexity of IT will have a negative effect on IT adoption. This effect of the complexity dimension was also supported by Chau and Tam [14].

Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1b. Higher level of complexity of UML is negatively associated with adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.2. Organization technology

Organizational Technology consists of two dimensions in the context of UML adoption: technological knowledge of UML and satisfaction with existing SAD tools. The technological knowledge of an organization is part of the organizational technology context that promotes (or inhibits) adoption [83]. Technological knowledge can extend to non-IT professionals, a group that has been shown to be more likely to support and accept new technologies when they have a relatively high level of IT knowledge about technological innovations [47]. Consistent with earlier research, technological knowledge is thus an integrative construct that includes both physical and human resources [84]. Firms with a high degree of technological knowledge are better-prepared to use UML in their organization. For these reasons, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2a. Higher levels of technological knowledge are positively associated with adoption of UML in organizations.

The level of satisfaction with existing systems also plays an important role in the adoption process. Earlier research has indicated that dissatisfaction with existing technology was a factor that increased the likelihood of adoption [14]. In our case, if users are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the classic SAD tools, they would more likely switch to the object-oriented SAD tools such as UML. As such we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2b. Higher levels of satisfaction with existing systems are negatively associated with adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.3. Environment

The relationship between IT innovations and competition, or environment uncertainty, has been explored in several previous IT adoption studies, with virtually all <sup>fi</sup>ndings supporting this relationship [14,79,83,84]. We echo this earlier work and suggest that IT innovations are often adopted in situations of competitive pressure because IT is seen as one way that organizations can become more agile and better-positioned to respond to emerging competitive threats [65] Similarly, it has been explained that innovations allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve an edge in competitive environments [40,55]. We, therefore, hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. Higher levels of competitive pressure are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.4. Organization structure

In this study, we utilize the organization size as the surrogate measure to study the organization structure's impact on UML adoption [14,44]. Organization size is an important factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the adoption of technology innovation [57]. Large organizations possess the resources and skills necessary for the effective adoption of innovation [21]; small <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to wait the technology to be tested by others before deciding to adopt it to conserve resources [77]. Consequently, the size of the organization is expected to have a positive impact on UML adoption. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. Larger organizational size is positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.5. Organization process

Humphrey [36] in describing the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) stated that the process capability maturity of organizations can greatly affect the technology change in a given organization whether in the software development area or in areas like software engineering, project management, software maintenance and risk management. The maturity model proposed by Richard L. Nolan [49] and further developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University [68] stipulates <sup>fi</sup>ve process maturity levels de<sup>fi</sup>ned along the continuum of the CMM. In the maturity model, each level ranks the organization according to its standardization of processes in the subject area being assessed. SEI [68] stated that predictability, effectiveness, and control of an organization's software processes are believed to improve as the organization moves up these <sup>fi</sup>ve levels. Based on the capability maturity model [49,68], we argue that the process variable (i.e., maturity of the IT development process) should be an integral part of the comprehensive model to study UML adoption and we believe that the more mature the IT development process is, the more likely the <sup>fi</sup>rm will adopt UML. Additionally, Lee and Shim [44] found that a process champion also played a major positive role in IT adoption. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5a. Higher levels of Maturity of IT development process are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

Hypothesis 5b. Higher levels of process champion presence are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.6. Organization culture

Research suggests that strong organizational culture enables people to expect what an organization wants from them and how they are to perform to get their work done [66]. It represents the particular ways of conducting organizational business and is critical in establishing the competence of the organization [7,66]. Several studies share a key message that an appropriate organizational culture facilitates IT adoption [7]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 6a. Higher degrees of positive work environment are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

Hypothesis 6b. Higher degrees of top management leadership are negatively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

Hypothesis 6c. Higher degrees of results oriented leadership style are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

## 3.1.7. Project culture

Because project culture is an area that all projects have in common, it can have a large impact on project success. It is characterized by the way in which project planning, execution, and controls are exercised; in other words, it is in alignment with the project management culture or work culture de<sup>fi</sup>ned by [5]. Kendra and Taplin [39] suggested that project culture is represented by a set of values that form linkages among the dimensions of project success. These dimensions are project manager competencies, common project management language, business processes, and project organization structures within an organization that de<sup>fi</sup>ne the individual value links that construct a project management culture. Thus, it is an important area of study when researchers are attempting to identify factors that impact UML adoption. Although limited studies have focused on exploring the relationship between project culture and IT adoption, it can be theorized that IT adoption is more promising when project team members under competent project manager's leadership can understand and are experienced at communicating using common project management language. Based on this we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 7a. Higher degrees of common project management language are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

Hypothesis 7b. Higher degrees of collaborative teams are negatively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

Hypothesis 7c. Higher degrees of project manager competencies are positively associated with the adoption of UML in organizations.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Variables and measurement

This study used a two-part research design in developing the survey instrument in order to increase the reliability and validity of the data collected. Part one involved proposing a research framework. This process began with reviewing and analyzing previous literature, and then moved to developing the theoretical framework. These steps have been reported in Sections 2 and 3 of this paper. The next step involved constructing the questionnaire.

Our research model includes 7 constructs: technology characteristics, organization technology, environment, organization structure, organization process, organization culture, and project culture. The technology characteristics construct consists of two dimensions, perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and complexity. Our items were adopted from prior research in IT adoption [14,38]. The organization technology construct also has two dimensions: satisfaction with existing SAD and technological knowledge. We adapted these measures from prior studies as well [14,37]. The items for competitive pressure are similarly adapted from prior research [14]. The items which we utilize here for company size have been explored in various studies [37,79,80,83]. To measure organization process measures, maturity of IT development process, we adapted items which were used in prior studies by Humphrey [36]. We adopted items for process champion from the study by Lee and Shim [44]. The organization culture construct consists of three dimensions: positive work environment, top management leadership, and results oriented and items for the organization culture were adopted from the study by Belassi et al. [7]. The items for project culture involve three dimensions (common project management language, collaborative teams, and competent project managers). These dimensions were also adapted from prior research [39]. Industrial effects were controlled using the dummy variables [33].

All opinion responses in this paper were adapted from previous studies and measured on a seven-point Likert scale except for “Maturity of IT development process” and “Company size”. A total of 43 items measure the 7 constructs (independent) in our proposed UML adoption model (see Table 2).

After assembling a preliminary version of our instrument based on prior literature, we then conducted 7 interviews with key employees of 3 different companies. After conducting these steps, a series of questions addressing the key variables of the study were developed. A pilot study was conducted by distributing the preliminary questionnaire to the 17 managers of 8 different companies in a Midwestern city. Managers were asked to examine the degree to which the preliminary questionnaire captured the measured constructs and how easy or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult the preliminary questionnaire was to complete. Based on feedback received in this pilot study, minor adjustments were made in the instrument before conducting the survey.

## 4.2. Sample

The unit of analysis in this study is the organization, and as such, the subjects for this study are the IT decision makers within the organization such as the CIO, CTO, and the Director of IT. An initial sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms for inclusion in this study was randomly selected from the 2007 North American Industry Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation System (NAICS) Manual. Surveys were mailed to <sup>fi</sup>rms in <sup>fi</sup>ve different industries: retail trade, information, <sup>fi</sup>nance and insurance, professional, scienti<sup>fi</sup>c, and technical services, and health Care and Social Assistance.

A total of 1000 questionnaires were distributed in a single mailing and we send out one survey to one company only. We explicitly stated in the introduction section of our survey that respondents need to be non-adopters at the time by answering a qualifying statement “Has your <sup>fi</sup>rm adopted UML or not, if not, you would proceed with the survey and if yes, please stop the survey?” From that mailing, 268 were returned. Of the 268 responses, 251 were usable resulting in an actual response rate of 25.1%. “The 17 unusable responses did not contain suf-<sup>fi</sup>cient or viable data for further analysis (e.g., incomplete surveys (8) or non-quali<sup>fi</sup>ed respondents such as <sup>fi</sup>rms (people) who are adopters of UML (5) or non-adopters but will not be involved in the future UML adoption process (4)).” This response rate is not unusual when the unit of analysis is the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the questionnaire involves extensive organizational level questions [31]. ## 4.3. Multicollinearity

To avoid the effect of multicollinearity, we used the summated scale measures for the independent variables. We then performed several diagnostic tests to evaluate the appropriateness of the assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity. An examination of the residual plots suggested that these assumptions were appropriate. Following the advice of Menard [48], we used OLS regression to assess the presence of multicollinearity in our logistic regressions. An application of the test for multicollinearity [57] revealed that all condition indices were below 19 and all Variance In<sup>fl</sup>ation Factors were less than 2. To examine possible non-response bias, we compared responding companies and non-responding companies on the distribution of their number of employees, with a t-test showing no significant differences (Flynn, Schroeder, and Sakakibara, [29]). By the same token, we also added tests on earlier vs. later respondents for non response and results (t-tests) suggested there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences existed.

## 4.4. Reliability

Cronbach's alphas were calculated for all constructs and dimensions in the conceptual model [29]. Cronbach's alpha is based on the correlations among the indicators that comprise a measure, with higher correlations among the indicators associated with high alpha coef<sup>fi</sup>cients [53]. Cronbach's alpha is the most widely used method of reliability assessment in business research [23]. The Cronbach's alpha values for all constructs and dimensions in this study (see Table 5) exceed the suggested alpha value of 0.70 rule generally considered as “adequate” for assessing reliability in empirical research [50]. Thus, it is assumed that the scale items used in this research can be considered reliable.

## 4.5. Validity

Instrument validity includes both criterion-related validity and construct validity. Criterion-related validity is the degree to which the survey instrument correlates with one or more criteria. The expected cross-validity index (ECVI) is one measure for criterionrelated validity [42]. The ECVI values of all seven constructs (largest being 0.76) in this research are well below the value of 1, which is satisfactory in a criterion-related validity test.

We examined construct validity using a two-step procedure. First, we conducted exploratory factor analyses (EFA) for all constructs. Factor solutions were consistent with prior studies. Second, to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs, we conducted con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analyses (CFA) [29]. We allowed the latent constructs to be correlated while constraining the measurement items and their error items to be uncorrelated. This study employs CFA to test the unidimensionality of the constructs because CFA (see Table 5) is deemed to be a better technique for assessing unidimensionality than EFA [51]. Stand-alone <sup>fi</sup>t indices are used to test construct validity. They are based on the maximum likelihood <sup>fi</sup>tting function, which performs much better than those indices derived from the generalized least squares approach [35].

Stand-alone <sup>fi</sup>t indices include competitive <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and root-mean-square-error of approximation (RMSEA) [46]. Hu and Bentler [35] recommended a maximum cut-off value close to 0.06 for RMSEA. A minimum cut-off value close to 0.9 is suggested for CFI, GFI, AGFI, and IFI [9]. Also the chi-square test $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ is employed to assess the discriminant part of the construct validity $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ difference test using a signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of p=0.01 level).

## 6. Discussion

This study has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a comprehensive IT adoption model from the organizational capabilities perspective and has explored the relationship between seven organizational related constructs (i.e., IT characteristics, organization technology, environment, organization structure, organization process, organization culture, and project culture) and the UML adoption process.

Results did not support Hypothesis 6a that higher degrees of positive work environment (one of the organizational culture dimensions) are positively associated with the adoption of UML in <sup>fi</sup>rms. It is not to our surprise that positive work environment does not play a major role in UML adoption as previous studies also show insignificant results of the impact of positive work environment on IT adoption in general [16,78]. Our results also did not support Hypothesis 7c that higher degrees of project manager's competency (one of the project culture dimensions) are positively associated with the adoption of UML in <sup>fi</sup>rms. That is to say a competent project manager would not necessarily be more likely to adopt UML.

Results pertaining to Hypotheses 1a and 1b (relationship between technology characteristics and UML adoption) are in line with theoretical support for both hypotheses [19,44,79] while results from H2a and H2b help to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that organizational technology imposes signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on UML adoption. The empirical support for environment on adoption (H3) demonstrates that <sup>fi</sup>rms in highly competitive markets tend to adopt UML more quickly than <sup>fi</sup>rms in less competitive markets. Our results also indicate that larger <sup>fi</sup>rms appear to be more willing to adopt UML thanks to their better capabilities. Results pertaining to Hypotheses 5a and 5b suggest that both higher level of process maturity and strong presence of process champion facilitate the <sup>fi</sup>rms' UML adoption. Finally, Hypothesis 6b, 6c, 7a, and 7b are supported, which indicates that stronger and results oriented leadership in organizational culture and better team communication and collaborative team work of in project culture, increase the likelihood of <sup>fi</sup>rms' UML adoption.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for both managerial practitioners and academic researchers.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

To our knowledge, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to construct an integrated model of organizational capabilities on IT adoption. This is also the <sup>fi</sup>rst comprehensive research to investigate the adoption of UML, an important business process modeling tool, in various organizational contexts. From the theoretical perspective, this study underscores the importance of proposing a holistic IT adoption model. Without exploring organizational culture contexts (i.e., organization structure, organization process, organization culture, and project culture), the salient organization culture factors would not have been revealed (i.e., process maturity, project champion, and top management leadership, results oriented leadership, common project management language, and collaborative teams). As a result, this study adds to the technology innovation and adoption literature by demonstrating the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of an integrated conceptual model that incorporates organizational theory.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

IT managers can take several important steps to increase the receptivity of adopting and implementing UML. Based on our results, perception of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts plays a key role. Leadership can greatly enhance receptivity to UML through clear and convincing explanations as to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of UML. The case for reducing complexity is an appealing aspect of UML, especially if the company is large in size, which in and of itself contributes to complexity. Illustration of complexity reduction can be achieved through different alternatives. In the event that IT staff is dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the existing SAD system our research indicates receptivity to UML is increased. To strengthen that aspect of receptivity the “before” illustration should vividly point out de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies as to why that status quo is inferior to the “after” or future UML process and environment.

Technical knowledge plays a key role in adoption. If that expertise currently exists within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, receptivity of UML should be enhanced. If knowledge is lacking, adding that skill set perhaps from <sup>fi</sup>rms that have already achieved positive results with UML could, play a meaningful role in facilitating adoption. By the same token, process maturity level can be enhanced through adopting, implementing, and utilizing UML.

Competition pressure has become an increasing challenge. UML adoption is a win/win situation in that it can decrease costs at the same time that it reduce design and modeling errors. Articulating this message bene<sup>fi</sup>ts leadership's efforts to facilitate successful UML adoption.

In summary, one of the major contributions of this research is to provide a viable framework for managers to adopt and implement

UML effectively which in turn to facilitate the business process improvement.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

One limitation of this study is that it is based on a cross-sectional design. Data were collected from diverse business types across various industries categorized by [74]. However, the cross-sectional design is limited and does not eliminate all the external factors in obtaining industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c information [64]. Although industrial effect is controlled in this study using dummy variables, we still suggest that further research exploring both individual categories of industries and a broader collection of industries should be considered as a viable avenue of future study.

Another limitation is that all the measuring instruments used in this research were based on manager's perceptions. While this is a time-honored and valid operational process for measuring various constructs [72], all questionnaire surveys are limited by the truthfulness of the respondents. The validation and reliability analyses undertaken in this study provided some level of assurance of the instrument ability to capture useful measures. It is interesting if we will be able to test our model using achieved data. We also welcome future studies by our colleagues to use and test our proposed holistic IT adoption model.
