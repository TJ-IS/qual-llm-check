# A community of practice view of intervention programmes: the case of women returning to IT

Niki Panteli

University of Bath, School of Management, Bath BA2 7AY, UK, email: n.panteli@bath.ac.uk

Abstract. The study presents an intervention programme for women returning to the information technology (IT) industry following a career break. This is interpreted through the lens of the community of practice perspective. The longitudinal nature of the case study offered opportunities for in-depth investigation of participants’ experience and development during the process. Using this datum, a mid-level analytical approach is adopted. It is found that learning about the path to return to IT emerged regardless of the degree of participation in the intervention programme, and this was supported by the diversity and integration embedded in this programme. The implications of the findings for our understanding of intervention programmes are discussed.

Keywords: career breaks, community of practice, gender, interventions, longitu dinal study, women and information technology

## INTRODUCTION

The under-representation of women in information technology (IT) is well documented and discussed with empirical and theoretical studies being carried out around the world (e.g. Henwood, 1993; Klawe & Leveson, 1995; Panteli et al., 1999; Pringle et al., 2000; Trauth, 2002; Adam et al., 2004; Diamond & Whitehouse, 2007; Roan & Whitehouse, 2007; Gillard et al., 2008; Simard et al., 2008). In general, there is a consensus in the literature that though the IT sector is dynamic and fast-growing, this is still male-dominated, causing many women to decide not to enter or to leave this occupation (von Hellens & Nielsen, 2006). It is within this context of the low representation of women in IT that interventions have been encouraged in both practitioners’ (Klawe et al., 2009) and academic forums (Trauth et al., 2009; Panteli & Pen, 2010). This study takes a focus on interventions in the UK specifically designed fo women attempting to return to IT employment following a career break, i.e. women returners.

Interventions for women returners refer to schemes that encourage and support return to employment after a period of absence (Shaw et al., 1999; Cunningham & James, 2000; Houston & Marks, 2003). Further to these, this study also takes the view that interventions serve as a ‘bridge’ for the transfer of knowledge and have potentials for learning and development for the participants involved. This paper discusses the case of an intervention programme for women returners in IT that has been deemed successful by its external evaluators. This is interpreted through the lens of the community of practice (CoP) perspective tha supports the emergence of learning and development within a community that has shared interests and values (Wenger, 1998). The paper contributes to the literature by providing an assessment of the learning effects of the intervention on participants.

In what follows, the conceptual foundations of the study are discussed: first, by theorising gender under-representation in IT and then by discussing the CoP perspective and its relevance to the study of interventions in this field. Further, the case of an intervention is presented and analysed, and implications for theory and practice are identified.

## WOMEN, CAREER BREAKS AND IT – TRENDS AND ISSUES

The skills shortage in IT during the 1990s was a catalyst for research into the low numbers of women participating in the development and management of IT with several special issues organised on this topic (see, e.g. Panteli & Stack, 2004; Trauth & Howcroft, 2006; Whitehouse, 2006). Trauth’s (2006) Encyclopedia on Gender and Information Technology is the best exemplar so far of recent and emerging areas of collective research on this topic with contributions from more than a hundred leading international experts. It is, on the one hand, gratifying to note how this research area has progressed to another phase, where gender stereotypes are questioned in research underpinned by theoretical foundations and where the complex relationship between gender and technology is brought to the fore. On the other hand, it is alarming that these issues are still unresolved and, in some cases, unrecognised in societies where equality is assumed (Panteli & Stack, 2004).

Career breaks have been found to restrict career development (Procter & Padfield, 1999; Hunter, 2006; Panteli, 2006). Research also showed that career breaks are more imposing on career development the less durable the knowledge one has (McDowell, 1982). The human capital theory explains women’s limited opportunities in the labour market by arguing that women due to their interrupted pattern of employment have less human capital (e.g. training and education), and therefore, their choices in the workplace are limited (Gustafsson, 2001). In support to this, Jenkins (2006) posited that if women show that they have gained qualifications when they were out of work, this is generally viewed favourably by employers. Other perspectives, however, exist that theorise gender under-representation in IT. Social and structural factors have been found to contribute towards the low status of women in IT (Ahuja, 2002), while others provided a socio-cultural explanation pointing to the differences in different countries (Trauth, 2002; Trauth et al., 2008). Organisational practices such as the availability of flexi-time and part-time schemes, discrimination in recruitment and promotion and the occupational culture of the sector have also been used to explain women’s underrepresentation (Panteli et al., 2001).

Beside these views, other theorists argued that women should not be treated as homogeneous; rather, their heterogeneity should be used as an explanatory factor for their employment experiences (Hakim, 1996). In alignment with this line of thinking, Trauth et al. (2009) have proposed an alternative way of theorising this issue, one that considers within-gender differences. As gender identity is neither fixed nor monolithic, Trauth et al. (2009) posited that the problem of women in IT needs to be addressed at the individual level, taking account of varied needs, values and backgrounds. Such theorisation as the authors argued ‘provides a deeper understanding of the gender imbalance in the IT field’ (p. 493) and explains possible inconsistencies in women’s participation in the IT sector.

It follows that though theorisations of gender in IT have not been consistent, the existing research provides important insights on women’s under-representation in this field. These insights cover individual, social and structural factors that exercise an influence. Therefore, notwithstanding the significance of the individual identity theory that women have choices (Trauth et al., 2009), it is also important to acknowledge that structural factors (e.g. organisa tional, educational, social) may constrain these choices. The CoP perspective, which views the individual within the collective (community), allows for an analysis of both individual and structural factors and is adopted here as the sensitising lens for analysing the intervention.

## THE COP PERSPECTIVE

CoPs (Wenger, 1998) describe an activity system where members are united in action and meaning and collectively share ideas and find solutions. For this, a CoP is not simply a network of relationships; it is a community that can shape the identities of its members. Thus, CoP exists because it produces a shared practice as members engage in a collective process of learning. With their ability to acquire, share and retain knowledge, CoPs have been described as a key organisational capability (Brown & Duguid, 1991) and even as instruments of governance (Kranendonk & Kersten, 2007). Thus, according to the advocates of CoPs, mutual engagement, joint enterprise that supports the sharing of a common goal and shared repertoire, which gives points of reference, constitute the natural characteristics of CoPs (Wenger, 1998). As Venters & Wood (2007) posited, however, these characteristics can not be imposed upon a community. Instead, organisations should encourage loose organisational structures around which CoPs are likely to emerge (Thompson, 2005).

It follows that the sense of belonging is very powerful in CoPs (Kranendonk & Kersten, 2007). According to Wenger, there are three modes of belonging in CoP: engagement, imagination and alignment. Thus, in these communities, members do not only participate by actively engaging in the work practice, they also align their activities and interpretations beyond the actual practice to include wider practices and use their imagination to develop connections with the broader context. Hence, a learning architecture based on CoP should be composed of facilities to support and effectively combine these different modes of belonging in communities.

The argument is made in this paper that the CoP view adds an important perspective to understanding the impacts of an intervention programme for women who want to return to IT employment. The three modes of belonging are particularly relevant due to the background of the women involved. Women returners are women who have been on a career break often for at least several months, even years. Research has shown that when on a career break, these women often feel disconnected from the labour market and from the IT sector and that their career break contributes to a knowledge gap and reduced confidence levels (Panteli. 2006). For this, engaging them in an intervention programme may not be sufficient for their return to employment, but it is nevertheless expected to be an important step in their return-to-work journey; further, imagination and alignment are particularly useful for building and sustaining motivation and enthusiasm during an often challenging return-to-work process.

In what follows, the intervention programme is presented and analysed.

## THE INTERVENTION PROGRAMME AS THE RESEARCH SITE

The paper is based on a qualitative case study of an intervention programme, known as Equalitec. The longitudinal nature of this study offered opportunities for in-depth investigation of different schemes and of participants’ experiences and transformation during the process. The triangulation of data through a variety of methods provided a deep account of ‘knowing in practice’ (Orlikowski, 2002, p. 251), which was important for the purpose of the study.

Equalitec was an intervention project funded under the Equal Program of the European Social Fund Project entitled ‘Equalitec: Advancing Women in IT’ and is the chosen research site. This 3-year programme (2004–2007) was an industry-wide partnership consisting of several key players in the UK IT industry encompassing government departments, professional associations, women’s organisations and several high-profile employer organisations as well as universities. The partnership involved a total of 22 active partners, known as developmental partners and who actively contributed to the development of the programme, with Portia being the lead partner. Portia, which operates as a non-profit company, was set up under a government initiative in the effort to create an umbrella organisation for women’s groups working towards greater engagement and participation of women in science and technology. With its completion, the Equalitec project was featured as an example of best practice at a European Union (EU) conference in March 2008 and it gave rise to a new European project, EU Return, under the ‘Transfer of Innovation’ theme of the Lifelong Learning Program, also led by Portia.

The purpose of Equalitec was namely to assist women returners in IT to define, align and adjust their attitudes, aptitudes and aspirations so that they are well equipped to enter the labour force. Within this programme, several return-to-work schemes were introduced including opportunities for funded postgraduate study, mentoring, placement schemes and career development and retraining workshops. Several of these schemes have already been found to constitute successful approaches in the recruitment, retention and advancement of women in IT (Klawe et al., 2009; Trauth et al., 2009) and have been adopted in programmes in other countries (e.g. Computer Research Association in the USA, http://www.cra-w.org).

In the case of Equalitec, the introduction of the schemes was based on the view tha implementing strategies to support women during their career break was a fruitful and effective way to attract women back to IT employment. The participants on the programme were known as ‘beneficiaries’. According to Equalitec, beneficiaries were those who were accepted by the project team to receive assistance with career planning, training and support. They had to pass through a registration process (completion of a formal application), followed by an interview with Portia to determine eligibility: ‘To be eligible, they must have permanent UK residency, have come from an IT background and have taken a career break’ (Portia manager, interview).

## RESEARCH METHODS

The researcher acted as one of the academic partners to the programme, initially undertaking research to understand women returners’ needs (see Panteli, 2006) and thereafter having the role of an internal evaluator of the schemes introduced. Within this capacity, it was possible to collect both primary data directly from beneficiaries and organisers, as well as secondary data about the schemes, which were collected by other partners and which also aimed to capture beneficiaries’ experiences. Data were collected during the 3-year programme. Initially, official Equalitec documentation and interviews with the Portia manager and its two administrative staff provided background information to the intervention. Thereafter, the data collection focused on the five return-to-work schemes that were introduced during the intervention. Further, upon completion of the programme, an external evaluation report that was carried out by an independent institution was also accessed and used for the purpose of this study. Other documents included records of email communications among participants and organisers, which provided essential insights of the beneficiaries’ experience.

They were designed by Portia in collaboration with the scheme organiser and were given out at the end of each workshop and mentoring circles. In addition, the researcher with the help of an assistant carried out her own primary data collection that included observations and interviews and the use of logbooks. Observations took place at all workshops. In particular, the beneficiaries’ interaction and the issues they raised for discussion were noted. The other main benefit of being present at these scheduled workshops was having the opportunity to speak with the women on an informal basis during breaks about the usefulness of the workshops. In total, 12 organised events were attended with an average number of 30 participants each. Most of the participants were regular attendants at these events, which enabled us to observe changes in their confidence leve especially in how they interacted and expressed themselves.

Moreover, 16 semi-structured interviews were used to gather information about placements, postgraduate and other training courses. Participants were asked questions about the expectations they had for attending, difficulties they faced in attending and whether their expectations were met. Where possible, interviews took place face-to-face (often following the end of an event), but in other cases, especially when participants were on placement, telephone interviews were carried out.

Finally, the logbooks provided contextually specific information about each participant and sometimes their private thoughts and feelings that they might otherwise be hesitant to express in public or may see as non-essential to raise in an interview. The logbooks provided the opportunity to tap into the women’s self-reflection of their journey in the programme away from the pre-structured questions of the feedback questionnaire and to provide the participants with a medium by which they could bring up whatever they felt relevant and important to them. They were distributed to all women upon their acceptance as beneficiaries. However, only eigh logbooks were received for analysis from the women who participated at the various work packages. Even though this is a small number considering the number of beneficiaries involved, the returned logbooks did provide insightful reflections and opinions that are used in this study.

Initially, the previous data set was collected with the aim to assess the benefits of the various return-to-work schemes for the beneficiaries involved in accordance with the researcher’s role in the Equalitec programme. As the aim of the present study, however, was to explore the potentials of this same intervention programme from the CoP perspective and its impact on participants’ learning and development, appropriate analysis of the data set needed to be pursued. First, data were coded and categorised based on the aim to understand participants collective experience in the intervention programme rather than to evaluate the differen schemes in isolation. In particular, analysis at this stage aimed to identify evidence of infor mation seeking and information giving that could form part of the broader category of ‘learning and development’. At the second stage of the analysis, the characteristics of CoPs, mutual engagement, joint enterprise and shared repertoire were used to gain insight on whether a CoP had emerged. For example, as in Orr (1996), personal stories and anecdotes of experience did not only help the researcher to understand women’s backgrounds and expectations, but also served as a way to bond participants and develop shared understanding and communal support and provided evidence of development of collective knowledge. This demonstrates how individuals, through their stories, could bring their individuality to the collective and, at the same time, link the collective to their individual work. By doing so and as supported by Wenger (1998), CoP was used as a mid-level analysis that links up structure and agency together as per the theoretical viewpoint of women’s role in IT that was adopted in this study. The analytical approach therefore is consistent with Miles & Huberman (1994) in terms of ‘grouping and then conceptualising objects that have similar patterns or characteristics’ (p. 249). Finally, the analysis took a focus on the different types of belonging as supported by the CoP perspective and experienced by the participants of the programme.

## THE EMERGENCE OF A COMMUNITY

The findings showed that Equalitec provided a diverse set of opportunities to women returners in terms of awareness about IT careers and networking. In addition, the women involved gained inspiration, motivation and confidence that were important for their successful return to employment. These findings are consistent with existing literature (e.g. Kram, 1983; 1985; Caruso, 1992; Herbert & Rothwell, 2005) and are discussed in detail in Panteli & Pen (2010).

Overall, Equalitec recruited 138 beneficiaries over the course of the programme. During this period, a fifth of these beneficiaries returned to employment and resumed their career. However, this should not be taken to mean that only a small number of participants benefited. In fact, the results of the study being interpreted from a CoP perspective showed that the benefits were wider than a return to employment as such, even though understandably this was the ultimate goal for those women who participated. The study shows that the programme helped in bringing the beneficiaries out of their isolation in tackling their career situation and placing them in a group situation where they could get to know other women in similar situations. In doing so, it ultimately developed into a community not just for the dissemination of information and awareness, but also for the sharing of knowledge and experiences as well as for providing support and encouragement. Within mentoring circles, e.g. the affirmation and realisation that these women were not alone often came through from the interaction with others that also helped them to feel supported and encouraged. Encouragement also derived from the openness of members in the group, who each shared their individual situations and dilemmas. The fact that evervone was there for the same purpose and shared similar experiences, both work-related and personal, helped to alleviate their anxieties about their situation often just by talking it through and being helped by others to see the problem differently. Similarly, at career workshops and training events, beneficiaries had the opportunity to mix and network with other delegates as well as to gain knowledge and awareness of job opportunities. Often, the discussions at such events triggered women to make applications for jobs or to pursue new avenues for job searches:

The first workshop was not only inspiring and confidence boosting but made me realize that a lot of other women were in a similar position as myself. That evening I came home really energized and feeling extremely good about my restored confidence. Next day I decided to put into practice some of the lessons learnt in the workshop about the importance o networking. I managed to get an interview within a few days and a week later – a job. [Emai communication to Portia]

The previous example also shows how beneficiaries remained in touch with the community even after they had found a job. As the quote illustrates, there was a desire to share thei positive experience but also to express their gratitude to the organiser of the programme. Such stories were disseminated online to help inspire others.

The online forum derived from participants’ own suggestion to have a medium through which they could be in touch with each other regardless of their geographical dispersion. In particular, following the first mentoring circle, there was the feeling that an online forum could considerably aid continuation of the relationships built during the period of the programme participation as well as to act as an appropriate complement during the period of the programme delivery to participating members. This suggestion was addressed by Portia who arranged for an online discussion forum to be introduced.

Examples such as the aforementioned show that Equalitec emerged as a CoP, bringing individuals together with similar interests, commitments and experiences. This was supported by the wider loose structure of the programme that enabled flexibility and customisation in the organisation of the schemes (Thompson, 2005). In what follows, the different modes of belonging within the Equalitec community are presented based on evidence in the data set.

## Engagement

When one was accepted as a beneficiary, engagement with the programme, its partners, the various schemes that the programme comprised of as well as other participants, became possible. All participants were therefore exposed to the same level of engagement as they all had invitations to attend the workshops and other events. Engagement was through partici pation in on-the-job placements, in-class training, mentoring circles, workshops as well as the online forum. In addition, the programme provided opportunities for engagement with potentia employers, government departments and support organisations. Thus, engagement was at a formal as well as at an informal basis. Mentoring circles provided a formal forum for the sharing of ideas, thoughts and feelings. Further, and similarly to Orr (1996), personal stories and anecdotes of experience have informally served in this case as a vehicle of a communal understanding of the return-to-work process (see Table 2, comments 2 and 5).

## Alignment

An alignment of interests was identified at both the group level and the industry level. First, the programme did not only enable beneficiaries to get involved and engage with numerous return-to-work schemes, but also to find support in each other, as a group, and at a personal level. As a participant noted:

It felt very liberating and supportive to hear some very well qualified and intelligent women describe difficult situations that everyone in the group empathized with and offered suggestions. I had not realised until this point how widespread these problems were. [Logbook]

Evidence of such alignment of interests and experiences is also seen in Table 2 (e.g. comments 1 and 14).

Second, there was evidence of alignment with wider practices as well as needs at the industry level, which was found useful for improving learning. The career workshops focussed on emerging career sectors in IT (e.g. health informatics and e-security) and provided opportunities to women to understand what employer organisations were looking for in terms of skills and competence in these areas. In these workshops, participants had the opportunity to interact with representatives from internationally known companies such as Microsoft, Google and SONY, and these were primarily women in senior positions who provided insight abou industry needs as well as inspiration. An example of this is shown n the following logbook entry:

It was helpful to think out what my vision and objectives were. I can still remember the ‘vision that I put down: to make a positive contribution and modernisation of health services using technology available. [Logbook entry]

## Imagination

Imagination was less widespread in the data than alignment and engagement. Though at the level of engagement, all beneficiaries may be doing the same thing (e.g. taking part in the same mentoring circles), at the level of imagination their experience may be, and often is, different. According to Wenger (1998), this is not just about personal fantasies, but it is a creative process of generating new images of oneself. In the data, there was evidence of imagination, though this was limited in the logbooks returned with the completion of the programme:

I am still thinking about being an entrepreneur. This time probably by my own without my husband. [Logbook entry]

I now feel that I am at the start of a new chapter in my life. This feels very exciting and very positive. [Logbook entry]

I am not sure where my career will head from this point onwards, but I have been equipped with skills and contacts that will help me fulfil my objectives. I also have an expectation tha great things are possible if you have the insight and the support to achieve them. [Logbook, last entry]

What these quotes show is that through engagement in the programme, participants were able to imagine themselves doing great things, not just so that they get a job, but also to fulfi their life’s aspirations. Thus, the findings on imagination and alignment show that the intervention programme has given the opportunity for not only learning about how to return to employment, but also and most importantly for providing an opportunity for building a career and seeing oneself as a contributor to the industry and life in general.

## DISCUSSION: EQUALITEC AS A LEARNING PLATFORM

Equalitec, an intervention programme that was officially viewed as successful by its sponsors and external evaluators (internal documentation), is found to have emerged to a CoP, which promoted the experiences of engagement, imagination and alignment among its participants. Collectively, these experiences enhanced the sense of belongingness to a community where, as in this case, all participants including various partner organisations had the same interest in enabling the return of women back to IT employment. Even though participants were not always collocated, this did not minimise the learning experience as other means, such as the online forum, were created to enable continuity in their communications and interactions as a community. Through the lens adopted here for interpreting the case findings, it was found tha the intervention developed to a learning platform that benefited the women returners in the IT industry. The loose and flexible structure of the programme, as suggested by Venters & Wood (2007) enabled the emergence of a community in which members were engaged by both sharing and contributing to a shared activity.

The findings suggest that the diversity and integration embedded in the intervention con tributed to learning opportunities despite the degree of participants’ involvement.

## Diversity and integration

The issue of diversity embedded in the character of Equalitec is particularly important for appreciating the potential of this intervention initiative. In this programme, learning and development was achieved through a diverse range of schemes organised by the different partners involved in the programme. Equalitec allowed participants, as long as they met the definition of ‘beneficiaries’, to cross schemes, and this, as a result, created opportunities for learning. Confirming the Trauth et al. (2009) argument that intervention programme in this field should not adopt a ‘one size fits all’ approach, the diversity of the return-to-work schemes has ensured that those involved were more likely to find the type of learning and development they needed. Participants were able to go through a degree of personalised development despite signing up to the same schemes as others. They were able to ‘pick and choose’ attendance at workshops that appealed to their interests and needs as well as to ask Portia, the lead partner, for a one-to-one session on specific skills they needed. In doing so, the participants felt empowered and their choice valued and supported. Collectively, therefore, the various return-to-work schemes have had a significant impact on each individual’s re-entry to employment and their further career progression. Through a series of varied schemes, these women gained inspi ration, motivation and confidence that were needed for their successful return to employment Thus, diversity in Equalitec opened up the scope of women’s personal understanding about their own abilities and options as well as career choices available to them. This has given them a renewed perspective on their situational context. The choices that flow from this can expand as their perspective allows them to consider more options.

The integration of the return-to-work schemes was another important factor for enhancing participants’ learning and development. All schemes were part of a unified, industry-wide programme that aimed to promote return-to-work in the IT industry. The schemes implemented were not randomly organised. Mentoring. placement and workshops were all in the initia proposal of Equalitec that was submitted to the funding body in 2004. There was, however, flexibility involved in the organisation of these schemes. For example, it was not specified in the initial proposal where placements would be taking place and what kinds of career workshops would be organised and kind of speakers to be invited. Also, the opportunities for postgraduate studies, specific training courses and the online forum emerged during the course of the programme and often from beneficiaries own’ needs expressed during mentoring circles. What this shows is that one scheme was informing the other. All the schemes provided were under the general guidance and support of the Equalitec programme leader, Portia. This ultimately contributed to building coherence and complementarities between the different schemes. I follows that though Equalitec may be criticised for not having a clearly learning journey for the individuals involved, the emerging learning journey through diversity and integration was much more beneficial and relevant to the women who took part. The intervention programme has provided several opportunities for learning to women returners. There was learning about themselves as well as learning about the sector, but in each of these cases, there were also instances of individual learning within a group situation. As informed by the CoP perspective, it is the interactions between an individual beneficiary with her peers and programme organisers that can contribute to an opening up of understanding of career aspirations and capabilities and rediscovering potentials and opportunities in the industry.

In alignment with the individual identity theory on gender and IT (Trauth et al., 2009), the study showed that different participants may experience a different level of involvement within their community. As such, participants’ involvement, as well as continuity, in the programme varied. Though Equalitec recruited a large number of beneficiaries, only a fifth of them returned to employment within the period of the programme. Some of the participants did not manage to attend all events and some even withdrew from the programme. Family and financia constraints were the main reasons for participants’ withdrawal from the programme earlier than they initially anticipated. Others who found employment within the 3-year period of the programme did not feel the need to continue their participation though some did stay in touch with the programme. Different levels of involvement in the intervention programme, however, do not mean that the community is undermined or that it is of less significance. Members, as with any other community, may indeed come and go, but the community will be there to support them as well as the others. Following the end of the 3-year funded period, Equalitec continued to provide support online by giving access to resources and discussion forums.

## CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

Intervention programmes are important in improving women’s position in the IT sector. Recognising their potentials to act as a bridge for the transfer of knowledge, this study examined an intervention specifically designed for women returning to IT from a CoP perspective. The diversity and integration embedded in the intervention enabled by an industry-wide support, with the involvement of different partners, were critical for the success of the programme as this ensured that beneficiaries were more likely to find the type of support that they needed. From these, the intervention programme under consideration was found to emerge as a learning platform through the opportunities it provided for engagement, imagination and alignment.

Though the intervention, which was UK-based, examined the specific case of women returners. its implications are wider. For example. this intervention provided an importan insight on how multi-stakeholder partnership initiatives can increase benefits if complementary skills and resources from partners are matched. The programme showed that learning to return is both an experience and a process that is embedded in a wider context of activities and partnerships that pay attention to the context of the action and the external environment that may enable or constrain this action. Though therefore the programme that was studied here specifically focused on women returning to IT, there are implications for other interventions that aim to encourage women to get into the IT workforce. The study makes the case for greater collaboration and communication between different stakeholders for developing and imple menting intervention programmes as these will make for successful endeavours in this important area. Ultimately, such programmes contribute to a more holistic intervention that would incorporate both individual and industry needs.

A limitation of the study is that this examined the effects of the intervention only during the course of the programme. Thus, the study is unable to draw conclusions beyond the 3-yea timescale of the intervention. Further, the notion of a career break in the study incorporated both short and longer periods of absence from the IT workforce and the learning potentials of the intervention have not been distinguished. These provide opportunities for future research.
