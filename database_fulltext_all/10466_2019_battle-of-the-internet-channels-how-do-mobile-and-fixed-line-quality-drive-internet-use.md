# Battle of the Internet Channels: How Do Mobile and Fixed-Line Quality Drive Internet Use?

Jiao Xu,<sup>a</sup> Chris Forman,<sup>b</sup> Yu Jefrey Hu<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. We study whether and under what conditions mobile Internet networks compete with traditional fixed-line Internet networks by examining how the quality of local fixed-line Internet service influences mobile Internet adoption and use. Empirical analysis shows that a 1 megabits per second (Mbps) increase in local fixed-line Internet speed leads to a 14.9% decline in the likelihood of purchasing a mobile data plan. However, we show there are substantial diferences in the extent to which users view these channels as competitors based on individual- and location-specific characteristics and the nature of the application. Fixed-line Internet speed has no efect on the use of mobile ofline services, such as taking photos or videos, that do not require a real-time Internet connection. Younger users and those who live in areas with lower fixed-line Internet speeds find the channels to be closer competitors.

## 1. Introduction

There remains significant variance in the access to and adoption of fixed-line broadband Internet technologies across people and regions. Within the United States, as of 2016, 1 in 10 persons did not have access to 25 megabits per second (Mbps) download/3 Mbps upload fixed-line broadband Internet; these numbers become significantly larger for those living in rural regions (Federal Communications Commission 2016). Given the implications of access to digital technologies for economic growth and consumer welfare (e.g., Forman et al. 2012, Kolko 2012), such variance is a concern for policy makers and receives significant attention in the media (Levitz and Bauerlein 2017).

Mobile Internet subscriptions grew 40% annually between 2010 and 2013, roughly three times the growth rate for fixed-line broadband subscriptions (International Telecommunication Union 2013). Given continuing disparity in access to fixed-line broadband and continuing growth in mobile Internet subscriptions, there has been increasing debate whether the mobile Internet can serve as a substitute for access over a fixed connection (Federal Communications Commission 2010, 2016; McHenry 2016). On the one hand, some have argued that fixed-line and mobile Internet provide the same basic service (e.g., Free State Foundation 2016). There is some evidence that some users are using the mobile Internet as their only connection: as of 2015, 10% of U.S. users owned a smartphone but did not have high-speed Internet access through any other means than their mobile data plan (Pew Research Center 2015). Moreover, the 2010 U.S. National Broadband Plan (Federal Communications Commission 2010, p. 35) advocates that

[t]he federal government, including the FCC [Federal Communications Commission], the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA), and Congress, should make more spectrum available for existing and new wireless broadband providers in order to foster additional wireless-wireline competition at higher speed tiers.

An alternative view is that these channels have different capabilities, and so users view them as distinct services. For example, the mobile channel ofers a user access to the Internet whenever and wherever she wants (Bang et al. 2013), but with a smaller screen size and lower connection speeds. Furthermore, the mobile Internet and fixed-line Internet are used by many types of users and for many uses; it is unlikely that the two channels are viewed similarly across all circumstances.

Perhaps surprisingly, there has so far been little direct evidence to inform this debate. We ofer a framework and analyze relevant data to understand how the quality of the services ofered over the mobile and fixed-line Internet influences the adoption and use of the mobile Internet. Our empirical approach is informed by prior research in the information systems literature that has investigated how changes in the quality of one channel influence the propensity to engage in purchasing within the other (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Forman et al. 2009). We employ a novel survey data set of 28,117 U.S.-based mobile phone users in the second quarter (Q2) of 2011, combined with local Internet coverage data from the National Broadband Map (NBM). We use these data to study how the available speed of the local fixed-line and mobile Internet influences the adoption and use of the mobile Internet. Our primary identification strategy instruments for local service speeds using variables that shift the costs of deploying local mobile and fixed-line Internet services.

Although better local mobile Internet speed increases the likelihood of adopting mobile Internet plans, the impact of the local fixed-line Internet speed on mobile Internet adoption is significantly negative. A one-unit (1 Mbps) increase in mobile Internet speed leads to a 28.89% increase in the probability of choosing a mobile data plan, while a one-unit (1 Mbps) increase in fixed-line Internet speed decreases the probability of choosing a mobile data plan by 14.94%. However, there is much greater variance in fixed-line speeds across locations, and so cross-sectional variance in fixed-line speeds has a large impact on user behavior. We explore the robustness of our results to a range of alternative models. In particular, to address concerns that our results may be driven by cross-sectional variation in local factors, we group individuals into cohorts based on zip codes and estimate a pseudo-panel over two time periods (Q2 2010 and Q2 2011).

We explore the circumstances under which these channels are closer competitors. We first show that the intensity of use for mobile online activities such as emailing, social networking, and searching for information is significantly influenced by local connection speed; however, time spent on activities such as taking photos, playing games, or listening to music is not influenced by speed.<sup>1</sup> These findings are consistent with our hypothesis about how connection speeds should shape behavior.

We next explore how local and individual characteristics influence when users view mobile and fixed-line Internet as competitors. Research on the presence of a digital divide in adoption of digital technologies has focused not only on the role of local connections but also demographic characteristics such as income, education, urban/rural location, and age as factors that influence adoption and use (Dewan et al. 2005, Hsieh et al. 2008). Prior descriptive work has shown that persons that rely on the mobile Internet are more likely to belong to specific groups such as low income, low education, and the young (Pew Research Center 2015). We explore the role of two factors in particular.

First, because fixed-line and mobile Internet speeds are more comparable in areas with relatively low fixedline Internet speeds, consumers living in such areas exhibit stronger competitive cross-channel efects. This result persists when we control for local geographic characteristics that may be correlated with speed, such as local income, local education, and whether the location is in an urban area. Second, we find that younger consumers are more likely to view the mobile and fixed-line channels as competitors. While we cannot test this hypothesis directly, we believe this is because younger consumers may be more adept at overcoming the disadvantages of the mobile platform, such as screen size and connection speed. We show that age continues to shape behavior even when controlling for alternative factors that may be correlated with it, such as income and education. In sum, we provide evidence that users are not identical in how they view the two channels.

## 1.1. Literature

Our findings contribute to several lines of research. First, a significant literature has sought to identify and measure the presence of substitution or complementarity between diferent types of communication technologies. In particular, one line of work has sought to measure substitution between fixed-line and mobile voice telecommunication services. Vogelsang (2010) provides a recent survey. Many of these studies have used cross-country data to study aggregate substitution patterns between fixed-line and mobile telecommunication services, though some studies have used individual data (e.g., Rodini et al. 2003, Ward and Woroch 2010). When viewed in totality, evidence of substitution between the channels remains somewhat weak (Vogelsang 2010). More recent studies have used individual data to study substitution between voice services and short message services (Kim et al. 2010) and between diferent types of fixed-line broadband connections (Liu et al. 2010).

At present, there has been relatively little work that has attempted to test for the presence of complementarity or substitution between mobile and fixed-line broadband services. A common approach has been to use choice models to infer market competition between fixed-line and mobile broadband services in countries such as Austria (Cardona et al. 2009), Sweden (Srinuan et al. 2012), and Slovakia (Grzybowski et al. 2014). Grzybowski and Verboven (2016) use the approach developed by Gentzkow (2007) to extend this prior work to allow for complementarity and substitution across fixed-line and mobile broadband technologies. While these studies have generally shown some degree of substitution between the two channels, the estimates have varied across countries.

This line of work has significantly advanced our understanding of how users view the interrelationship between fixed-line and mobile Internet. However, studies do not show how user behavior varies based on user demographics and Internet application, as we do. More significantly, they generally do now allow for diferences in connection speed across user locations. To our knowledge, the only paper that has focused on the efects of local speed on difusion of access technology is that by Cincera et al. (2014), who look 18 European countries to study how the country-level relationship between fixed-line broadband penetration and mobile broadband is influenced by the adoption of fourthgeneration (4G) mobile technology in a given country. Our paper advances the recent literature on fixedline and mobile Internet competition by demonstrating how local speed influences channel choice. This question is important, given the unequal coverage and quality of fixed-line service across regions and the potential for mobile service to fill in coverage gaps.

Our research also contributes to broader policy discussions related to the digital divide. Researchers and policy makers have long been interested in measuring whether there exists a digital divide that shapes adoption and use of the Internet due to diferences in access to digital technologies or skills (e.g., Forman et al. 2005, Goldfarb and Prince 2008, Hsieh et al. 2008). Geographic variance in adoption can also be influenced by the presence of local network efects (Dewan et al. 2010, Agarwal et al. 2009). By examining competition between mobile and fixed-line channels, we provide evidence that informs a recent question in the digital divide debate. We also study the efects of demographic factors such as age, income, and education— variables that have played a central role in digital divide research—on whether users view diferent communications channels as competitors.

Our research contributes to an evolving literature that has sought to understand the interplay between the online and ofline worlds.<sup>2</sup> Prior research has sought to understand how electronic commerce can enable consumers to overcome shopping constraints based on economic isolation in the ofline world (Forman et al. 2009, Brynjolfsson et al. 2009). Furthermore, location shapes how consumer behave online because much online content is local (Sinai and Waldfogel 2004), and because consumer preferences are spatially correlated (Blum and Goldfarb 2006, Jank and Kannan 2005), among other reasons. Last, the interplay between online and ofline worlds is particularly important for mobile use, because mobile advertising can influence demand for local products and services (e.g., Luo et al. 2014). We believe our paper adds to this literature by demonstrating how local connection speeds influence behavior online.<sup>3</sup>

## 2. What Factors Influence Adoption and Use of the Mobile Internet?

Our framework builds on existing research that examines competition between online and ofline channels (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Forman et al. 2009) by studying how changes in the utility that consumers obtain from one channel influences their use of another. In our setting, the connection speed and service quality are not as good on the mobile Internet as they are on a fixed-line Internet network because of technology limitations. However, the fixed-line Internet has strict limitations on geographical mobility and access to a fixed-line network, while the mobile Internet has fewer of the constraints related to time and space. The screen sizes of mobile devices and fixed-line devices are also diferent, which can influence search costs (Ghose et al. 2013). We focus on one dimension of the trade-of between diferent Internet channels: Internet speed. We investigate whether mobile Internet networks compete with traditional fixed-line Internet networks by examining how the fixed-line Internet speed afects the probability and intensity of use of the mobile Internet.

## 2.1. When Are the Channels Closer Competitors?

A major theme of the channel competition literature is to understand the conditions under which two channels compete with one another (e.g., Avery et al. 2012). The answers to two questions shape the extent to which two channels compete (Avery et al. 2012): (1) the extent to which the capabilities of the new channel will substitute for those in the existing one and (2) whether the capabilities of the new channel are quickly apparent to the user. We examine three sources of heterogeneity in our data that influence the extent to which the two channels ofer real or apparent diferences in capabilities to the user: the application of the Internet, whether the fixed-line and mobile speeds in a region are similar, and the demographic characteristics of the user.

The mobile Internet is used for many diferent types of activities. Because the diferent channels ofer varying capabilities to support these diferent applications, Internet speed may not influence engagement in different mobile activities in the same way. Users can engage in two major types of mobile activities other than voice calling and text messaging. First, consumers can use mobile phones to engage in online activities that correspond with activities performed using the fixed-line Internet (e.g., checking email, searching for information, engaging in social networking, reading news, using online banking, sharing photos/videos, streaming video/TV). We label these mobile online activities. Users can also engage in mobile ofline activities that do not require real-time Internet connections (e.g., taking photos or videos). These activities do not require a connection to the traditional fixed-line Internet. We examine whether, consistent with expected user behavior, the impact of fixed-line speed will be greater on the use of mobile online activities than on mobile ofline activities.

We explore nonlinearities in the efects of fixed-line speed on mobile Internet adoption. Fixed-line speeds are generally significantly higher than those of the mobile Internet. In those cases where speed diferences are large, it will be more dificult for users to view the two channels as ofering similar capabilities. However, there is wide variance in the fixed-line speeds ofered to customers in the United States (Federal Communications Commission 2010). For example, within our data, the minimum fixed-line speed ofered to consumers in a zip code is 0.42 Mbps, while the maximum speed is 126.26 Mbps. This compares to an average mobile speed of 2.11 Mbps, which has less variance (minimum 0.52 Mbps and maximum 4.59 Mbps). We investigate whether the fixed-line and mobile Internet are viewed by users as being more similar in locations where fixedline speed is low.

Last, we study how the demographic characteristics of users influence the extent to which they view the fixed-line and mobile Internet as being close competitors. Adoption and use of the Internet are shaped by demographic characteristics such as income and education, which have been a frequent point of focus in literature on the digital divide (e.g., Goldfarb and Prince 2008). While it has in the past been dificult to obtain systematic empirical evidence to support these assertions (Bennett et al. 2008), it is widely believed that young people are more sophisticated with the use of information technology because they have had access to digital media throughout their lives (Tapscott 1998, Hargittai 2010). If this is true, then young people will be able to identify more quickly how the mobile Internet ofers capabilities similar to those of the fixedline Internet. We investigate whether the fixed-line and mobile Internet are viewed as being more similar for younger users than for older users.

## 3. Research Setting

The market for mobile service provision is very concentrated in the United States. Most large mobile service providers face minimal competition from just a few other large mobile carriers. In introducing its National Broadband Plan to improve Internet access, the Federal Communications Commission (2010) showed that 98% of the U.S. population lives in census tracts with at least one third-generation (3G) mobile provider, of which 77% live in tracts with three providers, 12% in tracts with two, and 9% in tracts with one. Similarly, a lack of competition characterizes the fixed-line Internet market. According to the National Broadband Plan, most households can obtain fixed-line service from only one of two large firms in their area: 78% can choose between two providers, 13% have access to one, and 5% have none. Only 4% of the U.S. population lives in a location with three or more suppliers (Federal Communications Commission 2010).

While the number of active carriers varies across markets, prices within carriers do not vary much across diferent local markets (German 2013). However, the quality of Internet service in terms of the connection speed varies a great deal across local markets within carriers. These variations in speed are the result of carriers’ infrastructure investments, which vary based on factors such as the cost of providing service, competition, and demand. For example, the costs of delivering Internet service is higher in regions with steep terrain or fewer roads, and where popular density is low (Kolko 2012). Service providers will also invest more in regions where there is more competition (Federal Communications Commission 2010) and where subscriber willingness to pay is higher (Koutroumpis and Leiponen 2016). Competition and demand covary with unobserved factors that we cannot easily measure. To address the concern that our results may be influenced by omitted variable bias, we perform a variety of robustness checks, including the use of instrumental variables (IV) and a panel-data model that uses zip code fixed efects for a subset of zip codes in our sample.

On average, fixed-line Internet service ofers higher speeds than mobile Internet service (Federal Communications Commission 2010). For example, in the data from the National Broadband Map that we use in this study, in the second quarter of 2011, average fixedline Internet speed is 26.12 Mbps, and average mobile Internet speed is 2.11 Mbps. The fixed-line Internet network connection is delivered by telephone wire (asymmetric digital subscriber line) or fiber-optic cable. Mobile Internet service is delivered by cellular systems based on cell towers. The “last mile” refers to the portion of the telecommunications network that physically reaches the end-user’s premises and is typically the locus of the speed bottleneck in a communication network. Mobile cellular systems have an advantage over wired systems in that they do not require the installation of physical lines. However, they also have a disadvantage in that their unguided nature makes them more susceptible to unwanted noise and signals. Spectral reuse can therefore be limited (Elmore 2004). Over the past decade or so, most mobile service providers have built 3G and 4G networks and ofer data speeds similar to low-speed fixed-line broadband (Woodford 2015). Engineers are making mobile networks increasingly eficient. However, in the foreseeable future, it is unlikely that the speed of mobile networks will match the speed of fixed-line networks (Wheeler 2014).

## 4.2. Data

To estimate our empirical models, we use data obtained from comScore MobiLens from the second quarter of 2011. The MobiLens data are constructed from detailed, quarterly surveys of the mobile activities of a nationally representative sample of U.S. mobile subscribers, who have been recruited from a large, demographically diverse population through a variety of recruitment methods. Each quarterly sample includes responses from more than 20,000 individual mobile subscribers. The survey asks mobile phone users about their consumption behaviors across a wide variety of mobile activities, including whether they adopted a mobile data plan and the frequency of engagement in diferent mobile online and mobile ofline activities.<sup>9</sup>

We combine the MobiLens data with local Internet coverage data (fixed-line and mobile Internet) obtained from the NBM. NBM data are generated and managed by the U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration, with assistance from the FCC. These data were first released in June 2010.

NBM data have been used to compare broadband availability across geographic areas and demographic groups, which can inform policies to support private sector investments in deploying broadband (National Telecommunications and Information Administration 2011). Because NBM data are collected by individual states independently, the speed and availability measures in the NBM data could vary because of the collection methods and procedures across states. We can use the state fixed efects in our model to control for any diferences in data collection.

The finest geographic level in the comScore MobiLens data is a consumer zip code; in contrast, NBM data are reported at the census block level. We match zip codes with census blocks using the point-inpolygon method by determining whether the centroid of a block fell within a polygon of a zip code (Gordon-Larsen et al. 2006, Henry and Boscoe 2008). We also conduct robustness checks with other criteria, including whether the centroid of a block was within a certain radius range (20 miles, 30 miles, 50 miles) of the centroid of a zip code. The results remain consistent. Summary statistics are shown in Table 1.

We observe the frequency of engagement in many categories of mobile activities in our data, but we do not know the actual number of minutes or actual amount of data consumption for each activity. We would like to examine the intensity of use across multiple categories of mobile online activities, rather than any particular category of activity. To do this, we apply cluster analysis and classify consumers into two clusters, according to whether they engage in mobile online activities frequently. We aggregate frequency of use variables for a list of diferent categories of mobile online activities in our data (e.g., emailing, searching for information, social networking, reading news, online banking, sharing photos/videos) using a k-medians clustering algorithm.<sup>10</sup> Specifically, on the basis of the frequency of engaging in n categories of mobile online activities, we partition all consumers into two clusters, such that each consumer belongs to the cluster with the nearest median vector (Jain and Dubes 1988).<sup>11</sup> Thus, one cluster of consumers frequently engages in all categories of mobile online activities, and the other engages in all categories of mobile online activities much less frequently.

## 4.3. Identification Strategy

A relationship between Internet speed and mobile Internet adoption and use does not, in itself, mean that speed influences adoption and use. Our research design faces three broad sets of challenges that have the potential to threaten the consistency of our estimates. These are related to potential omitted variable bias arising from unobservables varying cross-sectionally, incomplete modeling of the processes of mobile Internet service adoption, and concerns related to some of the specific assumptions we make in our model.

4.3.1. Omitted Variable Bias Arising from Cross-Sectional Variation in Unobservables. Our primary estimation approach relies on cross-sectional variation in local service quality, mobile data plan adoption, and mobile data use. However, there may exist omitted variables that are correlated both with service quality and user behavior. These may be related to local demographic characteristics that we do not explicitly control for in our regressions. For example, vendor service quality decisions will depend on expectations of profits in a service region that will be shaped by demographic characteristics (Koutroumpis and Leiponen 2016) and other local features such as wealth, commute times, and occupation. Carriers make region-specific decisions regarding things like local advertising expenditures, and individual adoption and usage decisions will be shaped by the decisions of others within the same region (Agarwal et al. 2009). These features may be correlated with local service quality and use.

In light of these concerns, we take several steps to probe the robustness of our results. We first use instrumental variables in our linear probability model. The cost of delivering Internet service difers across locations in ways that do not correlate with consumers’ adoption and use (Kolko 2012). We use this fact to identify the efect of mobile and fixed-line Internet quality on mobile Internet adoption and use. We instrument for fixed-line Internet speed with a proxy for the engineering cost of wire service in the local exchange area, calculated according to the FCC’s hybrid cost proxy model (HCPM) as of January 2000.<sup>13</sup> The HCPM is an economic engineering model that calculates the cost of providing local wire service using eficient technology, given an area’s geographic terrain and subscriber density (Prieger 2003). Proxy costs are not available for approximately one-third of the wire centers (mostly smaller carriers), so in these cases, we use the cost of the nearest wire center available. Wire center boundaries are matched to zip code areas.

Next, we instrument local mobile Internet speed by using historical coverage information in adjacent areas, namely, the number of mobile carriers that ofered the service six years ago in areas adjacent to the focal zip code (Dai 2008).<sup>14</sup> An adjacent area is a zip code within the same census city. We also conduct robustness checks with diferent definitions of adjacent areas— such as zip codes in the same county or within a certain radius range (50 miles, 30 miles)—and find consistent results. Because of the economies of scale or scope associated with providing mobile service in a focal area (Boer and Evans 1996), there is a high spatial correlation in installation and maintenance costs for mobile services across adjacent locations. Moreover, preexisting service by a carrier influences its current service plan, because of the sunk costs of its investment (Czernich et al. 2011). Therefore, the number of carriers ofering service in adjacent areas six years earlier should be closely associated with the quality of mobile service ofered in the focal area, but the available mobile service ofered six years earlier in adjacent areas should not correlate with consumers’ adoption or use decisions in our sample period.

In the online appendix, we investigate the robustness of our results to a third instrumental variable that captures the slope of the terrain in the zip code where the focal user is located. It is more dificult to extend broadband availability to places with steeper terrain (Kolko 2012, Chan et al. 2016). For example, the Government Accountability Ofice (2006, p. 19) has stated that “infrastructure build-out can be dificult in mountainous and forested areas because these areas may be dificult to reach or dificult on which to deploy the required equipment.” For these reasons, terrain is one factor that is used in the HCPM (Prieger 2003). Delivering mobile service to mountainous areas also presents problems (Green 2001, Bowling 2013). As an additional robustness check, we use this instrument in place of the number of mobile carriers ofering service in adjacent zip codes. In these alternative regressions, both the HCPM and terrain instruments capture variance in local geographic factors that influence the returns to deploying data communication services, rather than the decisions or lagged decisions of providers.

To further address concerns that our results may be influenced by omitted variables at the zip code level, we construct a pseudo-panel across two time periods by grouping individuals into cohorts based on zip codes. We first-diference the dependent and independent variables and instrument for the change in mobile and fixed-line Internet speeds using our instrumental variables. Further details are outlined in Section 5.2.

Last, to provide further confidence that our results reflect causal relationships, we examine whether the pattern of results across multiple specifications is consistent with our theory. Specifically, we compare how local fixed-line and mobile Internet speeds influenced the engagement in mobile online and ofline activities. While it does not constitute a formal falsification test, a finding that local speed has a greater efect on mobile online than ofline activities adds further confidence to our interpretation of the results.

4.3.2. Incomplete Modeling of the Processes of Mobile Internet Service Adoption. The choices of whether to adopt a mobile data plan and the intensity of use are embedded within a much broader set of decisions. Users must choose, among other things, a mobile provider; a manufacturer, type, and model of phone; and a service contract. These decisions may be related and may be correlated with other factors that could influence a user’s decision process. For example, the choice of mobile provider may influence what phone models are available to the user (e.g., an iPhone), as well as shape what types of service contracts and prices are available (e.g., a sign-on bonus during the first year or service). The full richness of this decision process would be dificult to model directly, and we do not have access to data that shape each of these user decisions, so we focus on the decision to adopt a data plan only.

These decisions—and that factors that influence them—have the potential to influence the decision to adopt a data plan and the intensity of usage. This will influence our estimates only if the unobservables are correlated with service quality. This could be possible if, for example, some service providers decide endogenously not to enter markets with characteristics that are correlated with service quality (rural markets, for example). Our inclusion of mobile provider and fixedline provider fixed efects will help to address some cross-sectional heterogeneity that is correlated with provider availability. These will capture some of the (net) benefits to users of carrier-specific oferings unrelated to service quality, such as discounts, advertising, and phone oferings. To the extent these omissions can be viewed as a source of unobserved heterogeneity, our instrumental variable results will also be informative on how these factors might be influencing our results.

In other analyses, we addressed some of these concerns by probing the robustness of our results to the use of alternative dependent variables. For example, one potential concern is that our usage results may reflect the choice of a data plan, rather than usage per se. To explore this, we ran additional analyses using the monthly data plan spending as an alternative dependent variable. Our findings are robust.

4.3.3. Other Assumptions in the Estimation Model. We make several other assumptions that may influence our results. For one, we use a linear probability model rather than nonlinear model such as a probit or logit model. We do this for several reasons. For one, as we note previously, in some models we use location-specific fixed efects. The linear probability model allows us to incorporate fixed efects without losing observations in the data set (as would be the case in a conditional fixed efects logit model). Furthermore, the use of a linear probability model allows more straightforward interpretation of marginal efects. However, one concern is that the linear probability model may predict probabilities that lie outside of the unit interval (Greene 1998). To address these concerns, as a robustness test we reestimate our model using both probit and logit. The results are qualitatively similar.

Furthermore, our usage models classify all mobile behavior into two broad categories. If there are significant diferences in the efects of service quality on usage behavior within categories, this may lead to deceptive patterns in our results. As a robustness check, we reestimate our models by individual use cases (i.e., email, news applications), and the results are robust.

Last, our baseline adoption model examines whether a user has adopted a data plan by the second quarter of 2011, while our National Broadband Map data are from June 2011. If users adopted data plans during earlier time periods, this will create an errors-in-variables situation with our speed data. To address this issue, we reestimate our econometric models using only the users who adopted data plans within the last three months. This represents 35% of our total sample. Our results are robust to the use of this subsample.

While we are unable to provide direct empirical tests, we believe that this difference in interaction results between Fixed-Line Speed and Mobile Speed may be due to the heterogeneity in uses of the mobile Internet. Even within online usage, some sources of demand for the mobile Internet will be close substitutes with the fixed-line Internet (e.g., email, search), while some will be relatively independent (e.g., maps), and some may even be complements. Changes in fixed-line speed will directly decrease the utility of the outside option among those classes of activities that are substitutes (and so increase the relative utility of the mobile Internet), however changes in mobile speed will increase the utility of all types of mobile online demand. This may lead to diferences across users in the interactions of Mobile Speed with Low Fixed-Line Speed Area and Young. We leave further examination of this phenomenon to future research.

5.4.2. Robustness Checks for Interaction Efects. When an area has a low fixed-line speed, it may difer systematically in other ways: for example, prior work has argued that provision of wireless and wireline services may be correlated with factors such as local income, education, and whether a location is urban or rural (Koutroumpis and Leiponen 2016, Prieger 2003). If that is the case, then our estimates showing nonlinear efects of fixed-line speeds may be capturing crosssectional variation in these other characteristics. Similarly, users who are young may have lower incomes and education.

To address these concerns, we estimate the model used in Table 5 using interactions of mobile and fixedline speed with local and individual-specific demographic characteristics. Table I in the online appendix presents results including interactions of fixed-line and mobile speed with variables measuring low income and education in the user’s zip code, and whether the zip code is in an urban or rural area.<sup>18</sup> While the statistical significance declines somewhat (from the 5% to the 10% level), the estimates continue to show that increases in local fixed-line speed will be associated with greater declines in mobile Internet adoption and mobile online usage for young users and those in low fixed-line speed areas. In contrast, there is little evidence that the efects of speed vary based on local income, education, and urban/rural status.<sup>19</sup> Furthermore, there is little evidence that the efects of speed vary with user-specific characteristics such as income and education. In sum, Table I shows that our results in Table 5 are not acting as proxies for common local or user-specific demographic variables that have been shown to influence adoption behavior within the digital divide literature.

We examine the robustness of our results to alternative strategies to measuring our interaction terms and implementing them in regression models. We first reestimate the baseline model of Table 2 by splitting the sample based on age and fixed-line speed. The results, shown in Table J in the online appendix, continue to show that younger users and those in areas with lower fixed-line Internet speeds exhibit a stronger competitive efect between the fixed-line and mobile Internet.

We probe the robustness of our results to diferent cutof points to define the dummy variables for younger consumers and lower fixed-line Internet speed areas. To do this, we conduct additional analyses based on a spline model for each variable, namely, with interactions for several fixed-line Internet speed percentile dummies (10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles) and interactions for all age cohorts in our data (18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, and 65<sup>+</sup> years). The results are shown in Tables K and L in the online appendix. The estimated coeficients for the interactions between fixed-line speed and the percentile dummies of fixed-line Internet speed are significantly negative (i.e., stronger competition) at the 10th and 25th percentiles but insignificant elsewhere. The estimated coeficients for the interactions between fixed-line speed and the age categories are significantly negative (i.e., stronger competition) for 18–24 and 24–35 years but insignificant for the other age groups. Thus, consumers younger than 35 years and those living in areas with a fixed-line Internet speed lower than the 25th percentile view the two platforms as close competitors.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

We examine competition between the fixed-line Internet and mobile Internet. Using large-scale survey data and public Internet speed data, we find robust empirical evidence of a negative impact of local fixed-line Internet speed on mobile Internet adoption and use. Thus, we ofer evidence of competition between the fixed-line and mobile Internet. These efects are economically significant. We also study how changes in fixed-line speed influence the intensity of use of the mobile Internet. We identify similar patterns of behavior—increases in fixed-line speed are associated with declines in mobile usage—across a broad range of activities that require real-time Internet connections.

We further study the circumstances when consumers are more likely to use mobile Internet connections in place of fixed-line Internet services. We show that younger consumers and those who are located in regions with low fixed-line speed are most likely to view the fixed-line and mobile Internet services as competitors. This remains true even controlling for characteristics of the individual and local population such as age, income, and urban/rural location.

Our results have public policy implications. Given the widespread digitization of economic and social activity, broadband access is an important public policy issue. For example, in the United States, the Telecommunications Act of 1996 requires the Federal Communications Commission to “determine whether advanced telecommunications capability is being deployed to all Americans in a reasonable and timely fashion” (47 U.S.C. Section 1302(b)). Given the rapid difusion of mobile broadband services, an important question is whether users view the mobile Internet as a competing service when local fixed-line quality is low. Our results provide evidence that users view them as competitors, over this time period and specific set of applications and over a specific set of users. Of course, the usage over the Internet has changed significantly since its inception and continues to change. In particular, our data predate the widespread use of video streaming, a use case for which the mobile Internet may currently be an imperfect substitute because of network constraints and bandwidth limits in many user contracts. Future work should investigate how the use of the fixed-line and mobile Internet—and its relationship to local speeds— has changed over time.

Our results also have implications for policy makers who are responsible for evaluating the market structure of telecommunication services for purposes of regulation and antitrust. Households in the United States can choose from among an average of 2.9 mobile carriers (Federal Communications Commission 2010). This has led to policy recommendations from the FCC that the federal government should seek to stimulate wireless–wireline competition. Also motivated by this lack of competition, the U.S. Department of Justice thus sought to block a proposed purchase of T-Mobile USA by AT&T, ultimately leading AT&T to abandon its bid (AT&T 2011). Our results show that consumers treat these channels as competitors, particularly in environments in which fixed-line speed is low and where the two channels ofer similar levels of service quality. More broadly, our research informs a recent line of work that has sought to understand whether fixed and mobile line services share the same market (e.g., Cardona et al. 2009, Srinuan et al. 2012, Grzybowski et al. 2014). Our research advances this line of work by incorporating the efects of speed on user behavior, and by identifying the users and use cases for which the two services act as competitors.

Service providers can also learn from our findings. Investment in carrier infrastructure is among the largest cost outlay for mobile providers; investment in mobile infrastructure, though costly, improves service quality, encourages consumer adoption of mobile services, and enhances postadoption use. By demonstrating how local service quality influences user behavior, our insights should inform telecommunication carriers’ strategic mobile infrastructure investments. However, here the implications may be significantly different for providers of solely mobile service, solely fixed-line service, and providers of both fixed-line and mobile services, who must incorporate potential cannibalization efects into their decisions. More specifically, providers of both fixed-line and mobile services, like AT&T, may need to run these services in a very coordinated manner and calibrate their optimal pricing and service quality decisions together. Providers of solely mobile (or solely fixed-line) service still need to monitor the price and service quality of fixed-line (or mobile) service in the same market.

Our research also has implications for mobile advertising targeting. Our results show that the types of mobile activities that users will engage in may depend on the availability and quality of fixed-line services. For example, we show that, despite the smaller screen, mobile users in locations with low fixed-line speed are more likely to be engaged in search. This could influence the types of ads that firms will show to consumers.

Several important data and econometric limitations arise in this study. We have discussed these throughout this paper, but summarize the major issues here. First, because we do not observe individual behaviors on the fixed-line Internet, we investigate how fixed-line Internet speed influences mobile Internet adoption and use; we cannot examine how mobile Internet speed influences consumer behavior on the fixed-line Internet. Therefore, we can identify only the competitive efect of the existing channel on the adoption and use of the emerging channel, but not the other way around. Second, while we know whether a consumer has adopted a mobile data plan and monthly mobile data plan spending, these decisions are a subset of a complicated choice process that includes carrier choice, phone choice, data plan choice, and the extent to which the user uses his or her data plan or Wi-Fi for online activities. In keeping with many choice models of human behavior (Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Gentzkow 2007), we focus on a subset of these decisions. We believe that many of these decisions are complementary, and so focus on one margin that may be illustrative of the broader choice process. For example, we showed that mobile usage and monthly mobile data plan spending show similar results in our regression models, and we suspect that mobile data plan choice might also show qualitatively similar results. However, this remains to be empirically tested.

More broadly, we acknowledge the role that unobserved factors could play in influencing our results. We conducted a range of robustness tests, including the addition of more controls, use of instrumental variables, and implementation of a pseudopanel approach. Throughout this paper, we also examined whether our overall pattern of results—namely, diferences for mobile online and ofline activities—are consistent with our framework. These analyses lend credence to a causal story. However, we acknowledge the identification challenges that we face. We look forward to future work that could examine the validity of our findings using diferent data over a diferent time period.

Our findings ofer several directions for new research. Researchers could extend the current study by investigating the mechanism or source of competition. We note some similarities and dissimilarities between the two Internet channels, but the MobiLens data cannot reveal the mechanisms behind some of our findings, such as stronger competition between the two channels for younger consumers. If researchers could access Internet consumption data together with data about the specific content consumed on each platform, they could provide more detailed insights into consumer behavior. We look forward to future research in this important area.
