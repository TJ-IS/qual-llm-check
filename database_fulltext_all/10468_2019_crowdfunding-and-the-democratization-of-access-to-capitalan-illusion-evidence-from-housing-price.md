# Crowdfunding and the Democratization of Access to Capital—An Illusion? Evidence from Housing Prices

Keongtae Kim,<sup>a</sup> Il-Horn Hann<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Access to finance is arguably one of the most critical challenges in starting a new business. In this study, we examine how the difficulty of obtaining bank loans based on housing collateral, which is a form of traditional finance, relates to crowdfunding use by entrepreneurs. We obtained data on housing prices related closely to the cost of accessing such bank loans and matched these data to a 2009–2013 novel data set from a leading crowdfunding market. We adopted the first-difference estimator to address unobserved area-specific effects and used housing supply elasticity as an instrument for housing price changes. We found an increased decline in housing prices leads to a significant increase in the creation of crowdfunding projects. However, we did not find significant differential effects in housing price changes between successful and unsuccessful projects. Finally, the effect of housing prices on crowdfunding projects was more significant for areas with low socioeconomic status. Interestingly, the increase in crowdfunding projects in these lowstatus areas was driven wholly by a significant increase in unsuccessful projects, whereas the effect of housing prices on successful projects was significant only in areas of high socioeconomic status. Numerous additional tests support the robustness of our main findings. Overall, our study suggests that crowdfunding can supplement traditional sources of funding although socioeconomic status may still prevent disadvantaged people from receiving its full benefits.

## 1. Introduction

According to the U.S. Small Business Administration, small businesses employ more than 50% of the privatesector workforce and account for 66% of all net job creation.<sup>1</sup> Specifically, Haltiwanger et al. (2013) found most jobs are created by young, typically small businesses. Decker et al. (2014) also found high-growth start-ups contributed significantly to job creation in the U.S. economy.

Although aspiring entrepreneurs have considerable potential for job creation, the literature suggests that access to credit is one of the most critical obstacles in starting a new business (Kerr and Nanda 2011). By their nature, young businesses do not have an established business credit profile, making traditional lenders’ assessment of their degree of risk difficult. These common difficulties in accessing capital and credit have worsened in recent years because of the financial crisis and the resulting economic downturn. In fact, cash-strapped individuals and small businesses have been hit especially hard by this reduced availability of capital and credit (Laderman and Reid 2010, Greenstone et al. 2014).

Thus, increasing the accessibility of credit to small busi nesses is an urgent issue for policymakers.

Recent advances in digital technologies have made it easier for entrepreneurs to access funding from a large number of unaffiliated individuals online (Burtch et al. 2013). Crowdfunding platforms provide an infrastructure that facilitates the process of funding between project creators and funders. Crowdfunding marketplaces have grown rapidly in recent years; in 2015, the market attracted an estimated \$34.4 billion worldwide.<sup>2</sup> With its increasing popularity, crowdfunding has received significant attention from policymakers and practitioners. Upon signing the Jumpstart Our Business Startups Act in April 2012 that regulates equity-based crowdfunding, then U.S. President Barack Obama announced, “startups and small businesses will be allowed to raise up to \$1 million annually from many small-dollar investors through web-based platforms, democratizing access to capital” (Obama White House Archives 2012).

Academic research on crowdfunding has largely neglected the question of how access to credit from traditional markets affects crowdfunding use by entrepreneurs, which is surprising given that access to finance is one of the primary factors driving entrepreneurship. The view that entrepreneurs seek crowdfunding in response to worse financing conditions seems natural. However, who will acquire added benefits from crowdfunding would also be an important question for practitioners and policymakers. If crowdfunding does not increase the benefit of entrepreneurs from traditionally disadvantaged groups, then policymakers should rethink the role of crowdfunding as a means to democratize finance. Policymakers should also propose adequate policies to help entrepreneurs improve crowdfunding utilization.

To answer these questions, we focused on one of the most important types of credit for entrepreneurs—bank financing through housing collateral—and we examined how accessibility to these loans related to crowdfunding use by entrepreneurs. Collateral traditionally refers to secured lending, especially within banking, and housing collateral is a borrower’s pledge of a property to a lender to secure repayment of a loan. Prior studies have shown that housing wealth can ease credit constraints for entrepreneurs, thereby making housing wealth a primary factor in financing entrepreneurship (Robb and Robinson 2014, Adelino et al. 2015, Corradin and Popov 2015).

We conducted a region-level analysis to examine the relationship between local housing prices and crowdfunding use by local creators. Following the literature (Mian and Sufi 2011, Adelino et al. 2015, Corradin and Popov 2015), we focused on local housing prices as a proxy for collateral-based credit availability for entrepreneurs in a local market. If entrepreneurs living in areas with declining housing prices have an increasing degree of difficulty in raising sufficient capital, they may be inclined to use crowdfunding as additional funding source. Thus, we examined whether and how local housing prices relate to crowdfunding use by local creators. Although we recognize that bank financing and crowdfunding offer different funding conditions in terms of funding duration, success rates of funding, interest charges, and so on, the two channels considerably overlap. In fact, a significant number of creators are potentially able to use both channels and choose the optimal combination that offers the best terms and conditions.

We studied the research question in the context of Kickstarter, which is one of the leading crowdfunding markets. Established in April 2009, Kickstarter has emerged as a major online crowdfunding marketplace for various creative projects. We examined technologybased projects in the technology and games categories in which the trade-off between crowdfunding and bank financing is likely to be significant. We obtained data covering April 2009 through December 2013 and accounted for 9,120 projects that attracted more than \$257 million in pledges from approximately 3.4 million contributors.

We found that tightened credit constraints imposed because of declines in local housing prices lead to increased use of crowdfunding by local creators. This finding is consistent with the notion that crowdfunding serves as a supplement to traditional sources of financing. We further observed that a decline in housing prices has a significant effect on crowdfunding in areas with a large share of homeowners and in states with unlimited homestead exemptions, strengthening the idea that our main finding is driven primarily by a collateral effect. Interestingly, we generally did not find significant differential effects of housing price change between successful and unsuccessful projects, but we discovered the effect of housing price changes is greater in areas with low levels of income and education. We also observed a striking contrast in which a decrease in housing prices leads to an increase in successful projects primarily for areas of high socioeconomic status and an increase in unsuccessful projects primarily for areas of low socioeconomic status. This relationship suggests that, although entrepreneurs from areas of low socioeconomic conditions have at least equal access to online crowdfunding, they may still suffer from lower demand for their projects. This may be partly because of less support of their social networks.

The current study makes significant contributions to the literature. First, to our knowledge this study is the first to provide systematic evidence of a significant relationship between local credit conditions and crowdfunding use by local project creators. From this perspective, our study complements and extends recent empirical studies by shedding light on the importance of geography in the context of crowdfunding (Mollick 2014, Agrawal et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2015). Unlike most previous studies that focus mainly on crowdfunders influenced by their geographical locations, our study highlights that geography can also matter to project creators in crowdfunding. Second, our paper contributes to the literature on the role of access to finance in entrepreneurship, especially as leveraged by housing collateral (Kerr and Nanda 2011, Adelino et al. 2015, Corradin and Popov 2015). The literature highlights the importance of access to finance to entrepreneurs by showing that difficult access discourages entrepreneurship as measured by self-employment surveys and census data. However, our study shows that online crowdfunding, as an emerging source of finance, can be highly relevant to entrepreneurs under such conditions and that, to some extent, it may offset a decrease in entrepre neurship. Finally, we add to the literature on the digital divide. Although numerous studies have examined whether the digital divide is significant, they tend to focus on access to digital technologies (Riggins and Dewan 2005

Agarwal et al. 2009, Dewan et al. 2010, Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). By contrast, our study highlights that overcoming the access problem does not necessarily confer equal benefits on all individuals because socioeconomic status may still prevent disadvantaged people from receiving the full benefits of online crowdfunding (Rhue and Clark 2018, Burtch and Chan 2019).

## 2. Literature Review

Our study builds on three bodies of literature: access to finance and entrepreneurship, crowdfunding, and the digital divide. One body of research under the literature on access to finance and entrepreneurship uses regional variations in access to credit to examine its effects on entrepreneurship. Observable characteristics, such as depth of local credit markets and competition among local banks were then related to the access of entrepreneurs to credit and the resulting rates of entrepreneurship. Geographical differences in the ability of financial markets to offer financing to promising start-ups were found to lead to meaningful differences in regional entrepreneurship. A number of studies used such an approach to show that access to credit matters to entrepreneurs (Rajan and Zingales 1998). Our study is related directly to this line of research because we also examine local variations in housing price changes.

The role of local financing sources in local entrepreneurship is unclear despite access to credit being important to entrepreneurs. Local banks are likely to matter only when entrepreneurs prefer borrowing money from their local banks and local banks prefer to lend locally (Guiso et al. 2004). Although technology weakens the dependence of small businesses on local lenders, a stream of literature shows distance still matters in lending to small businesses (Petersen and Rajan 2002). Part of the reason behind such importance is that this kind of lending often requires collecting “soft” information on small businesses over time through relationships with their owners, making local presence critical.<sup>3</sup> Online crowdfunding has potential to reduce the constraint of geographical proximity in fundraising for start-ups because, in theory, all projects on a crowdfunding platform are equally visible to potential funders irrespective of their location.

The role of housing collateral in facilitating access to bank financing is well established in the literature theoretically and empirically. Information asymmetry often increases the difficulty for banks to lend to small businesses (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981). Providing personal collateral against business loans facilitates the lending process. Robb and Robinson (2014) report that debt is the most common outside source of financing for startups and that its availability is related to the value of real estate collateral. Kerr et al. (2015) provide direct empirical evidence of a significant relationship between appreciation in house prices and an increased number of home equity loans. Schmalz et al. (2017) conducted a region-level analysis and found a significant relationship between local housing prices and the local rate of entrepreneurship. They contend that such a significant relationship implies a compelling collateral channel effect. This line of research shows housing wealth is associated positively with accessibility to bank financing, which leads to increased rates of entrepreneurship (Adelino et al. 2015, Kerr et al. 2015).

Crowdfunding may alleviate credit constraints, which are due to declines in local housing prices. This appears to be plausible because, in the reward-based type of crowdfunding being studied, entrepreneurs receive funding in return for products during the early stages in which typically minimal tangible projectbased collateral is available. In addition, crowdfunders provide the needed financial resources without any financial obligations, such as interest rates or penalties for failed promises, and they are not bound geographically. Thus, crowdfunding allows entrepreneurs facing difficulties in accessing local bank credit to approach remote crowdfunders who enjoy improved economic conditions.

Nonetheless, we acknowledge that housing wealth itself may not be directly related with the use of crowdfunding. As discussed previously, the money derived from crowdfunding differs greatly by its very nature from that derived from bank financing in which borrowers are typically required to provide sound credit histories and personal collateral and to pay interest. Thus, crowdfunding and bank financing are two vastly different concepts in obtaining credit and may not be directly comparable. Moreover, although they are comparable, some entrepreneurs may not be able to shift easily from bank financing to crowdfunding because of the several costs associated with the latter. For instance, crowdfunding carries a public disclosure requirement (Agrawal et al. 2014). This concern may be serious, especially for technology projects examined in this study, which are often based on proprietary technologies.

Crowdfunder management presents another challenge. Entrepreneurs need to engage their many contributors, communicate with them on a repeated basis, and update them regarding their progress on the sheer number of funders who still need to be managed. Finally, crowdfunding may lead to some entrepreneurs having longer fundraising periods than others. Therefore, increasing difficulties in accessing bank loans should cause entrepreneurs to resort to crowdfunding As long as a significant number of entrepreneurs are qualified for both channels and attempt to determine the best combination, we should observe a significant relationship between housing price changes and crowdfunding use by entrepreneurs.

The various contexts of the digital divide (i.e., the great disparity in access to digital technologies across regions or groups) have been examined and reported in the literature. Previous studies show that this divide can often be explained by regional characteristics, such as infrastructure and socioeconomic conditions, and by individual characteristics, such as income and education (Riggins and Dewan 2005, Agarwal et al. 2009, Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). As authors of one of the earliest studies that explored these individual characteristics, Katz and Aspden (1997) show that wealthy and highly educated Caucasians are more likely to use the internet. Using demographic characteristics from different regions, other studies have shown the existence not only of structural barriers in overcoming the digital divide, including technical infrastructures (Chinn and Fairlie 2007) and social networks (Agarwal et al. 2009), but also that of forces such as complementary technologies that narrow this divide (Dewan et al. 2010). Both bodies of research highlight the importance of education and income as key differentiators between the advantaged and the disadvantaged (Hsieh et al. 2008, Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). Although prior research on the digital divide has examined the determinants of access to technology, whether such access leads to positive economic outcomes has not been examined adequately in the literature (Riggins and Dewan 2005, Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). This distinction is important because technology adoption itself is not the ultimate goal but is an intermediate outcome to help adopters obtain economic and social benefits.

In theory, crowdfunding has the potential to help socioeconomically disadvantaged people gain easy access to capital because of its low barriers in the adoption of crowdfunding technology. Nonetheless, whether crowdfunding adoption will actually lead to successful funding is unclear, especially for the disadvantaged. The quality of the ideas and social networks of creators have been shown to be crucial for successful crowdfunding (Lin et al. 2013, Agrawal et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015, Kim and Viswanathan 2019). Successful creators are likely to be well educated and have increased social capital and are also likely to leverage any local network externality that accrues from local contributors (Agarwal et al. 2009) who themselves are likely to be socioeconomically advantaged. Despite the reduced geographical barriers to contributions in online crowdfunding, many crowdfunders still prefer local projects (Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2015). In other words, the wealthy may be able to improve the capitalization of their existing socioeconomic advantages and convert these advantages into the launch of crowdfunding campaigns and especially into service for the success of such campaigns. Our study extends the literature on the digital divide by showing that, in terms of access, the digital divide may be overcome in online crowdfunding; however, the divide still exists in terms of benefits.

## 3. Data and Empirical Analyses 3.1. Study Context and Variables

We first gathered information on crowdfunding projects from Kickstarter, which began operation in April 2009 and continues to provide a market in which everything from films, games, and music to art, design, and technology can be supported financially with the help of a large number of contributors. We limited our sample to projects in two categories that most resemble conventional technology ventures: technology and games. Similar to traditional technology entrepreneurs, creators in these categories generally attempt to provide innovative products for financial gain. Popular technologies initiated in these categories are 3-D printing, wearables, and apps. Thus, these creators consider crowdfunding to be a useful path for starting new projects and growing ongoing projects. Meanwhile, creators in categories such as art and theater typically have no goal beyond simply bringing their projects to life without necessarily trying to grow them quickly, thereby suggesting that the two groups of projects may engage in fundamentally different types of fundraising. In addition, projects under technology and games are generally larger than other types of projects in terms of the amounts they intend to attract from crowdfunding.<sup>4</sup> This may imply that technology-based creators are active in seeking external sources of financing, such as bank financing and crowdfunding, but that other types of creators may fund their small projects successfully without seeking additional outside assistance. By limiting our sample to technology-based projects in the technology and games categories, we consider campaigns with homogenous projects likely to have similar funding drivers.

Bank financing is known to be a key source of funding for traditional technology ventures (Robb and Robinson 2014). Many technology projects in our sample resemble conventional small ventures that typically apply for bank financing. Robb and Robinson (2014) investigated entrepreneurs in their first year of operation and reported that 35% of firms in their sample had zero revenue and less than 17% of the firms exceeded \$100,000 in revenue. They further noted that even for the smallest firms with no revenue or no employees, bank financing was the single largest source of outside funding. Mollick and Kuppuswamy (2014) used a sample structurally similar to ours. Their sample was composed of projects in the technology, video games, and product design categories on Kickstarter. They found that 44% of the firms had zero revenue and only 11% reported more than \$100,000 in revenue. The ventures in the two samples appear to be quite similar in terms of revenue, suggesting that technology creators in this study are likely to be candidates for bank financing. Furthermore, according to Mollick and Kuppuswamy (2014), more than half of entrepreneurs initiating technology-based projects resorted to crowdfunding because they could not gain sufficient funding from conventional sources such as banks. This finding implies that technology entrepreneurs intending to use crowdfunding were also eager to use other outside funding sources, including banks.

Using extracted data on all U.S. technology-related projects on Kickstarter from its inception to December 2013, we identified 3,462 successful projects and 5,658 unsuccessful projects. We focused only on U.S. projects mainly because of the availability of their geographical data. For each project, we obtained information on project owner–specific characteristics (e.g., user name and location) and project-specific characteristics $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , goal amount, pledged amount, category, and project location). Knowing the location of each project by city and state allowed us to determine the local conditions for each project.<sup>5</sup> We matched each project to a metropolitan statistical area (MSA). Our use of MSA as the unit of location was driven by Kickstarter providing only city and state information. Although other variables are widely available for all MSAs, our main analyses focus on the subset of MSAs for which we have a measure of housing supply elasticity, which we explain in detail later. The measure of housing supply elasticity is available for 250 large MSAs. Our sample includes large MSAs, thereby covering approximately 80% of all crowdfunding projects.

After matching each project to an MSA, we measured crowdfunding use by creators in an MSA. We considered two measures as representatives of local crowdfunding usage: the number of total projects per million people and the sum of goal amounts of all projects in an MSA. Our key variable of interest is the housing price index, which we obtained at the MSA level from the Federal Housing Finance Agency and used as a proxy for the availability of housing collateral– based bank loans in a local market. We also used two years of moving differences in house price indices at the MSA level. Similar measures have also been used in other studies (e.g., Adelino et al. 2015). As shown in Figure 1, we generally observed declines in housing prices in a local region during the period although these declines decreased over time. We added relevant demographic and socioeconomic variables from multiple sources to the data. Tables 1 and 2 present the definitions and descriptive statistics, respectively, of crowdfunding activities as well as other variables. ## 3.2. Empirical Implementation

Our model for the change in the adoption of crowdfunding by project creators is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \Delta C R _ {j m t} = \phi + \beta \Delta \mathrm{HPI} _ {m t - 1} + \Delta \mathbf {W} _ {m t - 1} \pmb {\delta} + \mathbf {X} _ {m} \gamma \\ + \pi_ {j} + \rho_ {t} + \epsilon_ {j m t}, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where the subscript represents category $j$ (technology or games) in MSA m at year $t ; ~ C R _ { j m t }$ represents crowdfunding use by creators, which is measured as the log of the total number of projects (or the log of the sum of goal amounts of projects) per million people in category $j$ at MSA m in year t; $H P I _ { m t }$ represents the housing price index at the MSA-year level; and $\beta$ is the effect of housing price change on the change in crowdfunding adoption by creators, which is our parameter of interest. We expect $\beta$ to be negative because declining housing prices during the study period are likely to lead to increased crowdfunding use by creators as an additional source. Additionally, $\mathbf { W } _ { m t }$ represents time-varying, MSA-specific variables, namely unemployment rate and the number of small establishments, and $\pmb { \chi } _ { \mathbf { m } }$ is a vector of MSA-specific, timeinvariant variables and includes bank branch density, total population, median income, and number of ISPs as well as other demographic variables, such as age, gender, and race. All these variables were measured in 2008, which is a year before Kickstarter started operations. Also, $\pi _ { j }$ represents a dummy category to account for fixed category-specific differences between technology and games projects. We also included dummy year $\rho _ { t }$ to control for time-specific variations. Finally, $\epsilon _ { j m t }$ is a random error term. We log-transformed total population in the analysis and clustered the standard errors by MSA level.

Despite the differences setting and control variables, establishing a causal relationship between changes in housing prices and the creation of crowdfunding projects is challenging because there might be some omitted time-variant variables that could simultaneously affect both housing prices and crowdfunding adoption by creators. For example, changes in expected household income in the area or improvements in entrepreneurial opportunities can affect both housing prices and creators’ adoption of crowdfunding. As a result, we need an exogenous source of variation in housing price changes to identify its effect on the adoption rate of creators properly. We used the measure of MSA-specific housing supply elasticity proposed by Saiz (2010) as an instrument for housing price changes. This identification strategy has been implemented extensively in recent studies (Mian and Sufi 2011, Robb and Robinson 2014, Adelino et al. 2015).

Housing supply elasticity was constructed by applying geographical and regulatory constraints on the expansion of housing volume. Therefore, an increase in housing demand during an economic boom period is likely to translate into increased housing prices and increased collateral value in low-elasticity areas, whereas housing demand translates into a large volume of houses built in high-elasticity areas (Adelino et al. 2015). Following the same logic, when bad economic conditions reduced housing demand, we observed smaller housing price declines in high-elasticity areas than in low-elasticity areas. Our data in Figure 1 confirm this observation. Figure 1 shows housing prices changed more significantly during 2000–2012 in lowelasticity areas than in high-elasticity areas. Our regression result below confirms this result and shows that housing supply elasticity is positively associated with an increase in housing prices.

Although Kickstarter started operations one year prior, 2010 data were used because few MSAs were involved in crowdfunding in 2009. The test examined whether the early adoption of crowdfunding by creators across areas was systematically related to local housing supply elasticity. In addition to constructing an instrument for housing price changes, we implemented a number of strategies to identify the main effect. ## 4. Empirical Results

## 4.1. Main Effect on Crowdfunding

Figure 2 is a scatterplot of our raw data on the relationship between changes in housing prices between 2008 and 2012 and the total number of crowdfunding projects. We confined our sample to the 250 MSAs used for our main analyses to draw this scatterplot. The downward-sloping regression line suggests that, in our sample, the changes in housing prices are correlated strongly and negatively with the adoption of crowdfunding by creators.

We conducted a series of regressions to examine the effect of increases in housing prices on the adoption of crowdfunding by creators. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 4 report the ordinary least squares estimates without instrumenting housing price changes. Our coefficient of interest (housing price increase) is negative and highly significant for both dependent variables, thereby indicating that increased reductions in housing prices lead to a significant increase in the creation of crowdfunding projects. This finding confirms a decrease in housing prices drives creators with tight credit conditions to increase their dependence on crowdfunding. The results of control variables are generally in line with our expectations. For instance, bank branch density, although statistically weak, is negatively associated with the change in crowdfunding adoption by creators. This result was expected because increased competition among banks facilitates access to bank credit, thereby decreasing the incentive for the use of crowdfunding as an additional source of funding. In addition, areas with more population, a higher percentage of Caucasians, and a larger percentage of bachelor’s degree holders are all associated with a greater use of crowdfunding by creators.

4.1.1. Robustness Tests to Corroborate the Main Effect. As discussed earlier, the key relationship between housing prices and crowdfunding adoption by creators may face an omitted variable bias. We implemented several additional tests to resolve this issue. First, we analyzed the differential effects of changes in house prices between homeowners and nonhomeowners (Table 5). As shown in columns (1) and (2) of Table 5, we found that a housing price change is more negative in areas with a higher proportion of homeowners. This finding may be used to rule out several confounding factors, such as local demand conditions and local investment environments that may vary in time. We can exclude these factors because we should expect them to have a similar effect on homeowners and nonhomeowners. On the other hand, if our collateral channel story were significant, then housing price change should affect crowdfunding use by homeowners relatively more than it would nonhomeowners who would have no access to the collateral channel.

We also considered variations in homestead exemptions by state. These exemptions are used to protect homeowners from creditors by allowing the former to secure a certain amount of the value of their housing used as collateral. Prior studies have shown that these exemptions affect the lending decisions of banks because they have limited ability to seize collateral in a default in states with higher or even unlimited homestead exemptions. Consequently, banks have reduced willingness to lend to individuals even if they have housing collateral (Cerqueiro and Penas 2017). Thus, we expect to find a stronger effect in high-exemption states. When housing prices decline, entrepreneurs may be able to secure part if not all of the required funding in low-exemption states in which banks have increased willingness to lend. Conversely, entrepreneurs in states with high or even unlimited homestead exemptions encounter increased difficulty in securing bank funding, giving entrepreneurs further reason to use crowdfunding. We divided our sample on the basis of which states had unlimited homestead exemptions and which did not. Columns (3) and (4) show that, as expected, the effect of housing price changes on crowdfunding is higher in states with unlimited homestead exemptions. In addition to the robustness tests presented herein, we also conducted other extensive robustness checks to ensure the validity of our results. The details of these tests are in Online Appendix B.

## 4.2. Empirical Extensions

4.2.1. Successful Versus Unsuccessful Projects. One important question is whether tightened credit constraints attract entrepreneurs with less attractive projects over-proportionally. We used the funding success of a project as a proxy for its attractiveness. A project is successfully funded when the amount of funding attracted during the campaign period exceeds a predetermined goal amount. Given that successful funding is related to high-quality projects (Kim and Viswanathan 2019), such a variable is considered a valid measure. Table 6 reports the 2SLS estimates for successful projects in columns (1) and (2) and for unsuccessful projects in columns (3) and (4). Results for successful and unsuccessful projects are very similar. Effects of housing price increases are statistically significant and negative for successful and unsuccessful projects, which implies that declines in housing prices increase the creation of both projects. Therefore, the lessened financial constraints from introducing crowdfunding may have attracted high- and low-quality projects. Crowdfunding not only attracts low-quality projects that should not be funded but also high-quality projects.

4.2.2. Low Versus High Socioeconomic Areas. We examined whether the effect of credit availability in the form of housing prices is stronger for disadvantaged areas. This question guides us in understanding the potential of crowdfunding as a means of democratizing access to credit and capital. The stronger effects of crowdfunding in disadvantaged areas may indicate that people disadvantaged by traditional funding channels receive disproportionate benefits from crowdfunding. Building on the digital-divide literature, we focused on income and education as key differentiating forces between advantaged and disadvantaged areas (Hsieh et al. 2008, Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). Lowincome or less educated entrepreneurs face larger obstacles in accessing capital. Therefore, they may rely on crowdfunding for funding (McKenzie and Woodruff 2008). On the other hand, high-income or highly educated entrepreneurs may be more successful in obtaining funds from crowdfunding because of good social networks or existing financial resources that subsequently increase their incentive to initiate crowdfunding.

We first divided the sample of MSAs into quartiles according to their median household income in 2008 before introducing Kickstarter. We then interacted the housing price increases with each dummy for the top, bottom, and remaining quartiles. Columns (1) and (2) in Table 7 present the estimates obtained from 2SLS models. All interactions are negative, but the interaction terms with the bottom quartile are statistically significant and large in magnitude, indicating that lowincome areas have greater tendency than high-income areas to initiate crowdfunding projects. The difficulty in accessing bank loans drives entrepreneurs in low-income areas to use crowdfunding for their projects, implying that a temporary credit shock in the form of housing price decrease does not entirely discourage people in lowincome areas from initiating crowdfunding projects.

We further investigated the interaction variables between housing price changes and education level in MSAs. We divided our sample into quartiles according to the share of bachelor’s degree holders in each area in 2008. Similar to median household income, we interacted housing price changes with each quartile dummy after combining the second and third quartiles. Columns (3) and (4) show a similar pattern. Housing price decreases led to a significant increase in crowdfunding projects in areas with a low percentage of educated people.

The results for unsuccessful projects (columns (9)–(12) in Table 7) are similar to those for overall projects. Increases in housing prices are significantly and negatively associated with an increase in the level of unsuccessfu projects in low-income areas but not in high-income areas. Different results are obtained from the regressions that explain successful projects (columns (5)–(8) in Table 7). Interaction variables of housing price increases with either income or education are significant for high- and middle-income areas but not for lowincome areas. Taken together, our results suggest that tough credit constraints in the form of declining housing prices play significantly different roles in driving crowdfunding projects across various areas. Thus, a temporary shock can increase successful projects in high-income areas, unsuccessful projects in low-income areas, and successful and unsuccessful projects in middle-income areas.

If education is considered, then findings are similar but stronger. Effects of housing price decreases for unsuccessful projects are strongest for low-education areas and weakest for high-education areas. However, in terms of successful projects, high-education areas are the most influenced by declines in housing prices. This finding may imply that entrepreneurs in high-income and high-education areas are more skilled in launching high-quality projects than those in low-income and low-education areas. Therefore, the projects initiated by the former in poor credit conditions tend to be funded successfully. However, this finding may also simply imply that entrepreneurs from areas with good socioeconomic conditions, even if their projects are of similar or even worse quality, can generate significant demand for their projects because of their good social networks.<sup>7</sup> Our finding implies that differentiating crowdfunding adoption from its resulting economic benefit is important regardless of its underlying mechanisms.

## 5. Conclusion

We examined how bank loans based on housing collateral affect the creation of crowdfunding projects as a way to gain insights into whether crowdfunding can serve as an additional source for entrepreneurs who lack sufficient access to credit from traditional banks We found that entrepreneurs faced with tough credit constraints because of declining housing prices are more likely to use crowdfunding. Moreover, credit constraints have a stronger effect in areas with a higher percentage of homeowners and in states with unlimited homestead exemptions. These findings are consistent with our main argument of a significant collateral channel effect. However, we did not find any significant differences between successful and unsuccessful projects. This finding implies that crowdfunding attracts both high- and low-quality projects. Finally, we showed that the effect of changing housing prices is, on average, stronger in areas with low socioeconomic status. More interestingly, we found that declining housing prices increased successful projects in high socioeconomic areas but increased unsuccessful projects in areas with low socioeconomic status. These findings indicate that access to crowdfunding is no longer an issue for entrepreneurs from financially disadvantaged areas. However, crowdfunding effectiveness remains a significant challenge for these entrepreneurs.

Our findings present interesting implications for the growing literature on crowdfunding. Little is known on how crowdfunding relates to traditional funding channels (Drover et al. 2017, Ryu and Kim 2018). Our research indicates that crowdfunding can serve as an addition to traditional financial sources, implying that online crowdfunding has potential to democratize access to finance in the sense that it can be an option for entrepreneurs who have difficulty accessing traditional funding. One important question that warrants more investigation is whether crowdfunding supports entrepreneurs who are temporarily cash-strapped but have promising ideas or those who have flawed projects that do not deserve funding. However, we cannot address this question thoroughly in this study because the funding status we used is not an ideal proxy for the true quality of projects.

Our study also contributes to the digital-divide research. Prior studies on this area have generally examined the adoption and use of information and communication technologies, including the internet, personal computers, and mobile phones. We examined the adoption and effectiveness of a new form of technology called crowdfunding, which involves not only information sharing, but also monetary exchanges for developing products. By providing empirical evidence on the use and economic outcomes of crowdfunding from the digital-divide perspective, this work makes an important contribution to the relevant literature. Our study not only highlights the importance of examining technology access, but also the resulting economic outcomes (Riggins and Dewan 2005).

Our findings have implications for policymakers in terms of the digital divide. We found that crowd funding can serve as a feasible supplement to traditional sources of funding. Nonetheless, our findings are rather nuanced. Entrepreneurs from disadvantaged areas who have difficulty accessing credit have good access to online crowdfunding similar to those from advantaged areas. However, they may encounter low demands for their projects and have lesser chances of successful funding because of their weak supportive social networks. Therefore, policymakers must consider thoroughly the role of crowdfunding in democratizing finance and subsequently propose adequate policies to help entrepreneurs from areas of low socioeconomic status to obtain sufficient socioeconomic resources to raise money from online crowdfunding successfully.

Our study also has managerial implications for platform providers. We found that tough credit constraints result in bringing both low- and high-quality projects onto the crowdfunding platform. However, the possibility of creating proportionately low-quality projects is not precluded because our quality measure is based on funding status, which may not capture the quality of projects correctly. Studies by other researchers have provided evidence that relaxing credit constraints may attract mainly marginal entrepreneurs whose businesses are less likely to survive and more likely to perform poorly than others (Jensen et al. 2015). The screening problem becomes more serious in a loosely regulated crowdfunding market in which funders are remote and have limited opportunity to perform due diligence in person. Thus, individual platforms must implement policies to screen out low-quality projects, such as in those areas with tough credit constraints.

Our research provides important implications for entrepreneurs seeking financial resources. It suggests that entrepreneurs should be strategic in securing sufficient financial resources. Nearly all entrepreneurs suffer from insufficient financial capital because of unproven track records, unfavorable macroeconomic conditions, discrimination against minorities, etc. Therefore, they must seek out emerging funding sources that may be favorable to them. In our study, decreasing housing prices made banks reduce the credit supply to entrepreneurs in need, but crowdfunders were still willing to provide credit to entrepreneurs. Thus, entrepreneurs living in disadvantaged areas should exploit crowdfunding aggressively and attempt to either leverage their social networks or improve their projects for successful funding.

Our study focuses on the adoption decision of entrepreneurs in a reward-based crowdfunding market. However, market participants behave differently in an equity-based crowdfunding market. Equity-based crowdfunding is important in supporting “real” innovative firms. Therefore, its mechanisms should be examined further. Moreover, entrepreneurs seeking equity-based crowdfunding tend to create larger projects than those seeking reward-based funding, which explains their sensitivity to changes in housing prices. Nonetheless, if crowdfunders in equity-based crowdfunding are concerned with the subsequent fundraising ability of investing firms because of their location, then the geographical dispersion of equity-based crowdfunding projects may not be as large as that of rewardbased crowdfunding ones (Agrawal et al. 2014). Future studies should investigate this point further.

Our study sheds light on the differential effects of credit conditions between disadvantaged and advantaged areas. To help entrepreneurs from disadvantaged areas in achieving real economic benefits from crowdfunding, we must understand its underlying mechanisms. Our ad hoc analysis suggests that social networks may play a significant role in such a divide. However, we could not examine this view sufficiently because of data limitation. This factor is driven, in part, by quality differences across areas. Future research can examine under what conditions entrepreneurs from disadvantaged areas have difficulty raising successful funding and which factors are primary forces.

Our research has some limitations. First is the selected research design. We chose to aggregate our project-level data at the MSA level because we could not collect individual-level demographic data. Thus, identifying the causal effect of changes in housing prices effectively was difficult, especially when addressing different motivations and responsibili ties of entrepreneurs that drove them to use either crowdfunding or bank financing. Moreover, the research design prevented us from examining interesting questions, such as how individual demographics moderate the main collateral channel effect. Second, our quality measure is based on funding status, not on de veloped product performance although we note that measuring the true quality of a project accurately is challenging. A good quality measure would increase the validity of our findings. Third, we focused only on technology-intensive projects. Although we have a valid reason behind this choice, it implies that we have a limited ability in discussing crowdfunding as a new form of funding in other types of crowdfunded projects.
