## Abstract

The range of journals in which to publish scholarly papers in the management information systems (MIS) field has expanded over the years. The perceived ranking of the journals by those in the field is an important issue to academics. Another question of importance to academic personnel in the MIS field is whether the writing of books counts toward tenure and promotion. This article presents the results of a survey relative to these two questions. It was conducted in late 1989 and early 1990 among MIS professors in AACSB accredited business schools, with about half of all such schools participating.

The survey found that the most highly regarded journals for IS research include an assortment of IS-specific, computer science, and management science journals. This continues a long-standing tradition, despite the introduction of several new IS journals. It also showed that books are generally counted toward tenure and promotion.

## Introduction

As management information systems (MIS)/computer information systems (CIS) matures as an academic discipline in business schools, the range of journals in which to publish scholarly papers in the field is a moving target. In addition to a small but growing number of journals specifically dedicated to MIS, researchers in the field publish in journals of related fields, such as computer science, management science, and organizational science (Culnan and Swanson, 1986). We believed it was time to take a new, broad snapshot of the attitudes of professors in the field toward the academic quality of MIS journals. We also felt it would be helpful to survey the prevailing attitude toward published books in the tenure and promotion processes in this field.

We surveyed all AACSB accredited business schools on October 1, 1989, (with a follow-up mailing on January 15, 1990, to those schools that did not respond to the first mailing). The total number of such schools at the time of the survey was 269. Through the offices of the business school deans, we attempted to reach “the Chairman of your Computer/Management Information Systems Department or the professor in charge of teaching such courses if you do not have it organized as a separate department.” The chairmen were asked to fill out the survey themselves or to have another senior professor do it.

The response was excellent for a mail survey of this type: a total of 135 schools replied, representing 50.2 percent of the population. The large response suggests interest in the survey questions and the results.

## Academic Quality of Journals for MIS Research

For the question on journals in our survey, we selected 38 IS or IS-related academic journals, or related practitioner publications. We listed the journals alphabetically in a table with five check-off columns for rating purposes: top, high, medium, low, nil. Several lines marked “other”

were provided for the insertion of journals not included in our list. The survey directions read, "Check the appropriate boxes in the table below to indicate how you rate the following journals in terms of their academic quality for MIS publications." We did not attempt to distinguish academic from practitioner journals because many academic journals also claim to be relevant to practitioners. We decided to let the respondents' judgements speak for themselves on this point. Because no additional journals were named more than a few times in the "other" category, it appears that the 38 journals we selected were appropriate.

Somewhat arbitrarily, we gave the qualitative responses quantitative weights of 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, from top to nil, respectively. The journal scores are simply the weighted averages of the responses. Table 1 shows the results, in descending order of scores. Surveys such as this are not without pitfalls, and the data should be interpreted accordingly. For example, we do not know if the respondents' comments on individual journals are based on their general knowledge of them or their regular or sporadic reading of them. Also, note that the number of responses varied with the individual journals. This suggests that respondents tended not to judge journals about which they had no knowledge.

The scores in Table 1 show relatively large breaks after the third (3.39 to 2.93) and after the 13th (2.46 to 2.10) journals. Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Communications of the ACM rank as the top journals in the field. These might be termed the A+, or most favored, outlets for academic research. In the next group are highly regarded, refereed academic journals plus two well-known general management journals, Harvard Business Review and Sloan Management Review. A variety of ACM and IEEE journals are in this group, as are Decision Sciences, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Information and Management (which now incorporates the former Systems, Objectives, and Solutions). While the number of responses to the journals in the third tier of the rankings are generally lower than those in the first, three commercial publications evoked a high number of responses: Datamation, Infosystems, and the Computerworld “In Depth” section. Interestingly, Datamation has dropped in academic standing; it was named sixth of the nine top journals, just behind Communications of the ACM, in Patten's (1982) study eight years earlier. ACM Special Interest Group (SIG) publications were rated as a group, without being named. This may have disadvantaged the ranking for Data Base, the publication of the SIG on Business Information Technology.

Several journals were written-in in the “other” category enough times (four or more) to warrant mentioning here. In each case they were ranked at the higher end of the range. Together with the number of times they were mentioned, they are: Data Base (4), Decision Support Systems (5), Information Systems Research (9), International Journal of Man-Machine Studies (5), and Omega (4). Information Systems Research was announced but not yet in publication at the time of the survey. It is being produced by The Institute of Management Sciences (TIMS), which produces the top-ranked Management Science. With that background, it is reasonable to project, as did the respondents who marked it in the “other” category, that Information Systems Research will be a highly regarded journal.

Earlier studies (Culnan, 1987; Culnan and Swanson, 1986; Doke and Luke, 1987; Jackson and Nath, 1989; Koong and Weistroffer, 1989; Patten, 1982; Vogel and Wetherbe, 1984) addressed the issue of MIS journals in a variety of ways, no two quite the same. The list of journals included in the different studies varied. Nevertheless, some common patterns have emerged. As in our survey, Communications of the ACM and MIS Quarterly have almost always been at or near the top of the rankings (Culnan, 1987; Doke and Luke, 1987; Jackson and Nath, 1989; Koong and Weistroffer, 1989; Vogel and Wetherbe, 1984). Also, as in our survey, Management Science has often been at or near the top (Culnan, 1987; Doke and Luke, 1987; Patten, 1982). Among the journals that ranked in the second tier of our study, Decision Sciences also appeared prominently in Doke and Luke (1987); the Journal of Management Information Systems (which did not exist at the time of the earliest studies) was ranked highly by Doke and Luke (1987), Jackson and Nath (1989), and Koong and Weistroffer (1989); ACM Computing Surveys was ranked highly by Vogel and Wetherbe (1984); the Harvard Business Review was near the top in Doke and Luke (1987) and Vogel and Wetherbe (1984); Information and Management fared well in Jackson and Nath (1989), Koong and Weistroffer (1989), and Vogel and Wetherbe (1984); and the Sloan Management Review was prominent in Vogel and Wetherbe (1984).

It is interesting to note that most of the top 13 journals are primarily computer science, management, or management science journals. This is consistent with the fact that MIS as a discipline has been derived from several other diverse fields and is highly dynamic in nature (Culnan and Swanson, 1986). Many people in MIS originally came from the computer science and management science fields. Thus, it is not surprising that journals such as Communications of the ACM and Management Science, both of which publish MIS articles, have a record of being ranked highly in our field and continue to be so. By the same logic, but excluding a history of publishing MIS articles, the high ranking of Journal of the ACM, for example, can be explained. On the other hand, new journals can emerge and gain reputation fairly rapidly and others can decline. The relatively new Journal of Management Information Systems is fifth in our survey, and the recently introduced Information Systems Research has already gained a strong reputation. Some of the older practitioner publications, which at one time were considered highly for academic research reports, have declined in esteem.

## Academic Value of Writing Books

The writing and publishing of books by university professors is a long-standing tradition. The motivations behind this activity are wide and varied and include a genuine desire to help students learn, additional income, ego, status, a vehicle for disseminating research results, and so on. But with the prevailing academic emphasis on journal articles and grants, we were curious about the practical value of writing books in terms of furthering one's academic career in MIS. To put it bluntly, we wanted to know if books count toward tenure and promotion.

We are not aware of any prior work regarding attitudes toward published books in the tenure and promotion processes in MIS. However, some comments of a broad nature on the publishing of books can be found in general works on faculty evaluation. Miller (1987) suggests that various disciplines emphasize different publication categories. According to Centra (1979), "Research and scholarship in the humanities and social sciences are generally slower to develop compared with the natural sciences; moreover, they more frequently result in book publication. For this reason, departments that emphasize research in the humanities and social sciences put more weight on the publication of books than do natural science departments... The number and quality of journal articles and grants received are especially critical to the evaluation of natural science faculty members" (p. 123).

In 1983, Seldin (1984) surveyed academic deans at four-year undergraduate liberal arts colleges on the subject of faculty evaluation and compared the results to a similar study performed in 1978. The percentage of deans saying they “always used” books in the evaluation process (when the faculty member was the “sole or senior author”) increased from 53.1 percent to 58.1 percent in that five-year period. At the same time, the figures for “publication in all professional journals” increased from 46.0 percent to 54.1 percent. Seldin commented on this by saying, “College policies and practices used to evaluate faculty scholarship/research are becoming more systematic. Books, monographs, journal articles, papers at professional meetings, even unpublished papers and reports—virtually all relevant information—are considered in the evaluation process” (p. 74).

In our survey we asked, “Does the writing of books count toward tenure and promotion in your department?” The following five categories were provided—three for a positive response, one for negative, and one for other:

A. All books count.

B. Books count only if they contain new research contributions.

C. Books count only when an author has written multiple books in one or more areas and/or the books are established as among the top in the field(s).

D. Books do not count at all.

E. Other; specify.

In prior conversations with colleagues at a number of universities, we discovered that some departments have a rule of thumb that equates a book to a specific number of journal articles in this regard. We asked if there was a journal equivalency measure in the three positive choices.

Of the 135 respondents, there were 121 usable responses to this question (14 did not answer this question or wrote something of no value). Four of the respondents marked both choices B and C; that combination will be treated as a separate category. Table 2 shows the results for this question. Overall, books clearly do count in the tenure and promotion processes, although in a sizable minority of the schools they must describe new research or fall into the multiple/top of their field category. It is noteworthy that only 15 schools (13.3 percent) indicated that books do not count at all toward tenure and promotion; about half (50.4 percent) of the schools count all books.

The “other” category responses were quite varied. Three people indicated that a book is “evaluated for its contribution.” Other interesting responses included, “depends on type of book and level of person,” “depends on the quality of the book,” and “books do not count toward the research record but do count toward the teaching record.”

## Conclusion

The survey found that Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Communications of the ACM are regarded as the top three journals for IS research. These were followed by a second group of eight highly regarded journals along with two sets of organizational transactions (ACM and IEEE). Many of these top publications are primarily oriented toward computer science or management science owing to the backgrounds of the researchers and the continuing recent emergence of IS-specific journals. This follows a tradition that has been previously noted.

Most of the responding departments do count books in tenure and promotion, although some count only research-oriented books or ones that go on to be regarded as “top” in the field. We suggest that future surveys on the subject of tenure and promotion in IS ask about the relative importance of teaching versus research and the weight of external grants. Furthermore, future studies might make a distinction between the requirements for tenure and those for promotion to various ranks.

# Executive Overview

This article provides a useful way of classifying expert systems (ES) in terms of their relative complexity. Two dimensions of complexity are considered: the knowledge base underlying the ES and the technology required to implement it. A methodology is discussed for assigning a quantitative measure for each of these dimensions. Based on the resulting values, an ES can be assigned to a quadrant in a 2 x 2 table, where each of the two dimensions is broken down into a low and high range of values. Such information can be useful for organizing a project and in estimating budgets and schedules.

For each of the two dimensions, a number of factors are taken into account in calculating a weighted measure of complexity. In the case of knowledge base complexity, the factors are (1) the breadth of domains required (i.e., the number of separate areas of expertise needed to deal with a problem); (2) the depth of each domain (the amount of training and experience required of an expert; (3) the rate at which expert knowledge becomes obsolete (e.g., in ancient Greek history vs. semiconductor technology); (4) domain penetration (i.e., the fraction of the knowledge base for a domain that must be invoked for the ES); (5) the nature of the system's output (e.g., whether diagnostic or a recommended decision); (6) the number of information inputs; and (7) the degree of ambiguity of inputs. Each of these factors is assigned a rating, and then a composite score is calculated in which each factor is given equal weight. The score is normalized to fall within the range 0 to 100.

A similar kind of measure is computed for the technology complexity dimension. The factors used in this case are (1) diversity of development and target platforms (i.e., the variation in hardware architecture and systems software); (2) the diversity of technologies that must be applied (e.g., database management, graphics, imaging); (3) the database intensity of the application (i.e., the extent to which it must draw upon a dynamic database); (4) the network intensity (e.g., stand-alone vs. continual network connection); (5) the number of rules or the size of the knowledge base; (6) diversity of information sources; (7) the degree of diffusion of users of the system; and (8) the extent to which the system has to be integrated with other parts of the organization's overall information system.

Using the measure of complexity along each of the two dimensions, a given ES can be classified into one of the following quadrants:

I. Low knowledge/low technology (e.g., a stand-alone application developed with a PC-based ES shell and having a few hundred rules).

II. High knowledge/low technology (e.g., a stand-alone, PC-based system that requires mastery of several expert domains).

III. Low knowledge/high technology (e.g., an ES that deals with relatively simple decisions, but which is embedded within a transaction system and requires active access to a network and a shared database).

IV. High knowledge/high technology (similar in technical characteristics to Quadrant III systems, but involving complex decision rules drawn from several domains).

Based on a sample of 50 successfully developed ES, certain patterns were observed. Systems following within a given quadrant tended to have similar characteristics in terms of the amount of time and resources to develop a prototype and a production version. For systems falling within Quadrants I, II, and IV—where the knowledge issues tend to dominate technical concerns—control of the implementation process generally lies with the business units. Only in Qudarant III, where technical matters are of primary concern, does the technical staff tend to control the implementation process.

The authors provide a useful way of looking at expert systems. Developers embarking on an ES project would do well to determine the two complexity measures for the proposed system. This would force them to confront explicitly the factors that govern development effort, and give them a reasonable basis for estimating the time and resources likely to be required.
