## ABSTRACT

Expectations have been found to exert a significant impact on client satisfaction in outsourcing projects and have been utilized to understand how individuals accumulate experiences and

#

develop beliefs. In this study, we examine the impact of expectations on IT outsourcing success. Although extant studies identify multiple potential expectations, the expectations construct is often viewed as unidimensional with expectations examined at a general level. Furthermore, our multidisciplinary literature review indicates a gap in the literature regarding which expectations to employ. We therefore draw upon the Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) to determine a parsimonious set of expectations that combine to influence outsourcing success. Through an practitioners, we demonstrate the impact of expectations on IT outsourcing success. Moreover, we evidence that the should, minimally acceptable, and intolerable expectations can be used to predict outsourcing success. This study contributes to the outsourcing literature, specifically, and the multidisciplinary ECT literature, generally, by evaluating the multi-faceted nature of expectations. The results have practical implications for clients and vendors engaged in IT outsourcing and those who engage in expectation management.

## INTRODUCTION

Like most service industries, outsourcing vendors rely heavily on the satisfaction of their clients. Client satisfaction represents a strong motivation, as it drives future business and enhances reputation within the industry (Levina & Ross, 2003). Because of the importance of client satisfaction to vendors, recognizing the factors, which influence satisfaction in outsourcing projects is imperative.

It is widely recognized that satisfaction is largely the result of a comparison between performance and expectation. Generally speaking, individuals are most likely to be satisfied with a product or service if the performance of that product or service exceeds their a priori expectations. In this equation, we find two main variables, which influence satisfaction. While many studies have examined how outsourcing firms can influence one of the variables (performance) (e.g., Han, Lee, & Seo, 2008; Thouin, Hoffman, & Ford, 2009; Chang & Gurbaxani, 2012), none thus far have opened the black box that contains the other (expectations). If an outsourcing provider wishes to maximize client satisfaction, they must understand the expectations that they are required to meet.

Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) states that evaluative expectations pertain to how the product (or service) will perform (Bhattacherjee 2001; Cadotte et al., 1987). The types of expectations that individuals employ in forming their expectations vary from person to person and from context to context. The distinction between satisficing and optimizing serves as a prime example (Simon, 1959). In some contexts, individuals are inclined to optimize, utilizing an expectation based on the best possible outcome (e.g., expecting the best possible meal at an expensive restaurant). In other contexts, satisficing is more desirable, with expectations based on a lower standard of performance (a “good enough” expectation) (e.g., expecting hunger to be

satisfied at a ballpark concession stand). Both the expensive restaurant owner and the ballpark concession vendor base important decisions on the expectations utilized by their clients in determining the expected performance.

Expectations can vary in terms of degree (e.g., low, medium, or high expectations) and source (e.g., based on prior experience, competitors, personal cost, etc.). Miller (1977) specifies four expectations – ideal, expected (predicted), minimum tolerable (minimally acceptable), and deserved. Each of these leads to a different type of expectation for the product or service to which it is directed. Although researchers agree that “expectations serve as standards with which subsequent experiences are compared” (Zeithaml et al., 1993), consensus has not been reached regarding a number of issues relating to expectations, including the particular expectations that should be utilized in certain contexts (Zeithaml et al., 1993) such as outsourcing. Identifying the particular expectations relevant to outsourcing is important, as according to Woodruff et al. (1983):

“[T]he researcher must be careful to develop measures that correspond to different comparison standards. Measures that do not specify the basis for comparison…will not properly represent the proposed constructs. The most important factor is that true comparison standards may go undiscovered” (p. 302).

Recognizing that a variety of expectations can be utilized by clients, the relevant question for outsourcing vendors becomes the primary research question for this study: Which expectation(s) should be utilized in predicting outsourcing client satisfaction? We posit that a more complete evaluation of satisfaction can be achieved through decomposing expectations, rather than simply employing a generalized expectation measure. As satisfaction may still be measured without the consideration of expectations (Venkatesh et al. 2017), we seek to highlight the importance of considering not only expectation, but also its more granular specification. Addressing this issue will provide interesting insights into how expectations are formed within the outsourcing context.

Therefore, this study endeavors to delve into the black box of expectation confirmation in outsourcing arrangements. The impact of expectations on business arrangements is evident. Some outsourcing vendors have rejected large outsourcing contracts because of their belief that the other party’s expectations are not realistic. They would rather discard a potentially lucrative contract than enter into an arrangement with a partner whose expectations can never be met. While outsourcing experts may realize the significance of expectations in the outsourcing relationship, failure to meet expectations remains “the most frequently reported reason for outsourcing programs to be perceived as failures” (Sealock and Stacy, 2013). It may also cause organizations to backsource.

Thus, the purpose of this paper is two-fold. First, we illuminate the current state of dissension that exists in the field of Information Systems regarding the definition and measurement of expectations. Through a literature review, we highlight the multitude of expectations that have been utilized to determine the need for conceptual and methodological clarity. It would not generally be feasible to utilize all of the existing expectations in a study, even with prior empirical support. Therefore, researchers are left with the uncertain task of selecting from among these expectations, without any form of guidance as to the correct set to use or even if more than one is required. Second, utilizing data from IT executives, we empirically examine the outsourcing context, seeking to understand the expectations that are most prominent in predicting client satisfaction. We compare nine possible expectations and identify three that together predict satisfaction in outsourcing arrangements.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## IT Outsourcing

Since the genesis of IT outsourcing in the early 1960s and the growth of business-centric outsourcing in the mid-1980s (Currie & Seltsikas, 2001; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993), outsourcing has become a ubiquitous phenomenon (Sparrow, 2003) and the subject of considerable research (Schwarz 2014; Dibbern et al., 2004; Lacity et al., 2009). Despite the pervasiveness of outsourcing, many outsourcing ventures have been unable to achieve the elusive status of “success”. A PricewaterhouseCoopers report found that 55% of clients reported that they were not realizing the benefits they had expected from outsourcing, yet an overwhelming 81% stated that they would outsource again (Schwarz 2014). Thus, while the clients admit to having failed to experience anticipated benefits from outsourcing, they realize the necessity of it and hope to eventually achieve potential benefits as they continue in their quest for a successful outsourcing arrangement.

To combat the potential failure, organizations have focused on tightening their contracts and Service Level Agreements (SLAs). This approach, however, has not been particularly effective. According to KPMG’s outsourcing survey, 60% of respondents claim that problems with their outsourcing provider are almost always people-related. In essence, successful outsourcing is more highly correlated with relationships between clients and vendors than tight contracts and SLAs (Rossi, 2007). Recent research on outsourcing has focused more on the IT outsourcing relationship than on contracts, realizing the impossibility of creating an SLA that is strong enough to overcome any potential issues. By shifting the focus to improving the IT outsourcing relationship, an outsourcing partner can mitigate the most significant issues that may hinder the success of the outsourcing deal.

##

While it is recognized that expectations play a role in determining the success of the relationship, outsourcing expectations have been sparsely researched in the IT literature (the few examples include Ho et al., 2003 and Lacity et al., 1994)). Furthermore, most published papers on this topic have been either atheoretical or narrowly focused on a unique type of outsourcing arrangement. To further our examination of the role and source of expectations in outsourcing arrangements, we look to existing theory on expectations and confirmation in predicting satisfaction.

## Expectation Confirmation Theory

The role of expectations in influencing perceptions and human behavior has a long history within academic thought. Across a variety of fields, researchers have conducted studies to understand how individuals accumulate experiences, and then draw upon their perception of this history to create expectations that influence the development of their beliefs, alter their attitude, and spur them toward certain behaviors. Within the IS community, our field has utilized expectations to understand a variety of outcomes, including its impact on satisfaction (Schwarz and Zhu, 2015; Lin et al., 2009; Stayman et al., 1992; Szajna & Scamell, 1993) and perceived performance (Spreng et al., 2002; Staples & Chiou, 2002; Wanous et al., 1992). In fact, extant studies demonstrate that expectations about a technology can exert a more significant influence on satisfaction than experience-based norms (Susarla et al., 2003).

One of the prominent theories on expectations is the ECT. ECT delineates a process model in which an individual compares their preusage expectations about a product or service to their postusage perceptions of the product or service to determine the extent to which their expectations are confirmed, which is then utilized to determine their level of satisfaction/dissatisfaction (Bhattacherjee 2001). In this process, an individual assesses their

postusage belief about the performance of a product (or service) against their preusage expectations of how the product (or service) should perform (Cadotte et al., 1987). The performance can be better, the same as, or worse than expected (Oliver, 1980). If the performance is the same as was expected, the outcome is termed as “confirmation”. However, if the performance is better or worse than expected, then “disconfirmation” occurs.

Extant research has established the confirmation of expectations as a direct predictor of consumer satisfaction (Hsu et al., 2006; Szymanski & Henard, 2001). Specifically, ECT proposes that an individual’s state of satisfaction/dissatisfaction develops through the following process (also depicted in Figure 1):

1. Expectations: Expectations are pretrial beliefs about a service or product (Olson & Dover 1979). They reflect anticipated behavior (Churchill & Surprenant, 1982), and they serve as reference points against which product performance is judged (Zeithaml et al., 1993). Consumers form initial expectations about a product or service prior to initial use. If they have previous experience with the service or product, then their expectations tend to be more realistic; however, if they lack first-hand experience with the product or service, then their expectations may derive from alternative sources (Halstead et al., 1994). These expectations may be based on feedback from prior users, media reports, or marketing initiatives (Oliver, 1997). Nonetheless, initial expectations represent the benchmark (Halstead, 1999).

2. Performance: If the consumer decides to utilize the product or service, he/she will then evaluate the perceived performance of the product or service in a manner relevant to a priori expectations.

3. Confirmation: The consumer will then compare the perceived performance of the product or service to their initial expectation to form a confirmation judgment (Halstead, 1999). Three possible outcomes derive at this stage of usage:

o When actual performance fails to meet an individual’s expectations, then negative disconfirmation occurs (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004; Oliver, 1980).

o When actual performance exceeds expectations, then positive disconfirmation occurs.

o When actual performance equals expectations, simple confirmation occurs (Oliver, 1980; Santos & Boote, 2003).

4. Satisfaction: Satisfaction depicts the favorability of the individual’s evaluation related to their consumption experiences (Hunt, 1977; Martinez-Tur et al., 2011; Westbrook, 1980). Although there is a general agreement that satisfaction captures the postdecision evaluation of a service or product (Diehl & Poynor, 2010; Oliver, 1996), the nature of satisfaction resulting from the various cognitions does not constitute a resolved debate (Santos & Boote, 2003). Most researchers believe that individuals feel satisfied when there is positive disconfirmation, and they feel dissatisfaction when there is negative disconfirmation (Venkatesh & Goyal., 2010). However, simple confirmation does not enjoy this level of concurrence among researchers (Santos & Boote, 2003). Although some research suggests that satisfaction arises from an individual getting what they want [i.e., simple confirmation] (Hunt, 1991), others posit that simple confirmation merely leads to a neutral state of neither satisfaction nor dissatisfaction (Erevelles & Leavitt, 1992). Furthermore, other researchers have suggested that simple confirmation could in fact lead to dissatisfaction if the individual’s expectations relate to a negative outcome,

and the actual performance confirms their worst imaginable level of expectation (Buttle, 1996).

## Expectations

Historically, the rudimentary approach to understanding expectations has been to utilize a general comparison expectation (i.e., Bhattacherjee, 2001; Deng et al., 2010; Hong et al., 2011; Martinez-Tur et al., 2011; Wanous et al., 1992). The general comparison expectation asks a respondent to determine whether the performance of a product (or service) was better/worse than they expected. This approach fails to illuminate the types of expectations utilized in forming initial expectations.

Beyond the general comparison expectation, other researchers have proposed that there are multiple expectations and provided empirical support for their use (e.g., Tse & Wilton, 1988). According to the multiple expectations view, an individual does not possess simply a broad general expectation, but instead, utilizes distinct “types” of expectations regarding anticipated performance levels to judge against performance.

Miller (1997) discussed four types of expectations: ideal, expected, deserved, and minimum tolerable expectations. As opposed to the general expectation approach, Miller argued what the product “can be”. For example, use of the ideal expectation would involve asking a client to determine whether the vendor’s performance was better/worse than the ideal level of performance. Moreover, the deserved expectation reflects what the client feels that the performance “ought to be” (Miller, 1977). An example of employing the deserved comparison expectation would involve asking a client to determine whether the vendor’s performance was better/worse than “what [they] deserve…according to industry practices.” Over the years, researchers have evolved the four expectations proposed by Miller (1977) to include others.

To identify the expectations most applicable to outsourcing contexts, we first had to identify the list of potential expectations that could be used. In the following section, we discuss our review of ECT literature and the identification of nine possible expectations used in prior research.

## REVIEW OF EXPECTATIONS

In our review of the literature, we realized that although various expectations are employed in ECT studies, the discussion of the selection of the type of expectation utilized in the study was generally absent. A further review of the literature indicated the indiscriminate selection of expectations derived from a gap in the literature involving a methodological understanding of the composition and consociation of the disparate expectations. Therefore, we sought to develop a framework (Schwarz et al. 2007) of expectations.

We began by conducting a survey of the ECT literature to ascertain the collection of expectations that are employed in the literature. We searched throughout the IT, marketing, management, hospitality, sociology, and psychology literature. Our review of the research resulted in the identification of nine extant expectations utilized by researchers across disciplines, which include the following:

 The ideal expectation represents the perfect level of service a customer could desire.

The wanted expectation represents the desired level of service that a client believes can be delivered.

 The deserved expectation represents the level of service a client believes they are worthy to receive.

 The should expectation represents the level of service a customer believes they ought to receive.

 The adequate expectation represents the level of service a customer considers acceptable.

 The minimally acceptable expectation represents the lowest level of service the client feels must be performed.

The intolerable expectation represents the level of service that the client will not accept.

The worst imaginable expectation represents the level of service that exemplifies a client’s worst-case scenario.

 The predicted expectation represents the level of service that the client believes will be performed based upon the vendor’s past performance regarding the overall outsourcing arrangement.

We categorized and defined the extant expectations utilizing Wacker’s (2004) rules for developing conceptual definitions, along with our label for the expectation to develop a framework of expectations that outlines a set of nine expectations that have been used to predict satisfaction. ## Limitations of Past Research

Although expectations exist at these multiple levels, in most studies, expectations are examined only at the general level (i.e., Bhattachjee, 2001; Hong et al., 2011; Susarla et al., 2003). This limitation of prior research inhibits our ability to better understand the impact of various expectations on satisfaction. Moreover, little research has been undertaken to understand the relative impact of each expectation on the development of an individual’s level of expectation confirmation. Can we assume that all of these expectations exert equal power to explain satisfaction with a product or service?

Moreover, when researchers did employ expectations, they utilized multiple types of expectations in the research, with no consensus as to which set of expectations should be utilized to measure expectations. This unsystematic process of employing expectations, while understandable, limits our ability to comprehensively evaluate the performance of a product or service and inhibits the development of a cumulative research tradition (Keen, 1980). For example, if one researcher selects one expectation while another selects that same expectation in addition to another expectation, there might be an interrelationship between these two expectations that could either positively (or negatively) influence the impact of one (or both) of the expectations. Furthermore, what if one expectation is found to be insignificant when used alone, but when combined with a broader set of expectations becomes significant? There are negative consequences for studying only a subset of the expectations, and we need to examine the entire set to better understand the impact of expectations on outsourcing satisfaction.

Thus, we postulate that there is a gap in the literature regarding the impact of various expectations on outsourcing success. This study seeks to provide this knowledge to close the gap in the literature. Specifically, we argue that an organization, when assessing the success of the relationship with their outsourcing firm examines the extent to which the performance of the vendor matches a subset of the 9 expectations espoused in our literature review. What remains unclear is which of these expectations are utilized in determining client satisfaction.

## HYPOTHESES AND EMPIRICAL EXAMINATION

Research in ECT clearly demonstrates that satisfaction of a product or service is influenced by the extent to which prior expectations are confirmed (Brown et al., 2014; Islam et al., 2017). Our goal is not to reconfirm this relationship, but to explore its nature. Specifically, we seek to understand which expectations should be employed in determining the relationship between expectation confirmation and satisfaction in outsourcing arrangements.

Satisfaction is used as our dependent variable, as it represents one of the most utilized measures of success (Grover et al., 1996; Koh et al., 2004; Levina & Ross, 2003; Rai et al., 2009; Saunders et al., 1997; Seddon et al., 2007). Furthermore, it is believed to be a key indicator of overall performance (Babin & Griffin, 1998). As Pfaff (1977) states “there is little doubt that the maximization of consumer satisfaction is considered by most to be the ultimate goal” (p. 36). We concur with this prevailing view of the importance of satisfaction and believe that it theoretically characterizes one of the most important outcomes from the IT outsourcing relationship. We have therefore selected to employ client satisfaction as the dependent variable.

Our study explores 9 different expectations that potentially influence outsourcing satisfaction. Each expectation is hypothesized to influence satisfaction, based upon our literature review of prior studies. For each hypothesis, we detail the unique facet of the expectation under consideration.

The ideal expectation bases a comparison of experience on the perfect outsourcing relationship, from the perspective of the client. This expectation is the most difficult to meet, as it is the highest in the mind of the individual making the comparison.

H1: The degree of confirmation of the ideal expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The should expectation bases a comparison of experience on what the client feels they ought to receive, based upon industry practices. This expectation is influenced by the industry surrounding the outsourcing vendor, as the client is comparing their experience with the standard within the industry.

H2: The degree of confirmation of the should expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The wanted expectation bases a comparison of experience on what the client feels the outsourcing vendor can capably provide. This differs from the ideal expectation in its basis. While the ideal expectation is independent of the vendor, the wanted expectation considers the capabilities of the vendor to determine the expectation.

H3: The degree of confirmation of the wanted expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The predicted expectation bases a comparison of experience on the outsourcing vendor’s prior performance. This expectation is formed purely with the vendor in mind, comparing current performance to a reasonable expectation based off of prior performance.

H4: The degree of confirmation of the predicted expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The deserved expectation bases a comparison of experience on what the client feels they are owed. Costs are likely to influence this expectation, as clients who incur greater costs are more likely to have higher expectations based upon what they feel they deserve. This expectation is also based on the client’s perceived self-worth. Clients that believe they are worthy of a higher level of performance will have a higher deserved expectation.

H5: The degree of confirmation of the deserved expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The adequate expectation bases a comparison of experience on what the client feels is an acceptable level of performance. This expectation focuses on the most vital aspects of performance, according to the client. Performance that meets these most vital client needs is most likely to meet or exceed the adequate expectation.

H6: The degree of confirmation of the adequate expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The minimally acceptable expectation bases a comparison of experience on the lowest level of performance that the client is willing to accept. This level of performance is still acceptable, but only according to minimal criteria.

H7: The degree of confirmation of the minimally acceptable expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The intolerable expectation bases a comparison of experience on what the client would deem a possible yet unacceptably low level of performance. This expectation, as opposed to most others, would not be considered acceptable even if it is matched. The basis for the intolerable expectation may be unsatisfactory experiences from other outsourcing vendors.

H8: The degree of confirmation of the intolerable expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

The worst imaginable expectation bases a comparison of experience on the lowest level of performance conceivable to the client. This expectation comprises the client’s “worst-case scenario,” owing to the fears of the client in the level of performance they may receive.

H9: The degree of confirmation of the worst imaginable expectation is positively related to satisfaction with an outsourcing vendor.

## Research Methodology

Drawing upon Expectation Confirmation Theory, we have outlined a research model that examines our hypotheses related to the influence of expectations on IT outsourcing success. This model will also enable us to determine the relative impact of the extant expectations on satisfaction. We designed an empirical study to assess our research model. Before discussing the results, the operationalization of the constructs will be discussed.

## Survey Development

Based upon the conceptualization of the constructs, all of the constructs to be studied were defined and items were developed. The items utilized to measure IT outsourcing satisfaction were adapted from existing measures (Chin & Lee, 2000). Utilizing our framework of expectations, items were generated based on the definitions of each construct. Each expectation was measured by a consistent set of three items reflecting various aspects of vendor performance – a) overall performance of my vendor, b) extent to which the vendor met the needs of my organization, and c) overall experience with my vendor. ## Pretesting the Instrument

After the survey was designed, a pilot study was conducted to assess face validity and to improve the design of the research instrument. The individuals were provided with the online survey and asked to provide feedback on the clarity

## Sample

To test the proposed research model, a national survey was conducted to collect data for this study. The population of interest was the principal IT outsourcing decision makers in firms who have engaged in outsourcing. We followed a systematic approach in constructing the mailing list for the survey.

First, firms that could serve as the sample were identified. To locate firms, a database of top IT executives, The Directory of Top Computer Executives, was employed as the basis for the sample. The Directory has been utilized in prior publications (Schwarz 2014; Gorla & Somers, 2014; Schwarz et al., 2014; Schwarz et al., 2009; Ravichandran & Rai, 2000) and hence constitutes a reliable source for the sample.

As we were seeking a particular target population, specifically the principal IT outsourcing decision maker, we created a survey respondent pool of qualified subjects from the database. As we realized that some of the IT executives in the database would “not possess the attribute(s) necessary” (Dillman, 1978, p.42) to complete the survey (e.g., they do not engage in outsourcing or would not be considered the principal decision maker), the use of a survey respondent pool enables us to prequalify the computer executives in the database. Therefore, we sent an opt-in message to each of the e-mail addresses listed in the database. By narrowing the email database with this method, we allowed the key IT outsourcing decision makers to opt-in, and we can mitigate any unwanted messages (Mehta & Sivadas, 1995; Sheehan, 2001; Yun & Trumbo, 2000) to recipients not in the target population.

We utilized the following methodology. First, all the IT executives in the database were sent a personalized prenotification e-mail, offering the recipient an opportunity to “opt in” to the survey respondent pool if they qualify as part of the target population. Respondents indicated their interest in participating by opting in or responding to the e-mail. They were then sent an email with an embedded link that directed them to the web-based survey. ## Overview of the Sample

In the invitation letter to the sample group, the researchers requested that the principal decision maker in the organization complete the survey. In general, the respondents were employed in upper management positions in the organization or positions where they would typically be considered the primary decision maker with regard to the vendor organization.

Most of the outsourcing projects involve infrastructure, which typically involves a longterm partnership between the client and vendor organizations. While infrastructure constitutes a growing area of IT outsourcing, it also involves the development of a complex relationship as the

IT infrastructure impacts all areas of the corporation. Therefore, in these types of outsourcing arrangements which combine both the complexity of managing expectations across multiple departments in addition to the development of a long-term relationship, the expectations can be especially rich and developed. By studying these types of complex and perpetual outsourcing arrangements, we can assess a variety of expectations involved in these intricate IT outsourcing arrangements and determine the structure of the extant expectations.

## Results

With the latent constructs and items developed and the data collected, we selected to utilize Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to empirically test the research model and hierarchically structure the extant expectations. While many techniques of SEM exist, the two best known approaches are the covariance-based methodology (found in software such as LISREL, AMOS, and EQS) and partial least squares (found in software such as PLS-Graph). ## DISCUSSION

Utilizing the results of an empirical analysis, we present an OEM that decomposes expectations into three different expectations that combine to influence outsourcing satisfaction. We confirm existing research in ECT recognizing that the extent of confirmation significantly impacts satisfaction while extending this understanding in the outsourcing context by providing a multiple-expectation approach that differs from the traditional general-expectation approach. We now discuss the three expectations and how they influence outsourcing satisfaction.

## The Should Expectation

The should expectation involves an external standard (such as industry practice), which a client utilizes in the development of their confirmation evaluation. For example, when employing the should expectation, the client could employ industry practices in their conceptualization of what they should expect to receive from the outsourcing vendor, and then compare their vendor’s performance with what the client believes they should receive from their outsourcing vendor to determine their level of confirmation.

This expectation includes an element of what a client thinks they have been promised. When vying to be awarded a contract, vendors may make certain promises or clients may perceive certain statements as promises. When a client believes that a promise has been made, they will seek to determine whether this promise has been fulfilled, and the fulfillment of the promise (or lack thereof) will factor into their assessment of satisfaction with the outsourcing relationship. Thus, for example, the client will seek to employ the should expectation to assess whether they were given what they should get from the vendor based on perceived promises.

The should expectation has a normative nature, based upon the standard practices of the industry. The significance of the relationship between the should expectation and client satisfaction indicates that outsourcing clients are aware of industry norms and base their expectations on those grounds. Thus, it is imperative that outsourcing vendors assess the standard practices of their industry to more fully understand how clients form their expectations. Failure to meet the standards of the industry could lead to a surprise disappointment from the client.

## Minimally Acceptable Expectation

The minimally acceptable expectation represents the minimal level of performance that a vendor must meet and exceed if they ever hope for a client to be satisfied. This expectation represents the most accessible and manageable expectations to meet. Vendors who are considered low-cost providers may not be able to meet the should expectation. However, their focus should be on at least meeting this lower level expectation, which is easier to accomplish.

The minimally acceptable expectation is not the lowest expectation of the client, but the lowest value that would still be deemed satisfactory. For example, a client may internally identify a 10% cost overrun as the minimal acceptance level for a project budget. While the client would prefer that the project be at or below budget; at a minimum, there is the expectation that cost overruns will not exceed 10%.

## Intolerable Expectation

Intolerable expectations include the fears of the client, which may have developed from negative stories from colleagues, the news, or personal experiences. Clients hope to never

experience some of the worst-case scenarios associated with these intolerable expectations, and they most likely have never personally experienced (Santos & Boote, 2003) this level of performance. However, if the vendor fails to meet these baseline expectations, then the minimally acceptable expectations will surely not be met, and the client will not be satisfied.

Using the above example, the intolerable expectation for budget adherence in an outsourcing arrangement might be a 200% overrun. Perhaps the client has prior experience with an unacceptable project that incurred that level of expense, and thus they would use 200% as an intolerable benchmark. Where this expectation differs from the minimally acceptable expectation is in its expectation. Should an outsourcing vendor merely meet or match the intolerable expectation, satisfaction would be low. This expectation is one which is expected to be exceeded.

## LIMITATIONS

Although this research is the first to apply ECT to the outsourcing literature and determine which expectations influence success, certain limitations exist. First, the sample size from the survey was low (n=106), and we employed a small dataset. Although the sample contained enough power to analyze the data, the use of a smaller sample size may create a failure to detect a small effect. Therefore, we suggest that future researchers retest these expectations with a larger sample size to validate its significance.

Furthermore, we employed a cross-sectional design in the study, which involves an observation of the sample at one point in time. As expectations change over time and extent of confirmations adjust over time, a longitudinal study could provide evidence of how these expectations influence satisfaction over time. Several studies (e.g., Brown et al. 2014; Islam et al. 2017) have measured expectations using a pre-post evaluation, where perceptions are obtained both before and after experiencing a product or service. Furthermore, Brown et al. (2014)

highlight the necessity of considering curvilinear relationships between confirmation and satisfaction while decoupling expectations from experience. Future researchers can combine our findings regarding expectations in outsourcing with recent developments regarding modeling satisfaction to inform studies, which examine the antecedents of outsourcing success more completely.

To measure expectation confirmation, we utilized a direct measurement approach. While this approach offers simplicity, some researchers have advocated for more complex approaches to evaluate the relationship between confirmation and satisfaction. Brown et al. (2014) advocate for polynomial regression and response surface analysis to be used, partly because of the flexibility for various types of relationships, beyond simple linear relationships. Our findings could be elaborated through the use of such techniques, utilizing the three expectations highlighted in our results.

Although the findings demonstrate a sufficient level of discriminant validity from an SEM perspective (Schwarz et al. 2014), we recognize the cross-loadings and high correlations as potential limitations of the study. We, therefore, encourage other researchers to replicate these findings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides knowledge to the outsourcing literature and other research streams, which utilize ECT. In the following sections, we highlight implications for both research and practice.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR RESEARCH

This study offers both specific and general implications for future research. Specifically, it offers new insights into the determinants of satisfaction in outsourcing arrangements. Generally, it extends the current literature reexamining the best methods to evaluate and utilize expectations in ECT studies. In this section, we elaborate on the implications to both the outsourcing literature, specifically, and the ECT literature, more broadly.

This research contributes to the IS outsourcing literature by providing a more complete picture of the determinants of client satisfaction. Client satisfaction is the most often studied dependent variable in outsourcing research (Lacity et al. 2017). One stream of outsourcing research involves studying the determinants of satisfaction (Moon et al. 2016). It is recognized that the outcomes of an outsourcing project influence satisfaction (Gonzalez et al. 2015). While outcomes are important, it is also important to understand the expectations that those outcomes are measured against.

Our study highlights three different expectations that together influence client satisfaction in outsourcing projects. Our findings contribute to outsourcing literature by demonstrating that there are different and unique expectations that inform satisfaction. While many of the identified standards featured conceptual overlaps, our model combining all nine standards produced a more parsimonious three that can be used as an antecedent to satisfaction. This indicates that client expectations are not uniform, but multifaceted, and should be researched accordingly.

We encourage future researchers to extend our findings by evaluating the determinants for each of the three expectations discovered in the model. If all client expectations were Consequently, because three expectations emerged, the findings indicate that different factors influence each expectation. For example, the factors that clients consider for the minimally acceptable standard may not be the same as those considered for the should standard. With so many potential factors that could influence outsourcing success (Moon et al. 2016), we encourage researchers to utilize the three expectations from our results to continue their work.

For research on Expectation Confirmation Theory, this study contributes to the growing body of literature highlighting the inadequacies of former operationalizations. Many prior researchers have followed their predecessors in utilizing a generalized expectation, one which merely evaluates the level of performance they expect to receive. With the introduction of expectations into the ECT literature in 1977 (Miller), a basis was provided for examining the multifaceted nature of the expectations construct. Our findings indicate that future researche A seeking to most accurately evaluate the antecedents of outsourcing satisfaction should replace the generalized approach with one that incorporates the three expectations we have identified.

This study extends recent research on methods to evaluate expectation confirmation. While Islam et al. (2017) decomposed confirmation in technology utilizing scenarios, our findings similarly recognize that expectations should not be generalized but should be more specifically evaluated. While service quality research highlights the need to recognize levels of expectations (Kettinger & Lee, 2005), our study explicitly measures nine different standards to provide a diversified, yet parsimonious method of evaluating outsourcing expectations. Kettinger & Lee’s (2005) SERVQUAL measure includes two expectation standards (minimum and desired) to form a zone of tolerance evaluation. Our study extends their findings by delving deeper into expectation standards and providing a more granular understanding specific to outsourcing arrangements. Notably, we offer two main extensions. First, we demonstrate that both the intolerable and the minimally acceptable standards are influential on satisfaction, signifying granularity on their “minimum” expectation. Second, by decomposing the “desired” expectation into numerous possibilities, we were able to discover that the should expectation stood out above the rest. Overall, by gaining a deeper level of specificity on outsourcing

expectations, we offer vendors greater precision in understanding client expectations, both their levels and sources.

Finally, we note that while our results identify three expectations that apply to satisfaction in outsourcing, Brown et al. (2014) note that expectations differ across contexts. Therefore, we encourage future researchers to examine the expectations most applicable to other contexts such as technology adoption, CIO performance, and continuance. A significant amount of work has identified the complexity of expectation confirmation. Our findings add to this body of literature.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

The results of this study reveal the deeper mechanisms that influence expectations in outsourcing arrangements. While it has long been evident that outsourcing vendors benefit from exceeding client expectations, the genesis of those expectations was previously ambiguous. Through our empirical analysis, we demonstrate that client expectations are multifaceted, based on multiple expectations rather than a generalized expectation, and identify a parsimonious set of expectations that encompasses a very high percentage of variance in client satisfaction. Multiple implications abound for outsourcing vendors that seek to maximize such satisfaction in projects. In this section, we highlight how managers can benefit from our findings.

First, from our recognition that outsourcing expectations are multifaceted, managers can better understand the complexity in marketing their services. Outsourcing clients establish a priori expectations across multiple dimensions; thus, it would be inadequate for vendors to focus on only one source in advertising their services. For example, while we found that the minimially acceptable expectation was significantly related to client satisfaction, outsourcing vendors who tailor their marketing approach to demonstrate that they exceed minimum expectations may find their efforts lacking. Such an approach should be integrated with marketing efforts that also target the intolerable and should expectations to maximize their utility.

Second, our findings provide unique insights for each of the expectations identified in our final model. Regarding the intolerable expectation, managers must recognize that clients consider a level of performance that they deem unacceptable when conducting evaluations. This presents an opportunity for outsourcing vendors to inquire of their clients not only what that unacceptable level of performance may be, but also the genesis of that level of performance. It may be that a client has had a poor experience in the past, and this weighs heavily on their future expectations.

Regarding the minimally acceptable expectation, managers should seek to identify what the minimum expectation is for their performance. Our findings indicate that clients place a significant degree of importance on this minimum expectation; therefore, it is imperative that outsourcing vendors engage with their clients, or pay careful attention within their industry to determine the minimum level they must exceed. Additionally, it could benefit vendors to offer more realistic assessments of performance expectations, to ensure that the minimum expectation is set at an appropriate level. According to our results, it could be hazardous to engage with clients who have an unrealistic minimum expectation.

Regarding the should expectation, managers must understand that expectations are influenced by the norms of the industry. Therefore, it is imperative that managers assess their competitors to ensure that they have a full understanding of the norms in which they operate. The factors that clients consider when evaluating performance may be influenced by the standards set by other firms in the industry.

##

According to our results, the most satisfied clients will be those who determine that the performance of their outsourcing vendor was better than the intolerable level they feared, the minimum level they were willing to tolerate given the circumstances, and the level they felt they should receive. Vendors who fully understand the performance metrics that match these levels for their clients are those most likely to satisfy.

With industry’s demonstration of a desire to engage in the risky and complex world of outsourcing, this study informs practitioners on a concept that leads to a successful outsourcing relationship. With a more comprehensive understanding of the complexity of expectations, practitioners can focus on the salient dimensions of expectations to achieve higher rates of success in their costly pursuit of customer satisfaction. The issue arises, however, regarding the process of how to understand client expectations and the best method to address them. One option involves including an intermediary or an outsourcing consultant in developing the contract and shaping realistic expectations. An intermediary with a considerable amount of experience with IT outsourcing arrangements represents a neutral party who can assure the client that they are getting a good deal, while simultaneously ensuring that the vendor presents a realistic picture of what outsourcing can provide the organization. The addition of an intermediary can also assist in shaping realistic expectations, so that the partners enter the relationship with a more accurate view of what an IT outsourcing relationship entails. Entering the relationship with more realistic expectations increases the potential for achieving success.

## CONCLUDING THOUGHTS

The role of expectations has a long history within academic thought. However, no research to date has examined outsourcing success through the lens of ECT. This work represents the first effort to identify specific expectations in shaping outsourcing satisfaction. We urge other researchers to incorporate a multifaceted approach to expectations into their ECT models, and to mimic our approach in alternative contexts to deepen our understanding of the role and genesis of expectations.
