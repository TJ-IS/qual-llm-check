RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Information systems as a discipline in the making: comparing EJIS and MISQ between 1995 and 2008

Jose´-Rodrigo Co´rdoba<sup>1</sup>, Alan Pilkington<sup>1</sup> and Edward W.N. Bernroider<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London (UK), Surrey, U.K.; <sup>2</sup>WU, Vienna University of Economics and Business, Institute for Information Management and Control, Vienna, Austria

Correspondence: Jose´-Rodrigo Co´rdoba, School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London (UK), Egham Hill, Egham, Surrey TW20 0EX, U.K. Tel: 01 78 427 6401; E-mail: j.r.cordoba-pachon@rhul.ac.uk

## Abstract

This paper aims to shed light on the dynamics of information systems (IS) as a discipline in the making. We use the ideas of the sociologist Abbott to propose three different stages of a discipline’s development: differentiation, competition, and absorption. These stages reflect how disciplines go through different cycles and how they acquire, consolidate or lose elements of knowledge. We map these stages using citation and co-citation analyses of two main IS academic journals (EJIS and MISQ) from 1995 to 2008. Our results indicate that IS is currently in a stage of absorption, with research being consolidated around the theme of ‘IS acceptance’. Dominant models and frameworks related to this theme are predictive and thus lend themselves usable for positivistic and quantitative research. In this stage there is also a healthy degree of variety in IS including dormant elements which could reignite. Implications derived from our findings aim to help in the consolidation and extension of knowledge about IS in both academia and practice. Keywords: information systems; disciplines; citation analysis; co-citation analysis; EJIS; MISQ

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) is a body of knowledge that has developed and spread in many different ways over recent decades. As such, IS has been mainly concerned with the study of how people adopt, modify, use or reject software applications and their underlying processes and impacts in different types of organisational settings. A number of proposals have emerged to define and map different elements of the knowledge that IS academics and practitioners (should) use in their work (Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Dwivedi & Kuljis, 2008; Klein & Hirschheim, 2008). These proposals reflect an interest by some members of the IS community to clearly define what the IS discipline is about, so that knowledge about it can be consolidated and offered to the outside world (Baskerville et al, 2011; Somers, 2010). To other members of this community there is an inherent risk in this attempt as any definition can be exclusive and potentially detrimental to the diversity of contributing groups that form the IS community and to the fluidity of the discipline (Introna, 2003; Bryant, 2008; Paul, 2008; Oesterle et al, 2011).

With recent debates in journals like European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS) about the inclusion (or exclusion) of design science-oriented articles in publication outlets (Baskerville et al, 2011; Oesterle et al, 2011), the question of how best to understand IS regarding the knowledge that it can offer to academics, students and practitioners becomes essential to address. In this paper we contribute with a perspective about both the elements of knowledge that the IS community has produced as well as the associated stages of unfolding through which the field of IS has been in recent decades. Through different stages, some knowledge elements persist or are re-defined whereas others become dormant but nevertheless may reignite at any time. Moreover, understanding where IS is currently at could allow us to define avenues for development in both academia and practice.

We use Abbott’s ideas on disciplines (1988, 2001) to analyse the dynamics of IS article citations and co-citations in two prominent IS journals from 1995 to 2008: The European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS) and the Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ). These are two highly ranked journals in the field (AIS, 2007) and influence the work of many academics and practitioners worldwide. We identify similarities and differences between these journals in three different time periods: 1995 to 1999; 2000 to 2004; and 2005 to 2008. We show how through these different periods IS people have proposed, revisited or discarded ideas so that their community, as represented by these journals, continuously repositions itself in both academia and in society in general.

Our findings suggest that IS is currently in a stage of absorption, in which knowledge is being consolidated around the theme of ‘IS acceptance’. This topic bears strong relationship with a previously dominating one: ‘user satisfaction with IS’. Furthermore, the topic is currently studied and advanced through a number of predictive models and frameworks which have been mainly developed via positivist orientations. Both models and frameworks can be regarded as ‘portable’, ‘usable’ and ‘prediction oriented’, with an orientation to explain and predict IS users’ behaviour. The dominance of IS acceptance as a theme can contribute to increase the visibility of IS in research and practice. However, it can also limit the exploration of new themes and areas of work. Academics and practitioners need to assess the challenges and opportunities lying within this theme by considering other types of research (i.e. interpretive, constructivist and critically oriented) which have persisted through time and could help enhancing our understanding of IS phenomena.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we revisit current debates about the status of IS as a discipline. We then present Abbott’s ideas and derive a process of unfolding disciplines in three stages (differentiation, competition, absorption). This is followed by our methodology section and findings. Lastly we discuss the results regarding the IS discipline and its processes of unfolding, and conclude by drawing a number of implications for education, research and practice which we see as relevant for IS’s next steps.

## IS as a stable or fluid ‘discipline’?

To some individuals IS is already an established discipline that can stand up to or collaborate with other disciplines in addressing social phenomena involving the adequate adoption and use of information technologies in organisations (Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Benbasat & Barki, 2007). These individuals argue that within IS there is already a sufficient set of ‘core’ elements of knowledge (i.e. topics, concepts and phenomena) that can be considered unique in IS and which should fuel future research and practice within and outside the field. Knowledge elements include but are not limited to (Davis, 2000; Iivari et al, 2004; Benbasat & Barki, 2007): IS development and management processes; human behaviours influencing or resulting from these processes; models, methods and concepts for software coding, testing and quality assurance; representations of data, events and systems structures; expert, decision support, collaborative and transactional application systems. The availability of these elements can help IS become an established discipline in two specific ways (Hassan, 2006; Somers, 2010): First, it can serve as a basis or reference from which to pursuit a coherent set of ideals and themes in IS along with concepts and procedures to develop further knowledge and theories. Second, these elements can be offered to several audiences of students and practitioners as to make the work of IS people more valuable and relevant.

A contrasting perspective to the above is that advocated by other individuals who conceive of IS as community which thrives continuously on promoting mutual understanding between different groups and their ideas (Bryant, 2008; Klein & Hirschheim, 2008; Somers, 2010). Within this diversity there is a number of philosophical research paradigms and concepts that can be adopted and used to understand IS-related phenomena (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Vessey et al, 2002; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Advocates of this perspective consider that key to a healthy development of IS is the degree of fluidity, flexibility and variety with which this community can respond to new challenges and opportunities by using, adopting or exchanging concepts and ideas. By hardening the boundaries of what constitutes IS knowledge, attributes of flexibility and fluidity in IS research and practice can be compromised, given that a boundary can exclude groups in the IS community or beyond as well as their knowledge (Bryant, 2008).

On closer examination of IS journal citations evidence supports the above perspectives (Vessey et al, 2002; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Dwivedi & Kuljis, 2008), as we find that current studies on IS assume that knowledge is being ‘accumulated’ and/or ‘retained’ in a linear fashion, with little or no explicit consideration of stages through which IS, as a potential discipline in the making could go through. IS is in continuous interaction with other disciplines, borrowing or exchanging concepts and ideas in order to respond to new possibilities and challenges. A more dynamic perspective is needed to look at how IS unfolds. Therefore, we propose exploring the following aspects about IS: (1) What processes or stages can be identified in the dynamics of IS? (2) What elements of knowledge can be associated with such stages, and how these elements relate to each other? (3) What can we learn about stability or fluidity in consideration of where IS has been and where it could go next? As IS academics, educators or practitioners, focusing on these aspects will help us in assessing knowledge that can be offered from IS as well as to fully justify challenges and opportunities lying ahead. To investigate these aspects we now turn our attention to the work of Abbott on knowledge disciplines.

## Abbott on knowledge disciplines

The sociologist Abbott (2001) proposes that social science disciplines are part of a wider cultural and social system. A discipline provides knowledge that contributes to diagnose, treat and infer on a particular set of problems in practice. Disciplines continuously compete with each other to gain jurisdiction to a domain of knowledge, abstraction and education. They do so by claiming that their knowledge can help solve specific problems and therefore challenge similar claims from within or from other disciplines.

Abbott (1988) also describes how knowledge disciplines have to continuously show that what they do is relevant to the professions that they serve in society. Internally, disciplines work on developing new insights on ways of diagnosing and treating problems as well as inferring new ones. Externally they check on and compete with others for ownership and recognition. According to Abbott (2001), disciplines go through self-recurring patterns of development as they respond to cultural and societal demands, reflecting what society values at particular periods of time. He suggests the following iterative stages (Abbott, 2001):

a. Differentiation: A discipline claims ‘jurisdiction’ (i.e. territory or ownership) on specific problem areas that are being vacated by others. In order to do so, a discipline differentiates itself from others in order to lay its claims over a number of problems, with this also occurring internally within a discipline. In the social sciences, differentiation generates a number of knowledge distinctions (mostly dichotomies) both within and between disciplines which reflect different assumptions made about problems and how to treat them. This includes, for instance, distinctions between agency and structure, conflict and consensus, quantitative and qualitative research, social history and historic sociology. Distinctions are often associated with others, so for instance distinctions in economics is more often associated with quantitative than qualitative analysis, whereas distinctions in sociology are more qualitative in nature.

b. Competition: The different distinctions made about problem diagnoses, treatments or inferences between the two generate competition within and between disciplines. Competitors deny each other’s relevance of the methods, concepts or paradigms used to address specific problems. For example, both medicine and psychology compete for problems of human health with their own distinctions about quality of life and how to improve it. Within a discipline it is common to see competition between qualitative and quantitative research to address the same problem. Informationrelated issues in organisations still generate competition between IS, management or computer science professionals regarding how to go about them.

c. Absorption: This stage manifests itself when one set of distinctions and connections between them absorbs or ingests others who were previously their competitors. The ‘winning’ of jurisdiction by one group means that it has to integrate their rivals’ distinctions to diagnose, treat and infer on problems into their own. As Abbott says: ‘A generation triumphs over its elders, then calmly resurrects their ideas, pretending all the while to advance the cause of knowledge’ (2001, p. 17). Even if a winner absorbs the losers, the distinctions of the latter will inevitably re-emerge in the next cycle(s) of differentiation. According to Abbott, this is because society needs to rescue and maintain this knowledge regardless of who ‘owns’ it at a particular time: ‘We get to keep our best concepts forever yet can retain our belief in perpetual, intellectual progress’ (Abbott, 2001, p. 27).

With the above, Abbott (2001) proposes that disciplines do not unfold linearly but in continuous and multifarious stages of expansion, stabilisation and/or contraction. As part of this dynamic unfolding, Abbott (1988, 2001) argues that a discipline consolidates knowledge about its problem diagnoses, treatments and inferences by making them simple to grasp and use, portable, and therefore accessible to others. However, this is not a stable stage of affairs because by doing this a discipline can lose jurisdiction: Knowledge distinctions can become subject to internal or external attack or ‘hijacking’ when they become too ‘generic’, so it can be challenged, used and re-combined elsewhere. To maintain their status, disciplines can adopt two types of strategies. First, they can enter into new domains of practice by gaining new insights into problems owned or vacated by other disciplines. Second, they strengthen any linkages between theory and practice so that their work continues to be valued by different audiences. In implementing both of these strategies the work of academics is fundamental. Academics can contribute to fill existing gaps in a discipline by exploring new areas of practice, by abstracting new elements (diagnoses, treatments, inferences or problem classifications) and by making these available to other academics, students or practitioners in appropriate formats.

In the domain of IS and using the ideas of Abbott (1988), Somers (2010) has recently suggested that in order to continue developing IS as a discipline, IS people should build consensus about the problems they ‘own’ or have jurisdiction over, as well as the knowledge distinctions which they can offer to address them. Somers’ idea of ‘building on what we are good at’ opens up the possibility of exploring in more depth how IS has unfolded through time. We take this possibility forward to enhance our understanding of the knowledge that IS has consolidated and can offer, with a view to suggest possibilities and challenges for the future. In the next section we detail the methodology we use to explore the unfolding of IS in terms of the stages already outlined.

## Methodology

Our study requires an alternative methodological approach from those that only aim to show the accumulative existence of certain core elements in IS (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Dwivedi & Kuljis, 2008), or from those that focus on looking at how diverse IS has become (Vessey et al, 2002). We need identification and interpretation of the knowledge distinctions in IS as occurring through stages of differentiation, competition and absorption, and how they relate to each other to give us a view of IS as a body of knowledge.

To identify knowledge distinctions and their connections we use a combination of bibliographic citation and co-citation analyses of a U.S.-based journal (Management Information Systems Quarterly or MISQ) and a European one (European Journal of Information Systems or EJIS), both of which are classified as A\* (AIS, 2007). The first journal (MISQ) focuses on organisational topics related to IS implementation and use, aiming to establish itself as a reference in the discipline (Vessey et al, 2002). MISQ is regarded mainly as positivist and quantitative in orientation. The second one (EJIS) is regarded as a distinctively European and more diverse in relation to the types of research it publishes, although it is also seen as following MISQ in publishing mainly positivist and behaviourally oriented research (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Oesterle et al, 2011) (we expand on this later in the paper). By using citation and co-citation analyses, we aim to reinterpret these claims by considering how maps of knowledge distinctions and connections represent a particular process of IS unfolding.

Citation analysis relies on the idea that a heavily cited article will have exerted a greater influence on a subject than those less frequently referenced (Sharplin & Mabry, 1985; Culnan, 1986). According to White and Griffith (1981), citation analysis represents ‘the field’s view of itself’. Using citation analysis, we can examine the growth in citations and get a sense of when the major articles in the field were written, how article popularity fared over time, and whether an article is still useful to current researchers. Following Abbott (1988, 2001) we assume that a popular article that addresses one or several topics (keywords) contains knowledge distinctions related to IS diagnoses of problems, treatments and inferences linking these two. In the identification of distinctions from citations we limit the impact of data accuracy issues of articles (keyword, author, journal or book names when dealing with similar items) by carefully selecting the source documents, and also by drawing on the contents of two distinct journals. A further concern is how to weight the different elements identified. In our study, we attribute equal weight to each article as we intend to map the dynamic unfolding of the field from two perspectives.

Regarding the interpretation of citations, there is the danger of focusing on one author (i.e. most cited or prolific), or on a publication as units of analysis (Garfield, 1979). Moreover, citation analysis alone does not show the structure of ideas in a field as such (Leong, 1989); neither it would show us how knowledge distinctions are connected, or if there are gaps in knowledge that could be addressed. Co-citation analysis helps us to map connections between the articles initially identified through the citation analysis. These connections also represent relationships in the intellectual structure of a discipline as White (1990) notes. White also recommends ‘nothing better for reconnoitering “macro level” intellectual structure as it evolves. The maps are essentially a new kind of graphics for revealing inter-textual relationships’.

By building on co-citation analysis, other tools can be used to graphically plot citation influence on each other, how they relate, the strength of relationships, and how central to groups a particular citation (article) is. In reporting the relationships with co-citations, several approaches are often used including using factor analysis or multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) to identify implicit dimensions. Factor analysis was used for example by Pilkington & Meredith (2009) to identify that the most cited articles in operations management could be fitted to 12 groups and so repeatedly cover these topics. MDS examples include Ramos-Rodrı´guez & Ruı´z-Navarro (2004) and Hoffman & Holbrook (1993) who use this approach to represent the structural knowledge of the strategic management and consumer research disciplines. Another approach is to utilise network analysis tools to represent the information and also develop measures that allow comparisons between different networks (in different disciplines or from alternative sources) and the roles of the nodes (authors or articles) themselves. These techniques have been employed to examine the development of a range of disciplines, including service and technology management (Pilkington & Teichert, 2006; Pilkington & Chai, 2008).

We also found that if we set certain limits on the number of links shown in the networks of co-citations, the number of nodes connected becomes more manageable and the relationships clearer. Thus a limit of, say, at least five co-citations may be set and a clear diagram produced. It is important to remember that the values represent total co-citation in the relevant database and so if a set limit were to be used, the same level of detail or congestion will not result and the diagrams for one data set may look substantially different from those for another even though the same limit was used in each. For our diagrams of networks we set the limits to show only publications cited four or more times and with co-citation links greater than 3, 2 and 5 for the different periods of the EJIS data, and 4, 5 and 7 for the MISQ set, and then only to show the nodes which are still connected to at least one other node. These levels are useful in that they show the relationships between the central, most highly cited references and also, at these levels, the networks fragment to allow us to talk of groups. The same issue was faced by Pilkington & Meredith (2009) and they present a robust defence of the approach.

Following Abbott (2001), we conceive of co-citations as representing connections between knowledge distinctions through time. Once built, we proceeded to interpret the networks of co-citations in relation to how IS has gone through different stages by using the following criteria:

\- We regard differentiation in IS as the existence of different groups or clusters in co-citations. Clusters represent distinctions and connections, with weak connections between clusters being interpreted as distinctions originating to and from several distinctive journal articles or books. We corroborate distinctiveness with our own interpretation in terms of how clusters propose a generic theme for research (i.e. diffusion) or how they establish a set of methodological distinctions (i.e. positivist, interpretive or critical).

\- We interpret competition within IS as the existence of clusters of co-citations in a map which are both strongly connected to hubs (one or several articles or books ) while exhibiting a stronger degree of intraconnection from a previous time period. The strength of intra-cluster connection indicates that a set of distinctions is becoming prominent, so that it is being used by a particular group in IS to protect or expand their current jurisdiction. Connections between clusters of co-citations indicate that there are relations of collaboration or dispute between IS groups because they might be sharing a common set of claims or points of foci. The end result of these inter-cluster connections through time is, according to Abbott, the prevalence of one group over the other (s) (Abbott, 2001).

\- We associate absorption with the resulting concentration of clusters of co-citations into a particular area, with a decline or a reduction of concentrations in other clusters. Internally for IS, this could be attributed to the ‘winning’ of some sets of distinctions over others, whereas externally, it could signal that IS has stabilised, gained or lost its jurisdiction claims. As we will discuss later, this stage can also signal the potential beginning of a new stage of differentiation in IS.

To identify the above stages, we chose periods of five years; this gives sufficient data to present meaningful results, and yet retains the contemporary nature of any change. We were wary of producing too large segregations and so hide changes by amalgamating over say 10 years. There is little guidance from other studies as to how to divide citation data to track changes, with examples from 1 to 10 years being common (Pieters et al, 1999; Leydesdorff & Zhou, 2005; Biehl et al, 2006; Pilkington & Teichert, 2006; Kim et al, 2009; Shibata et al, 2011). Following Pilkington & Meredith (2009) we displayed the results graphically using the techniques developed for social network analysis (Scott, 1994; Wasserman & Faust, 1994), which allow us to show connections between citations as well as the strength between them. The resulting diagrams were produced using NETDRAW part of the UCINET SNA package (Borgatti et al, 2002). NETDRAW is one standard tool for graph mapping along with PAJEK. The figures show links in the co-citation matrix and are produced by first reducing all the co-citation values to binary zeros and ones, with the strength of the links added later in the form of line thicknesses. Node positions on the diagrams result from a spring-based algorithm developed by Kamada & Kawai (1989), which iteratively reduces the stress in the graph from co-locating unconnected nodes together by trying alternative node positions. So papers that are often co-cited with each other appear close together and have thicker lines joining them.

## Data management

The normal source of data for studies using citation and co-citation techniques is the ISI social science citations index, which contains both information on source articles and their citation lists. We used this as the source for our data for this study. Specifically, the data used in the study included the contents of MISQ and EJIS between 1995 and 2008, specifically, MISQ from Vol. 19, No. 1 (1995) to Vol. 32, No. 3 (2008) and EJIS from Vol. 4, No. 1 (1995) to Vol. 17, No. 3 (2008), as recorded in the ISI Social Science Citation Index. Note that the MISQ data are available back to 1984 from ISI, but as the EJIS is only available from 1995 we chose to select the periods shown above.

We chose these publications as they are the leading journals on both sides of the Atlantic and can give us a relevant glimpse of the discipline: the U.S. and wider North American centred MISQ and the European EJIS (Dwivedi & Kuljis, 2008). The MISQ data contained 415 source papers containing 34,555 citations to 19,926 different publications. The EJIS data contained 381 papers making 19,596 citations to 17,163 publications.

Several steps are required to start working with ISI data, including converting it into a standard format and checking for different spellings and abbreviations of names, journal and book titles as well as book editions. To try and reduce these inconsistencies as much as possible we adopted a process of manually checking and re-checking ranked frequency tables of separate and combined data fields and using complex search and replace routines to generate standardised records. For example, ACAD\_MANAGE\_J (Academy of Management Journal) was one of the most listed titles and so corresponding entries such as AC\_MANAGE\_J and ACAD\_MANAG\_J were altered. As such, our data contain the source article information such as publication information, authors, titles and keywords; cross-linked to their standardised citations. The level of information contained in the standardised citations was first author (with one initial), publication (journal or book title) and publication year. Due care was taken to double check that information was not amalgamated when removing an author’s second initials and publication issue information. Standardisation of different book editions into one single reference was accomplished using a similar frequency check approach.

## Results

## Citation analysis

Table 1 shows the countries of origin of the authors identified for correspondence for the source articles in the data. There are clear geographical divisions between the journals, with North American-based authors being the dominant contributors to MISQ and European-based (together with Australasia) as the dominant major writers of EJIS articles. This clearly shows that the division between the journals reflects, as expected, the respective groups that compose the IS community and their diversity. Also it is valuable for this study to note the limited degree of cross publishing between the groups and also that the Rest of the World entries are of small but equal importance to both journals.

Following from our validation of the geographical division of authors who publish in each journal, we then proceeded to examine whether there is any difference in the general citation behaviour between the journals. One simple way to examine whether the journals show different citation habits is to look at the age of the citations. Figure 1 gives the mean age of citation in each journal as around 12 years. This 12-year peak has also been seen in other studies (Ramos-Rodrı´guez & Ruı´z-Navarro, 2004; Stremersch et al, 2007; Pilkington & Meredith, 2009) and gives us confidence that there is no bias generated by the citation practices of each journal.

The above establishes that the two journals do not differ in terms of the nature of their citations and we can then start to analyse if there are distinct areas covered.

## Co-citation analysis: The first period 1995–1999

With a general idea about knowledge distinctions in IS obtained from the previous analysis, we now proceed with an interpretation of different stages of IS as seen in the networks of co-citations in both journals. The first periods in MISQ and EJIS (1995–1999) seen in Figures 2 and 3 can be characterised by differentiation, with IS putting forward knowledge distinctions that link both technology and people, in particular those who link human and organisational dimensions of perceived acceptance with what is regarded as IS successful use (Davis, 1989; Walsham, 1995). Reaction against merely technical implementation of IS seems generalised. With these distinctions, both journals at this stage show efforts to claim jurisdiction on issues which were traditionally occupied by other disciplines (i.e. management, computer science). For instance and drawing from Strategic Management, scholars explore whether and to what extent information and information technology contributes to the strategic objectives of an organisation (McFarlan, 1984; Porter & Millar, 1985). Linking into Marketing, IS research in MISQ made use of the model for assessing service quality (Parasuraman et al, 1985b). Although topics from other disciplines are revisited, IS research adds new information and technology-related views, also generating the need to look into human and technology relations in more detail.

Within IS itself, the positivist paradigm is dominant but also challenged and complemented, leading us to suggest an early internal stage of differentiation. This can be seen in the different clusters of articles that are formed and their internal connections (MISQ), and also in EJIS where clusters are connected via some key articles. MISQ shows clusters characterised by some unique foci in quantitative research such as IT investment and firm performance studies (Weill, 1992), and qualitative approaches to conceptualise information processing and organisational design (Daft & Lengel, 1986) or electronic markets (Malone et al, 1987). In EJIS, some varieties of IS research including interpretive and critical approaches are put forward and with different examples (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993, 1995). A generalised reaction against purely technical IS implementation is taken in different forms, adding to the internal differentiation, showing a distinction between positivism and anti-positivism, with some key articles playing a crucial role. This is the case for instance of articles referring to power and politics in IS implementation (Markus, 1983), soft systems thinking (Checkland, 1981) and IS for competitive advantage and value adding (Porter & Millar, 1985) in EJIS, and, information and organisational design in MISQ (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Other articles detail a variety of assumptions about the nature of the IS phenomena as well as the processes of IS development that took place (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

Differentiation also generates a number of new IS diagnoses and treatments. There are a number of small clusters which emerge in relation to potentially different types of IS research. However we’re not in a position to clearly articulating their underpinning assumptions or why they consolidated under specific and linked clusters. Within these clusters though, one could see that certain linked management and IT topics have lifecycles within the relatively new discipline of IS. For example, the business re-engineering hype of the 1990s is reflected by clusters in early MISQ (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993) and EJIS research (Venkatraman, 1991; Davenport, 1993) but disappeared from 2000 onwards.

In what appears to be a logical association between knowledge distinctions, an identifiable cluster is that of user satisfaction which also shows its connections with varieties of positivism. Examples are the development of constructs such as user information satisfaction (Ives et al, 1983) and Delone and McLean’s set of constructs to predict IS success (DeLone & McLean, 1992), which absorbed communication research of Shannon and Weaver (1949), the information influence theory of Mason (1978) and empirical IS-related research studies from the 1980s. These constructs help to identify aspects required in designing and maintaining IS to develop IS usage and resulting benefits, thus linking diagnoses and treatments. While in early EJIS research, no strong cluster emerged that focused on user satisfaction and behavioural approaches, MISQ shows two distinctive groups: The IS success model (DeLone & McLean, 1992), which defines the first cluster and the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), the second dominating one. The latter construct is reflected much more strongly in MISQ. It was later enriched by different variations and extensions of the model, in particular by the very successful Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003). It should be noted that both seminal articles were also published in MISQ. Interestingly, the two clusters remained separated in MISQ, while they eventually converged into one in EJIS.

## The middle period 2000–2004

Within the second period we see two phenomena in IS: (1) further differentiation as well as (2) some signs of internal competition.

1. Differentiation comes in two forms. First, IS continues claiming jurisdiction from other disciplines (i.e. management) by providing alternatives diagnoses and treatments to organisational themes related to IS adoption and use. Regarding MISQ and in relation to Figure 4, while the unified positivistic acceptance and satisfaction research stream establishes itself even more tightly with links into its foundations in innovation diffusion (Rogers, 1962), a smaller connected group in the area of qualitative research methodologies emerged, potentially supporting new organisational themes. The co-citation network also shows an explicit new interlinked structure on dynamic capabilities (Teece et al, 1997), IS capabilities and change (Rockart et al, 1996), and the resourcebased view of the firm (Barney. 1991: Wernerfelt.

1997). In EJIS (Figure 5), alternatives to organisational themes which reflect a potential degree of expansion of IS jurisdiction for new issues and methods to deal with them can be seen in clusters like:

\- Sociology of IS as actors and networks (Latour, 1987)

\- Successful IT outsourcing decisions (Lacity & Willcocks, 1998)

\- IT investment decision-making processes (Irani et al, 1998)

\- IS usefulness as a predictor of IS acceptance (Davis, 1989)

\- Knowledge management and organisational learning (March, 1991; Nonaka, 1994)

\- Systemic thinking (Checkland, 1981)

Second, EJIS differentiation tends to be more internal within IS. In Figure 5, there is an umbrella of qualitative and interpretive IS research that ‘brings in’ distinctions about qualitative data analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994), building theory from case studies (Eisenhardt, 1989), case research (Benbasat et al, 1987) or grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) as useful research approaches. These distinctions also help consolidating the umbrella abovementioned by relating to the evaluation of interpretive IS research (Klein & Myers, 1999). There is thus an association of qualitative distinctions to plan, design and assess interpretive IS research.

2. However, some of the above distinctions branch out in what can be seen as a stage of internal competition. In addition to similar distinctions associated to interpretive and qualitative research, in EJIS (Figure 5) we find a number of clusters which are being connected by several mediating articles, with the number of internal connections being strengthened from the previous period. Two of the key mediating articles are: The work of Walsham in interpreting IS in organisations (1993) and the work of Markus on power, politics and IS implementation (1983). Both of these aim to capture and reflect on human aspects that influence IS implementation. In the previous period they had a strong connection which helped in the previous differentiation of IS both internally and externally.

However, in this current period in our view, their branches show distinctive perspectives which have similar and thus competing claims about how people adopt and use IS. Linked to Markus’ work, one of the emerging branches is that which is focused on IS success in organisations (DeLone & McLean, 1992), now an identifiable cluster in EJIS (it was identifiable in the previous period in MISQ). Linked to Walsham’s articles but also to Orlikowski & Baroudi’s (1991) there is a cluster that brings sociological and cognitive theories to explain how IS are (or not) designed, implemented and used by individuals considering their particular circumstances and what they (re) create in action (Giddens, 1984; Suchman, 1987). Emphasis in either sociological or cognitive orientations to address what appears to be a theme of (successful) implementation and adoption of IS could lead researchers to privilege one or the other, with the results impacting on what is considered a valuable set of distinctions for the future.

In MISQ (Figure 4), a similar degree of competition can be identified between two apparently isolated clusters: One which offers an orientation on building strategic IT assets (which has a core article in Ross et al, 1996) and another which seems oriented to ensure IS/IT users satisfaction (with a core article in Davis, 1989). From different orientations (one organisational, the other behavioural) and in many respects similar to the previous two identified in the earlier period of MISQ (success and technology acceptance models), both of these varieties aim to ensure successful and competitive use of IS/IT in organisations and could be embedded into their effective management.

Overall, this middle period of IS can be regarded as one of ‘branching out’ of IS into varied foci (e.g. IS diffusion, investment decisions, success, satisfaction and acceptance) which, while still contributing to differentiate IS from other bodies of knowledge, offers different orientations in similar topics and their treatments, and in this way sets out the conditions for internal competition. A clearer set of varieties of IS research can also be distinguished which reflects potential competition between distinctions in terms of approaches within the same epistemology (interpretive and qualitative).

## Last period 2005–2008

The last period in EJIS and MISQ (2005–2008) can be characterised as one of absorption, in which efforts have been converging and consolidated towards the issue of understanding and managing (or even predicting) ‘IS acceptance’ in organisations, embracing previous theory at the outset of knowledge creation. This can be explained in several forms in both journals. First, there is a concentration of knowledge distinctions and connections around this topic and less variety of other topics and/or connections (see Figures 6 and 7 below). Second, by looking at core articles in the concentrated topic areas, there has been an update of IS acceptance from a previous topic of IS success/satisfaction. Acceptance seems to have ‘won the battle’ against other organisational models (based on strategic thinking) to ensure IS/IT effectiveness.

![](/api/attachments/NC9GK3J7/fulltext/images/6bde7def73ef7515eba62fb3fd2285d2141d8da47bea3249cde2bd36dba4557b.jpg)

Moreover, IS success as a topic is now linked with related issues like diffusion of innovations (Rogers, 1962), and updated in a more holistic way, thus synthesising previous research into a revised framework and thereby absorbing what the study of IS success achieved previously (DeLone & McLean, 2003). Delone and McLean’s update preserves the theoretical core (DeLone & McLean, 1992) and challenges re-specification (Seddon, 1997) of their previous model. The updated model, however, also reflects further attempts to absorb theory from other disciplines, such as service quality measurement constructs from Marketing (Parasuraman et al, 1985a) also seen in other major IS research (Pitt et al, 1995). Similar work (i.e. user satisfaction in IS) (Ives et al, 1983) loses dominance given its lack of association with the main issue of acceptance. Acceptance is now ‘better’ researched in a more behavioural way (Taylor & Todd, 1995) so that a set of constructs or aspects (i.e. usefulness, ease of use) can capture people’s perceptions about IS and hence induce IS usage and hence acceptance (Benbasat & Barki, 2007).

In MISQ, IS research focused on acceptance has taken a strong orientation towards a particular type of systems adoption (ERP) (Stefanou, 2001), which in our view continues contributing to maintain the jurisdiction of IS knowledge, generating a strong association that concentrates varieties of IS research epistemology regarding the focus of analysis (organisations rather than organisational factors), with focus on different human factors to be investigated in both individuals and organisations as well as in the methods employed for such investigation (case studies, surveys).

Despite the dominance of the theme of IS acceptance, in MISQ other competing but not dominant topics include conceptual work on knowledge management (Nonaka, 1994; Alavi & Leidner, 2001), which temporarily appeared as a dominant structure in the EJIS’s previous period. Also, other epistemologies (qualitative research, case studies) maintain their separate identity and hence their lack of ‘connection’ as Figure 6 shows.

In addition, as seen in Figure 7, EJIS follows a similar orientation of concentration in the topic of IS acceptance. However, there are more varieties than in MISQ which up to this point in time are able to keep their separate identity (not strongly linked with this topic): One which values enactment and technology embedding in social practices (Orlikowski, 2000), and another that focuses on Enterprise Systems implementation (Davenport, 1998). Although this can still signal some degree of differentiation and competition, the latter variety could well be subsumed under the topic of acceptance as happened in MISQ.

Similar to MISQ, in EJIS the use of case studies and qualitative research (Yin, 1984; Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994) remains unlinked to acceptance, giving us support for the exploration of this and other (and possibly competing) foci of research and practice (more on this later). In MISQ though, IS research seems to be even less pluralistic, with clearer dominance of empirical, quantitative and positivist traditions.

As we see it, this period shows that despite a concentration on a particular topic, when it comes to arguments about stability or diversity and fluidity in IS there are no winners or losers. Positivist and behaviourally oriented IS acceptance-related knowledge distinctions could be seen – temporarily – as core and ‘winning’ knowledge elements. From this stage of absorption in IS, what we see is a number of possibilities to be further developed in research and practice. In the next section we discuss our findings in more detail and suggest a number of ideas about how IS people can use them to advance research and practice.

## Discussion

As a result of its focus on studying the use of applications and technologies in organisations and elsewhere, IS has gone through some exciting periods which signal that it is still a discipline in the making. In relation to the two perspectives about IS presented earlier in the paper, the following discussion aims to help both those individuals advocating the need for IS to become a discipline as well as those who maintain it is in a constant state of flux. To both of these audiences, we say that there is no doubt IS has gained and maintained a presence, but its future would depend on how cautious we are with what we have gained and what we do next.

Regarding what we can present as ‘core’ knowledge elements in IS, our findings suggest that IS has produced distinctions which show a degree of dominance for those with a positivist orientation as reported in previous studies (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004), with some degree of diversity about other epistemologies and methods to investigate IS phenomena. Currently, positivist distinctions are strongly associated with addressing the topic of IS acceptance in organisations. The topic in turn is a consolidation of early work to understand user satisfaction (Ives et al, 1983) and/or IS success (DeLone & McLean, 1992).

The consolidation of an area of research and practice in IS (around the subject of acceptance) also means that dominant methods, approaches and techniques to investigate this subject are becoming more portable so that newcomers can use them ‘safely’ (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). In this case, and again following Abbott (2001), IS is becoming more recognised by the use of a number of ‘transferable’ elements to achieve IS acceptance, which could, at any time, be at the service of other academic disciplines. As we see it, there are a number of implications for research and practice:

a. IS researchers and practitioners can and should continue to consolidate knowledge about IS acceptance. Our review of IS has shown an intensified trend towards unified positivist and behaviourally oriented research in general, which is more pronounced in MISQ than in EJIS. As mentioned before, evidence of this trend is the strong emphasis on popular models of user behaviour linked to IS acceptance and diffusion. As Abbott (2001) suggests, maps of knowledge distinctions and connections in a discipline show what is both explored as well as what is unexplored, of what is dominant and what is to re-emerge. There is scope to investigate IS acceptance from nonpositivist and non-behavioural perspectives, to build new inferences as well as treatments about it and to offer them to different audiences. The development of more case studies on this area, or including design-science approaches could also help.

b. While aiming to consolidate and extend its jurisdiction on the above topic, IS could also be subjected to attack by other disciplines or internal competitors in other themes that are left unattended. Other disciplines can claim jurisdiction on aspects of acceptance (i.e. antecedents of beliefs in IS usefulness as well as impacts on IS design) (Benbasat & Barki, 2007) or on other topics, for instance adoption of online systems, largescale IS implementations, organisational re-design, IS development, getting value from IT investments, social networks and the Internet, trust, etc. As we see it, there is a stock of knowledge in IS which people in IS could build upon to revisit these and other topics, and thus to protect if not extend IS jurisdiction by enabling the development of new diagnoses, treatments and inferences. Knowledge includes, for instance, interpretivist IS studies (Orlikowski, 1992). Research studies are still present (regardless of whether the data are qualitative or quantitative) but relatively less manifested. In developing these and other possibilities, it is important to acknowledge the merits of different philosophical assumptions which could inform further studies of the relationship between information technology and people (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

c. By building on existing distinctions about IS acceptance, researchers and practitioners can venture to claim jurisdiction on topics like diffusion of technology in different settings or different types of organisations, as this is an emergent area from which concepts and models have been imported. These are problems which other disciplines might still have control of but could be addressed with existing methods/concepts already used in the IS field (predominantly positivist as well as empirical), and our findings show that they are not all being produced within IS.

The above possibilities can also be seen in the context of IS as a diverse and fluid discipline which is in a continuous expansion. However, attributes like diversity could be seen in a new way. First, our findings show that a great degree of ‘consolidated diversity’ of research epistemologies is reflected neither in EJIS nor in MISQ, at least not in the ways in which people investigate IS adoption, use and acceptance. Second, the lack of strong connection of distinctions about qualitative and critical research to the emerging theme of IS acceptance leads us to suggest that both other varieties of non-positivist IS research (interpretive and critical) research still persists although at the margins. If IS is in a stage of absorption, diversity could be stem from dominant areas of research and practice (i.e. acceptance) and branch out from there. Research that focuses on different approaches or paradigms (interpretive, critical), with the use of alternative methods (i.e. design science), and to tackle related and emergent topics (i.e. diffusion) can well support diversity and fluidity in IS.

Third, in relation to how diversity can be nurtured, a degree of competition between distinctions within and outside IS to investigate this and other topics should be promoted in IS research and practice, with acknowledgement that groups who could be ‘winning’ in a period of absorption (i.e. positivist and behaviourally oriented IS research) having to include other distinctions later on. Both the promotion of consolidation of knowledge and the nurturing of diversity within this stage can contribute to a new period of differentiation in IS. We believe that rather than a fragmentation, the end result may be a wider degree of recognition of IS as a valuable body of knowledge that can benefit people in academic and practical settings. In the concluding section of the paper we draw a number of implications for different IS audiences.

## Concluding remarks

In this paper a study of IS as a potential discipline has been developed which aims to understand the dynamics of IS. Using the work of Abbott on disciplines and how they unfold through stages of differentiation, competition and absorption, we have employed a citation and co-citation analysis of two key journals (EJIS and MISQ) to distinguish certain features of the knowledge that has been produced in IS. From this analysis, it can be said that overall the multi-disciplinary and hybrid nature of a developing IS discipline remains visible over time as new topics from other disciplines emerge, re-emerge or submerge during our three-phased time window. Some of these topics (i.e. IT and strategy) have created space for others to emerge. Others (i.e. knowledge management) have become sub-disciplines or disciplines on their own. Others (i.e. satisfaction) have re-emerged (i.e. acceptance), showing us that IS continues exploring how to ameliorate the introduction of technology into the organisational workplace (Bryant, 2008, p. 697), thus making IS focus on analysis information technology in use (Paul, 2008).

In more detail, our insights indicate that IS has gone through stages of differentiation, competition and is currently in a stage of absorption, in what appears to be a consolidation of positivist and behaviourally oriented research oriented to study and manage IS acceptance and use. For practitioners and researchers who are interested in seeing IS as a discipline with core elements and properties, they can claim that it now exhibits a selection of concepts in particular relating to IS research on IS acceptance with common statistical measures of rigour and clear guidelines for validation (e.g. Boudreau et al, 2001). There is a set of distinctions about IS acceptance which could help in the adoption and use of IS in organisations. Practitioners can benefit from the knowledge about how researchers have diagnosed and treated acceptance, but also from further inferences that arise from looking at the organisational problems that acceptance helps to address (diffusion and learning about technology being one of them). For researchers and academics, there is scope to explore the different varieties that this type of research generates as IS continues producing new studies. While established behavioural IS research seems to successfully provide generic cause – effect relations, interpretivism to support generic means – ends relations exist but are less well developed as structural knowledge groups.

The current debate about IS diversity and in particular on IS design science as reported in EJIS calls for a change in incentive systems and stipulates abstract research processes and principles to protect a diversity of methods (Oesterle et al, 2011). For IS practitioners this conclusion may seem worrisome. However from our perspective, this is one possibility among many in which variety in IS can be nurtured if topics like IS acceptance are to be revisited or new topics are to be explored. From our findings, we suggest the following strategies to nurture diversity in IS. First, in order to maintain as well as extend IS jurisdiction on organisational issues related to the adoption of systems and technologies in organisations, connections between research and practice should be further developed. Dialogue should be nurtured and maintained between practitioners and researchers in areas that have been explored (e.g. acceptance) as well as in areas that remain unexplored, for instance in the use of alternative design approaches or in the use of alternative IS research paradigms (i.e. qualitative positivism, quantitative and critical). These suggestions concur with the idea of Bryant (2008) about making IS a ‘permeable’ and diverse discipline, more open to debate about its relevance in practice and thus relevant to what happens in society.

Second, academics and educators should continue providing IS audiences with diagnoses, treatments and inferences about dominant and new topics. In this regard, educational textbooks and resources are still very influential in both mainstream and alternative perspectives about IS. In fuelling educational efforts, alternatives like interpretive and critical education in IS could also explore how IS has been linked to organisations, and how acceptance is a topic that could be challenged constructively, for instance by reviewing the assumptions, scope and impacts of IS acceptance models (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). There are many possibilities which are currently ‘dormant’, and there is scope for thinking that they can resurface either under another term or because IS professionals (both in academia and practice) find ways to make them more visible and with it to re-enact the status of IS within a cycle of natural regression (Abbott, 1988; Corbett & Van Wassenhove, 1993). Our study confirms that as a body of knowledge, IS is in need of continuous consolidation as well as differentiation. In our work, we could use some basic and accepted content (predictive models, qualitative research methods for IS, interpretive IS research, IS case studies, enterprise systems) and from there we could show our students a variety of perspectives and ideas which are to re-emerge if IS is to maintain its status as a discipline in the making. The door is open to continue developing IS and to continue studying problems of their adoption in organisations and elsewhere. We hope our paper makes a valuable contribution to show our audience where IS has been and where it could go next.
