# Managing bandwidth allocations between competing recreational and non-recreational traffic on campus networks

David C. Novak <sup>⁎</sup>

School of Business Administration, University of Vermont, 55 Colchester Avenue, 310 Kalkin Hall, Burlington, VT 05405, United States

Received 1 December 2006; received in revised form 8 January 2008; accepted 20 January 2008 Available online 4 February 2008

## Abstract

Network performance is a serious concern faced by many campus network managers across the country. As demand for entertainment-based Peer-to-Peer (P2P) applications that involve the transfer of large audio and video files continues to grow, managers are faced with the increasingly difficult task of determining how much bandwidth should be allocated to these applications. Unrestricted P2P traffic has the potential to monopolize bandwidth and severely degrade network performance. University IT managers are placed in a particularly difficult position, because they must juggle demands for non-recreational traffic without severely restricting recreational use of the network. This paper discusses a solution for optimizing bandwidth allocations on a campus gateway Internet link.

## 1. Introduction

The introduction of new applications, changes in user behavior, and increasingly faster access technologies all have contributed to an increased demand for bandwidth — the maximum amount of data transferred over a communications link during a fixed time interval [27]. Network management involves the allocation of finite resources, such as bandwidth, to many different services and applications [16]. Managing bandwidth is critical in terms of traffic engineering, and can have a dramatic impact on both local and end-to-end network performance. In particular, the provisioning of bandwidth associated with recreational peer-to-peer (P2P) file sharing applications has become a major concern for Internet Access Providers (IAP)s in recent years, as applications like Napster, KaZaA, Gnutella, and WinMX impose enormous bandwidth demands on networks and can cause serious performance problems [10,31,32,35].

This paper demonstrates a decision support methodology to set optimal bandwidth allocations for competing recreational P2P traffic and non-recreational traffic (here referred to as non-P2P, or NP2P) with respect to minimizing the total cost of network operations. Total costs include the explicit costs to the IAP associated with network management, as well as the implicit costs resulting from unsatisfied users under various bandwidth allocation scenarios. Management decisions include bandwidth allocations for both P2P and NP2P traffic during specific time periods, as well as the number of allocation changes made during the day. A goal program (GP) is used to estimate both P2P capacity demand and P2P user demand at different time periods and bandwidth allocations. Capacity demand refers to the amount of bandwidth (Mbps) demanded by P2P users, and user demand refers to the number of users actively involved in P2P downloads. A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is used to solve the cost minimization problem.

A real-world example for optimizing bandwidth allocations between competing P2P and NP2P interests is provided using empirical data from a large university. While underlying network topologies, service quality architectures, and data may differ from provider to provider, the example illustrates how the methodology can be applied in practice. Contributions include: the introduction of a relative cost approach for managing bandwidth allocations between competing traffic types, the demonstration of a technique for estimating P2P demand for different time periods and bandwidth allocations that cannot be observed in practice under existing policy allocations, and the demonstration of a practical solution methodology for a real-world network management problem using empirical data from an actual university IAP.

## 1.1. P2P traffic and traffic shaping

Internet applications typically follow a client/server model, where a dedicated host, the server, functions as the service provider for numerous clients who use the offered services [11,34]. In the P2P model, there is no semantic difference between client and server. P2P allows users to exchange files directly, without using a centralized server. While P2P applications are not “bad”, many popular applications are recreational in nature. Recreational P2P exchanges tend to involve the transfer of large files that may be hundreds of megabytes (MB) or even many gigabytes (GB) in size [35]. P2P file requests often search a large number of hosts, and transfers may occur from multiple hosts in separate locations simultaneously, generating large amounts of overhead traffic [34]. Many recreational P2P applications also bypass or alter TCP's congestion control mechanism, creating bandwidth “hungry” applications that monopolize the available bandwidth and cannot be effectively managed via TCP congestion and flow control.

Although network management problems attributed to recreational P2P file sharing are not restricted to university networks, the severity of the P2P problem and the wide spread use of traffic shaping solutions is well documented on campuses across the nation [14,23,31,32]. In response to external pressure from the recording and film industries, and due to the network management problems resulting from unrestricted P2P traffic, many universities are actively exploring management solutions to mitigate the negative effects associated with recreational P2P traffic [14,23]. A simple management solution would be to completely eliminate P2P traffic, however, the use of university and overlay networks is not restricted to non-recreational activities. While a few universities have imposed an outright ban on certain P2P applications, many choose to impose bandwidth restrictions on P2P traffic using some type of traffic shaping solution. This approach is less likely to anger students and allows administrators to minimize the copyright debate [14]. In many cases, universities have no desire to impose restrictive policies on recreational P2P activities, as P2P file sharing applications are very popular among students and such policies may be viewed as oppressive and result in unsatisfied users [14,26]. It is important to note that not all recreational P2P file sharing involves the exchange of copyrighted material, and all exchanges of copyrighted material are not necessarily illegal [14]. The use of networking protocols associated with P2P file sharing is not inherently illegal and IT managers often view demands to restrict P2P file sharing as excessive and intrusive. A more detailed discussion of copyright and legal issues associated with P2P file exchanges can be found in Refs. [1,22].

The solution methodology described in this paper is based on a widely used management technique/tool called traffic shaping. Traffic shaping refers to a range of technologies used to promote quality-of-service (QoS) by classifying and prioritizing traffic to control bandwidth utilization [7]. Traffic shaping is not the only QoS management approach available, and IAPs may employ multiple techniques, practices, and/or tools simultaneously. As QoS is a broad topic that can be defined differently and can encompass various techniques, protocols, and services, QoS is explicitly defined here as the ability to guarantee and limit bandwidth for certain users and services [5]. Traffic shaping products, such as Packeteer's PacketShaper™ facilitate network management and improve performance by allowing managers to identify and allocate bandwidth to specific traffic types like Web traffic or recreational P2P file sharing applications. University providers must therefore manage the trade-off of bandwidth between competing recreational P2P traffic, and non-recreational traffic keeping in mind that as the bandwidth allocated to a particular service or traffic type decreases, user satisfaction also tends to decrease [3,14].

## 2. Literature

This research builds directly on a theoretical methodology for optimizing bandwidth allocations between competing traffic presented in Ref. [21], where a generalized utility maximizing framework is presented. Additional published research on bandwidth management and network resource allocation is also applied. Prasad et al. [27] provide an overview of bandwidth estimation metrics, measurement techniques, and tools that are commonly used in end-to-end network performance assessment. Firoiu et al. [8] survey recent advances in models and theories for Internet service quality and discuss issues such as differentiated services and performance guarantees. Cheng and Zhuang [4] investigate statistical multiplexing performance and admission control on a multi-service network with Markovian traffic sources. Kalyanasundaram et al. [16] develop an optimal resource allocation scheme for multi-service networks that is formulated as a continuous-time MDP. Nair and Bapna [20] examine strategies for optimizing the network capacity of an IAP faced with dynamic customer behavior. The authors formulate the problem as a continuous time MDP and offer a summary of the similarities and differences between communication-based resource allocation and more traditional applications. Park et al. [24] suggest a QoS provisioning model where users choose the service class and bandwidth allocated. User preferences are modeled using heterogeneous utility functions. Savage and Waldman [33] present econometric results showing that speed, reliability, and the ability to share music and video files are highly valued Internet attributes. Gupta et al. [11] model generalized Internet services using priority queues and stochastic arrivals as an economic resource allocation problem. The paper discusses relationships between throughput, wait times, and the total welfare of users.

Although traffic shaping solutions are commonly applied in practice [14,23], there is very little research that considers the trade-off between network resources allocated to P2P and NP2P uses, or how bandwidth allocation decisions are made. Much of the existing literature concentrates on technical solutions rather than on managerial solutions for controlling P2P traffic, and focuses on the design and implementation of P2P search algorithms, protocols, and architectures or topologies for P2P-based networks. Goel et al. [9] present a service-based P2P overlay network architecture for solving complex business processes. Junginger and Lee [15] propose a middleware solution for improving the operational performance of a wide range of P2P applications. Mischke and Stiller [19] introduce a top-down, two dimensional middleware framework for improving search and routing functions of P2P file sharing applications. Menasće [18] suggests improving P2P network resource usage through the use of scalable resource location protocols that ease problems created by current P2P broadcast-based protocols that flood the network with control traffic. Ripeanu et al. [29] discuss problems caused by P2Pbased systems by demonstrating mismatches between the protocols used by Gnutella and the physical Internet infrastructure.

Other sources explore the statistical characteristics of P2P traffic and discuss various performance problems caused by this traffic. Bhattacharjee et al. [2] collect and analyze P2P music file sharing data over an 8 week time period and provide evidence of piracy, as well as a relationship between sharing activity and direction of movement of albums on the Billboard charts. Sen and Wang [35] analyze P2P traffic flows for a large IAP and investigate three popular recreational P2P applications. P2P traffic characteristics are compared to Web-based traffic. Kim et al. [17] discuss how modern Internet traffic is difficult to categorize and propose an algorithm for identifying P2P traffic flows on a network. Schollmeier and Dumanois [34] investigate the properties of P2P traffic and compare those properties to traditional Web traffic. Gummandi et al. [10] analyze P2P trace data from a large university and develop a model of multimedia workloads. The results of the analysis reveal dramatic differences between Web traffic and P2P file sharing traffic. Saroiu et al. [31] perform a measurement study of two popular P2P file sharing systems at a large university to accurately characterize the population users. Saroiu et al. [32] investigate Internet content delivery systems to isolate and characterize traffic belonging to four different delivery classes.

## 3. Data

The data used in the research are empirical TCP stream data obtained from a large university. Data collection occurs on the edge router to campus, and captures only those TCP streams destined for offcampus sources, or incoming from off-campus sources. The data are collected and aggregated into hourly time intervals for analysis. The raw TCP session log data for each hour comprise many gigabytes (GB) of data. Table 1 provides an example of TCP stream data. The data are converted from their original format for illustration. Based on the time period considered, and consultations with engineers for the test IAP, the data are not biased by any unusual events. It is unclear to what degree the data are representative of all universities, as different behaviors may be exhibited at different universities and may change over time. Given the evolving nature of Internet traffic and P2P applications [17,35], providers should consider conducting a detailed traffic characterization at least once a year, if not more frequently.

Bandwidth allocations for P2P and NP2P traffic are determined given a finite link capacity. At the time the research was conducted, a full-duplex 105 Mbps Internet link was used by the test provider. Bandwidth is optimized in an asymmetric fashion and only downloads are considered. Many universities, including the test IAP, impose very stringent restrictions on uploading files because at any point in time a very large number of off-campus P2P users could be attempting to upload files from local hosts, while the number of local P2P users attempting to download files from remote hosts is much smaller [10,14,32]. Adding capacity on an ad hoc basis is not a realistic solution for most providers, as the cost of leasing an Internet link connecting a campus network to an overlay network is directly related to the bandwidth of the link, and increased demand for capacity is not directly linked to an increase in operating budgets. While P2P demand may be growing and consuming much of the available bandwidth, simply adding capacity to accommodate the new demand is generally not a cost effective solution; particularly since many recreational P2P applications crave unlimited bandwidth. Increasing capacity also does not preclude the proactive management of network resources.

Traffic is categorized as P2P, or NP2P. Only certain types of recreational file sharing traffic such as video and music downloads are characterized as P2P traffic. Traffic other than recreational P2P file sharing traffic is characterized as NP2P traffic. While this classification is simplified, comparing general categories of well-known recreational P2P file sharing traffic to general categories of NP2P traffic, such as Web traffic, is an approach that is used in Refs. [10,17,31,32,35], and is sufficient to effectively demonstrate the solution methodology. Future research could involve a more granular distinction between specific P2P and NP2P applications. The data required to provide a detailed stratification by application were not available.

The allocation policy currently used by the test provider heavily restricts P2P traffic and results in excess capacity during many hourly time periods. Fig. 2 provides the number of unique IP addresses engaged in download activities. This approach is consistent with Refs. [10,17,35]. The data in both figures are hourly averages from a seven day sample period.

Recreational P2P traffic is identified using well known TCP/IP port numbers. TCP stream data with either remote or local port 1214 (KaZaA download), 6699 (WinMX download), 6346 (Gnutella download), and 4662 (eDonkey, overnet, eMule download) are used to classify P2P traffic. These ports are used by popular P2P file sharing applications and generate the majority of P2P traffic on the test campus. The same P2P ports are also used in Refs. [31,35]. Web traffic (remote port

80) is used as a proxy for NP2P traffic. Based on the observed data from the test provider, Web traffic accounts for over 50% of all NP2P traffic. Because it is the single largest component of NP2P traffic that can be clearly identified, it is used for general NP2P traffic characterization. Comparing the characteristics of well known recreational P2P file sharing traffic to Web traffic is also done in Refs, [10,17,34].

The bandwidth allocation policy utilized by the test provider is simplified as: 40% NP2P between 2 AM– 6 AM, 90% NP2P between 6 AM–6 PM, 75% NP2P between 6 PM–10 PM, and 60% NP2P between 10 PM–2 AM. P2P and NP2P traffic is treated separately via a virtual link for each traffic category. On a single physical link, bandwidth can be virtually allocated to each traffic type, as if two separate physical links are being used. Given an incoming link capacity of 105 Mbps, between 2 AM and 6 AM 40% of the capacity is reserved for NP2P usage (42 Mbps), and 60% for P2P usage (63 Mbps). Traffic is not allowed “to overflow” into the other virtual pipe at any time.

## 4. Methodology

For convenience, Table 2 summarizes the methology and shows the inputs and outputs associated with each step. Observed demand refers to the empirical NP2P and P2P traffic data collected from the test IAP under the existing bandwidth allocation policy. Since the observed data are biased by the bandwidth policy currently used, P2P demand estimates are required for both the bandwidth and number of users for all alternative policy allocations (those not observed in the empirical data). Unconstrained P2P demand is estimated for situations where no bandwidth allocation policies or restrictions are assumed to be in place. This represents the maximum estimated P2P demand for a given time period. An alternative approach could involve turning off the existing policy, publicizing this, and then collecting data on observed usage. This alternative was not viable since changing the policy to observe what happens under different allocation scenarios would have created serious network performance problems during business hours. Experimenting with various policy allocations, collecting the data for each policy change, and monitoring network performance as a result of each allocation change required too many different scenarios and was unrealistic from the prespective of the test provider.

Demand is considered in terms of both the capacity – the portion of the 105 Mbps incoming pipe P2P users require; and the number of users – the filtered IP addresses involved in P2P downloads (definitions on page 3). P2P demand is treated as elastic and decreasing with respect to both capacity and users. P2P demand decreases as the bandwidth allocated to the P2P virtual pipe decreases. As P2P bandwidth decreases, the wait time per user increases, leading to a reduction in the number of users and/or a reduction in the bytes downloaded per user over a given time period. This assumption is consisent with Refs. [6,11,16,25]. The functional form of the demand equation and the supporting assumptions are discused in detail in the following sections.

NP2P demand is treated as inelastic because the general category of NP2P traffic is considered mission critical, or non-recreational. The unconstrained, maximum NP2P demand is captured in the empirical data associated with the current bandwidth allocation policy, as the policy currently employed by the test provider is set to make sure NP2P demand is met regardless of P2P demand. The result is a cautious policy that allocates more bandwdith to NP2P traffic than appears necessary (see Fig. 1). The GP is used to estimate P2P demand for all possible time periods and policy allocations. Once P2P demand is estimated for both capacity and users, the unsatisified users for each time period and possible bandwidth allocation are computed for both NP2P and P2P traffic. Given a baseline download transfer rate below which the time required for a download to be completed is not acceptable, the number of users that are not served is calculated. An MDP model is then employed to determine the cost minimizing bandwidth allocation between P2P and NP2P traffic for each hour of the day. This approach is also used in Ref. [21].

## 4.1. Goal program for estimating P2P demand

The discussion is presented in terms of the capacity demanded (Mbps), although the methodology is applied to both types of P2P demand. There are 19 possible bandwidth allocations considered (5% NP2P to 95% NP2P allocation in 5% increments), and 24 time periods in a day. P2P demand is estimated for all possible policy allocations and time periods (19×24=456 data points). The observed P2P data have 24 data points, one for each hour (e.g., 60% NP2P allocation at hour 1, 40% NP2P allocations at hour 2, etc.) based on the existing bandwidth allocation policy used by the test provider. The unobserved P2P demand is estimated by interpolation from the 24 data points observed from the emprical data. Table 3 provides a visual illustration of the problem. ## 4.2. Simulated scenarios in the goal program

The robustness of different functional forms is tested via simulation to determine the functional form that should be used in the GP. The objective is to minimize the total absolute mean deviation across all simulated demand scenarios. The simulation involves generating 50 random demand scenarios, where three parameters, NP2P demand, P2P demand, and the NP2P bandwidth allocation are randomly generated by the simulation for each hourly time period. Each simulation scenario represents a full day consisting of 24 h. The three simulation parameters are estimated independently of one another for each hour of the day. In total, there are 1200 (50 × 24) independent demand/ allocation simulations. As the objective of the simulation is to test the robustness of widely varying demand scenarios, the simulated policy scenarios are intended to be totally random. They do not represent suggested policies, or even realistic allocation policies. A visual representation of a single scenario is shown in Table 4. For example, at t = 1, the simulated NP2P allocation is 55%, NP2P demand is 38 Mbps, P2P demand is 7 Mbps and the estimated unconstrained P2P demand is calculated as 56 Mbps.

Using the empirical data from the test IAP, simulation parameters are generated for each hour from a truncated normal distribution for each traffic type. For NP2P demand the simulation parameters are: mean 45.2 Mbps, standard deviation 18.7 Mbps, maximum 71 Mbps, and minimum 10 Mbps. The simulation parameters for P2P demand are: mean 13.8 Mbps, standard deviation 12 Mbps, maximum 33 Mbps, and minimum 1.25 Mbps. The NP2P allocations for each hour are generated from a discrete density where each possible NP2P bandwidth allocation (10%, 15%, 20%, etc.), has an equal probability of occurrence. The ratio of the simulated hourly NP2P demand to the simulated maximum NP2P demand for the entire day is used to estimate the maximum, unconstrained P2P demand for each hour a the NP2P allocation of 5%.

A summary of the simulation results from select demand functions is provided in Table 5. Many different functional forms were tested, but only a few results are reported based on spatial considerations. $\tilde { D } _ { t , a } ^ { P }$ is the estimate of the actual demand $D _ { t , a } ^ { P }$ . The functional form with the minimum absolute mean deviation is considered the “best” choice.

The GP is also used to estimate P2P user demand. Obviously, the parameter inputs are different from capacity demand. In solving the GP for user demand, the maximum unconstrained demand, $D _ { t , ( 5 \% ) } ^ { P } { = } \hat { D } _ { t } ^ { P } ,$ is set to half of the number of observed NP2P IP addresses. The relationship between unconstrained NP2P demand and unconstrained P2P demand is based on a comparison of P2P and NP2P request rates in Ref. [31], where it was observed that even when excess capacity is available, the number of P2P users is about half of the number of NP2P IP users. Based on the empirical data from the test IAP, this assumption is reasonable.

## 4.3. Estimating unsatisfied users

Next, the number of unsatisfied users is estimated using an M/D/1 queueing model. The use of the M/D/1 model is supported by the initial traffic characterization, and the M/D/1 model is commonly used for packet processing applications [30]. Arrivals follow a Poisson process and service time is deterministic. Given the bandwidth available at the time a download begins, the transfer of packets (for that particular download) is assumed to occur at a constant rate. Other queueing assumptions, such as M/G/1, may also be employed; however, as the coefficient of variance becomes large, expected wait times for the M/G/1 queue can become very large — even under low utilization. The M/D/1 assumption is nice because it yields relatively conservative wait time estimates.

Unsatisfied demand is defined with respect to a particular minimum threshold flow rate in kilobits per second (Kbps) that users will tolerate for downloads. Alternatively, the threshold can be viewed as a “wait time” that is dependent upon the size of the file being downloaded and the traffic type. While Internet users are sensitive to latency [10,11,33], the literature does not directly addresses minimum threshold values for Internet traffic in terms of either throughput (the observed transfer rate) or the wait time. The threshold values used in this paper are illustrative, and are based on the assumption that the minimum P2P user throughput threshold is lower than the minimum NP2P user threshold. This assumption is supported by Ref. [10], who show that P2P users are more patient than NP2P users with respect to how long they will wait for a download. It logically follows that given a constant file size and wait time, the minimum throughput requirement for NP2P users is higher than that of P2P users. The minimum P2P user threshold rate is set to 50 Kbps. This value is based on the maximum throughput associated with dialup Internet access — P2P users require a minimum threshold equivilent to the maximum throughput associated with a 56 Kbps modem. The threshold assumption is supported by observations regarding P2P use and network access presented in Refs. [31,32]. From the empirical data, the average ratio of bytes per P2P IP compared to bytes per NP2P IP during the times when the bandwidth allocation is least restrictive to P2P users is about 6:1. The minimum throughput for NP2P users is therefore set to six times that of P2P users, or 300 Kbps. The threshold values used here are 50 Kbps for P2P users and 300 Kbps for NP2P users.

## 4.4. The MDP model

An MDP is used to model the optimal bandwidth allocation, where an optimal bandwidth allocation is selected each hour to minimize the total costs. Total costs include the implicit costs incurred by not satisfying both P2P and NP2P demand as well as costs incurred from changing the allocation of bandwidth such as writing, testing, and porting of code, monitoring and collecting network performance data associated with the new bandwidth allocations. These costs are difficult to estimate in practice because they are comprised of a number of variable and implicit costs that cannot be easily identifed, separated, and quantified. If the cost of changing the policy is relatively low, then the bandwidth allocations will be changed frequently to provide a more dynamic management approach that closely mirrors hourly changes in demand. If the cost of changing the policy is relatively high, the bandwidth allocations will be not be changed as often and the management approach will be more static. The current policy employed by the test IAP is changed only four times a day, although it is known that the policy is not optimal and that there are times when there is a noticable amount of unused capacity (see Fig. 1). The test provider does not implement a more dynamic policy because of concerns over the potential costs associated with changing the policy in a more dynamic fashion.

A relative cost approach is employed, where P2P costs are compared relative to NP2P costs. The costs associated with unsatisfied demand for each user type are expressed in relative terms, and the costs of changing the policy allocation are relative to the cost of unsatisfied demand. This way, individual IAPs can place their own relative values on servicing different users at different time periods. Microeconomic theory states that all resources have some market value, or opportunity cost, regardless of whether the organization pays cash for the resource or not. IAPs may associate different costs with with meeting the demand of different user types depending on factors such as excess capacity, service guarantees, the time of day, and the characteristics of the user base. By using sensitivity runs at different relative cost values, managers can become more comfortable with the implications of the costs used in the model.

A finite number of incremental change options are available based on 5% bandwidth allocations. Changing the policy by one 5% increment in either direction, imposes a relative change cost associated with data collection, analysis, and network performance monitoring. Changing the allocation by two increments in either direction imposes a marginal cost greater than that of the one increment change. With a small change in either direction, usage patterns will remain fairly stable because the bandwidth allocation for each traffic type has not changed that much. As the magnitude of the change increases, usage patterns can change much more dramatically, and more time and effort must be devoted to monitoring network conditions and the potential of serious performance problems increases. In the absense of perfect information as a 1st-order approximation, a linear, increasing cost function is used to represent the marginal values of inputs. The cost associated with changing the policy allocation increases linearly as the number of incremental allocation changes increases from hour to hour. The methodology is not dependent on the form of the change cost function, and other functional forms could be used.

Suppose $c _ { t , a } ^ { N }$ and $c _ { t , a } ^ { P }$ are the costs per unsatisfied NP2P and P2P user respectively at time $t ,$ and allocation a. C is the relative cost coefficient of changing the bandwidth allocation policy by one increment and $\Delta _ { t }$ represents the number of incremental changes. For example, changing the policy from 50% to either 45% or 55% is an incremental change of 1 unit. Changing the policy from 50% to either 40% or 60% is an incremental change of 2 units. The total cost of unserved users, $\hat { c } _ { t , a } ,$ at time period t and NP2P bandwidth allocation $a$ is shown in (7).

$$
\hat {c} _ {t, a} = C \Delta_ {t} + c _ {t, a} ^ {N} m _ {t, a} ^ {N} + c _ {t, a} ^ {P} m _ {t, a} ^ {P}\tag{7}
$$

The cost minimizing optimal bandwidth allocation is modeled as an MDP. $\operatorname { I f } { f _ { t , a } }$ is the optimal cost in time t of having a NP2P bandwidth allocation of $^ { a , }$ , and $\overline { { a } }$ represents the actual policy allocation currently used by the test provider at time $t ,$ then the optimal cost, $f _ { t , a }$ is solved using (8), with the terminal condition, for all allocations T, a (9).

$$
f _ {t, a} = \min _ {\overline {{a}}} \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \text { Change   to } \overline {{a}} \text { at   time } t: & c _ {t, \overline {{a}}} + \hat {c} _ {t, \overline {{a}}} + f _ {t + 1, \overline {{a}}} \\ \text { No   change }: & \hat {c} _ {t, a} + f _ {t + 1, a} \end{array} \right.\tag{8}
$$

$$
f _ {T, a} = c _ {t, a} + \hat {c} _ {T, a}.\tag{9}
$$

There is no actual terminal hour in the bandwidth allocation problem, the next day starts immediately after hour 24. Therefore, (9) is not a sufficient condition by itself. The following algorithm using the terminal condition in Eq. (10) resolves this problem.

Step 1: Solve the DP using (8) and (9).

Step 2: Observe the solution for t = 1, a<sup>⁎</sup>.

Step 3: Re-solve DP using (8) and the terminal condition in (10)

$$
f _ {T, a} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \hat {c} _ {T, a} & a _ {T} = a _ {1} ^ {*} \\ c _ {t, a} + \hat {c} _ {T, a} & \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right..\tag{10}
$$

## 5. Sensitivity results and management implications

Sensitivity analyses are performed to provide insight into how bandwidth allocation decisions may be impacted by changes in relative costs. In practice, IAPs may elect to assign different relative cost values to different traffic types. For example, NP2P traffic could be weighted much more heavily than P2P traffic. This approach would yield solutions more favorable to NP2P users. Likewise, the relative change cost coefficient can be adjusted to favor more static or dynamic policies. Change costs may be largely dependent on the degree to which the IAP has automated its network monitoring and management practices, and how much slack the IAP has with respect to reallocating staff time to bandwidth allocation issues. The various assumptions regarding costs, demand, bandwidth allocations, etc. are supported via empirical evidence and existing literature. Sensitivity results are discussed in the context of the test IAP. As the purpose of this research is to demonstrate a practical problem solving methodology, readers should note that the various independent assumptions described in this paper may not hold for all providers. As individual assumptions are changed, the cummulative impact of these assumptions will obviously result in different solution outcomes.

NP2P traffic are examined. Optimal policy allocations and total costs under different relative NP2P cost scenarios are presented in Fig. 5.

The solution is not sensitive to changes in the relative cost associated with NP2P traffic. The optimal policy allocation and the total cost are impacted only slightly by changes in the NP2P cost. This is because there are a large number of NP2P users compared to P2P users on the test network. Increasing the relative NP2P cost from 1-to-1 to 2-to-1 results in exactly the same optimal allocation and total cost as increasing the relative cost from 1-to-1 to 100-to-1. The implication of this result for the test IAP is that a more dynamic policy is much more cost effective than the current policy. At the same time, the solution is very robust with respect to different possible relative values associated with NP2P traffic. The IAP can greatly improve total user satisfaction by implementing a much more dynamic policy. The optimal policy using a 100-to-1 NP2P to P2P cost ratio involves 18 allocation changes compared to the 4 currently used.

![](/api/attachments/PFAS823Y/fulltext/images/6d150801ef192767184969f429d7c0fcd85e5cb3ee08b3db84b9c4b131022b66.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Policy allocation using different relative change costs.

Decisions regarding how often change the allocation, and the magnitude of each change are impacted by the relative cost associated with making each incremental change. To evaluate the sensitivity of the change cost, the relative cost ratio between NP2P and P2P traffic is assumed to be 1-to-1, where equal value is placed on both NP2P and P2P traffic. Optimal policy allocations are presented for four different scenarios in Fig. 6. The total cost of the current policy assumes no change cost. As the relative value of the change cost increases (policy changes become more costly), the incremental allocation changes from 1 h to another become less dramatic. Although the result is not explicitly shown in Fig. 6 (the figure becomes too crowded), a relative change cost of 700 results in a completely static policy throughout the course of the day at an NP2P allocation of 65%.

The recommended optimal policy for the test IAP involves 10 allocation changes throughout the day and yields a 54.7% cost savings over the existing policy. The cost of implementing a more dynamic policy that closely mirrors observed NP2P demand should not deter the test IAP from making more allocation changes. The IAP greatly over compensates for NP2P demand with respect to setting the policy allocations. For example, beginning at 7 AM the existing policy heavily restricts P2P traffic, allocating only about 10% of the total bandwidth for P2P services. Empirical data indicate that NP2P usage does not exceed 50% of the total bandwidth until around 10 AM. The existing policy also underestimates NP2P demand during the early evening hours from 7 to 9 PM. Overall, there seems to be little practical justification for the existing policy from either a cost minimization or a bandwidth utilization perspective.

## 6. Conclusions

A practical, relative cost-based approach for optimizing bandwidth allocations between competing NP2P and P2P traffic is discussed. A GP is presented to estimate P2P demand in terms of both the number of P2P users as well as the bandwidth demanded by those users for different time periods and bandwidth allocations. Sensitivity analyses are performed for both changes in the relative user costs where the value of each traffic type is expressed as a ratio, and for the relative cost of making incremental changes to the policy allocation. This approach allows providers to incorporate costs that are very difficult to accurately calculate in practice – writing, testing, and porting code, monitoring and collecting network performance data, and responding to various service issues that result from changes in the bandwidth allocation over time – into the bandwidth allocation decision making process. Results are provided using empirical data from an actual university provider. The existing bandwidth allocation policy of the provider is examined.
