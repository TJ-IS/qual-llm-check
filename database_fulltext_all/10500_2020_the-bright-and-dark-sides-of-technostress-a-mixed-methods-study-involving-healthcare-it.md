# THE BRIGHT AND DARK SIDES OF TECHNOSTRESS: A MIXED-METHODS STUDY INVOLVING HEALTHCARE IT<sup>1</sup>

Christopher B. Califf College of Business and Economics, Western Washington University, Bellingham, WA 98225 U.S.A. {califfc@wwu.edu}

Saonee Sarker McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22093 U.S.A. {saonee@virginia.edu}

Suprateek Sarker McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22093 U.S.A. {sarkers@virginia.edu}

Today’s healthcare workers, specifically nurses, are experiencing technostress associated with the use of healthcare information technology (HIT). Technostress is often characterized by IS researchers as negative, or as being on the “dark side” of technology. However, a broader reading of the stress literature suggests that technostress may be both positive and negative, and can therefore have a “bright side” in addition to a dark side. The objective of this study is to conceptualize a holistic technostress process that includes positive and negative components of technostress embedded in two subprocesses: the techno-eustress subprocess and the techno-distress subprocess, respectively. The study instantiates this holistic technostress model through a sequential mixed-methods research design in the context of HIT. Phase 1 of the design is a qualitative, interpretive case study involving interviews with 32 nurses. Based on the findings from the case study, the paper builds a research model that operationalizes the concepts embedded in the holistic technostress model and identifies contextually relevant challenge and hindrance technostressors and outcomes. In Phase 2, the research model is empirically validated by analyzing survey data collected from 402 nurses employed in the United States. Results reveal that several challenge and hindrance technostressors are related to positive and negative psychological responses, respectively, and that such responses are related to job satisfaction and attrition, which impact turnover intention. Contributions to theory and practice are also discussed.

Keywords: Technostress, healthcare IT, eustress, distress, nursing, stress, mixed-methods

## Introduction

Healthcare information technology (HIT) is radically transforming the healthcare systems in many countries (Agarwal et al. 2010; Romanow et al. 2012). While there is much promise associated with HIT, the accelerated pace at which it is being adopted can pose a technological burden on care providers, who are reporting elevated levels of stress associated with it (Bhattacherjee and Hickmet 2007). Doctors are recognized as the primary care provider in the context of healthcare. Nurses, however, are considered by the Institute of Medicine as the care providers that are vital to the success of the U.S. digital healthcare revolution (IOM 2011). Nurses use HIT to carry out a variety of work-related tasks (Strong et al. 2014). For example, nurses use HIT to document and coordinate patient care and to communicate with physicians and other clinicians (Anderson and Robey 2017). It follows, then, that in addition to the myriad of other types of stressors experienced by nurses, such as dying patients, uncertainty concerning treatment, and conflict with physicians (McVicar 2003), nurses are likely to experience technology-related stressors and hence technostress by using HIT. Despite this, little is known about how nurses experience technostress related to HIT.

The information systems (IS) discipline has established a valuable research program on technostress (e.g., Ayyagari et al. 2011; Galluch et al. 2015; Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2007; Tarafdar et al. 2010; Tarafdar et al. 2011; Tu et al. 2005). Most technostress research to date conceptualizes technostress as negative by investigating the effect of five technostress creators (e.g., techno-complexity and techno-insecurity), which are assumed to be harmful to individuals or organizations and can lead to several deleterious work-related outcomes, such as decreased job satisfaction and low workplace productivity. Consequently, technostress is widely acknowledged as a phenomenon associated with technology’s “dark side” (Tarafdar et al. 2013). A closer look at the empirical literature on organizational stress, however, reveals two important findings: (1) stress may not always be negative and, in fact, may be positive, depending on how an individual appraises a stressor (Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Hargrove et al. 2013; LePine et al. 2005; Podsakoff et al. 2007) and (2) an understanding of technostress in the context of healthcare remains overlooked. Our study attempts to reconcile these gaps by investigating technostress in the context of healthcare as experienced by nurses.

Organizational stress is often conceptualized as a process comprising several components: stressors, psychological responses, and the effects of the psychological responses on individual and organizational outcomes (Hargrove et al. 2013; Nelson and Simmons 2004). Stressors embody two types: challenge stressors and hindrance stressors (Podsakoff et al. 2007). Challenge stressors are stressors associated with accomplishing workplace tasks and are appraised by individuals as beneficial (Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Podsakoff et al. 2007). Hindrance stressors are associated with constraining work-related tasks and are appraised by individuals as damaging (Hargrove et al. 2013). In a recent conceptual paper, challenge and hindrance stressors were applied to technology in terms of technostressors (Tarafdar et al. 2017). Challenge and hindrance stressors (including technostressors) are especially relevant to our study on nurses using HIT. For example, on one hand, nurses may appraise the software associated with the HIT as beneficial to providing efficient and effective patient care, while, on the other, nurses may appraise the frequent changes to HIT as a hindrance to accomplishing workplace tasks.

The overarching stress process also contains two subprocesses associated with the positive and negative aspects of stress: the eustress subprocess and the distress subprocess. The eustress subprocess involves “good stress” and encompasses challenge stressors and their impacts (Nelson and Simmons 2004; Selye 1974; Simmons and Nelson 2007). The distress subprocess involves “bad stress” and encompasses hindrance stressors and their outcomes. This dualistic view of eustress and distress also holds true in the context of technology (Califf et al. 2015; Tarafdar et al. 2017); however, IS researchers have not yet empirically identified or investigated challenge and hindrance technostressors and their effects, particularly in the context of healthcare.

While the literature on challenge and hindrance technostressors is still emerging, and the discussion on technoeustress and techno-distress remains largely conceptual, there is a need for a theoretically robust and empirically validated research model in order to understand the impact of challenge and hindrance technostressors with some level of confidence. Our aim in this paper is to contribute to this area. We strive to do so by drawing on the tenets of the theory of stress in organizations (McGrath 1976), and on emerging research in psychological stress and technostress (e.g., Califf et al. 2015; Hargrove et al. 2013; Tarafdar et al. 2017), to develop and test a holistic model of technostress. The holistic model incorporates and identifies empirically testable challenge and hindrance technostressors, their associated psychological responses, and important workplace outcomes, and is instantiated in the context of nursing.

To conduct the current investigation, we relied on a mixedmethod empirical design informed by several guidelines for context-based studies (e.g., Hong et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatsh et al. 2016). Following these guidelines, we adopted a layered approach in three steps. First, to help conceptualize the topic, we identified general theories and concepts concerning psychological and organizational stress, technostress, and stress in healthcare. Second, to illuminate the context of healthcare IT, we conducted a qualitative, interpretive study of registered nurses employed in two hospitals in the United States. Based on the analysis of the interview data, and informed by the literature base, we identified relevant and context-specific challenge and hindrance technostressors as well as several workplace outcomes. Third, once the research model was developed, we empirically tested the model using survey data gathered from hospital nurses. Given that turnover in the field of nursing is a recurring problem for healthcare organizations (Jones and Gates 2007) that can lower the quality of patient care and cost hospitals millions of dollars (Utriainen and Kyngäs 2009), we specifically chose to focus on outcome variables that are associated with turnover: job satisfaction, nursing attrition, and turnover intention (Joseph et al. 2007).

The rest of this article is organized as follows: First, we discuss the study’s theoretical foundations. This is followed by a review of our mixed-methods design and the qualitative study (Phase 1 of the mixed-methods design). We then present our research model including its associated hypotheses, followed by the details of the quantitative study (Phase 2 of the mixed-methods design). We conclude with a discussion of the results and theoretical and managerial contributions.

## Theoretical Foundations

## A Holistic Stress Process

Stress is often discussed as an “ongoing process that involves individuals transacting with their environment” (Cooper et al. 2001, p. 12). This ongoing stress process, hereafter referred to as the holistic stress process (Figure 1), is an overarching representation of how occupational stress unfolds from the perspective of an individual interacting with his or her workplace environment (Simmons and Nelson 2007). The holistic stress process is comprised of four stages: environmental conditions, stressors, psychological responses, and outcomes. Each of the four stages is connected by one of three evaluation processes: the appraisal process, the decision process, and the performance process (McGrath 1976). The appraisal process connects the environmental conditions and the stressors, the decision process connects the stressors and the psychological responses, and the performance process connects the psychological responses and the outcomes (McGrath 1976). Below we detail the holistic stress process and each of its components through supported research.

An individual’s behavior in organizations is governed by three systems: the physical-technological environment (i.e., the technology and other material and tangible items), the social-interpersonal environment (i.e., colleagues and coworkers), and person-system or self-system (i.e., the system of the individual) (McGrath 1976). The intersection of the three systems embodies the environmental conditions of the holistic stress process. The environmental conditions represent several potential sources of stressful situations in the workplace, such as role demands, task demands, technological demands, physical demands (e.g., noise, temperature, and office designs), interpersonal and behavioral expectations, job conditions, and workplace policies, among others (Hargrove et al. 2013; McGrath 1976). The environmental conditions can (but do not automatically) become stressful situations, which are known as stressors (Hargrove et al. 2013). It should be underscored that stressors are inherently neutral. That is, a stressor is defined by how an individual appraises the stressor in the context in which he or she works (Hargrove et al. 2013). In this sense, individuals can appraise the same stressors differently, and the same stressors can vary across contexts.

When the individual begins to experience a stressor, he or she evaluates the stressor through a cognitive appraisal process (McGrath 1976). In the appraisal process, the individual determines whether the stressor is beneficial or harmful to his or her job tasks (Hargrove et al. 2013). Several different factors influence how an individual appraises a stressor, including job experience and relevant individual differences (Hargrove et al. 2013; McGrath 1976). Once the individual appraises the stressor as a beneficial or harmful, the individual enters a decision-making process during which she or he decides how to respond to the stressor (McGrath 1976). The individual can decide to respond positively or negatively to the stressor. The effect of the decision process is called the psychological response, which is manifested in the form of positive or negative emotional states, depending on whether the stressor is appraised as beneficial or as harmful, respectively (Hargrove et al. 2013; McGrath 1976; Simmons and Nelson 2007). A positive psychological response is indicated, for example, by the presence of emotional states such as hope, positive attitudes, or engagement, while the indication of a negative psychological response includes the presence of emotional states such as negative attitudes, hostility, and anxiety, among others (Nelson and Simmons 2004; McVicar 2003).

After the individual experiences a psychological response, he or she enters the performance process during which he or she decides how to act or perform in the workplace, based on the psychological response (McGrath 1976). The effect of the performance process is a behavioral or workplace outcome, which is the consequence of the actions of an individual in reaction to the psychological response (Hargrove et al. 2013; Nelson and Simmons 2004). Examples of outcomes in organizational stress research include job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and turnover intention, among others (Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Podsakoff et al. 2007). Outcomes could be also termed strain (Ayyagari et al. 2011). It should be noted that an excess of positive emotional states may also result in detrimental outcomes, such as burnout (Hargrove et al. 2013).

More recent work on organizational stress suggests that it is critical for researchers to identify commonalities about how stressors are appraised so that their intended and unintended outcomes can be anticipated by managers (Hargrove et al. 2013; Podsakoff et al. 2007). This has led to research on identifying and classifying stressors as challenge stressors or hindrance stressors (Hargrove et al. 2013; LePine et al. 2005; Podsakoff et al. 2007). Challenge stressors are stressors “that tend to be appraised as promoting the accomplishment of job tasks and the personal development of the individual,” and hindrance stressors are those “that tend to be appraised as barriers or obstacles to the accomplishment of job tasks and personal development of the individual” (Hargrove et al. 2013, p. 62). Empirical research suggests that, more often than not, challenge stressors are linked to a positive psychological response and hindrance stressors are linked to a negative psychological response (Hargrove et al. 2013). In this paper, we specifically focus on the role of challenge and hindrance stressors in the context of organizational tasks and workplace outcomes only, rather than the role they may play in personal development, for example. In terms of the holistic stress process, the individual determines whether a stressor is a challenge stressor or a hindrance stressor in the appraisal process. If the individual appraises the stressor as related to promoting job tasks, the stressor is a challenge stressor; however, if the individual appraises the stressor as related to hindering job tasks, the stressor is a hindrance stressor (Hargrove et al. 2013).

Research suggests that stress should be conceptually framed in terms of two subprocesses embedded in a holistic stress process: the eustress subprocess and the distress subprocess (Califf et al. 2015; Tarafdar et al. 2017). The eustress subprocess is related to positive stress, or good stress (Selye 1974), and involves understanding challenge stressors and their association with positive psychological responses and, in turn, positive outcomes. The subprocess of distress is related to negative stress, or bad stress (Selye 1974), and involves understanding hindrance stressors and their association with negative psychological responses and, in turn, negative outcomes (Hargrove et al. 2013).

Based on the discussion above, and drawing on the aforementioned research on stress, in Figure 1, we present our conceptual framework surrounding the holistic stress process, which includes eustress and distress as two important subprocesses (Nelson and Simmons 2004; Hargrove et al. 2013).

## Technostress

Technostress is conceptually defined as “a modern disease of adaptation caused by an inability to cope with new computer technologies in a healthy manner” (Brod 1984, p. 16). In IS research, technostress is composed of five dimensions. These dimensions are collectively known as technostressors, which are considered harmful stressors that induce deleterious individual and workplace outcomes (Tarafdar et al. 2007; Tarafdar et al. 2017). The technostressors are defined as “IS stress creators appraised by the individual as threatening” (Tarafdar et al. 2017, p. 5). The five technostressors are techno-overload, techno-invasion, techno-complexity, technoinsecurity, and techno-uncertainty (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Techno-overload occurs when individuals appraise events related to stressful situations that contribute to users having to work longer and faster than normal. Techno-invasion involves situations in which people are constantly connected to technology outside of the workplace context. Technocomplexity comprises situations in which the complexity of technology is associated with users feeling inadequate about their technology skills, resulting in users spending more time and effort to understand the technology (Tarafdar et al. 2011). Techno-insecurity represents stressful situations that make users feel “threatened about losing their jobs to other people who have a better understanding of the ICT” (Tarafdar et al. 2011, p. 119). Techno-uncertainty addresses the pace at which software, hardware, and computer networks change (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008).

IS researchers have also identified several technology characteristics important to the context of technology and stress. The characteristics, according to Ayyagari et al. (2011), involve three types: usability characteristics, dynamic characteristics, and intrusive characteristics. These categories are instantiated in terms of variables such as usefulness and reliability, among others (DeLone and McLean 2003; Moore and Benbasat 1991). Usefulness and reliability are viewed as being part of usability characteristics (Ayyagari et al. 2011). For example, users who perceive technology as useful are more productive and appraise technology as reducing their work overload (Straub and Karahanna 1998). Within the technostress literature, reliability is often discussed as problems associated with the dependability and consistency of technology, thereby making it more akin to unreliability. For example, Ayyagari et al. (2011) state that “users interacting with unreliable ICTs are reported to be frustrated and strained” and that “the threat of unreliability increases perceived workload” (p. 840). In this sense, the unreliability of technology is related to individuals not being able to use the technology to carry out work-related tasks and responsibilities.

A recent opinion paper uses the ideas embedded in the challenge–hindrance stressor framework to discuss technostress in terms of challenge and hindrance technostressors (see Tarafdar et al. 2017). Challenge technostressors are defined in terms of individuals appraising technology associated with “challenges that they are motivated to tackle because they expect that doing so is within their wherewithal and would lead to betterment” (Tarafdar et al. 2017, p. 14). Conversely, hindrance technostressors, or threat technostressors, occur when an individual appraises the characteristics of the IS as “threatening and disturbing factors,” and are conceptualized as comprising the five harmful technostressors mentioned previously (Tarafdar et al. 2017, p. 15). While challenge and hindrance stressors have been manifested within the concept of technostress as technostressors, little empirical research exists on how to identify and/or evaluate challenge and hindrance technostressors empirically.

Conceptually, eustress and distress have been theorized in relation to technology through two concepts: techno-eustress and techno-distress (Califf et al. 2015; Sethi et al. 1987). Techno-eustress has been defined as “the phenomenon that embodies the positive stress that individuals face in their use of $\mathrm { I S } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and occurs when “individuals appraise IS as challenging or thrilling” (Tarafdar et al. 2017, p. 14). Conversely, techno-distress embodies “how and why individuals appraise IS as a threat, experience consequent ‘bad’ stress, and are faced largely with detrimental outcomes” (Tarafdar et al. 2017, p. 14). Empirical research on technostress frequently frames technostress as inducing several harmful outcomes, both for the individual and the organizations. For example, the hindrance technostressors have been linked to several outcomes, including low innovation (Tarafdar et al. 2011), low job satisfaction, low organizational commitment, and low continuance commitment (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Recent literature suggests that challenge technostressors could be linked to positive outcomes such as innovativeness, creativity, or productivity (Tarafdar et al. 2017), although no empirical research exists on the topic. The literature on technostress also operationalizes several mechanisms that can help reduce the negative impacts of technostress, including involvement facilitation, literacy facilitation, and technical support (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2011).

## Stress in Healthcare

Although not much is known about the challenge and hindrance technostressors in the context of healthcare and/or in nursing, researchers have identified several stressors relevant to nurses. These include inadequate preparation, problems with peers and supervisors, workload, and discrimination (McVicar 2003). Healthcare researchers regard eustress and distress conceptually related to several psychological and emotional states (Gibbons et al. 2008; Nelson and Simmons 2003). For example, eustress has been conceptualized and measured through constructs such as motivation, positive affect, excitement, and increased level of mental acuity (Hargrove et al. 2013; McVicar 2003). Distress has been conceptualized and measured through constructs such as unease, sadness, depression, negative attitudes, and lack of selfesteem (McVicar 2003). Recent research suggests, however, that in the context of healthcare, little is known about the key organizational outcomes of stress such as job commitment and performance, and an understanding of the key variables that impact these outcomes is critical for hospital managers and administrators, as well as for overall health policy (Hargrove et al. 2013). One critical outcome in nursing is discussed in terms of the nursing turnover problem (Jones and Gates 2007). Nursing turnover has been estimated to cost approximately \$22,000 to \$64,000 per nurse, due to issues such as job advertising and recruitment, decreased productivity, patient errors, loss of organizational knowledge, and overtime for other nurses (Jones and Gates 2007). Unfortunately, turnover in nursing has also been cited as a factor contributing to reductions in the quality of patient care (Utriainen and Kyngäs 2009).

Regarding research on technostress in healthcare, with few exceptions (e.g., Burke 2009), there has been little empirical work. While not focusing on technostress specifically, Lapointe and Rivard (2005) examine factors that impact physician resistance to clinical information systems. The authors find that when physicians interpret the consequence of using clinical information systems as threatening, articulated through several terms such as “distress” or “fear,” they begin to resist the technology, resulting in low levels of use or high levels of misuse. Outside of the IS discipline, Khuntia et al. (2015) investigate technostress linked to nurses using an intelligent care system. They assume that technostress is negative and found that the care system created “alert fatigue,” which induced technology-related distress in nurses. Recent IS research also sheds light on how nurses use various types of HIT during a shift, and how such use could be related to positive and negative psychological responses. For example, Anderson and Robey (2017) examine how nurses use electronic healthcare record (EHR) systems to complete several job tasks, such as documenting patient information, coordinating care, and validating medications. The authors specifically found that, when the features of HIT are not working, or the nurses are not familiar with the software and hardware, nurses may experience distress (Anderson and Robey 2017).

It should be underscored that nurses regularly interact with a hospital environment that includes HIT in order to accomplish several work-related tasks (Hargrove et al. 2013). In this sense, nurses are likely to appraise stressors associated with technology as related to promoting or impeding task accomplishment (i.e., in terms of challenge technostressors or hindrance technostressors), but the effects of such stressors are unknown. One example may be that a nurse appraises the HIT’s software as promoting the task of documenting patient information. The same nurse, or another nurse, may appraise a mobile workstation as a barrier to providing effective patient care. In this sense, nurses are likely to appraise and experience technostress and its associated subcomponents (e.g., technostressors) in various ways.

To summarize, there is a plethora of valuable research on stress, technostress, and stress in nursing; however, to date, no known study has identified and empirically tested the effects of challenge or hindrance technostressors, particularly in the healthcare context. Further, past studies have not empirically tested the associated (and holistic) effects of challenge and hindrance technostressors in any specific context. Thus, the instantiation of our overarching stress framework (Figure 1) and the potential variables embedded in it need to be contextualized (Hong et al. 2013). To contextualize the framework, and to identify several challenge and hindrance technostressors appraised by nurses, we relied on a qualitative study involving hospital nurses (Phase 1 of a two-phase mixedmethods empirical design). We discuss the two phases of the mixed-methods design and contextual guidelines below. Figure 2 also provides details of the mixed-methods design.

## The Mixed-Methods Design

Mixed-methods research designs “contain elements of both quantitative and qualitative approaches” (Tashakkori and Teddlie 1998, p. 5). Within the IS discipline, mixed-methods designs are particularly useful since the nature of the context changes frequently and researchers often have difficulty drawing significant insights from existing theories and perspectives (Venkatesh et al. 2013). Mixed-methods designs offer three specific benefits: the ability to “address confirmatory and explanatory research questions,” to “provide stronger inferences than a single method or worldview,” and to “produce a greater assortment of divergent and/or complementary views” (Venkatesh et al. 2016, p. 437). Given the general paucity of studies on technostress within a healthcare setting, a mixed-methods design is well suited to our work.

The mixed-methods design for the current study began by articulating three research questions (one qualitative, one quantitative, and one mixed-methods) (Venkatesh et al. 2016). We chose a developmental purpose whereby we conducted a qualitative study first and used the results from this strand to develop the hypotheses and the research model tested in the second strand of research (Tashakkori and Teddlie 1998; Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016). We adopted multiple paradigms as our epistemological stance. During the qualitative phase (Phase 1) we took an interpretive perspective. During the quantitative phase (Phase 2) we adopted a positivist approach. The methodology can be classified as “mixed-methods multistrand” (Venkatesh et al. 2016, p. 443). Our study also falls within the realm of “dominant/less dominant design” where “one paradigm is dominant, while a small component of the overall study is drawn from alternative paradigm” (Tashakkori and Teddlie 1998, p. 44). We used a sequential sampling strategy with parallel samples and performed data analysis sequentially to help build the research model for the quantitative study from the results of the qualitative study. In Appendices G, H, and J, we elaborate on our choices and research questions that guided the mixedmethods design and articulate how we followed established criteria for mixed-methods designs.

Overall, the mixed-methods design was divided into two phases and influenced by the guidelines for contextual research studies (see Hong et al. 2013). In Phase 1, we accomplished the following: (1) grounded our research in a general conceptual framework, which provided a holistic view of stress and technostress (Figures 1 and 2); (2) conducted interviews with individual nurses to identify how they use and interact with HIT in their workplace by understanding the environmental conditions that may give rise to a technostressor; (3) analyzed the interview data and iteratively reviewed the core literature base to uncover and identify relevant challenge and hindrance technostressors in terms of being apprised as related to task accomplishment (Hargrove et al. 2013). In Phase 2, we did the following: (1) revisited the relevant empirical literature and categorized the challenge and hindrance technostressors that emerged from the qualitative study to build a testable research model that included challenge and hindrance technostressors, psychological responses, and relevant outcomes; (2) used the qualitative data and literature base to inform and justify our hypothesizing and theorizing the relationships among the constructs; and (3) tested the model using survey data provided by hospital nurses. See Figure 2 for an illustration of how the phases of the mixed-methods study unfolded in relation to the conceptual framework. Below, we discuss the phases in more detail.

## Phase 1: The Qualitative Study

A qualitative, interpretive approach to study technostress was adopted in the context of two large hospitals in the western United States (Myers 2008; Walsham 1995, 2006). Data collection and analysis were guided by several principles and suggestions for interpretive research (Klein and Myers 1999; Sarker et al. 2013; Walsham 1995). These principles provided us the ability to maintain criticality regarding our data and interpretations and to demonstrate appropriate levels of transparency in our analysis and findings.

## Context

In the late 1980s, several healthcare organizations in the western United States formed a health services organization to oversee the integrated delivery of healthcare. Central to this goal was collaboration among a member network of hospitals in three states. The organization selected a popular EHR system, hereafter known as HealthTech (a pseudonym), to be at the heart of the integrated delivery of healthcare. Our study examines the phenomenon of interest in the context of two hospitals in the network. At the time of our interviews, both hospitals were running HealthTech, but slightly different versions. In one hospital, HealthTech was implemented approximately five years before data collection; in the other hospital, approximately three years before data collection. One hospital was running HealthTech version 5, while the other one used HealthTech version 6. Both versions are similar in appearance.

## Data Collection

The researchers of this study played the role of a neutral outside researcher (Walsham 1995). In total, three researchers were involved in the interviews. Full-time or part-time nurses providing direct care to patients were selected as part of the sample. We used a snowballing technique to obtain interviewees once the initial interviews were completed. Participating nurses held a combination of shift types and shift lengths and worked in a range of hospital units. Full-time and part-time nurses were included, as were nursing managers. Appendix D provides more information about interviewees.

In total, four rounds of interviews with 32 nurses were conducted between April 2012 and January 2014. If permission was granted, interviews were audiotaped and subsequently transcribed by the first author and by a transcription service. Field notes were also taken during the interviews. To supplement interview data, the first author worked with members of the research committee on the hospitals to obtain screenshots of HealthTech. During the study’s initial stages, interview questions focused on the job responsibilities of each nurse, the hospital’s history (with an emphasis on HealthTech), how the nurses use HealthTech, and what the nurses liked or did not like about HealthTech in relation to their organizational environment. During later stages, interview questions underscored the emerging concepts and themes of the iterative analysis, paying particular attention to how nurses discussed and appraised HealthTech in terms of challenge or hindrance stressors associated with HealthTech, as well as the environmental conditions that gave rise to such stressors. To collect these interpretations, nurses were encouraged to describe the features and characteristics of HealthTech they used most often and were asked to elaborate on specific examples and feelings about work-related situations involving HealthTech that promote or impede a nurse’s job tasks.

## Data Analysis

The theoretical foundation of our study evolved over time (e.g., Walsham and Sahay 1999). Throughout the iterative analysis, we adopted the principle of theoretical engagement (Sarker et al. 2013). The principle of theoretical engagement involves “utilizing a meta-theoretical lens in the initial and iterative stages” of qualitative data analysis (Stumpf and Califf 2018, p. 123). In other words, we drew on the conceptual foundation of stress and technostress, as well as the associated vocabulary to interpret the interviews (see Figures 1 and 2). For example, the definitions of challenge and hindrance stressors provided by Hargrove et al. (2013) were used to identify the specific technostressors, since these concepts have been instantiated in the psychological stress literature for several years (e.g., Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Hargrove et al. 2013; LePine et al. 2005).

In the initial analysis, we iteratively analyzed the data for evidence about how individual nurses described their work and appraised HealthTech and its associated environmental characteristics as playing a role in their work. The analysis unearthed stressful feelings about HealthTech in both positive and negative ways (i.e., there seemed to be good stress and bad stress). This contradicted much of the current research on technostress at the time. We, therefore, examined the psychological stress literature and found the concepts of challenge and hindrance stressors (Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Hargrove et al. 2013). These concepts were subsequently applied to the initial analysis in order to determine the common instances of the challenge and hindrance stressors related to HealthTech, which we later referred to as technostressors. The qualitative data were reexamined through this lens, vocabulary, and associated concepts. Specifically, data were reinterpreted for references to the definitions of challenge and hindrance stressors regarding appraisal in terms of task accomplishment (Hargrove et al. 2013). In other words, the stressors were categorized as challenge technostressors if they were appraised as related to promoting task accomplishment and were categorized as hindrance technostressors if they were appraised as presenting a barrier or obstacle to task accomplishment. For example, several nurses appraised HealthTech as useful in terms of documenting patient data, while other nurses appraised HealthTech’s unreliability as a barrier to documenting patient data.

Once the stressors were reduced to a core set of challenge and hindrance technostressors, we reviewed current research and found a conceptual paper on technostress that discussed challenge and hindrance technostressors (Tarafdar et al. 2017). To understand the potential positive and negative emotional states associated with the technostressors, we revisited the data again and questioned several nurses about their relationships with technostressors during the final interviews. Saturation was reached once the associations were made through analysis in conjunction with the empirical literature.

We summarize our data collection and analysis techniques and provide more detail about how we followed suggested guidelines for formative and summative validity in Appendix E. A table illustrating how the interview data were matched to empirical constructs can be found in Appendix I.

## Building and Hypothesizing the Research Model

In this section, we hypothesize about the relationships between the challenge and hindrance technostressors and their associated positive or negative psychological responses. Our hypotheses are based on the relevant empirical literature as well as on evidence from our qualitative study (Phase 1 of the mixed-methods design) in the form of positive and negative emotional states expressed by the nurses during interviews. (Hargrove et al. 2013; Nelson and Simmons 2004). We also used our Phase 1 findings to hypothesize about the relationships between positive and negative psychological responses and several outcome variables related to turnover. Following this section, we will move to a discussion of Phase 2 of our study, in which we quantitatively test our research model and validate our hypotheses.

## Challenge Technostressors and the Positive Psychological Response

We begin by discussing the relationships among three challenge technostressors that we identified in Phase 1 of the mixed-methods design (the qualitative study) and their attendant positive psychological responses (see Figure 2 for a definition of challenge technostressors and positive psychological response): namely, usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation. It should be underscored that technical support and involvement facilitation have been defined and empirically investigated in previous technostress research; they have also been empirically employed in contexts outside of technostress. However, usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation have not been specifically contextualized as challenge technostressors. In this sense, based on Phase 1 of the mixed-methods design, we recontextualized usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation as challenge technostressors critical to nurses using HIT to carry out work-related tasks. These variables are regarded as challenge technostressors because nurses in the qualitative study used language that matched the conceptual definitions of the three variables. In other words, when articulating how they used HealthTech for work, nurses tended to discuss concepts related to usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation in terms of promoting task accomplishment rather than hindering task accomplishment.

Below we discuss these three challenge technostressors in more detail and provide specific examples of how the language used by the nurses participating in our study supports the recontextualization of usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation as challenge technostressors and hypothesize how each is related to a positive psychological response.

Usefulness. When end users of a technology perceive it to be useful, the technology represents an opportunity to improve their work (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Several nurses in the qualitative study expressed sentiments associated with the usefulness of HealthTech in relation to accomplishing tasks. For example, a Pre-Operation and Recovery nurse discussed how HealthTech provided her with the ability to access several pieces of patient information and to pass this information along to other hospitals in the region. She thus characterized this as related to improving and facilitating her job tasks:

I like the fact that if I have a patient that’s coming from the floor, I can quickly and easily go to the screen to see when that patient left, had his or her antibiotics, or what his or her last temperature was. So, I like the accessibility that it affords me to be able to look at that information. There are maybe five hospitals overall that are on the same system and built together, so if a patient was at another hospital I actually could look at that visit and pull up that information. I like that a lot. (Nurse\_13, Pre-Op and Recovery Nurse)

Existing research suggests that the usefulness of HIT can also induce positive feelings and can enhance an individual’s behavioral intention to use HIT (Chau and Hu 2002). In this sense, usefulness is likely to induce a positive psychological response in nurses because they are likely to view the usefulness of healthcare technology as a way of facilitating their job skills and tasks. Based on these arguments, we posit:

H1: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high usefulness will have a positive psychological response.

Technical Support. The qualitative study revealed many comments about the technical support staff helping to promote task accomplishment. We categorized these comments as related to the construct called technical support. For example, a float nurse (a nurse who changes units depending on where he or she is needed) explained that though problems with HealthTech were often frustrating, the technical support staff helped her complete several tasks that required HealthTech. She regarded this assistance as beneficial for the patient and her job:

We have a help desk, and they’re really nice. I never feel stupid about calling and asking, “How do I fix this?” That helps me to know that there’s someone I can go to if there’s a problem, and just knowing that it’s best for the patient ultimately. (Nurse\_10, Float Nurse)

Comments like this were shared by several other nurses. For example, like the float nurse, an Emergency Room (ER) nurse also said that the technical support staff were accessible and responsive, and often helped her overcome technical issues related to HealthTech:

Our help desk is very responsive. They’ve created a lot of avenues to try and correct the problems as quickly as they can. (Nurse\_26, Emergency Department Nurse)

Empirical research also suggests that the presence of end-user support is associated with positive emotions in workers since it helps workers complete tasks more swiftly (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Moreover, it has been argued that the presence of technical support reduces workloads and offers a positive support system for employees (Brod 1984). Based on these arguments, we posit:

H2: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high technical support will have a positive psychological response.

Involvement Facilitation. Involvement facilitation is defined as the inclusion of end users when planning, developing, and implementing technology (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Several nurses detailed that they were able to submit requests to the on-site IT department in terms of suggesting changes to HealthTech’s software features and capabilities. This, in turn, helped them feel empowered as part of the change process, given that such changes foster a supportive workplace environment. For example, a nurse working in the Operating Room (OR) mentioned that the hospital she works for updates HealthTech regularly based on feedback from nurses, who ask for certain features to enhance their job tasks. She explained:

[The hospital] does updates all time based on nurses saying, “Can we have it do this,” or “Can we have it do that?” They’re doing updates all the time. Even just little things. Even just adding one of those pop-up boxes. A nurse might say, “Can we have a box that says this?” and [the hospital] can make that change. I think that’s good. (Nurse\_4, Operating Room)

Similarly, a float nurse elaborated on the fact that her hospital has a department-specific nursing representative who serves as a liaison for nurses, technical support, and management. She specifically discussed this in terms of advising nurses about upcoming changes in HealthTech that may affect them:

We have what’s called a nursing subcouncil that has been formed, and it’s supposed to have a representative from each unit. Their role is to be that champion for these changes that are being made. We also help with the decisions in the process with our specific department’s best interests in mind … what works best for our workflow. Our role as that person on the council is to go back to the staff and our managers and say, “Hey, this is what’s coming. This is the change that’s about to happen. If you have time during your shift, one on one, tell people the change, explain it to them.” You kind of have that open ear when you’re on the unit all the time so that you can help people when they’re struggling. (Nurse\_10, Float Nurse)

The literature suggests that end users who are involved with the initial stages of IT development are likely to have positive attitudes toward the system, given that they understand how such changes can help them accomplish their work (Barki and Hartwick 1994). Both of these arguments hold true in healthcare, where the early involvement of nurses and other care providers in the design and implementation of HIT such as barcode medication scanners has been identified as a principal way to increase positive feelings toward the IT (Weckman and Janzen 2009). Based on these arguments, we posit:

H3: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high involvement facilitation will have a positive psychological response.

## Hindrance Technostressors and the Negative Psychological Response

Below, we discuss the relationships between the specific hindrance technostressors (that emerged from the Phase 1 study) and the negative psychological response (see Figure 2 for definitions of hindrance technostressors and the negative psychological response). Our qualitative analysis (Phase 1) and literature review revealed five primary hindrance technostressors that were associated with presenting obstacles for nurses in completing work tasks.

Unreliability. Reliability of technology is a way to maintain user engagement and can increase a user’s confidence when using technology, thereby creating a positive user experience (Ayyagari et al. 2011). In fact, reliability is a central component of the IS success model (DeLone and McLean 2003). However, our qualitative study uncovered that when discussing reliability, nurses tended to use language about the unreliability of HealthTech. In particular, several nurses commented that the unreliability of HealthTech presents a barrier to maintaining high-quality patient care. For example, a nurse working in the Advanced Care Medical Unit commented on the reliability of the computer hardware and software by saying “I think none of them are stressful as long as they’re working.” Similarly, a float nurse described HealthTech in terms of the “unpredictability” associated with the “sign-on” process. She said that the sign-on process required her to type in her username and password by pressing the “enter” button on a keyboard or by using a mouse to click a “sign-on” button each time she signed in to HealthTech. She explained that HealthTech was “unpredictable” because start-up time varied, which created negative feelings related to perceptions of unreliability. She elaborated:

As soon as you hit “Enter” or “Sign-On” after you type in your username and password, you can tell if [HealthTech] is going to take a bit [to sign on] or not. Sometimes it’ll happen quicker than others. It’s really unpredictable. It’s hard to say, but once you hit “Enter,” you kind of have a feeling that this is going to take a bit. Just kind of assessing the situation all the time ... I’ll start my assessment on my patient, tidy up the room. I’ll grab my meds and organize those. You have to, otherwise, you’re going to be behind all day long. (Nurse\_24, Float Nurse)

Empirical research corroborates these findings. For example, in a seminal paper on technostress, Ayyagari et al. (2011) argue that users who interact with an unreliable technology experience negative psychological states. Moreover, in the healthcare context, it has been argued that reliable technology is associated with supporting the ability to deliver safer and more effective patient care, while unreliable healthcare IT compromises the quality of patient care (Krueger 2010). We therefore posit:

H4: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high unreliability will have a negative psychological response.

Techno-Complexity. When users perceive technology as complex, they do not know enough about the technology to adequately perform their job responsibilities (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Moreover, users may require long periods of time to use new technologies and may find that newer employees may understand technology better (Tarafdar et al. 2007). Users may also view the complexity of the technology as damaging to completing their work (Tarafdar et al. 2017). Our qualitative analysis echoes these claims. For example, a nurse who works in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) and Advanced Care Medical Unit said that even though she felt that the responsibilities of being a nurse are often stable, she did not know enough about how to document her patients information on HealthTech, which ultimately impeded her work. She explained:

The job responsibilities of the nurse are pretty steady. I think the documentation and that part of the job changes a lot. That’s hard to keep up on.… The documentation in [HealthTech] was a little hard. (Nurse\_32, ICU/Advanced Care Medical Unit)

Like the nurses in our sample, research suggests that healthcare technology is often perceived by care providers as highly complex (WHO 2010). Such complexity has resulted in “impeding the development of mental models” in care providers (WHO 2010). Based on the above arguments, we posit:

H5: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high techno-complexity will have a negative psychological response.

Techno-Uncertainty. Uncertainty about technology materializes when constant changes to the technology occur and when such changes are not communicated effectively to the users of the technology, thereby serving as an obstacle to completing work-related tasks (Tarafdar et al. 2017; Tarafdar et al. 2011). Under such circumstances, individuals experience highly negative emotions (Arnetz and Wiholm 1997). In the context of the current study, several nurses indicated that constant changes in HealthTech act as a barrier to caring for patients. For example, an OR nurse described that her hospital changes the features of HealthTech often. She also discussed that while the hospital communicates updates to the staff through email, the emails are vague, making it difficult to ascertain whether the updates are relevant to her specific department and specific tasks. She explained:

Sometimes [the hospital] does updates.…We’ll get an overhead log-off, maybe for a couple hours or something. They’ll be working on [HealthTech] and we won’t be able to access anything on the computer .…[The hospital] sends out emails all the time saying “[HealthTech] update. [HealthTech] update,” and mostly you don’t pay attention to them because [HealthTech is] always updating. It doesn’t say what they're going to be updating, so it doesn’t say [on the] main Operating Room screen this [update] will have that. It just says there will be a package of 100 updates. (Nurse\_4, Operating Room)

Likewise, research suggests that since nurses are often unfamiliar with technology at multiple levels, especially at the hardware and network levels, they are more likely to experience negative feelings due to high levels of uncertainty (Bucknall and Thomas 1997). Based on the above arguments and evidence, we posit:

H6: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high techno-uncertainty will have a negative psychological response.

Techno-Insecurity. When individuals feel insecure about technology, they tend to feel vulnerable about losing their jobs to users who are more knowledgeable and “tech-savvy” (Tarafdar et al. 2011). Our qualitative analysis revealed sentiments that support this. For example, a nurse who works on the Cardiac Medical Floor described her insecurity with HealthTech by saying she felt “threatened” by a younger nurse:

You’re being threatened because somebody younger comes in and they’re very computer savvy; they’re very quickly picking up the technology. (Nurse\_31, Cardiac Medical Floor)

Another nurse who works in the Pre-Operation and Recovery Unit discussed her perceptions that that newer employees (i.e., “younger ones”) have a better understanding of technology than she does:

The learning curve is challenging … I think it might be easier for the younger ones who have come up with this technology probably a lot easier, and maybe not quite as challenging because they know their way around a lot better. I just go where I’m comfortable and I don’t explore a lot. (Nurse\_25, Pre-Operation and Recovery Unit)

Insecurity is also associated with the reticence to share and discuss technology-related knowledge with coworkers, and a sense that coworkers tend to withhold technology-related knowledge from each other (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). A pediatric nurse mentioned that she used HealthTech at a previous place of employment, but she noted that she avoided suggesting changes to HealthTech use at her current hospital:

I never would be that person who’s like “That’s not the way we did it.” I keep that comment to myself, but internally, I’m like man, we sure did it better that way. (Nurse\_32, Pediatric Emergency Room)

When nurses experience insecurity, they tend to hold negative views toward HIT because they are insecure about how HIT will impact their current roles (Kuo et al. 2013; Wichowski 1994). Nurses have also been cited as feeling an increased amount of pressure to learn HIT because they are increasingly expected to exhibit a high level of computer proficiency (Evans 2013). Based on these arguments, we posit:

H7: Nurses who appraise healthcare technology as associated with high techno-insecurity will have a negative psychological response.

Techno-Overload. Techno-overload is defined as situations in which technology forces users to work faster and longer (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008). Nursing often involves working in shifts. This means that when nurses are overloaded by HIT, they may have to work faster and longer and perhaps outside of their normal work hours. Nurses specifically use HIT to provide patients with “continuity of care,” which refers to the passing of patient information from one nurse to another or from one nurse to several care providers or families (Unertl et al. 2012). If a nurse does not have a chance to enter such information during his or her shift and must thus work faster or stay longer, he or she will likely experience negative psychological states. A nurse on the Cardiac Medical Floor discussed this by describing how HealthTech often pressures her to document so much information about her patients that it reduces the amount of face-to-face time she can spend with her patients. Because of this, she reported regularly spending her breaks documenting patient information, and therefore experienced changes in her work habits:

Sometimes I’ll go back into a computer that’s in the back, like in our break room, and if I’m really pushed for time, I’ll eat the rest of my lunch and chart so that I can get my summaries done. And that’s stressful because you don’t get your full break. (Nurse\_29, Cardiac Medical Floor)

Similarly, a nurse in the Adolescent and Child Psych Unit said that she often hears a “ticking clock” in her head when interacting with patients, which she described as an incessant reminder that she needed to enter the required information into HealthTech before the end of her shift. She relayed that she feels this creates a barrier to spending time with her patients and elaborated on this sense of a “ticking clock”:

There are some things you have to get in the computer. You have to get certain things done every day. I think it limits that one-on-one time. There’s a lot of push and a lot of stress…You have to put this, this, and this in the computer every day, every shift.…That’s a struggle for me personally. I’ve always got this ticking clock in my head. I still have to do X, X, and X. I’ve got a patient over here who’s in trouble. I need to spend time with them. Sometimes, I think that affects the one-on-one with my patient because I am sort of pushing that process that shouldn’t be pushed because I’ve got 10 assessments that I have to get into the computer before the next shifts comes.” (Nurse\_9, Adolescent and Child Psych Unit)

Based on these arguments, we posit:

H8: Nurses who appraise a healthcare technology as associated with high techno-overload will have a negative psychological response.

## Outcomes

Outcomes represent how individuals elect to respond to stressors based on their psychological responses (Hargrove et al. 2013; McGrath 1976). We chose to focus on three outcomes that have been studied in the organizational literature and have been identified as critical for determining turnover: job satisfaction, attrition, and turnover intention (Joseph et al. 2007). These three variables were primarily chosen due to their relevance in curbing the turnover problem in nursing (Utriainen and Kyngäs 2009).

Job Satisfaction and Nursing Attrition. In the context of nursing, job satisfaction is defined in several different ways (e.g., Conrad et al. 1985; Lu et al. 2012). Generally, job satisfaction represents “the affective orientation that an employee has towards his or her work” (Chu et al. 2003, p. 176). Nursing attrition occurs when nurses begin to exhibit thoughts of quitting the field of nursing (MacKusick and Minick 2010). A related idea of valence has been used by Sarker et al. (2010) to describe professionals’ unfavorable feelings about their work situations, which can influence such professionals to leave their line of work. Attrition defined in this way is conceptually associated with withdrawal cognitions, which include thoughts of quitting, job search evaluations, and job search intentions (Joseph et al. 2007).

Empirical research in the context of nursing has discovered that positive psychological states are associated with positive workplace outcomes, while negative psychological states are associated with negative workplace outcomes (Simmons and Nelson 2001). For example, in a literature review of stress in nursing, McVicar (2003) emphasizes that the psychological indicators associated with eustress have positive workplace outcomes, such as high job satisfaction, while those associated with distress have negative workplace outcomes, such as low job satisfaction. In terms of nursing attrition, it has been argued that positive psychological states, such as selfesteem and self-efficacy are predictors of low attrition, given that employees tend to find their work engaging and exciting (Peterson-Graziose et al. 2013). In fact, research suggests that distressful feelings are a primary reason for nursing attrition (MacKusick and Minick 2010). Nursing research has also uncovered a critical relationship between job satisfaction and attrition suggesting that job satisfaction decreases one’s thoughts of quitting the field (Lu et al. 2002). Similarly, Joseph et al. (2007) suggest in a model of turnover that “job dissatisfaction is proposed to trigger a series of withdrawal cognitions” (p. 548), which include intentions of quitting the field.

In the context of the current study, nurses who feel that HealthTech is associated with promoting task accomplishment, for example, and experience a positive psychological response, are more likely to be highly satisfied with their jobs and thus less likely to experience withdrawal conditions related to intentions to quit the field of nursing. On the other hand, nurses who feel that HealthTech is a barrier to task accomplishment are more likely to be dissatisfied with their jobs and thus more likely to exhibit such withdrawal conditions. Based on the previous arguments, we therefore hypothesize:

H9: (a) Nurses who experience a positive psychological response will have high job satisfaction.

(b) Nurses who experience a negative psychological response will have low job satisfaction.

H10: (a) Nurses who experience a positive psychological response will have low attrition.

(b) Nurses who experience a negative psychological response will have high attrition.

H11: Nurses who experience high job satisfaction will have low attrition.

Turnover Intention: Turnover intention has been defined as “a conscious and deliberate willingness to leave the organization” (Egan et al. 2004, p. 286). In nursing, several workplace variables have been linked to turnover intention such as job satisfaction (Collins et al. 2000; Tai et al. 1998) and thoughts of quitting the field (i.e., nursing attrition) (Coomber et al. 2007; Hayes et al. 2006). This holds true in the context of IS as well. For example, in a meta-analysis of workplace variables and their effects on turnover intention, Joseph et al. (2007) show that job satisfaction can reduce turnover intention and that employees who develop withdrawal cognitions linked to quitting intentions, such as attrition, are likely to experience high turnover intention. It follows that nurses who are satisfied with their jobs will be less likely to leave their organizations, while those who exhibit withdrawal conditions associated with quitting the field of nursing will be more likely experience high turnover intention. We therefore hypothesize:

H12: (a) Nurses who experience high job satisfaction will have low turnover intention.

(b) Nurses who experience high attrition will have high turnover intention.

Based on the above hypotheses and conceptual development, we present our research model of technostress in healthcare (Figure 3), as developed in Phase 1 of the mixed-methods design. We now describe Phase 2, which involves empirically testing the research model.

## Phase 2: The Quantitative Study

Phase 2 of the mixed-methods design involved the empirical test of the research model that was developed in Phase 1. The research model was empirically tested using data collected from a survey of nurses who worked in several areas of practice. Obtaining views from nurses employed in a variety of practice areas allowed us to survey and analyze a range of stress perceptions and to find general commonalities of HITinduced stress across several departments.

## Survey Development

Existing scales were adopted to suit the context of this study. Each item was scored on a seven-point scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. To ensure face validity, five individuals involved in academic research, including one individual who is a family nurse practitioner reviewed the survey. Minor changes were made after this assessment. Details of each construct and its associated measurement items can be found in Appendix C. The challenge technostressors include usefulness, which was adopted from Moore and Benbasat (1991), as well as technical support and involvement facilitation, which were adapted from the work of Ragu-Nathan et al. (2008). Measures of the five hindrance technostressors were adapted from prior work on technostress (e.g., Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2007) and reliability (Jiang et al. 2002). Low scores on the reliability scale indicated high unreliability.

To measure psychological responses, we adapted scales from the literature on stress found in psychology and organizational research. The positive psychological response was adapted from O’Sullivan’s (2011) scale on eustress, which has been demonstrated to be a reliable construct and a way to identify reliable positive emotional states related to vigor, meaningfulness, and hope. High scores on this scale indicate a positive psychological response. The negative psychological response was adapted from the Kessler 10 Psychological Distress Scale (K10) (Kessler et al. 2003). The K10 has been demonstrated to have a valid and reliable factor structure (Sunderland et al. 2012). The K10 was originally created by Kessler et al. (2002) as a self-report measure to yield “a global measure of distress based on questions about anxiety and depressive symptoms that a person has experienced in the most recent 4 week period” (Barbic et al. 2019, p. 5). In this sense, high scores on this scale indicate a highly negative psychological response. Job satisfaction was adopted from three items from the Hackman and Oldham (1980) scale. Turnover intention was measured using three items developed by Hom et al. (1984). Attrition was assessed using two items adapted from the work of Bluedorn (1982).

Based on previous research in related areas, we chose two control variables: negative affect and computer self-efficacy (CSE) (Compeau and Higgins 1995; Judge et al. 2001). Both of the control variables were adopted from previous scales. CSE was adapted from the ten items created by Compeau and Higgins (1995). Negative affect was adapted from the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS) (Watson et al. 1988). The PANAS items for negative affect comprise 10 single words that describe different feelings and emotions. Negative affect was used as a control variable for a negative psychological response, attrition, job satisfaction, and turnover intention. CSE was used as a control variable for the positive and negative psychological responses.

## Data Collection

We distributed an online survey to registered nurses employed in four hospitals in the United States. Nurses in each of the hospitals use HIT on a daily basis. The link to the online survey was distributed to nurses in three ways: (1) via an email listserv by a member of the hospitals’ nursing research council; (2) through an announcement published in the hospitals’ newsletters; and (3) through a flyer printed and distributed to several departments within each hospital. In total, 409 nurses completed the questionnaire. Seven of these responses were removed due to missing data. A total of 402 usable responses were included in the analysis. Approximately 88% of the participants were female and 12% were male. Such gender percentages are not inconsistent with those provided by the U.S. Department of Labor, which indicates that women comprise 92.1% of registered nurses. The average and median age for participants was 35 to 44 years old. Most respondents held a bachelor’s degree or above and had more than 15 years of experience in nursing. The demographic characteristics and other details of the sample can be found in Table 1.

## Analysis: Common Methods Bias

To assess common methods bias (CMB), we performed several tests (Burton-Jones 2009; Podsakoff et al. 2012). First, we conducted Harman’s single factor test to determine if the variance yielded a single latent factor. To do so, we included the latent variables and controls in our measurement model in the test. Our results indicate that the largest variance explained by a single factor was 24.19%, which is lower than the minimum of 25% (Ayyagari et al. 2011). Second, we performed a test using a common latent factor (Podsakoff et al. 2012). We first tested the measurement model without a common latent factor (Model A). Next, we included a common latent factor (Model B). Prior research related to technostress (e.g., Ayyagari et al. 2011) suggests that significant differences in CFIs can indicate the presence of CMB. Table 3 shows the results of this test. The difference in the CFIs from Model A (without the common latent factor) and Model B (with the common latent factor) is 0.02. The threshold for evidence of CMB is a difference in CFIs that is greater than 0.05 (Ayyagari et al. 2011). Thus, the results of this test indicated that CMB was not a serious validity threat. Third, we used procedural remedies during the survey design to help eliminate CMB. For example, when constructing the survey, we separated the independent variables and dependent variables so that they did not appear in linear order (Podsakoff et al. 2003). We also made sure that no ambiguous terms were present by distributing the questionnaire to one registered nurse and several Ph.D. students and faculty members for corrections prior to its administration.

## Analysis: Structural Model

We tested the structural model using structural equation modeling in IBM Amos. To do so, covariances of the nonindependent variables were removed, and the structural paths were added based on the hypotheses. The tests revealed a good fit with the data (Hair et al. 2014). Table 4 shows the goodness of fit indices for the measurement model and the structural model. The values in Table 4 are at or above or matching the proposed cutoffs of less than 2 for the chisquare/df ratio, 0.92 for CFI, 0.08 or less for SRMR, and 0.10 for RMSEA (Hair et al. 2014; Markus 2012). The results from the analysis of the structural model are shown in Figure 4 and Table 5.

Overall, several of our hypotheses were supported and significant at the p < .05 level. Our results help highlight the specific challenge and hindrance technostressors that impact positive and negative psychological responses. The results also reveal that these psychological responses can significantly impact job satisfaction and attrition, which, in turn, affects turnover intention. The total percentage of variances explained was 31.5% for a positive psychological response, 36.2% for a negative psychological response, 30.6% for job satisfaction, 44.7% for attrition, and 41.9% for turnover intention. Three hypothesized paths were not significant. These were H5, H6, and H10a. We summarize the results involving the control variables in Appendix A, and the overall results in Table 5 and Figure 4. As they show, the quantitative results largely support the qualitative inquiry of our mixed-methods design and our theorizing and hypothesizing efforts.

## Discussion of the Results

Usefulness (H1) was significantly and positively related to a positive psychological response $( \beta = . 3 0 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ ). This supports the argument that usefulness is indeed associated with users viewing technology as a means to enhance their work life. In fact, hospital nurses commonly report dissatisfaction with current software (Miliard 2014). In this sense, when nurses view the technology as conducive to completing one’s work tasks, they experience positive emotions. Involvement facilitation (H3), which has been commonly associated with reducing negative outcomes, was a significant predictor of a positive psychological response $( \beta = . 2 4 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ . This extends the previous literature on technostress, which argues that sharing IT knowledge can create a shared frame of reference among colleagues (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008), and may indicate that such mechanisms play a role in helping managers understand how to “savor eustress” in employees (Hargrove et al. 2013).

While technical support was significantly related to a positive psychological response (H2), it had the opposite significant effect than hypothesized $( \beta = . . 1 5 ; p < . 0 1 )$ . In other words,

In fact, after viewing the results of the quantitative analysis, we revisited the qualitative data and found instances of nurses saying that while they appreciated the help of the technical support staff, they viewed technical support staff as speaking a different “language.” This may be a reason why the presence of technical support can lower nurses’ positive psychological responses. Three of the hindrance technostressors were significantly related to a negative psychological response, as hypothesized. Unreliability (H4) had a significant relationship with a negative psychological response $( \beta = . 1 7 ; p < . 0 1 )$ Technoinsecurity (H7) had a highly significant positive relationship with a negative psychological response $( \beta = . 2 9 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ , and techno-overload (H8) was a significant predictor of a negative psychological response $( \beta = . 2 5 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ . Technocomplexity (H5) and techno-uncertainty (H6) were not significant predictors of a negative psychological response. In other words, the mechanisms underlying complexity and uncertainty were not related to negative emotional states. Complexity and uncertainty are fundamentally related to a chaotic work environment in terms of constant changes to computer software, hardware, and networks, and the complicated nature of technology. Contextually, nurses are used to working in chaotic and uncertain environments and thus can cope with such situations when they arise. As such, nurses may be resistant to experiencing negative emotional states when they are associated with complexity and uncertainty linked to technology as well.

The results revealed significant relationships between the psychological responses and outcome variables. A positive psychological response increased job satisfaction (H9a) (â = $1 8 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ but did not demonstrate a significant relationship with attrition (H10a). A negative psychological response decreased job satisfaction (H9b) $( \beta = . . 2 6 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ and had a significant positive relationship with attrition (H10b) (â = $1 2 ; p < . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate that the psychological indicators associated with a positive psychological response, while impacting a nurse’s satisfaction with his or her job, may not directly impact wanting to leave the field itself. Our study suggests that intention to leave the field, or attrition, is more likely influenced by negative psychological indicators. Moreover, job satisfaction had a significant negative relationship with attrition (H11) (â = -.61; p < .001) and turnover intention (H12a) (â = -.37; p < .001) and attrition had a significant positive relationship with turnover intention (H12b) (â = .34; $p < . 0 0 1 )$ . These findings underscore that how individuals experience the subprocesses of eustress and distress in the context of technology can indeed have a significant impact on how satisfied they are with their job, their likelihood of quitting, and their likelihood of leaving the field altogether (O’Sullivan 2011). Such perceptions are the result of how individuals appraise and psychologically respond to technostressors. In line with suggestions of prior researchers (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2016), in Appendices G and H, we provide details of the meta-inferences drawn collectively from our qualitative and quantitative studies, as well as additional explanation of our results. To ensure that our research model best captured the phenomenon of interest, in Appendix F, we compare the research model (Figure 3), which is termed Model 0, to three competing models. As the results in Appendix F highlight, Model 0 performs better than (or equal to) the competing models, suggesting that it provides a more suitable and holistic understanding of the bright and dark sides of technostress, especially in the context of healthcare IT.

## Contributions

It is well-acknowledged that few environments are more stressful for a workforce than hospitals. There is also agreement that nurses are fundamental to providing and maintaining high-quality patient care. The aim of this study was to take a first step toward recognizing the role that healthcare technology plays in inducing psychological states associated with technostress in nurses and to understand how such psychological states influence outcome variables that may significantly impact care providers and hospitals. Moreover, our study answers a call to identify challenge and hindrance technostressors and important indicators of the eustress and distress subprocesses in a technological context (Tarafdar et al. 2017), as well as variables that may accentuate positive psychological states, or “savor eustress” (Hargrove et al. 2013). Furthermore, the study answers the call to conduct more studies using a mixed-methods research design (Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016).

## Research Implications

The current study extends previous stress and technostress research in several ways. First, rather than assuming technostress and its associated outcomes are negative, the study builds a conceptual foundation for a holistic model of technostress that embraces the bright sides and dark sides of technostress. The holistic view of technostress departs from previous research in that it emphasizes the role of components embedded in two subprocesses of technostress—technoeustress and techno-distress—and underscores that technostress is a process that unfolds based on cognitive appraisal. Depending on the appraisal of the individual, the process unfolds in terms of challenge and hindrance technostressors, positive and negative psychological responses, and positive and negative outcomes, concepts that remain unclear in previous research. Moreover, while not investigated empirically, the decision process and the performance process, which have been overlooked in most technostress research, were conceptualized as important intrapersonal processes embedded in the holistic technostress process. IS researchers can use the holistic technostress model (Figure 2) to understand and investigate both the bright and dark sides of technostress, rather than only focusing on the (assumed) negative sources and outcomes, as previous research does. This view of technostress aligns with a more positive approach to organizational behavior and a positive view of psychological stress in organizations (Hargrove et al. 2013; Luthans 2002; Simmons and Nelson 2007), and answers a call by psychological stress researchers to emphasize “emotions, attitudes, and actions that lead to well-being, positive individuals, and thriving workplaces” (Simmons and Nelson 2007, p. 40). A holistic view of technostress also builds on the work of Chen and Karahanna (2018), who allude to the concepts of technoeustress and techno-distress by finding that the same technology, depending on how the interruptions are appraised by individuals, can have both beneficial and detrimental psychological effects.

Second, our study is a first attempt to theorize and operationalize challenge technostressors in IS research. Previous research has only narrowly defined challenge technostressors (e.g., Tarafdar et al. 2017), and has not provided much information about how IS variables may be recontextualized as challenge technostressors. Based on well-known psychological stress research, we conceptualized challenge technostressors in the context of organizational stress as technologyrelated stressors appraised by individuals as related to promoting task accomplishment. Using this overarching definition, we conducted a qualitative study with hospital nurses to understand how they articulated and identified challenge technostressors in the context of healthcare. In turn, we recontextualized three well-known IS constructs as challenge technostressors: usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation. These variables are by no means the only IS constructs that may be reframed as challenge technostressors, but are the ones unearthed from the qualitative study. These variables have not been articulated as challenge technostressors in IS research and have not been empirically investigated under the conceptual umbrella of techno-eustress, particularly in the context of HIT and nursing. Moreover, this paper is the first to empirically test the relationship between challenge and hindrance technostressors and their associated positive and negative psychological responses, as measured by positive and negative psychological states. The measures of the psychological responses for the study appear to be valid indicators of positive and negative emotional states. Thus, IS researchers can use and refine these measures, and draw on similar ones, to identify and investigate the impact of challenge and hindrance technostressors as well as their associated and relevant outcomes in future studies and additional contexts. This study also contributes to a recent call to theorize and investigate the impact of mixed emotions in IS research and technostress (see Stein et al. 2015).

Third, the present study takes a first step toward investigating technostress using a mixed-methods design through quantitative and qualitative inquiry (Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016). Many psychological stress researchers believe that stress itself is a rich, complex, meaning-centered relational process, and to fully understand it, researchers should look beyond conventional measures which “may be too blunt to capture this complexity” (Cooper et al. 2001, p. 219). Departing from these variable-based only approaches has been encouraged by organizational stress researchers for several years. In particular, they advocate for using “personbased methods” which “position the individual (rather than the variables) as the central focus of analysis” (Cooper et al. 2001, p. 213, emphasis added). Most stress and technostress researchers, however, do not deviate from positivist-based methods to understand organizational stress (Riedl 2013). Our qualitative inquiry specifically takes an incremental step toward identifying the ideographic experiences of the individual in regards to technostress. For example, in the qualitative study, we make the individual the central focus by interviewing individual nurses to understand how nurses appraise stress related to HealthTech, as articulated through their own language and ideographic experiences. Doing so, we argue, reveals some insight into the cognitive appraisal process, which is critical to the holistic technostress process. IS researchers can use this qualitative approach as a guide to investigate how the individual views technostress and, in turn, promote a more holistic and person-based understanding of technostress.

Finally, our empirical investigation helped to pinpoint the role technology plays in the nursing turnover problem. This is the first study to empirically identify challenge and hindrance technostressors relevant to nurses and, in doing so, aims to help researchers and healthcare administrators understand how HIT can be associated with beneficial and harmful feelings and effects. In particular, the study unearthed several contextually dependent insights about technology’s role in the turnover problem. For example, our analysis uncovered how technology may play a role in a concept called moral distress (Corley 1995; Epstein and Delgado 2010). Moral distress is said to occur in nurses when “they are unable to translate their moral choices into moral action” (Rushton 2006, p. 161). In our analysis, several nurses felt that they were morally committed to spending time talking with and comforting their patients and that HealthTech often created a barrier to completing this moral commitment. For example, nurses explained that because of the unreliability of HealthTech, they often left their patients’ bedsides to search for a working computer or to call a help desk, thereby compromising what they deemed valuable one-on-one time with patients. Therefore, it is likely that nurses might be appraising HIT, a technology supposedly designed for delivering better patient care, as morally distressing, and thus this warrants future attention.

## Managerial Implications

The present investigation offers several insights for managers and stakeholders of hospitals. First, this research identifies several technology-related stressors that are associated with positive and negative psychological states in nurses. For example, we found that usefulness and involvement facilitation are related to high levels of positive psychological states, while overload and insecurity are related to high levels of negative psychological states. Managers who recognize the variety of ways that technology-related stressors are linked to positive psychological states can craft strategies to influence how nurses and other care providers cognitively appraise HIT and uncover how HIT plays a role in accomplishing work-related tasks. Several nurses in our interviews articulated that giving nurses the ability to request changes to the features of HealthTech helped them feel involved in the process of updating the software. In such cases, nurses perceived the hospital as facilitating their involvement in designing technology, which the nurses discussed positively. Second, our research highlights that technical support, which is generally assumed to alleviate negative psychological states of mind, is associated with low positive psychological states. The nurses we interviewed explained that the technical support staff often used technology-centric jargon, which nurses were unfamiliar with, and in turn, they felt alienated and uncomfortable about requesting help from the technical support team, leading to feelings of frustration about HealthTech. Finally, while we found that technostressors can indeed be associated with positive psychological states, the results also emphasize the negative consequences of technostressors, such as low job satisfaction and high turnover intention and attrition. Thus, hospitals and hospital managers should strive to recognize when their workers are feeling distressed and develop strategies to alleviate such perceptions, potentially by reinforcing the usefulness of the technology in accomplishing work tasks and involving nurses when making decisions about using HIT to deliver effective and safe patient care.

## Limitations

Despite the contributions discussed above, our current study is not without limitations. First, our survey viewed HIT as a generalized aggregate of several technologies. In this sense, the perceptions of the nurses included in Phase 2 of the study could fluctuate depending on the specific type of technology in use. Future research should rely on a more detailed (and perhaps qualitative) inquiry into the appraisal process to understand the specific features of the technology that afford care providers the ability to appraise challenge and hindrance stressors positively and/or negatively. Secondly, our research was conducted in a hospital setting with nurses, and the findings may thus not apply well to other types of care providers or hospital employees such as doctors and pharmacists, or in private healthcare practices. Further, findings may only be relevant to the United States, and perhaps even to the specific region of the United States in which the survey was distributed, given that technology can be influenced by workplace culture and that cultures vary in different regions of the United States. Future research should consider understanding technostress from the perspective of multiple stakeholders in a healthcare and hospital setting from the perspective of doctors, pharmacists, managers, and other important stakeholders, as well as across other geographical areas, in order to gain a comprehensive and culturally relevant understanding of technostress in healthcare. Third, the study was based on cross-sectional data and therefore provides little information on how the results may change as the use of HIT in these hospitals becomes more routinized. In this sense, future research should be conducted in longitudinal research designs to understand how the stressors and psychological responses may change, depending on the length of exposure of the healthcare workers to (and use of) the technology. Finally, the study’s model focused on understanding the direct effects of the key constructs. We recognize that the interactions among many of the key stressors can have different effects on psychological responses. Given that this was one of the early empirical studies on this topic in this particular context, we believe that an important first step is to under stand (and confirm) which key variables play an important role in affecting psychological responses and job-related outcomes. Future research should focus on the role of moderators in this important phenomenon.

## Conclusion

This study attempts to establish a holistic view of technostress. That is, technostress is not always negative and therefore has bright sides and dark sides. The paper introduces (and tests) a holistic model of technostress and instantiates the model through a mixed-methods research design in the context of healthcare IT and nursing. The study identifies several challenge and hindrance technostressors that are associated with positive and negative psychological responses, respectively, and investigates how such responses are related to job satisfaction, attrition, and turnover intention. Overall, we hope that this work encourages IS researchers to reconsider the positive and negative role that technostress may play in organizations and that it prompts them to conduct further research on technostress, especially in healthcare settings.

## About the Authors

Christopher B. Califf is an assistant professor of Management Information Systems in the Department of Decision Sciences at Western Washington University. His research interests include technostress, healthcare IT, qualitative research, cloud computing, and IT in emerging economies. His research has been published in outlets including MIS Quarterly Executive, Journal of Information Technology, AIS Transactions on Human–Computer Interaction, and Computer Networks, as well as in several conference proceedings such as the International Conference on Information Systems, Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and the Americas Conference on Information Systems. Chris has also served as a guest editor for a special issue of Journal of the Association of Information Systems, and regularly reviews for several high-quality journals.

Saonee Sarker is the Senior Associate Dean and Rolls Royce Commonwealth Commerce Professor at the McIntire School of Commerce in the University of Virginia. Earlier, she was the Hubman Distinguished Professor of MIS and the Chair of the Department of Management, Information Systems, and Entrepreneurship at Washington State University and also served as the department’s Ph.D. coordinator. She is currently a visiting professor at Lund University, Sweden, and in the past has been a visiting professor at Aalto University, Finland, University of Augsburg and University of Mannheim, Germany, and IMT, Ghaziabad, India. Her research focuses on globally distributed software development teams, green IS and smart electricity, IT-enabled innovation, and healthcare IT, and more recently on AI and its implications in organizations. Her publications have appeared in outlets such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, Decision Sciences Journal, European Journal of Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, MIS Quarterly Executive, and Information and Management, among others. Her research has also been funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF). In the past, she has served as an associate editor at MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences Journal, and Communications of the AIS, and has received the Outstanding Associate Editor award at both MIS Quarterly and Decision Sciences Journal. She currently serves as a senior editor for MIS Quarterly.

Suprateek Sarker is Rolls-Royce Commonwealth Commerce Professor at the McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia. His research, which largely uses qualitative or mixedmethod approaches, has been published in many leading journals. He serves/has served on a number of editorial boards, including Information Systems Research and Information & Organization (current as a senior editor), Journal of MIS (member of the board of editors), MIS Quarterly and Decision Sciences Journal (former senior editor), and JITCAR and the Journal of the AIS (former editor-in-chief). He is a Senior Scholar and a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems He has been awarded an honorary doctorate by the University of Jyväskylä (2016), and by Copenhagen Business School (2020).

## Model 1 (No Positive or Negative Psychological Responses)

## Overview of Model 1

In Model 1 the positive and negative psychological responses are removed from consideration. In other words, the impact of the challenge and hindrance technostressors are all directly related to the outcomes.

## Results for Model 1

While this model may unearth several significant relationships, there are several reasons why Model 0, the Holistic Technostress Model is superior to Model 1.

• First, Model 1 is far from parsimonious. For example, there are 24 total relationships from the independent variables to the dependent variables.

• Second, the majority of the technostressors do not have significant direct relationships with the outcome variables, particularly the challenge stressors.

• Third, little is known about the impact on the psychological states of the individual viewing the stressor as associated with a positive or negative psychological response. For example, in Model 1, for the three challenge technostressors, only two are significantly related to one outcome variable (attrition). The rest do not have any effect on the outcome variables, with technical support not being significantly related to any outcome variable. In Model 0, usefulness, technical support, and involvement facilitation are directly related to a positive psychological response, which in turn increases job satisfaction and lowers attrition. In this sense, it is important to underscore how individuals view the stressor (that is, with a positive or negative psychological response), and in turn, understand the effect of the psychological response on outcome variables.

• Finally, Model 0 is more in line with the organizational stress theory and the holistic stress process (Hargrove et al. 2013; McGrath 1976). Model 0 also has more potential to aid practitioners in understanding how to impact the positive or negative psychological states of their employees and how the individuals psychologically respond to the stressors.

## Overview of Model 2

In Model 2, the positive psychological response variable is removed from consideration. Model 2 holds up to the goodness-of-fit standards that Model 0 does. However, each stressor in this model is assumed to be associated with a more negative psychological effect, and thus could be considered a “hindrance stressor” or “threat stressor” (Tarafdar et al. 2017). In this sense, the underlying assumption of this model is that the stressor variables are generally negative, much like the “dark side” of the technostress literature.

## Results of Model 2

The results show that the three challenge technostressors do not significantly affect the negative psychological response. The other results were similar to those in Model 0. For example, unreliability, techno-insecurity, and techno-overload were significantly related to the negative psychological response. Moreover, the negative psychological response was significantly related to the outcome variables (similar to Mode 0). In this sense, Model 0 uncovers the importance of the challenge stressors, while Model 2 does not.

## Model 3 (No Negative Psychological Response)

## Overview of Model 3

In Model 3, the negative psychological response variable was removed from consideration. Model 3 holds up to the goodness-of-fit standards that Model 0 does with an increase in the SRMR. However, each stressor in this model is assumed to be associated with a positive psychological response. In this sense, the underlying assumption of this model is that the stressor variables are generally positive, which deviates from the previous literature.

## Results

The results indicate the three challenge stressors are significantly related to the positive psychological response, which is significantly related to job satisfaction and attrition, as in Model 0. Moreover, it reveals that four of the hindrance stressors (unreliability, techno-uncertainty, techno-insecurity, and techno-overload) are not significantly related to the positive psychological response.

One interesting result that has emerged from this ad hoc analysis is the impact of techno-complexity on the positive psychological response. Techno-complexity was found to significantly lower one’s positive psychological response. This finding can help shed more light on the effect of the so-called “hindrance stressors” which, depending on the context, could have a negative impact on a positive psychological state, rather than only accentuating negative psychological states.
