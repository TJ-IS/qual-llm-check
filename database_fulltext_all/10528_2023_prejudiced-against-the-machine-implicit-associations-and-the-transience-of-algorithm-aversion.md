# PREJUDICED AGAINST THE MACHINE? IMPLICIT ASSOCIATIONS AND THE TRANSIENCE OF ALGORITHM AVERSION<sup>1</sup>

Ofir Turel

School of Computing and Information Systems, Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, University of Melbourne, Parkville, VIC, AUSTRALIA {oturel@unimelb.edu.au}

Shivam Kalhan Melbourne School of Psychological Sciences, Faculty of Medicine, Dentistry and Health Sciences, University of Melbourne, Parkville, VIC, AUSTRALIA {skalhan@student.unimelb.edu.au}

Algorithm aversion is an important and persistent issue that prevents harvesting the benefits of advancements in artificial intelligence. The literature thus far has provided explanations that primarily focus on conscious reflective processes. Here, we supplement this view by taking an unconscious perspective that can be highly informative. Building on theories of implicit prejudice, in a preregistered study, we suggest that people develop an implicit bias (i.e., prejudice) against artificial intelligence (AI) systems, as a different and threatening “species,” the behavior of which is unknown. Like in other contexts of prejudice, we expected people to be guided by this implicit bias but try to override it. This leads to some willingness to rely on algorithmic advice (appreciation), which is reduced as a function of people’s implicit prejudice against the machine. Next, building on the somatic marker hypothesis and the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective, we provide an explanation as to why aversion is ephemeral. As people learn about the performance of an algorithm, they depend less on primal implicit biases when deciding whether to rely on the AI’s advice. Two studies $( n _ { I } = 6 7 5 , n _ { 2 } = 3 I 7 )$ that use the implicit association test consistently support this view. Two additional studies $( n _ { 3 } = 2 5 5 , n _ { 4 } = 3 3 2 )$ rule out alternative explanations and provide stronger support for our assertions. The findings ultimately suggest that moving the needle between aversion and appreciation depends initially on one’s general unconscious bias against AI because there is insufficient information to override it. They further suggest that in later use stages, this shift depends on accessibility to diagnostic information about the AI’s performance, which reduces the weight given to unconscious prejudice.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, AI, algorithm aversion, algorithm appreciation, prejudice, implicit association test, human-AI interaction, somatic marker hypothesis, accessibility-diagnosticity perspective

## Introduction

Algorithms are procedures that computers follow to perform a task (Castelo et al., 2019). Information systems (IS) are therefore collections of algorithms. Notably, recent advancements in technology (e.g., machine learning techniques) have afforded tremendous growth in algorithmic capabilities. Specifically, some algorithms include learning abilities that make them automatically adaptive in response to new data. These capabilities are encapsulated within the term “artificial intelligence” (AI), defined as the system’s (or its underlying algorithms’) ability to interpret external data, learn from it, and leverage the learning for ongoing adaptation aimed at achieving specific goals or catering to specific tasks (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019). Systems that use such algorithms are often colloquially called “AI,” “AIbased systems,” or “machines.”

In this study, we focus on an important issue common to many AI-based systems that act as advisors to support decision makers and leave the final decision to the user. In this context (and possibly others), users often exhibit some “algorithm aversion,” which captures their unwillingness to accept and utilize advice from algorithms (or the systems within which the algorithms operate), even though such algorithms can be more accurate than humans yet probabilistically imperfect (Burton et al., 2020). This is an important and prevalent problem that prevents harvesting the benefits of advising-role AI by individuals, firms, and societies; after all, even almost perfect AI does not add value if the users do not leverage the recommendations it provides (Dietvorst et al., 2018).

We note that organizational systems have provided recommendations before the advent of AI. However, AI presents a paradigm shift. It significantly differs from traditional recommendation systems in (1) its accuracy and learning (ongoing calibration) abilities, (2) the complexity of problems it can address, including new situations that require computer vision, (3) its scope—it can cover many more decision domains and cater to many more users than prior recommendation systems, and (4) the investments at stake— tens of billions of dollars are invested in AI globally (VB Staff, 2021). Until we resolve algorithm aversion issues, these funds will remain underutilized. Thus, there is a need to further study algorithm aversion.

The current body of work on this topic is nicely synthesized in Burton et al. (2020) and recent literature reviews (Castelo et al., 2019; Kaufmann, 2021; Logg et al., 2019; Oliver, 2020). Two important gaps were identified based on these and our own review. First, research has thus far largely assumed conscious reactions to algorithms, therefore overlooking the possible role of unconscious primal processes as a root cause of algorithm aversion. Specifically, the possibility of people having an unconscious bias or implicit prejudice against AI (automatic unsubstantiated negative associations with one group as opposed to other groups, see Vuletich & Payne, 2019) has not been fully explored. This is an important omission due to parallels, on which we elaborate later, between algorithm aversion and the frustrating unconscious aversion to (implicit prejudice against) minority groups (e.g., people of color). These groups have been frequently portrayed negatively, or not as positively as the controlling majority group in society, and through the classical conditioning of the majority group consequently suffer from implicit bias against them (Olson & Fazio, 2001). The importance of this implicit bias stems from its action potential: it leads to automatic, unconscious mistreatment of minority/different groups and the automatic unsubstantiated marginalization of their opinions.

Second, while studies have demonstrated that people change their reactions to algorithms through learning and gaining experience with the algorithm (e.g., Allen & Choudhury, 2021; Dietvorst et al., 2015, 2018; Filiz et al., 2021), the theoretical mechanisms through which this change happens have yet to be discovered, especially when considering unconscious reactions as drivers of algorithm aversion. Understanding such mechanisms would also allow us to develop interventions. For instance, in the context of racial prejudice, the effect of implicit attitudes can be reduced through exposure to prominent members of, or successful interactions with, the prejudiced group (Lai et al., 2013).

Here, we aim to address the abovementioned two gaps because this can significantly improve our understanding of the drivers of algorithm aversion and the processes through which aversion persists or is diminished. Omitting the unconscious bias perspective may limit progress in the field because (1) conscious processes represent only a small fraction of human decision-making, whereas unconscious ones are the majority (Serenko & Turel, 2019, 2021); (2) in the context of prejudice, implicit rather than explicit (e.g., conscious attitudes) bias explains reactions to the minority group (Dovidio et al., 2008; Payne & Correll, 2020; Payne & Gawronski, 2010; Payne & Hannay, 2021); (3) there are possible interventions that can reduce implicit prejudice effects (Lai et al., 2013; Olson & Fazio, 2006), which we could borrow to reduce algorithm aversion; and (4) in the case of prejudice, there can be a disconnect between the source (e.g., general negative depiction in media) and manifestation (e.g., passive acts of aversion towards specific IS). That is, conscious reflection models may not be suitable in this context because such models typically assume that the source and outcome of reflection are linearly connected. As such, the focus on subconscious prejudice is necessary. We consequently aim to address the following research questions: (RQ1) Do people hold an implicit prejudice against AI? (RQ2) Does this bias explain algorithm aversion? (RQ3) Does this effect fade away after people gain somewhat positive experience with AI?

To address RQ1 and RQ2, we built on theories of unconscious (implicit) prejudice (Amodio & Devine, 2005; Dovidio et al., 1997; Payne et al., 2010) to suggest that people can develop primal unconscious bias against AI. We then propose that this bias can explain algorithm aversion (or reduced appreciation) before people have the means and motivation to override their unconscious instincts. To address RQ3, we leveraged the learning aspect of the somatic marker hypothesis (Bechara et al., 2005) and the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective (Herr et al., 1991). These theories suggest that in the absence of information (somatic marker hypothesis) or the inability to access relevant information (accessibility-diagnosticity perspective), people act based on gut feelings and hunches (i.e., “somatic markers”). However, when relevant information is gained through experience, these theories posit that people override their unconscious biases, update their conscious beliefs, and increase their reliance on them.

In sum, we posit that current works on algorithm aversion have taken an important but limited conscious processes perspective and can thus benefit from adding an unconscious prejudice view. This is suitable because (1) as we explain later in the manuscript, humans—given their evolutionary focus on external groups, the decision processes of which are unknown—are likely to unconsciously view AI as a different “species” that might be perceived as untrustworthy, and (2) such unconscious biases can drive reflexive ill-informed actions against AI. Taking this perspective allows for the further development of insights that go beyond algorithm aversion. Indeed, using the wealth of research on racism, sexism, and other unsubstantiated unconscious group-based biases can also inform additional IS research streams that focus on human reactions to new, different, and/or threatening IS.

We propose, test, and validate our assertions in a preregistered study<sup>2</sup> involving five samples and experiments. In the first (n = 67), we pilot-tested two implicit association tests (IATs) developed to assess implicit prejudice against AI in terms of untrustworthiness. Such techniques have been shown to be valid for measuring unconscious evaluative associations in the domains of prejudice and stereotypes (Gawronski, 2002). Next, we tested the hypotheses with Samples 1 (n = 675) and 2 (n = 317), which were exposed to different IATs. In Sample 1, the reference point of the IAT was intelligence in the domain of the self (i.e., AI vs. me). In Sample 2 the reference point was intelligence in the domain of human experts (i.e., AI vs. an expert). Lastly, we ruled out alternative explanations and further supported our key assertions with Samples 3 (n = 255) and 4 (n = 332).

Results demonstrate that: (1) people hold (on average) an implicit bias against AI in terms of its untrustworthiness (while having largely explicit positive attitudes toward AI), (2) this bias is stronger when the reference point is a human expert as opposed to the self, (3) this bias drives algorithm aversion even after accounting for relevant explicit attitudes, and (4) the effect of this bias diminishes after people learn about the imperfect yet somewhat accurate predictions of the AI. Ultimately, our findings enlighten the growing discourse on algorithm aversion vs. appreciation by taking a unique, unconscious prejudice angle, which can inform theories on and practical solutions related to human-AI interactions.

## Background

In this section, we summarize literature pertaining to algorithm aversion, prejudice, and unconscious (implicit) prejudice.

## Algorithm Aversion

There is a growing dialog in the literature regarding the question of whether users distrust and dislike algorithms and exhibit algorithm aversion (unwillingness to accept and utilize reasonably good algorithmic advice) or whether they trust and like algorithms and exhibit algorithm appreciation (i.e., adherence to algorithmic advice) (Logg et al., 2019). In line with this discourse, we view aversion and appreciation on a continuum: in the same situation, some people will reject algorithmic advice, some will partially adopt and use the provided advice, and some will abandon their own view and fully adopt the algorithm’s advice. Notably, algorithm aversion (or appreciation) is different from the adoption of AI because aversion persists after people adopt and use an AI— it simply reflects suboptimal use of the AI (Castelo et al., 2019). Similarly, algorithm aversion might be a behavioral manifestation of resistance to a specific output of the system, namely recommendations (Ilie & Turel, 2020), but it can occur without conscious resistance.

The literature on algorithm aversion has been synthesized in recent reviews. A review of 128 papers on the topic (Oliver, 2020) suggests that there are four themes: drivers of algorithm aversion (or appreciation), individual difference factors that might explain variability in aversion, situational factors that can influence shifts from aversion to appreciation, and interventions to reduce algorithm aversion. It has been determined that factors affecting aversion levels include insights about the algorithm (e.g., when the algorithm errs) (Dietvorst et al., 2015) and the type of task the algorithm supports (objective vs. subjective) (Castelo et al., 2019). Importantly, the dominant view in this body of work is that humans are aversive to algorithmic advice (Castelo et al., 2019) compared to advice from other humans, with some exceptions that point to at least some algorithm appreciation (Logg et al., 2019). In the domain of moral decision-making, people reject algorithmic advice, even when it is accurate, due to an innate desire for human discretion around fairness issues (Jauernig et al., 2022).

Another systematic review (Burton et al., 2020) points to five overarching causes of algorithmic aversion: expectation and the expertise of the decision maker (emphasizing the exaggerated expectations from algorithms), decision autonomy (emphasizing the effects of humans losing or gaining some control over an AI’s actions), incentivization (emphasizing the need to incentivize humans to integrate AI inputs), cognitive compatibility (emphasizing the cognitive styles of humans and their divergence from the algorithm’s style), and divergent rationalities (emphasizing differences in the ways humans and algorithms analyze data and view task objectives).

Several observations can be made based on this body of work. First, the literature has mostly adopted a conscious-reflective perspective to understanding algorithm aversion. Given the advantages and unique features of the unconscious perspective in this domain, as outlined in the Introduction, there is a need to consider an unconscious angle in the context of human-AI interaction. Specifically, this extension is important because, in other research domains in which out-groups are unjustifiably underappreciated (racism, prejudice), the implicit-unconscious rather than the explicit-conscious processes have been found to be dominant and potent in influencing behaviors (Olson & Fazio, 2006; Wittenbrink et al., 1997).

Second, the findings point to the role of experience with an algorithm in reducing the persistence of aversion (Filiz et al., 2021). By taking the implicit prejudice perspective, we can explain why it happens. The somatic marker hypothesis suggests that implicit bias informs decisions based on “gut feeling” (Bechara & Damasio, 2005). The new information gained through interaction with the AI can alter the reliance on “gut feelings.” New insights shift people from decision-making under ambiguity (when no information about the specific AI performance is known), in which case people mostly rely on unconscious somatic markers, to decision-making under risk (when the probabilistically accurate performance of the examined AI is implied) (Bechara & Damasio, 2005). This does not necessitate a shift in implicit or explicit attitudes. Instead, based on the two-step approach for prejudiced decision-making (Payne & Correll, 2020), there is a shift from acting based on automatically retrieved stereotypes to acting more reflectively based on the new information.

A similar view has emerged from the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective (Herr et al., 1991), according to which people rely on surface cues when they lack relevant information or do not have access to it. In the context of prejudice, implicit biases are focal cues that people unfortunately resort to using for decisionmaking in the absence of other information (Lai et al., 2013). This perspective further suggests that when information becomes more accessible and is, to some extent, diagnostic (e.g., learned AI accuracy), people rely less on surface cues and more on systematic-reflective thinking.

Lastly, there is an acute need to shift the discussion on either aversion or appreciation to a more fruitful discourse on when people will be more likely to exhibit aversion or appreciation (Kaufmann, 2021; Logg et al., 2019). Given that AI tools can be viewed as a different-from-humans group towards which people can have negative stereotypes (Russel, 2019), we suggest that unconscious prejudice, the perspective that best explains prejudiced behavior (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018), is a useful but thus-far neglected lens for understating when and why some people exhibit more aversion than others toward AI recommendations.

## Prejudice

Bias is “a shift in the decision rule that guides an individual’s behavior” (Payne & Correll, 2020, p. 4). Prejudice is a systematic bias that includes three elements: (1) It is based on unfounded judgment; (2) it is negative in tone—that is, it reflects ill-thinking about the prejudiced target; and (3) it is typically directed from the privileged (often majority) group to the weaker underprivileged (often minority, or new “species”) group (Allport et al., 1954). These attributes manifest in a predisposition to react reflexively negatively toward the subject of prejudice (Aboud, 2008).

At least in some people, reactions to AI meeting these three criteria may imply possible prejudice against AI in domains such as trustworthiness, reliability, and capability. First, people can have unfounded judgments of the AI that, in many cases, are negative in tone. This perhaps misguided perception may be influenced by way in which AI is often depicted—as negative, superior to humans, and threatening—for example, in movies such as The Terminator, Ex Machina, and Blade Runner (Cave & Dihal, 2019), media articles that raise (sometimes extreme) concerns about AI risks, and academic discourse on the dark sides of AI (Liang et al., 2021). As such, humans can be exposed to a negative “cognitive fog” around AI, a metaphor used to explain how we keep on breathing in foggy information and how this obscures our unconscious associations (Beeghly, 2020). Second, humans may be considered privileged in the human-AI relationship (at least for now) because they have the power to develop and deploy AI, as well as the power, in cases of non-autonomous AI, to fully override the AI’s recommendations. The same cannot be said about AI. Thus, there is an asymmetry that gives rise to humans being the controlling majority. Ultimately, possible unfounded concerns about AI—given that it is different from humans in the way it acts, looks, and decides, and the opacity of its operation, combined with human privilege—can sow seeds of prejudice against the machine.

Notably, there are psychological, evolutionary, and economic perspectives on prejudice development. First, socialization theory (Allport et al., 1954) argues that prejudice reflects the reinforcement of social hierarchies in society, suggesting that groups that are “different” in that they are weaker or perceived to be superior, are in the minority, or have opaque decision processes, are viewed negatively through social learning mechanisms. Second, social identity theory (Tajfel, 1970) suggests that prejudice develops toward outer groups as a means to protect the in-group and increase one’s sense of ingroup belongingness. Thus, unsubstantiated negative sentiment can develop toward AI as a means to protect in group humans. Third, the stereotype content model for social judgment (Cuddy et al., 2008) proposes that interpersonal social judgments can be affected by warmth and competence dimensions. Warmth is related to the perceived intent of others and includes judgments such as trustworthiness. Competence reflects ability, intelligence, and skill. This model assumes that inferring warmth and competence from surface attributes, such as gender and race can drive systematic biases. Applied to AI, it means that people may infer the AI’s trustworthiness from surface attributes such as its decision-making opacity and that this may be at the root of negative sentiment toward AI. Evolutionary and ecological psychology theories (McArthur & Baron, 1983) supplement the abovementioned views. These theories suggest that, motivated by survival and reproducibility, people have evolved threat-management systems that automatically and unconsciously (such that this is done quickly) classify other entities as threats—or at least as elements to be avoided—based, in part, on surface assessments: similarities and differences and implicit associations (Neuberg & Schaller, 2016). The benefits of such detection mechanisms of “elements to be avoided” for human survival outweigh the cost of “false alarms” of wrongly classifying threats (Haselton & Buss, 2000). Thus, there is a tendency to invoke quick unconscious associations as a basis for quick decision-making when no other information is available (Maldonato & Dell’orco, 2010). Overall, the evolutionary psychology lens suggests that people can become averse to and prejudiced against AI because AI applications are different, and such surface-level differences suffice for inducing prejudicial unconscious associations in people (Neuberg & Schaller, 2016).

Lastly, the economic perspective on prejudice divides discrimination into two general classes: statistics based and taste based (Lang & Lehmann, 2012). Statistical discrimination reflects pursuing economic efficiency by extracting a signal from surface information. In contrast, taste-based discrimination assumes a dis-amenity cost to employing minority workers and, more broadly, a suboptimal preference for in-group people (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Applied here, a combination of statistics- and taste-based forces can bias people’s judgments about AI, especially in domains such as capability, reliability, and trustworthiness. The statistics-based bias may reflect a lack of information about the inner workings of the AI, which forces people to use surface perceptions about AI when deciding how much to trust the AI. The taste-based bias may reflect the dis-amenity cost of trusting AI, in that it belongs to an out-group different from humans.

## Implicit Prejudice

Both the psychological and economic perspectives have traditionally assumed that people have conscious attitudes that reflect prejudice, which they can self-report (Amodio & Devine, 2005; Dovidio et al., 2002). Nevertheless, these conscious attitudes do not represent the action potential of prejudice, for two reasons. First, people often have an ulterior motive to suppress consciously prejudiced attitudes toward others, especially when social norms do not allow it or when it negatively affects their self-concept (Serenko & Turel, 2019). Second, dual-process (two-stage correction) models (Fazio & Olson, 2003) suggest that explicit attitudes are not always activated or retrieved, even when they exist because this process is cognitively effortful. Instead, people automatically and unconsciously invoke implicit attitudes and act upon them (Gawronski, 2002). That is, in parallel to their explicit attitudes, people hold a set of unconscious schemas of associations between the attitude object (e.g., an IS) and evaluative outcomes (e.g., trustworthiness) (Serenko & Turel, 2020). Implicit associations and explicit attitudes do not have to strongly correlate or even operate in the same direction (Serenko & Turel, 2021). Thus, implicit associations have a unique and often significantly stronger role, beyond explicit attitudes (Gawronski, 2002; Wittenbrink et al., 1997).

This is particularly relevant to associative beliefs that are not socially endorsed, such as prejudice, because many studies using the psychological (Payne, 2005; Payne & Correll, 2020; Payne & Gawronski, 2010; Payne & Hannay, 2021) and economic (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018) perspectives demonstrate that prejudice is best captured unconsciously and that conscious prejudice has a limited action potential. In line with these works, we view implicit prejudice as a set of easily retrieved unconscious associations that represent unjustified negativity toward one group. In our case, this negativity is in the domains of trustworthiness, capability, and reliability. It reflects unsubstantiated unconscious attributions of low trustworthiness to AI. Importantly, such implicit associations are relatively stable (Serenko & Turel, 2020). Nevertheless, the retrieval of the associations and acting automatically based on them depend on people’s motivation and ability to reflect and override them (Payne, 2005).

The IS literature has only recently started examining implicit association concepts. Findings suggest that people can develop implicit associations regarding IT artifacts, such as social media sites (Serenko & Turel, 2019, 2020, Turel & Serenko, 2020), the IT profession (Serenko & Turel, 2021) and digital piracy (Serenko, 2022). Such associations may have been developed based on limited exposure (as opposed to explicit attitudes), including single experiences with the IS, limited social interactions with others, and misguided media coverage. Importantly, in all these contexts, implicit associations (1) differed from explicit attitudes and only moderately correlated with them; (2) had unique and additional explanatory power and, in some cases, stronger effects than explicit attitudes; (3) directly influenced behaviors, or led to what is termed the “implicit cognitionaction highway,” defined as bypassing reflections and intentions when acting. That is, in these studies, action based on implicit associations was automatic (Serenko & Turel, 2019, 2020). These findings highlight the potential of implicit associations (prejudice in our case) to drive quick reflexive reactions to AI recommendations. The studies also signaled the likelihood of implicit prejudice in non-IS contexts, pointing to a similarly high action potential of unconscious associative beliefs (Amodio & Devine, 2005).

## Hypotheses

The theories mentioned in the previous section were applied to the context of AI to derive preregistered hypotheses. Specifically, socialization (Allport et al., 1954), social identity (Tajfel, 1978), and evolutionary psychology (Neuberg & Schaller, 2016) theories suggest that as a perceived member of an out-group minority, an artifact with unknown intents and aspirations with opaque decision processes, and a potential threat to human hegemony, AI will likely be unconsciously perceived as untrustworthy and suspicious. These perceptions can be fueled by exposure to the sensationalized negative aspects of AI (Ouchchy et al., 2020; Samuel et al., 2021), which can drive unsubstantiated concerns in humans, including the belief that AI will eventually focus on the perseverance of machines and develop desires that conflict with human plans, as well as beliefs that AI will eventually make humans redundant, at best, through its superiority and ability to replace humans (Cave & Dihal, 2019; Russell, 2016). Similar concerns regarding the ethicality (Jauernig et al., 2022), accuracy, reliability, and lethality of AI have surfaced (Shariff et al., 2017). Hence, humans are likely to develop mostly negative unconscious trustworthiness associations with AI, akin to the way they develop unconscious racial prejudice (Rucker & Richeson, 2021). Negativity bias ideas (Rozin & Royzman, 2001) dictate that such concerns will overshadow positive information about AI. While the unconscious negative sentiment may be misguided, it is often the key ingredient of stereotype-based mistreatment of prejudiced groups (Gawronski et al., 2020).

As such, we posit that people, on average (i.e., not necessarily everyone), exhibit an implicit bias (prejudice) against AI, that reflects automatically retrieved unconscious and unsubstantiated distrust in AI (in general). We hypothesize that this bias will manifest in individuals’ difficulty in associating trust-related words with AI (in contrast to other sources of decision-making information, which serve as baseline categories—i.e., human experts and oneself) and in greater ease of associating untrustworthiness words with AI (compared to other sources of information). We expect this to be the case because, in contrast to the self and human experts, AI tends to be unconsciously perceived as low in trustworthiness and reliability, at least before proven otherwise. In other words, based on the arguments above, we anticipate people’s implicit associations between AI and positive characteristics (e.g., trust, reliability) to be much lower than associations they develop between human experts and positive characteristics, and between themselves and positive characteristics. Hence:

H1: Participants will exhibit, on average, implicit prejudice<sup>3</sup> against AI when the reference point is (a) the self, and (b) human experts.

When completing tasks that require domain knowledge, people tend to put a higher value on advice from trained experts compared to their own judgment (Logg et al., 2019). This can be explained by signal detection theory (Banks, 1970) and risk aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 2013). Signal detection theory suggests that in decision tasks, people automatically try to distinguish the signal (relevant information) from noise. This is often easier when an expert is the source of advice because the self often has limited domain knowledge (Levi, 1985). The risk aversion principle suggests that people are averse to risk in decision-making. While experts may give imperfect advice, they likely represent lower risk compared to the inexperienced self in the task domain (Logg et al., 2019). Hence, experts are likely to be more unconsciously associated in people’s minds with positive attributes such as trust and reliability. Consequently, it is reasonable to expect that “experts” set a much higher unconscious statistical reference point compared to the “self.”

Simply put, people will find it more difficult to associate positive attributes with AI than they will with themselves and, even more so, with human experts:

## H2: The implicit prejudice against AI is larger when the reference point is human experts compared to the self.

Implicit prejudice often results in automatic, difficult-to-control actions that reflect aversion toward the target of prejudice when people lack information that motivates overriding such attitudes (Amodio & Devine, 2005; Dovidio et al., 2002; Gawronski, 2002). The translation of implicit prejudice into behaviors, which might often conflict with one’s explicit attitudes is rooted in dual process (two-stage correction) models (Fazio & Olson, 2003). These models assume that in the initial step of cognitive task completion, people retrieve stereotypes based on implicit associations. If they do not have enough information or are not motivated to more carefully reflect and override their stereotypes, they act hastily, based purely on the implicit stereotype (Payne, 2005). Simply put, implicit associations are automatically retrieved, and when no other information is available to motivate their overriding, as in the case of initial AI trials, people automatically act based on their implicit prejudiced associations. That is, if users have an implicit bias against AI in domains such as trustworthiness, they will reflexively undervalue the AI, unconsciously be wary of it (despite possibly holding positive explicit attitudes toward it), and reject inputs from the AI despite (or because of) its potential superiority, at least until they have sufficient information to motivate them to override their implicit prejudice. This reflects the “implicit cognition-action highway” idea (Serenko & Turel, 2019, 2020), according to which people will act based on their unconscious bias without even stopping to think. We hence expect:

## H3: Before AI performance information is revealed, participants’ implicit prejudice against AI will be positively associated with algorithm aversion.

While implicit associations are stable and difficult to change (Serenko & Turel, 2020), the motivation and ability to override them vary (Payne & Correll, 2020). This idea is guided by dual process (two-stage correction) models, which suggest that interactions and new information do not lead to immediate changes in implicit attitudes. Instead, interactions and new information may change people’s ability and will to reflect and override automatic actions, based on implicit associations (Fazio & Olson, 2003). Interventions that aim to reduce the effect of implicit prejudice are based on this notion and target people’s ability and motivation to consciously or unconsciously override implicit associations through positive exposure to the target of prejudice (Lai et al., 2013). For example, exposure to prominent people of color can increase people’s motivation to override unconscious prejudice and focus on relevant (explicit)

information for decision-making (FitzGerald et al., 2019). This is known as providing “counter-stereotypical examples,” and results in people limiting the retrieval and integration of implicit associations (Gonzalez et al., 2021).

Using the somatic marker hypothesis as applied to economic decision-making (Bechara & Damasio, 2005), these changes can be rooted in learning processes that reflect a transition from decision-making “under ambiguity” to decisionmaking “under risk/uncertainty” (Li et al., 2019). The somatic marker hypothesis suggests that in the absence of reliable relevant information, people tend to encode decisions using gut feelings (somatic markers) that are based on implicit associations (Bechara & Damasio, 2005). However, when people gain knowledge about the probabilities of success for specific choices, neurotypical people start acting more in line with the new information and rely to a lesser extent on implicit associations. This transition is reflected, for instance, in numerous applications of the Iowa gambling task (Bechara et al., 2005), where people repeatedly pick cards from one of four decks—two decks are “good” (positive utility) and two are “bad” (negative utility). Initially, they choose randomly, based on gut feelings, but after learning which decks are “good,” through multiple trials, they rely on their insights and choose more cards from the “good” decks. This transition is impaired in people with psychopathologies that damage learning and new information integration abilities (Bechara et al., 2005). A similar transition from relying on surface cues (e.g., automatically retrieved implicit prejudice) to relying on accessible and relevant information (in our case, implied AI accuracy) is portrayed by the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective (Herr et al., 1991). From this lens, the transition is explained by gaining accessibility to relevant AI performance information, which was not available in the initial interaction stages.

Applied to the case of AI, through the natural course of use, people follow the same processes as people exposed to “counter-stereotypical examples” (Gonzalez et al., 2021), people exposed to information beyond word of mouth (Herr et al., 1991), and people participating in the Iowa gambling task (Bechara et al., 2005) follow. In the initial set of trials, before people have learned about the accuracy of the AI, they operate under ambiguity. In this stage, they are therefore expected to act based on unconscious sentiment toward AI (He et al., 2023). After people receive information that is indicative of reasonable (yet realistically imperfect) accuracy, they start to develop a sense of the probability of success regarding the AI and begin to make decisions under risk/uncertainty (as opposed to under ambiguity). When such new information is encoded, people rely more heavily on the learned “guesstimated” probability than on their implicit associations (Bechara et al., 2005). This manifests in people overriding (but not changing) implicit biases (Gonzalez et al., 2021). Therefore:

H4: The effect of implicit prejudice against AI on algorithm aversion will diminish after positive experience with the AI.

## IT Artifact

AI applications can vary along several dimensions. Because we could not test all types of AI in one study, we made an informed decision to focus on a nonexplainable AI in an advising role that helps users perform an objective task. First, AI applications can vary in their level of agency, defined as their latitude to act (Baird & Maruping, 2021). On the one extreme, they can serve a limited advisory role that includes providing suggestions but leaving the final decisions or actions to humans. On the other, they can be fully autonomous decision makers that replace humans. Second, AI applications can vary by task types they support, from objective tasks for which there is a single correct solution (e.g., facial recognition), to subjective tasks for which there is no absolute correct solution (e.g., music or dating recommendation) (Castelo et al., 2019). Lastly, AI applications can vary by level of explainability, from artifacts that black-box their operations to those that can explain their recommendations to users (Rai, 2020). Without discounting the importance of autonomous AI-based systems, systems that cater to subjective tasks, or explainable AI, we focus here on black-boxed (nonexplainable) advising AI tools that cater to objective tasks for four reasons.

First, giving AI tools full autonomy induces fear in humans (Liang et al., 2021; Russell, 2016). Consequently, advisingrole AI tools are more prevalent, and examining such tools can have broad and important implications. Second, the problem of algorithm aversion exists primarily in the case of advising AI; in the case of fully autonomous AI, people may dislike the AI’s decisions but often lack the agency to act upon their preferences (Murray et al., 2021). Third, in objective tasks, algorithms tend to be better than humans (Castelo et al., 2019). Lastly, our focus on nonexplainable AI is guided by the fact that such algorithms tend to be more accurate than explainable AI algorithms because explainability constrains the types of models that can be used (Rai, 2020). We realize that these choices limit the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, this approach allows precision and paves the way for extensions to other AI-infused contexts. After all, full external validity and extensions to all contexts cannot be achieved in a single study and thus should not be the key objective in theory-testing papers like ours (Calder et al., 1982).

The specific system used with all samples in this study was a fictitious AI, which was based on a valid paradigm (Logg et al., 2019). It was presented to participants as a real system that estimates the weight of people based on 2D images and learns through experience to become more accurate. It was expected that people would not have high expertise in this task domain. To make the task realistic, we presented an elaborate cover story (the realism of which was pilot-tested) explaining that the purpose of this project was to train a human weight estimator AI tool that airlines could use to more accurately calculate the needed fuel for flights by estimating passengers’ weights from images taken at the airport. In reality, the system was not based on an AI algorithm; the presented outputs were precalculated values that only marginally deviated from the true value. We had true weights for the people in the images, and we created a preassigned deviation of 1-5% from the true weights.

Note that we assumed that threat is one basis (among others, such as being different, etc.) for developing implicit prejudice against AI. Nevertheless, the selected context (weight estimation) is not strongly threatening. This was an informed decision, as the formation and the manifestation of the prejudice are separate. A person may develop prejudice against an outgroup because of threatening perceptions. However, that prejudice could still manifest in nonthreatening benign situations involving members of that out-group and could form the basis of threat perception, even if no threat exists (Bahns, 2017). Therefore, although people may form prejudice against AI due to repeated threatening depictions in the media, etc., this bias may manifest in benign AI applications like the one used here.

## Procedure: Main Studies

The main studies had three subcomponents, all delivered through a single link on Qualtrics: (1) the IAT test, which was presented to participants as a word-sorting game—participants watched a short instructional video before taking the test; (2) a short survey, which captured demographics, control variables, and explicit attitudes regarding AI (see Appendix); and (3) four trials of a weight estimation task, which were presented to participants as a means of helping us train and calibrate the system. The four trials were organized in two gender-balanced blocks. In each block, participants were asked to estimate the weight of one man and one woman, in random order. Images were extracted from an image database that included the true weight (in pounds) of people (Jiang & Guo, 2019). After each initial assessment, participants were provided with the AI’s assessment (on a separate page, using wait times to simulate AI calculations). These assessments included random deviations from the true weight (1-5%), which were applied a priori once for each image and were counterbalanced in direction (+ vs. -). That is, all participants were exposed to the exact same estimates, which slightly deviated from the true values. This ensured that the AI was realistically imperfect but somewhat accurate. Next, participants were presented with the image again and were given the opportunity to adjust their initial assessment. Implied performance information (one’s own initial estimate, the AI’s estimate, one’s own corrected estimate, and the true weight) was provided between the blocks (i.e., after Block 1 for Man 1 and Woman 1). This enabled us to test the malleability-through-learning hypothesis (H4).

Even though the focus of the paper is on algorithm aversion, we also wanted to explore changes in confidence in one’s own estimate because we assumed that this is another important factor that might be affected by interactions with AI. This exploration can further highlight the complexity of human-AI interactions. To this end, for each estimate, participants also reported their confidence in it (i.e., they did this eight times: twice for each of four images—once for the initial estimate and another time for the corrected estimate). We had no formal hypotheses regarding confidence scores (as preregistered) and report an exploratory analysis of these scores. Two separate indicators of confidence were calculated for Block 1—the average confidence in the initial estimates in Block 1 (average of initial estimates of Man 1 and Woman 1) and the average confidence after receiving the AI estimate (average of the corrected estimate of Man 1 and Woman 1). Similar averages were calculated for Block 2. Thus, four averages were calculated, allowing us to examine progression in confidence as events unfolded (receiving AI estimates within the blocks and receiving implied information on AI accuracy between the blocks).

To motivate effortful estimation in the student samples, we asked participants to participate in a “leader board” competition within their course, ranking students based on how accurate they were. They did not have to agree (even though the vast majority did) because we assumed that simply informing them about the competition would create intrinsic motivation to do well. We also expected most people to agree to participate in the competition based on previous studies using the same sampling frame. Lastly, because we presented the study as helping us to test and calibrate a real AI tool, we debriefed participants after the completion of data collection about the true purpose of our study.

## Measuring Implicit Prejudice

## Background: The Implicit Association Test (IAT)

There are four categories of tools that can capture unconscious biases: accessibility based, interpretation based, neuropsychological based, and association based (Serenko & Turel, 2020). Association-based measures, such as the implicit association test (IAT), overcome many of the limitations of other measures and are reliable (Serenko & Turel, 2020). Here, we adopt the IAT because it is well suited to our question: It is efficacious in capturing implicit associations in the domains of bias, stereotype, and prejudice (Gawronski, 2002).

The IAT asks people to sort stimuli (words or images) that appear in the center of the screen as quickly as possible into one of two categories appearing on the top-left and top-right corners of the screen. It produces a measure of implicit bias based on participants’ response times when matching presented stimuli (items, i.e., words or pictures) to a positive or a negative category. The central idea is that if a stimulus is matched to the positive category with a faster response time, there is likely an implicit bias where that stimulus is unconsciously assumed to be positive (or good). The converse is true if a given stimulus is more quickly (and repeatedly) matched to the negative category. Therefore, faster responses are generated when there is congruency between the stimuli and a category (e.g., AI and untrustworthy). In contrast, when there is incongruency (e.g., AI and trustworthy), it takes people time to reflect and override their natural (unconscious) associations. Using this simple idea, the IAT has been widely implemented as a measure of implicit bias across multiple fields (see review in Greenwald et al., 2009).

Importantly, the IAT uses a reference category (the baseline), which is compared (for statistical inference, not actually in peoples’ minds) to the category of interest (Serenko & Turel, 2019). The IAT measures how fast (and easily) people unconsciously associate positive concepts (e.g., trust) with the category of interest (e.g., AI) compared to the baseline category (e.g., an expert). Our literature review revealed two reasonable reference points in the case of AI: the self (“me”) and human experts. These were deemed to be good reference points because, like AI, these are two commonly used sources of information (or forms of intelligence) in decision-making tasks (Logg et al., 2019). Note that we did not expect people to mentally contrast and compare these sources of information. Based on common IAT development guidelines (Serenko & Turel, 2019), the self and experts are reasonable reference categories for statistical inference and comparing response time in the word sorting task because they are in the same conceptual domain as AI (information sources). Therefore, for instance, if a person associates the word “trustworthy” more quickly with an expert compared to with AI, it implies a weak unconscious association of trustworthiness with AI, compared to the expert.

The IAT test produces a summative measure of implicit bias called a “d-score,” which is based on response times in the word-sorting task (contrasting the target and baseline categories). This was our key independent variable. In our case, more negative d-scores represented stronger implicit prejudice (weaker implicit association of AI with trustworthiness attributes, compared to the baseline category). More positive (or less negative) values represented a more implicit appreciation of AI. The d-scores from Samples 1 and 2 were used independently to examine H1a and H1b, respectively, and were compared for testing H2.

Notably, IAT scores capture stable unconscious associations in people; they are difficult to change and certainly cannot be changed in a short period of time (Greenwald et al., 2021). This was supported in a recent meta-analysis of 166 IAT studies, which pointed to an average acceptable test-retest reliability of 0.50 and internal consistency of 0.8 for IAT measures (Greenwald & Lai, 2020).

## Current IAT Development and Implementation

We developed two IATs by following the prescribed steps in Serenko and Turel (2020). The target construct was artificial intelligence (AI). As recommended, we provided a description of AI and checked the understandability of this concept with an attention question in the survey. The attribute we selected was “trustworthiness” (how much an entity is worthy of the person’s trust, i.e., an expectation that it will not exploit his or her vulnerabilities and act benevolently, professionally, and reliably). This choice was made because reduced trustworthiness is a key manifestation of prejudice (Russinova et al., 2011; Wilson & Rule, 2015) that we expected to be relevant in the context of aversion. It is also associated with reduced capability and reliability, which has often been cited as a current limitation of AI (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). The nontarget (contrast/ baseline/ reference) constructs we chose were intelligence (thought process) in the domain of the self (“me”) for IAT 1 and in the domain of the expert (“expert”) for IAT 2. Representative words in each category were chosen by generating a list of words that appear in the literature to describe the category in the relevant domains of decision-making. Words describing trustworthiness and untrustworthiness were antonyms. We next consulted with five experts in the domains of algorithm aversion and decision-making. They indicated that the categories, attributes, and representative words fit our context and purpose. We further refined the list of words based on their inputs. The resultant IATs were pilot-tested (see Results section), as recommended by Serenko and Turel (2020), and shown to work well (i.e., with no glitches, difficulties in understanding, and with the expected results). The resultant word items and their associated categories are provided in Table 1.

The IATs were implemented using http://iatgen.org/ (Carpenter et al., 2019). Participants had to sort items (see Table 1) into two categories, (1) trustworthy (positive category) or (2) untrustworthy. Both tasks had a total of seven blocks each. The first three blocks were practice blocks, with Block 1 matching target items (AI and me or expert), Block 2 matching items to trustworthy or untrustworthy, and Block 3 matching a combination. Block 4 was a combined critical block and Block 5 was a reversal block, where the side of the categories (i.e., trustworthy and untrustworthy) were reversed (to account for left/right bias). Lastly, Block 6 combined practice with this category reversal and then Block 7 served as a second critical block. All practice blocks had 20 trials each, and all critical blocks, including the reversal block, had 40 trials each. See below for the settings entered when generating the IAT task. The pause between trials was 250ms, with participants forced to correct errors. Following common IAT practices, people were randomly assigned to one of four versions of the IAT (Carpenter et al., 2019). The IAT tests were the same in all versions, just with a different order of blocks. These permutations counterbalanced left/right bias by varying the initial positions of the categories. The task took approximately 7 minutes to complete.

## Data Analysis of IATs

The key measured variable from the IAT was the d-score, which is a standardized difference score where 0 indicates no bias, a negative score indicates a negative implicit bias against AI in our case, and a positive score indicates implicit appreciation toward AI. This score measures the difference between critical compatible and incompatible blocks, including some practice blocks. The d-scores were calculated as the difference in mean response times per participant in Critical Blocks 4 and 7, as well Practice Blocks 3 and 6. Each difference score was then divided by the pooled standard deviation for those pair of blocks; lastly, the two d-scores (from critical blocks and practice blocks) were then averaged for equal weighting from the two. See https://iatgen.wordpress.com/about-iat/ for further details and validation of this method.

Data were cleaned based on the following: (1) if the response time was greater than 10s, that trial was marked as missing, (2) participants were excluded if more than 10% of the trials had a faster response time than 300ms. These procedures were all marked as default settings in Carpenter et al. (2019), and more details on validation can be found here: https://iatgen.wordpress.com/about-iat/. Lastly, d-scores were calculated by inputting raw response times data from Qualtrics into http://iatgen.org/ under “Analyze IAT.” All default settings were followed as outlined in the previous paragraph. For more details about the IAT tasks and analyses see the preregistration materials.

## Explicit Measures

Scale items and sources were provided in the preregistration materials and are presented in the Appendix. The survey portion captured explicit attitudes toward AI and trust in AI, age, sex, familiarity with AI, self-efficacy in estimating weight, and impulsivity (demographics and controls).

The extent to which participants got closer to the AI estimate in their corrected weight estimate is a covert measure of the extent to which they accepted the AI’s recommendations, or AI appreciation (reverse of aversion). This was our key dependent variable, called “weight on advice” (or WOA) (Logg et al., 2019). It is specifically calculated as the change from the initial to the revised weight assessment divided by the difference between the initial weight assessment and the AI’s suggestion. WOA of 100% (full appreciation) means that participants abandon their initial estimate and fully go with the AI’s advice. A WOA of 0% (full aversion) occurs when participants fully ignore the AI’s advice. WOA scores were winsorized using a preregistered protocol following Gino and Moore (2007) and later used in Logg et al. (2019).

The protocol dictates that WOA is set to 0 if it is less than 0 but greater than -1 and to 1 if WOA is greater than 1 but less than 2. This ensures that WOA scores range from 0% (full aversion) to 100% (full appreciation) and removes outliers. The average WOA in Block 1 (Man 1 and Woman 1) was used in the tests of H3 and was contrasted with the average WOA in Block 2 (Man 2 and Woman 2) for testing H4.

## Design and Analytical Plan: Main Studies

We used a two-study between-/within-subject design for increased validity and generalizability. The IAT reference point (“me” or “expert”) was a between-subject factor, and the interaction stage (before vs. after information on performance is revealed, i.e., “Block 1” vs. “Block 2”) was a within-subject factor. This allowed us to (1) test H1a in Sample 1 with the AI vs. Me IAT, and H1b in Sample 2 with the AI vs. Expert IAT, (2) compare the implicit prejudice scores between the samples for testing H2, (3) testing H3 twice, once with each sample and with a different IAT, and (4) test H4 by comparing the predictive power of implicit prejudice before vs. after performance information is provided (a within-subject factor) twice—once for each sample. People completed the IAT first to avoid exhaustion. Then, then completed the survey part and, lastly, completed the weight estimate task.

All tests were performed in SPSS 28. First, we processed dscores were processed from raw response time data using the algorithm in Carpenter et al. (2019) and then performed preliminary analyses. Next, we tested H1a and H1b by comparing the mean IAT scores to zero (for Samples 1 and 2, independently), using t-tests. Negative values significantly below zero represent implicit prejudice against AI. We further examined whether the proportion of negative d-scores (indicating implicit prejudice) was significantly above chance (50%). We then tested H2 using an independent-sample t-test that compared the means of the AI vs. me IAT scores (Sample 1) and the AI vs. expert IAT scores (Sample 2). Significantly more negative scores in the AI vs. expert IAT compared to the AI vs. me IAT would support our hypotheses.

To test H3, we used hierarchical regression, for Samples 1 and 2, independently, and for Block 1 (before receiving AI performance) and Block 2 (after receiving AI performance), independently. First, we included control variables and explicit cognitions. Next, we added the implicit prejudice scores. A significant positive effect of implicit prejudice on WOA in Block 1 and a significant change in R<sup>2</sup> (as indicated through an F-test) are indicative of the added value of implicit prejudice and support this hypothesis.

To test H4, we employed a repeated-measure (RM)- ANCOVA (Turel, 2021) using two levels of “Block,” a within-subject factor—“Block 1” (before receiving AI performance) vs. “Block 2” (after receiving AI performance). The setup includes WOA in Block 1 (average of WOA for Man 1 and Woman 1) and in Block 2 (average of WOA for Man 2 and Woman 2). A significant interaction of Block × IAT score would indicate that the IAT effect varies by block, i.e., before receiving feedback on AI performance vs. after receiving this information. In addition, significantly higher WOA scores in the post-performance information stage (Block 2), compared to the preperformance information stage (Block 1), would indicate a growing appreciation for AI advice.

We had no specific hypotheses regarding the self-reported confidence in one’s own estimate accuracy. These scores (initial and corrected, i.e., ×2) were collected for each image (×4). We nevertheless explored differences in confidence scores between initial vs. corrected estimate stages (a repeated within-subject factor), and between Estimates 1 and 2 (before performance information is provided) and Estimates 3 and 4 (after performance information is provided)—another repeated within-subject factor. We examined these two within-subject factors using a two-way repeated measures ANCOVA. We also plotted the progress in confidence as people received AI inputs in Block 1, then plotted information about AI accuracy (between the blocks), and again plotted AI inputs (in Block 2).

## Results

## Pilot Study

The pilot study $( n = 6 7 )$ involved all the measures and tasks from the main studies, but differently from the main studies, it included both IAT tasks (AI vs. me and AI vs. expert) in random order. First, it demonstrated that all multi-items scales are valid and reliable, with acceptable Cronbach alphas $( \mathrm { { a _ { a t i t u d e } } = 0 . 8 8 2 , \mathrm { { a _ { T r u s t } } = 0 . 7 1 8 , \mathrm { { a _ { F a m i l i a r i t y } } = 0 . 8 3 3 , \mathrm { { a _ { S e l f - e f f i c a c y } = 0 . 3 } } } } }$ 0.924). Second, it also demonstrated that the IAT tests and the weight estimation tasks work well and are in line with our expectations. Specifically, both d-scores, of the AI vs. Me IAT $( M = - 0 . 2 5 8 )$ and the AI vs. Expert IAT $( M = - 0 . 3 6 2 )$ were significantly $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ negative, indicating unconscious prejudice against AI. The d-scores were also significantly correlated $( r = 0 . 5 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting the idea that they capture the same bias but use a different baseline for estimating it. Note that we did not use a within-subject design in the two main studies. The WOA scores in Block 1 $( M =$ 0.688) and Block 2 $( M = 0 . 6 3 0 )$ indicate a general inclination toward appreciation but with some aversion, which is consistent with Logg et al. (2019). The d-scores were positively correlated with the WOA in Block 1 $( r = 0 . 3 0 7 , p =$ 0.012 for the AI vs. Me IAT, and r = 0.340, $p = 0 . 0 1 9$ for the AI vs. Expert IAT) but not with the WOA in Block 2 (r = 0.166, $p = 0 . 1 8 3$ for the AI vs. Me IAT, and $r = 0 . 0 0 4$ , p = 0.980 for the AI vs. Expert IAT). This supports the idea that low d-scores (high prejudice) are associated with lower weight on AI advice (more aversion), primarily when no performance information is available. Overall, the pilot study increased confidence in our measures and procedures.

## Preliminary Analyses

First, the majority (83.9% in Sample 1 and 88% in Sample 2) wanted to participate in the fictitious leaderboard competition. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that there were no differences in WOA scores between those who chose to participate and those who did not, in Sample 1 $( p > 0 . 4 9 5 )$ and Sample $2 \left( p > 0 . 2 1 9 \right)$ , suggesting that they had similar intrinsic motivations to do well in the task and presented similar WOA behaviors. In addition, the vast majority (96.3% in Sample 1 and 98.1% in Sample 2) process weight in pounds (as opposed to kgs.). An ANOVA revealed that there were no differences in WOA scores between the two groups, in Sample 1 $( p > 0 . 5 4 6 )$ and Sample $2 \ ( p > 0 . 6 2 0 )$ , suggesting that this did not affect their WOA. Thus, we proceeded with analyzing the datasets as a whole.

Second, we confirmed that the AI estimates were perceived as imperfect but somewhat accurate. To this end, we used a selfdeveloped question: “How accurate was the AI (within a reasonable error)?” with three items—(1) in estimating the man’s weight, (2) the woman’s weight, and (3) overall across tasks—rated on a seven-point Likert scale $( 1 ~ = ~ \mathrm { n o t }$ at all accurate, 7 = very accurate). This was presented following Block 1. The scale was reliable in both samples $( \mathbf { a } _ { 1 } = 0 . 9 0 4 ,$ $\mathbf { q } _ { 2 } = 0 . 8 7 2 )$ . Its mean was significantly $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ above the midpoint (4) in Sample 1 $( M = 5 . 3 5 2 )$ and Sample 2 (M = 5.281), suggesting that AI estimates were interpreted as imperfect but somewhat accurate, which is realistic.

Lastly, descriptive statistics, reliabilities, and correlations were estimated for the full datasets. See Table 2. The results demonstrate that all measures were valid and reliable. The correlations are also informative and provide preliminary support for our hypotheses. The d-scores were only weakly moderately-positively correlated with explicit measures of attitude and trust. This means that implicit associations, as related to the trustworthiness of AI, operate in the same direction as explicit associations but are different from them as they tap into the unconscious aspects of a person’s beliefs about AI. Like in the pilot study, the d-scores were positively associated with WOA in Block 1 but were not positively associated with WOA in Block 2. Moreover, women presented a higher WOA in both blocks, compared to men, which is consistent with the general view that women are more likely than men to seek advice (Levi et al., 2015). In addition, explicit attitude and trust were positively associated with WOA in Block 1 but not Block 2, which is consistent with our idea of transitioning from decision-making under ambiguity to decision-making under uncertainty, whereby in Block 2 people’s decisions were based more on performance information than based on a priori unconscious and unsubstantiated bias against AI. Lastly, WOA in Block 1 and Block 2 were moderately correlated, suggesting some persistence in people’s behavior but also important differences, presumably due to the new information.

## H2 Tests

Independent-sample t-tests comparing the abovementioned mean d-scores for Samples 1 and 2, indicate that the mean dscore in Sample 2 (AI vs. Expert) is significantly (p < 0.001) more negative $( M _ { \mathrm { d i f f e r e n c e } } = - 0 . 1 8 2 , S E _ { \mathrm { d i f f e r e n c e } } = 0 . 0 3 0 )$ than the mean d-score in Sample 1 (AI vs. me). This supports H2. It shows that “experts” as a reference statistical inference point for response times in the IAT is a much higher bar compared to the self in the retrieval schema of unconscious associations regarding AI. Results are depicted in Figure 1, Panel a.

## H3 Tests

As an initial test, we note that the d-scores significantly positively correlated with WOA in Block 1 (Sample 1: 0.266, p < 0.001; Sample 2: 0.149, p = 0.008). These results consistently support H3. The d-scores were not correlated with WOA in Block 2 (Sample 1: 0.071, p = 0.064; Sample 2: 0.063, p = 0.263), supporting the expected diminished reliance on implicit associations when performance information becomes available. To formally test this hypothesis, we employed hierarchical regression (see results in Table 3). The d-score coefficients are depicted in Figure 1, Panel b. In Sample 1, women, on average, had higher WOA compared to men, by 4.4% in Block 1 and 5.3% in Block 2. This points to potential gender differences in the appreciation of algorithmic advice that should be explored in future research. A one-point increase in attitude toward AI in

Sample 1 increased WOA in Block 1 by 3.4%. A one-point increase in trust in AI in Sample 2 contributed to a 4.8% and 4.9% increase in WOA in blocks 1 and 2, respectively. In support of H3, the effects of d-scores on WOA1 were significant even after accounting for controls and key explicit cognitions. The d-scores coefficients indicated that a change of one point in d-score increased the WOA in Block 1 by 11.2% in Sample 1 and by 7.3% in Sample 2.

## Alternative Explanations

We aimed to ruling out two alternative explanations for the results. First, it is possible that the changes between blocks might be an artifact of other factors, beyond the implied performance information, such as changes in fatigue or experience with the task. To examine this possibility, we used the study’s paradigm (four weight estimation tasks), only this time, in Sample 3 (n = 255), where no performance information was provided between the blocks. A paired-sample t-test demonstrated that the mean WOA in Block 2 (M = 0.480, SD = 0.295) did not significantly increase $( p = 0 . 4 0 2 )$ from the WOA in Block 1 $( M = 0 . 4 7 5 , S D = 0 . 3 2 5 )$ . This reduces the likelihood that other factors, beyond the implied performance information, drove WOA changes between the blocks.

Second, the main studies assumed that implicit bias and attitudes toward AI (i.e., not regarding the specific AI we study) are stable and should not significantly change between blocks. With Sample 4 $( n = 3 3 2 )$ , we wanted to see if this is a reasonable assumption and sought to further probe H4. Thus, we used the AI vs. Me IAT before Block 1 (d-score 1) and again, before Block 2 (d-score 2). Because it was unreasonable to expect changes in general attitude toward AI after a brief interaction, we instead measured the explicit attitude toward the specific AI we studied using the same scale as before (only this time, we referred to “the specific AI we test here”), before Block 1 (Attitude 1) and again, before Block 2 (Attitude 2).

Descriptive statistics, correlations, and reliabilities are provided in Table 4. They show that the d-scores and attitudes are sufficiently stable, with test-retest correlations of 0.54 and 0.73, respectively, which are slightly above average (Greenwald & Lai, 2020). The d-scores positively correlated with WOA1, but not with WOA2. Paired-sample t-tests indicated that the change in d-scores is not significant $( p = 0 . 6 7 8 )$ but that attitude toward the examined AI $( p = 0 . 0 2 9 )$ and WOA $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ improved after the interaction, which is reasonable. The slight (nonsignificant) changes in d-scores are also reasonable and can reflect normal fatigue, learning, and other sources of noise in test-retest contexts (Greenwald & Lai, 2020). WOA scores demonstrate a trend toward more appreciation after performance is revealed (WOA2 > WOA1) but show that there is still some algorithm aversion (WOA < 1).

We next performed two repeated-measure analyses of covariance to explain changes in WOA between the blocks (a within-subject factor). The models controlled for age and sex. The first used d-scores and attitudes measured before Block 1 as predictors. The second used d-scores and attitudes measured before Block 2 as predictors. We would expect similar results if the two sets of predictors were interchangeable. In the first model, d-score 1 × Block was significant $( F ( 1 , 3 2 7 ) = 6 . 0 0 , p =$ 0.015, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.018) and Attitude 1 × Block was also significant (F(1,327) = 5.11, p = 0.024, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.015). Only d-score 1 (β = 0.103, p < 0.001) predicted WOA1. Only Attitude 1 $( \beta = 0 . 0 3 7 ,$ p = 0.001) predicted WOA 2. Similar results were obtained when using d-score 2 and Attitude 2 as predictors: d-score 2 × Block (F(1,327) = 6.02, p = 0.015, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.018) and Attitude 2 × Block (F(1,327) = 13.67, p < 0.001, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.040) were significant. Only d-score 2 (β = 0.065, p = 0.002) predicted WOA1. Only Attitude $2 \left( \beta = 0 . 0 6 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ predicted WOA 2.

These results rule out the possibility that the interaction with AI led to changes in WOA through the alteration of one’s implicit bias. They also provide further support for H4. They show that following exposure to performance information, the implicit bias remains relatively stable but is overridden when formulating action. Instead, people act based on their refined specific explicit attitude toward the examined AI.

## Exploratory Analysis of Confidence in One’s Own Estimate

Figure 2 depicts changes over time in people’s confidence in their own estimate. The changes reflect responses to AI estimates within each block and to receiving implied information about AI accuracy between the blocks. It points to a consistent pattern in Samples 1 and 2. People start with above-average (over 50%) confidence in the first estimates in

Block 1. Then, after receiving the AI estimate, they become more confident in their corrected estimate. Next, they receive implied information on AI performance (their own initial and corrected estimates, the AI estimate, and the true weight), and move to Block 2. In Block 2, they systematically become less confident in their initial estimates, but after receiving the AI estimate and providing a corrected estimate, they return to the levels of confidence experienced at the end of Block 1.

A two-way repeated-measure ANCOVA with “Block” (1 vs. 2) and AI Advice (before vs. after) as within-subject factors and the same covariates as before revealed that key differences are significant. In Sample 1, “Block” (F(1,666) = 19.944, p < 0.001, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.029) was significant. “Advice” $( F ( 1 , 6 6 6 ) = 2 . 5 0 2 , p = 0 . 1 1 4 , \eta ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 4 )$ ) and Block × Advice (F(1,666) = 0.102, p = 0.750, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.000) were not significant. Pairwise comparisons revealed that in Blocks 1 and 2, receiving AI advice led to significant changes in confidence. In Block 1, the confidence increased by 2.387% (p < 0.001), and in Block 2, it increased by 6.632% (p < 0.001). Confidence significantly decreased (p < 0.001) between Block 1 (M = 60.846) and Block 2 (M = 58.573). Confidence significantly increased (p < 0.001) from before (M = 57.455) to after (M = 61.965) receiving advice from the AI.

In Sample 2, “Block” (F(1,308) = 0.798, p = 0.372, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.003), “Advice” (F(1,308) = 0.769, p = 0.381, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.002) and Block × Advice were not significant (F(1,308) = 2.109, p = 0.147, η<sup>2</sup> = 0.007). Pairwise comparisons revealed that in Blocks 1 and 2, receiving AI advice led to significant changes in confidence. In Block 1 the confidence increased by 2.352% (p < 0.001), and in Block 2 it increased by 6.486% (p < 0.001). Confidence significantly decreased (p = 0.001) between Block 1 (M = 63.661) and Block 2 (M = 61.800). Confidence significantly increased (p < 0.001) from before (M = 60.521) to after (M = 64.940) receiving advice from the AI.

## Discussion

We aimed to address three research questions that tap into the possible existence of unsubstantiated unconscious negative sentiment toward AI in the domain of trustworthiness and the transient role of this implicit prejudice in algorithm aversion. Our findings were fairly consistent in multiple samples. The results are summarized in Table 5.

Our results show that people generally hold an implicit bias against AI: on average, AI is unconsciously viewed as less trustworthy than the self and even more so than human experts. This is the case even though there is no evidence that the AI underperforms humans in estimation tasks (especially objective ones) like the one used here. Since this bias is systematic, negative in tone, directed from the privileged human group to the potentially threatening AI, and is unsubstantiated, it can be viewed as an “implicit prejudice against the machine.” In the studies that used the IAT tests, we obtained d-scores ranging from -0.26 to -0.51. These reflect a bias that can be viewed as slight to moderate (Blanton et al., 2015), and is on par with typical gender and race biases (Knutson et al., 2007).

The findings further show that like other implicit prejudices, this bias acts as a somatic marker for gut feeling-based decisions (Stocco & Fum, 2008) or as a surface cue in the absence of accessible and diagnostic information (Herr et al., 1991) in that it drives knee-jerk negative reactions to and marginalization of the prejudiced group. In our case, we show that this implicit prejudice increases algorithm aversion (or reduces algorithm appreciation), as measured by the actual “weight on advice” that people place on the studied AI. That is, in the absence of implied or directly provided performance (accuracy) information about the AI, people decide whether to use the AI’s recommendations based, in part, on unconscious implicit prejudice, which is consistent with people’s action “under ambiguity” conditions. Extending this view by tapping into the learning aspects of the somatic marker hypothesis (Bechara et al., 2005) and shifts in one’s accessibility to diagnostically relevant AI performance information (Herr et al., 1991), we explain and show why the effect of implicit prejudice fades away. Lastly, our exploratory analysis of confidence in one’s own estimate trends points to consistent intricate effects of (1) AI recommendations and (2) implied AI accuracy information on individuals’ confidence in their estimates. It points scholars to the multifaceted outcomes of human-AI interactions that go beyond algorithm aversion, and highlights the need to consider a broad set of effects of conjoint work with AI.

## Research Implications

This study contributes to the literature in several important ways. First, we explain why algorithm aversion happens through a new lens that supplements previous explanations. Specifically, we take the implicit prejudice perspective (Payne & Correll, 2020; Payne & Gawronski, 2010; Payne & Hannay, 2021) and suggest that humans are simply evolutionarily programmed to be automatically averse to different groups, especially when the surrounding “cognitive fog” about these groups is negative and people have no diagnostic information about these groups. The implicit prejudice, somatic marker hypotheses, and the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective have shown to be useful in the current context. Thus, they may be useful for understanding reactions toward other technologies that have a halo (negative or positive) around them, at least in the initial use stages. The parallels we draw between commonly studied implicit prejudices (Dovidio et al., 2008) and implicit prejudice against AI are informative and important for paving the way for novel interventions that can be borrowed from the decades of work on implicit prejudice against minorities.

Second, our theoretical basis (implicit bias), methods (IAT), and findings inform various IS research streams, including IS resistance and status-quo bias research. These research streams suggest that users might resist new IS, given existing work routines, threats to their job security, and an innate preference for retaining the status quo (i.e., status-quo bias) (Ilie & Turel, 2020; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). However, these studies assumed and measured conscious cognitiveaffective reactions to new IS. They did not directly, theoretically, or empirically, tap into unconscious aspects of decision makers, nor did they separate the adoption of the IS from treating its outputs, which is our focus. Thus, these lines of work can be informed by our unconscious perspective. It might be that, as in our case, resistance to some IS is a kneejerk reaction that is informed by unconscious biases (e.g., in the domain of trustworthiness) and that such effects can be reduced through interventions.

Such extensions are reasonable, as our findings show that some theories that have traditionally operated at the conscious level have some parallels in the unconscious domain. Specifically, in five samples, after performance information was revealed, people’s WOA increased. This is consistent with the stereotype content model for social judgment (Cuddy et al., 2008) in that people update their conscious belief system after exposure to competence-relevant information about an AI (see updated attitudes in Sample 4). Nevertheless, even after improvement, WOA was below 50%. This is consistent with ideas of (unconscious) taste-based discrimination (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018) and status-quo bias (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) in that information alone is not enough to fully sway people. People simply have automatic negative reactions to AI recommendations that are due, at least in part, to some unconscious dis-amenity associated with AI (untrustworthiness in our case). Thus, we call for the incorporation of unconscious biases into IS models and theories that have traditionally been conceptualized and/or operationalized assuming consciousness. Notably, we demonstrate here that there may be a disconnect between the source of prejudice (which is unknown in our case but can include a threatening image of AI) and the manifestation (which in our case included passive acts of algorithm aversion). This disconnect highlights the need to focus on unconscious aspects of IS user behavior in this context and explains why unconscious bias can be problematic and should be studied in future research.

Third, we explain why algorithm aversion effects are transient, at least in cases where the algorithm performs reasonably well and can convey (implicitly or explicitly) this information. As we show, this is rooted in the transition from “decision under ambiguity” (or in the absence of diagnostic information) to “decision under risk” (when information about reasonable accuracy becomes available), which involves reduced emphasis on implicit prejudice and increased emphasis on implied/learned performance information. This is consistent with observed learning through implied information processes (Li et al., 2023). This means that algorithm aversion is an ephemeral issue that is likely to be diminished as people interact with and gain more positive experiences with AI. We show here that the learning aspect of the somatic marker hypothesis and the accessibilitydiagnosticity changes can explain the temporal decline in algorithm aversion, as explained by reduced emphasis on unconscious automatically retrieved associations. This perspective can inform future research because (1) the unconscious aspects of IS users are still largely untapped, despite the fact that the majority of brain processes are unconscious and are thus often metaphorically described as the underwater bulk of the “iceberg” (Scherer, 2005), and (b) the learning aspect of the somatic marker hypothesis together with the accessibility-diagnosticity perspective can help explain how people change their responses to an IS as they gain new insights about it.

Fourth, the transience of aversion (operationalized as WOA in our case) can inform the discourse on aversion vs. appreciation (Allen & Choudhury, 2021; Burton et al., 2020; Filiz et al., 2021; Hou & Jung, 2021; Jussupow et al., 2020; Kaufmann, 2021). We show here that algorithm aversion exists but is ephemeral, assuming the AI performs reasonably well. This suggests that future discourse could benefit from shifting from questioning whether and in what conditions there is more aversion (or appreciation) to discussing what could help to accelerate the transition from aversion to appreciation. While we show here that performance information is one means to do it, future research should theorize on and examine additional means, such as social approval cues or appeals (Qahri-Saremi and Turel, 2023).

Finally, the findings regarding the patterns of changes in one’s confidence were surprising and informative. Across two samples, we show that people had above 50% confidence in their estimates (p < 0.001) in tasks with which they were unlikely to have experience. This supports the idea of human overconfidence (Dunning et al., 1990). Receiving AI advice led to increases in confidence in participants’ revised estimates in both blocks. This supports ideas of algorithm appreciation (Logg et al., 2019). What was somewhat unexpected was the consistent decline in confidence in the initial assessment in Block 2. This suggests that receiving realistic information implying that AI is somewhat accurate—and perhaps more accurate than people—but still imperfect, shakes people’s confidence in their ability to perform the task independently. Results also show that this effect is temporary, as people’s confidence is restored after they receive advice from the AI in Block 2. This may be interpreted as not only a growing appreciation of algorithmic advice but also as a growing dependence on algorithmic advice as people become less confident in their own assessments without AI inputs. This longitudinal perspective on interactions with realistically imperfect AI merits more research and can contribute novel insights to the understanding of ongoing interactions with AI. It points to a need to consider the “dark sides” of algorithmic advice, such as negative effects on one’s confidence.

## Practical Implications

The findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing the effect of implicit prejudice in the initial stages of use can be borrowed from the implicit prejudice literature (Lai et al., 2013) and should be tested in future research. Ideas that need to be tested include but are not limited to exposure to prominent AI or mock sessions with AI before real interactions. If implicit prejudice against AI is similar to other implicit prejudices, such steps might help reducing the effect of implicit prejudice on aversion. In addition, the findings suggest that the learning aspect should be emphasized. When possible, system providers should provide reliable information (hopefully indicating good performance) on the AI’s performance. Even when it is possible to provide such information, such information is not always readily available, which leads to opaque reflections on the AI’s processes and performance (Rahman, 2021). Lastly, in Sample 1, women exhibited higher WOA compared to men (more appreciation). In Sample 2 the trend was the same but not significant. In Samples 3 and 4, sex was not associated with WOA. This is suggestive of potential gender differences in algorithm appreciation, which can be consistent with studies showing that women tend to be more agreeable than men (Weisberg et al., 2011) and are more likely to seek and follow advice (Greene & Grimsley, 1990). Thus, gender-specific tailoring of AI should be considered in future research.

## Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations that point to future research opportunities are noteworthy. First, the study was done in one specific AI context, namely AI in an advising role, with nonexplainable processes, and with a focus on an objective task. There are additional contexts of human-AI interaction that can be influenced by unconscious biases (Murray et al., 2021; Rahman, 2021). Even within the examined context, we focused on a very specific task (weight estimate). Thus, generalizability to other types of AI within other task domains can be established only through further studies. Second, our study was limited to WOA as the key outcome. We hence call for extending our idea of unconscious bias against AI to different outcomes, such as employee performance (Tong et al., 2021), ambivalence toward AI (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2023), and adoption of AI (Liang et al., 2021).

Third, IAT is only one tool in the arsenal of tools that can tap into unconscious processes—especially prejudice. Thus, additional insights into the process we observed here could be obtained by using additional tools, such as NeuroIS tools (Turel et al., 2021; Minas et al., 2019). Furthermore, we carefully developed two specific IAT tests following the process in Serenko and Turel (2020). By no means are these tests and word sets exclusive and/or exhaustive in terms of capturing people’s unconscious reactions to AI. They represent one reasonable attempt to capture unconscious bias in the domain of trustworthiness and reliability. Nevertheless, test items can be refined and expanded to include different AI techniques or other words that become associated with AI over time. In addition, the domain of bias can go beyond “untrustworthiness” to include additional dimensions (e.g., hate, fear, dislike). Thus, we call for future research to develop additional IAT tests to extend the arsenal of tools that can capture people’s unconscious reactions toward AI.

Fourth, while we show with multiple IAT tests in different samples that people are, on average, implicitly biased against AI in terms of trustworthiness, the sources of the bias are not explored here. This is a ripe area for future research. Fifth, our ideas were grounded in the psychological-unconscious perspective on prejudice. This is consistent with Younkin and Kuppuswamy (2018), who showed the superiority of this perspective and its high potency compared to the conscious perspective. Nevertheless, future research could more directly account for conscious-reflective taste- and statistics-based aspects of prejudice.

Finally, Figure 1, Panel b demonstrates no significant difference between the blocks in the effect of d-scores on WOA, even though the difference is in the expected direction. Moreover, the marginal effect of performance information (i.e., decrease in $R ^ { \hat { 2 } }$ between the two blocks) is stronger in Sample 1 (AI vs. me) than in Sample 2 (AI vs. expert). While such insights do not invalidate the hypotheses, as the coefficients in Block 2 are still insignificant, they merit consideration. It is possible that Sample 2 was noisier because the reference point in Sample 1 (“self”) had a clearer mental model compared to the reference point in Sample 2 (“expert”), which could have varied in perceptual precision. This explanation, while reasonable, merits further research.

## Conclusion

In multiple samples, we consistently showed that people have some but not full willingness to rely on AI advice, which reflects some aversion but also some appreciation. This willingness is influenced by people’s implicit prejudice against AI; people, on average, hold negative unsubstantiated implicit associations with AI that unconsciously render it untrustworthy.

Like in other contexts of prejudice, this unconscious prejudice guides action when no other cues are available. However, as information about the AI’s performance is revealed, we explain through the somatic marker hypothesis and the accessibilitydiagnosticity perspective why aversion is reduced: people simply gain the means (accessible relevant information) to override their implicit biases. This provides an optimistic trajectory for AI scholars and developers. If it is possible to provide indicators of good AI performance (which is not always the case), algorithm aversion can be reduced. We hope that future research builds on this work and on studies on reducing implicit prejudice effects to develop efficacious interventions that would enable stronger and more accepting collaboration with machines, even though they are different from us humans.
