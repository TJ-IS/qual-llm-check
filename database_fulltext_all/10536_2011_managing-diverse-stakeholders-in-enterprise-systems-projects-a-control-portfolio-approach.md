# Managing diverse stakeholders in enterprise systems projects: a control portfolio approach

## Abstract

While substantial research has examined the control of information systems (IS) projects, most studies in this area have only examined how one controller manages a single group of controllees. However, many IS projects, especially enterprise systems projects (often initiated by an organization’s corporate headquarters, and involving business unit users and consultants), have multiple stakeholders. The corporate headquarters (the project’s principal controller) must simultaneously ensure that the various stakeholders are aligned with the project’s goals despite their diverse motivations, and that the stakeholders collaborate with each other to achieve project success. Behavior control theory argues that the controller enacts a control portfolio of formal and informal controls. However, the presence of multiple controllee groups increases the complexity of vertical controller– controllee relationships, the salience of controllee–controllee relationships, and the interaction between these vertical and horizontal relationships. We therefore examined the creation and evolution of the control portfolio in a multi-stakeholder project over a period of 14 months. We found that (1) the principal controller did enact separate controls for the user and consultant groups; (2) there was more than one controller – the principal controller co-existed with subordinate controllers; and (3) controls enacted by the subordinate controllers and other controllees that cut across stakeholder groups required the support of the principal controller.

## Introduction

arge enterprise systems (ES) projects are often begun by the corporate headquarters of organizations to standardize processes across business units (BUs) and to integrate business functions. Achieving this goal requires the involvement of many different parties. These include: (a) the organizational headquarters unit, which initiates these ES projects; (b) the individual BUs, representing diverse functional, product and geographic interests, which provide their requirements and facilitate the adoption of standardized processes; and (c) implementation partners, which are IT consultancies that provide manpower and expertise specific to the system being implemented. Each party has a stake in the project: headquarters wants to improve performance by increasing its operational scale and obtaining more timely information, BUs want to ensure their interests are addressed with minimal disruption to their operations, and implementation partners want to complete the project at the lowest cost with the highest profit margin. Thus, the multiple stakeholders in ES projects have divergent objectives. This increases the risk of a project failing (Kirsch, 1996).

Behavior control theory has been applied to the problem of how to achieve project goals. From this perspective, a controller (e.g., the project manager) applies a portfolio of controls on controllees (e.g., the project team members) to ensure the achievement of project goals (Kirsch, 1996, 1997). Controls are actions taken by the controller that specify desired outcomes or behaviors, measure and evaluate controllee’s behavior and/or outcomes, and administer rewards or penalties (Ouchi, 1980; Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 2004).

While this approach has yielded insights, behavior control theory’s roots in agency theory have meant that the literature has focused on the operation of controls within dyads. There has been no explicit consideration as to how controls operate in the presence of multiple stakeholders, even though this is the norm in most information systems (IS) projects. This makes it difficult for researchers to apply prior findings to study the management of projects. For example, Choudhury and Sabherwal (2003) found that the control portfolio was dominated by outcome controls when the controllees were vendor consultants, while Kirsch (2004) found informal controls were used most often in the analysis and design phase, where users were the controllees. The literature, unfortunately, does not articulate what controllers do when they have to deal with both user and consultant controllees at the same time.

Prior research has also noted that control portfolios sometimes include controls initiated by controllees, usually in the form of informal clan controls (Arnott and Stiglitz, 1991; Kandel and Lazear, 1992; Barron and Gjerde, 1997). Controllees initiate controls because value is co-created (Zeithaml, 1981; Parasuraman et al., 1985; Bettencourt et al., 2002). Thus, the outcomes on which a particular team member’s performance is assessed are the result of the combined actions of all team members. This motivates team members to oversee the actions of other stakeholders. However, it is unclear whether controllee-initiated controls can be effectively enacted when there are controllees who are from other stakeholder groups, and who may have different norms and priorities.

This study therefore seeks to examine the question, ‘How does the presence of multiple stakeholders on the IS project team influence the control portfolio?’ This question is studied via a case study of the 14-month requirements analysis and design phase of a very large ES project. Prior research has suggested that the requirements analysis and design phase for such projects tends to be less structured than the implementation phase (Kirsch, 2004), and the controller faces the problem of creating a control portfolio that will motivate diverse stakeholders, who may not have worked together before, to collaborate and work towards achieving the project’s goal. Furthermore, as this is the first phase of most projects, it is also the best phase to observe the creation and implementation of the control portfolio.

The existing literature suggests that the set of controls within a project control portfolio is applied to all members of the project team (Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003). Our paper contributes to the literature by demonstrating that in addition to controls that are applied to all team members, the control portfolio in a multistakeholder context also includes separate controls targeted at distinct stakeholder groups. We argue that this arises from the distinct attributes of each stakeholder group. Second, we expand the concept of the controller in the multi-stakeholder project context. Prior research has conceptualized a single controller in a project. We found that there can be multiple controllers, comprising a principal controller and one or more subordinate controllers. The subordinate controllers exercise control over distinct groups of controllees, and are themselves subject to control by the principal controller. Finally, we contribute to the prior research on controllee-initiated controls, by showing that effective enactment of cross-stakeholder controlleeinitiated controls requires the support of the principal controller. The presence of multiple stakeholders may make it more difficult for controllees from one group to effectively enact controls across the project team.

The next section summarizes prior research on IS project controls and considers its applicability to the multi-stakeholder context. We then describe the research methods, discuss our findings, and present our conclusions.

## Prior research

Researchers concerned with how IS projects can be managed to achieve project goals have applied behavior control theory (Henderson and Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1997, 2004; Kumar and Palvia, 1998; Gallivan, 2001; Kirsch et al., 2002; Ma¨hring, 2002; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Crisp, 2003; Nidumolu and Subramani, 2003/2004). Behavior control theory describes the methods by which a controller (the principal) ensures that controllees (the agents) perform their assigned tasks appropriately (Eisenhardt, 1989). Controls are required because controllees have substantively distinct goals and desires compared to the controller (Eisenhardt, 1989; Wright et al., 2001; Farrelly and Quester, 2003). Prior research in the use of controls in IS projects have established that there are different modes of control, and that controllers rely on a portfolio of controls comprising multiple modes of control when managing projects. We briefly summarize this prior research below.

## Control modes and the control portfolio

Prior research has distinguished between formal and informal controls. Formal controls use organizational power to encourage controllees to behave in a particular way. Two modes of formal control are outcome and behavior control (Henderson and Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1997). Informal controls are enacted with minimal reliance on organizational power. The two modes of informal control are clan and self control (Ouchi, 1980; Kirsch, 1997). Each mode is associated with multiple control mechanisms. Mechanisms can also support more than one mode (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003).

With outcome controls, the controller defines outcomes, measures and evaluates performance, and provides appropriate rewards or sanctions (Kirsch, 1997). Examples of outcome control mechanisms include implementation dates and interim milestones, budgets, expected levels of performance, project plans, requirements documents, standards, organizational roles, presentations to controller, and system testing (Sappington, 1991; Henderson and Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003).

Behavior control requires that the controller articulate rules and procedures, observe behavior, and reward or sanction controllees based on behavior compliance (Kirsch, 1997). Example behavior control mechanisms include job descriptions, work assignments, supervisor-subordinate hierarchy, mandating development methodologies, meetings, walkthroughs, system documentation, conference calls, progress reports, and observation of vendor personnel by on-site client (Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003).

Clan control relies on the cultivation of consensus on goals, values, and approaches to problems, often by selection and socialization mechanisms (Ouchi, 1980). Often, researchers identify a control mechanism as a clan control when the mechanism does not rely directly on formal authority to control (Kirsch, 1997). Monitoring, evaluation, and rewards or sanctions are a group function. Clan control mechanisms include hiring and training practices, rituals and ceremonies, opportunities for social interaction, side contracting, peer pressure, team member exchange, and structuring a strategic relationship with the vendor (Ouchi, 1980; Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003).

Self-control requires that controllees have the freedom to determine what they must do and how to do it (Henderson and Lee, 1992). The controllee sets his own goal for the assigned task, self-monitors, and self-rewards (Kirsch, 1997). The controller can promote self-control by hiring from self-controlled populations (e.g., degree holders or ‘trusted’ professionals) (Spence, 1976), and by providing self-controlled agents with challenging goals and ways for them to measure goal achievement (Baumeister, 2002).

A controller relies on multiple control modes to manage a project. Henderson and Lee (1992) found that both formal and informal control together resulted in higher performance than only formal control or informal control alone, whereas Kirsch (1997) noted that a portfolio of controls was needed to address the challenge of managing many people with diverse knowledge and skills.

While prior research has provided important insights, the studies have not explicitly examined the context where the project has multiple stakeholders that need to be managed. We extend prior research by first considering who are the controller and the controllees in the context of a multi-stakeholder ES project.

## Controller and controllees

Prior research has identified a variety of people as the controller, depending on the context of the study. Earlier studies of IS projects tended to define the controller as a single person, often the project manager (Henderson and Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996, 1997) or the user liaison (Kirsch et al., 2002). In more recent studies, the increasing emphasis on business–IT partnerships has broadened the concept of the controller to encompass a team comprising the IS manager and user liaison as co-controllers (Kirsch et al., 2002). Choudhury and Sabherwal (2003) also similarly extended the concept of controller in outsourced projects to a team of individuals representing the client organization’s interests. The overarching concept of the controller therefore is the person or group of persons responsible for managing the project, and who enact controls to ensure that the project successfully meets its goals.

In ES projects that are initiated by corporate headquarters, the controller is usually a team of individuals comprising business and IS managers from the corporate headquarters, who sponsor the project and are responsible for meeting the enterprise’s goals of standardization, and keeping the project on schedule and within budget. This is consistent with prior conceptions of the controller as a team comprising both IS and business managers (Kirsch, 1997). In the common situation where there is a significant consultant presence in the project, the controller (comprising corporate business and corporate IS managers) also represent the client organization’s interests in managing the implementation partner.

Various individuals or groups of individuals such as the IS project leader (Kirsch, 1996, 1997), users and IT personnel (Henderson and Lee, 1992) have been identified as controllees. In outsourced projects, the controllees are the vendor consultants (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003). Essentially, controllees are members of the project team who execute tasks needed for the project. In all studies where controllees are a team of individuals, no distinctions have been made about variations in attributes among members of the team.

However, the typical ES project has at least two very distinct groups: user representatives from the BUs and consultants from implementation partners. Although this combination of controllees has not been studied before, they are a common feature of ES projects. Users are controllees since they participate as members of the project team (Henderson and Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 2004) by describing their units’ requirements and helping with testing, among other things. Consultants are controllees in IT projects because they provide the required skills and expertise to the project team. Previous studies have examined vendor personnel as controllees (e.g., Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003), but not as part of project teams together with user representatives.

The controller in this situation faces a challenge that has not been addressed in prior research: the need to create a control portfolio that controls both user participants and consultants. Thus, our research question, ‘How does the presence of multiple stakeholders on the IS project team influence the control portfolio?’

## The control portfolio in a multi-stakeholder ES project

Prior research has identified the key factors that influence the control portfolio. These include task characteristics (Govindarajan and Fisher, 1990; Kirsch, 1996), the controller’s and controllees’ role expectations of themselves and others, the knowledge and skill of the controller and controllees, and the organization’s existing control practices (Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003).

The task characteristic of outcome measurability is associated with greater outcome control (Kirsch et al., 2002), whereas behavior observability (together with the controller’s knowledge of the transformation process) is associated with behavior control (Govindarajan and Fisher, 1990; Kirsch, 1996). When outcomes are difficult to measure, and behaviors are not easily observable, then clan control is preferred (Ouchi, 1980; Kirsch, 1996). The controller’s and controllees’ expectation of their roles also influences the control portfolio. For example, Choudhury and Sabherwal (2003) noted that client and vendor beliefs about their respective roles would influence whether the expectation was that the vendor would be formally monitored and evaluated, or whether clan and self-controls would be more heavily applied. A controller with a high level of project-related knowledge can more easily apply behavior control (Eisenhardt, 1985; Jaworski, 1988; Kirsch and Cummings, 1996; Kirsch et al., 2002), as the controller would be able to specify and assess procedures linked to achieving the project’s goals. Researchers also argue that more knowledgeable controllees increase controller confidence in using outcome control or self-control (Kirsch, 1996; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003). Finally, the control portfolio is influenced by the organization’s current control practices. For example, an organization that relies on formal authority to manage its business is likely to also rely on formal controls in its IS projects.

While prior research has identified the project task, controllees’ role, knowledge and skills as factors that influence the control portfolio, it has largely treated controllees within a project as being homogenous, and has not explicitly considered how the presence of heterogeneous controllees within a project would affect the control portfolio. We believe that in a multiple stakeholder ES project, users and consultants are likely to have distinct attributes, with different tasks, role expectations, and knowledge and skills. The controller may need to enact separate controls for each stakeholder group that take into account each group’s attributes, to align each group’s contributions to the project’s goals.

Nonetheless, despite the presence of multiple stakeholders, we expect that there will be some controls that the controller enacts commonly across all stakeholder groups. For example, prior research has found that all projects have formal mechanisms such as budgets, delivery schedules, milestones, standard methodology, project hierarchy, steering and review committees (Faraj and Sproull, 2000; Sabherwal, 2003; Gosain et al., 2005; Espinosa et al., 2007). These control mechanisms help direct the efforts of different stakeholder groups towards project goals by imposing a common timeline, project hierarchy, and methodology.

Finally, the presence of multiple stakeholders raises the issue of controllee-initiated controls. The notion of controllee-initiated controls in the context of IS projects was raised by Henderson and Lee (1992), who termed it team member control. They conceptualized it as actions taken by team members to influence how team tasks are conducted. They provided the example of team members adopting the structured walk-through as a norm for all program modules that they developed. In the diverse stakeholder context, the issue of controllee-initiated control is likely to be more complex, since there are multiple controllee groups. We know little about how controllees effectively initiate controls across stakeholder groups.

Why would controllees initiate controls across stakeholder groups? First, value in an IT implementation project is co-created by users and consultants (Zeithaml, 1981; Parasuraman et al., 1985; Bettencourt et al., 2002). The coproduction of value in an IT project implies that poor performance by one stakeholder has negative consequences for others. For example, limited knowledge sharing by users makes it difficult for consultants to draw up a list of system specifications. Similarly, when users are uncooperative during system testing, the chances that the system will have a smooth roll-out are diminished. Users and consultants have a stake in the success of the project. Users want a system that meets their requirements, while consultants want to avoid any negative impact on their reputation of being associated with a failed project. Consultants may also create controls because they feel a professional responsibility for utilizing their experience and doing what they can to ensure project success.

In summary, we believe that the control portfolio in a multi-stakeholder group will comprise controller-initiated common controls that serve to coordinate the efforts of the different stakeholder groups, as well as separate controls, targeted at each stakeholder group that address the distinctive attributes of each group. Finally, we believe that there are likely to be controllee-initiated controls that are particularly helpful in promoting the sharing of knowledge among stakeholder groups.

## Methods

The qualitative literature on control has generally adopted a rational positivist approach (Kirsch, 1997, 2004; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Crisp, 2003). This approach deemphasizes the unique characteristics of the researcher, and treats interview subjects as representatives of a larger pool rather than as distinct individuals. This positivist outlook stems in part from the control literature’s roots in agency theory (Eisenhardt, 1985; Eisenhardt, 1989; Govindarajan and Fisher, 1990). Given that this study draws its primary theory base from this literature, a rational positivist epistemology was adopted.

Given the paucity of research on the use of controls to manage diverse stakeholders in IS projects, we chose to examine a single case over a period of more than a year to study in-depth the phenomenon of interest (Miles and Huberman, 1994) – how the presence of multiple stakeholders in an IS project influenced the control portfolio. We therefore selected a large project which met several criteria important for this study (Yin, 2003). First, it involved diverse stakeholder groups (three autonomous BUs and two implementation partner organizations), with each stakeholder group having distinct attributes as well as priorities that were not fully aligned with those of the controller. Second, the success of this project hinged on the active engagement of the various stakeholder groups, because each group possessed unique knowledge and skills that were needed to design a robust set of standardized processes. Hence, it was important that the different groups work together effectively. Third, we were able to shadow the project from its inception to completion, with access to representatives from all stakeholder groups, as well as review all project documentation. This allowed us to examine how the multi-stakeholder context influenced the control portfolio from the start of the project.

We chose to focus this paper on the first 14 months of the project, from its initiation to the delivery of the standardized design. This was the phase of the project that was most challenging, because all stakeholders had to be actively involved, and attaining the deliverable (a common design) was dependent on their effective collaboration. There was therefore a wealth of data and insights on the question of managing diverse stakeholders from the analysis and design phase alone. The subsequent phases of the project (sequential implementation of the common design in each BU) would involve only one BU at a time, and the implementation task was more structured.

## Site description

Alpha Logistics is a transportation company based in Asia, focused on moving personnel and cargo within and between countries. The organization has three major lines of business – land, sea, and air logistics. It has over 6000 employees. The organization structure comprises a corporate headquarters that drives strategic planning, and also provides corporate services in finance, human resources, and IT. The organization has three major BUs for the land, sea, and air logistics businesses, respectively. The majority of Alpha’s employees work in its BUs. Each BU operates with a high degree of autonomy, and owns and maintains significant transportation assets. However, in 2000, the increasing global reach of competitors led Alpha to seek greater integration of its three main lines of business to achieve increased responsiveness and efficiency. This was a challenge given that the three BUs had implemented highly customized versions of an ES package in the early 1990s. The level of data and process standardization across the BUs was low, making it difficult to integrate their processes.

It was against this organizational backdrop that the sea BU, experiencing system capacity constraints, raised a proposal to replace its ES. Corporate’s response was to propose that all three BUs replace their ES with one standard package. The objective of corporate’s proposed project was to have an integrated logistics system so as to better control and coordinate equipment and personnel. Corporate also felt that the adoption of an industry-leading

ES package would give it access to the latest in technologyenabled functionality and industry best practices.

Two years were spent evaluating various packages and implementation partners, and obtaining funding approval for the project. The decision was made to adopt a major ES package, and to minimize customization and aim for a good level of standardization. The organization faced resource constraints, and the budget and timeline for the project were therefore very tight.

The plan was to begin with a year-long requirements analysis and design phase. In this phase, logistics and finance data and processes that were common across the BUs would be identified, and a standardized design was to be agreed upon by all three BUs. The design was then to be implemented sequentially in the sea, land, and air BUs over the succeeding 3-year period.

The project was to be managed by a small team of senior business and IT executives and their support staff from corporate headquarters. This group of corporate managers were the principal controller for the project. They managed representatives from the three BUs who were seconded full time to the project. The corporate project managers were also responsible for oversight of the implementation partner. A primary implementation partner (PIP) was appointed to design and implement the package. Due to the size of the project, the PIP sub-contracted the implementation to a secondary implementation partner (SIP). The requirements analysis phase began in January 2003, and ended in April 2004, 2 months behind schedule. ## Data collection

The corporate project managers invited us to observe the case site and develop case reports for the internal training of project managers. We were to be neutral observers, shadowing the project, and were not otherwise involved in the project. The data collected for this paper was part of a broader multi-year study of the ES implementation. We had access to written project documentation and conducted interviews with all stakeholder groups.

The data for this paper covers the 14-month analysis and design phase which began in early 2003. We visited the project once every 6–8 weeks throughout the project and interviewed representatives from the corporate project managers (principal controller), implementation partners (controllee), and BU representatives (controllee). In all, we conducted 36 interviews during the analysis and design phase. Table 2 provides summary information on the number interviewed from each group.

The main decision-makers on the corporate project management team, responsible for enacting controls to ensure that the project met its goals, were the corporate Vice President (VP) and Assistant Vice President (AVP) of Logistics, and the corporate VP and AVP of IT. We therefore interviewed them several times over the course of the analysis and design phase to understand their perspective of the project as it progressed. We also interviewed several corporate support staff (IT and Logistics) who supported these four senior executives in their oversight of the project. We interviewed a representative mix of primary and secondary implementation partner consultants. We conducted repeat interviews with the primary and SIP managers, as we found that they had important roles in the initiation of controls as the project progressed. Finally, we interviewed representative members of the sea, land, and air BUs who were actively involved in providing requirements and inputs to the design.

Interviews were always conducted by multiple interviewers and transcribed within 24 hours. The interview protocols included a number of standard questions. We first asked each interviewee to explain their role in the project, their key tasks, and also their prior experience. We then asked what they perceived to be the major issues or challenges, and how they were addressing these concerns. When an interviewer raised a major issue and steps taken to address the issue, we would take note and probe other interviewees on the issue and steps taken. This allowed us to understand controller and controllee perspectives of the project issues and challenges. The principal controller was also asked how they ensured that the project was on track. Interviewees’ answers to this and the question of how the issues/challenges were being addressed provided information about controls that were initiated by the controller or by the controllees.

Documents obtained included minutes of steering committee and project management meetings. These meetings were co-chaired by the corporate VP, Logistics and the corporate VP, IT, and comprised representatives from the users and implementation partners. We also reviewed contracts, meeting presentation slides, timeline and other project management charts, project status and review reports, change requests and other project documents.

## Analysis

The analysis was conducted in several steps. We first read through transcripts and documents to identify the key roles, tasks, knowledge, and skills for each stakeholder group. For example, implementation partner roles were identified in the contract, as well as from interview comments by the corporate project managers, implementation partners and BU representatives. We also identified the controller’s perception of the motivation and concerns for each controllee group. We triangulated the controller’s perceptions with controllee interview transcripts (Klein and Myers, 1999). For example, in our data, the controller was concerned that BUs would prioritize their own requirements over enterprise-wide standardization. This concern was triangulated with user comments that questioned the value of standardization, and emphasized the uniqueness of their BU’s operations. The roles, tasks, major issues, and concerns were also included in a 70-page case that was reviewed by corporate project managers. Their detailed feedback on the document enabled us to confirm, and in some cases, revise our analysis (Mason, 1996).

We then coded the controls enacted in the project. Two authors read the documentation and transcripts to identify control mechanisms. The control mechanisms were then classified by the mode of control. In coding, we were guided by the definition for each mode, as well as the mechanisms associated with each mode of control as culled from a comprehensive review of prior literature. Consistent with prior research in behavior control theory (Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003), we did not analyze self-control mechanisms (Kirsch and Cummings, 1996)

In addition, our coding took the multi-stakeholder context explicitly into account, by considering, for each control, the initiator of the control (e.g., the principal controller, or one of the controllee groups), and the target of the control (e.g., all controllees, or a specific controllee group such as the users, or the implementation partners).

## Results

We examined in detail the portfolio of controls which enabled the successful completion of the challenging analysis and design phase. In particular, we describe the control mechanisms and modes, including whom they were targeted at, and the reasons they were enacted.

We present first an overview of the key stakeholders and their attributes, including their behaviors and motivations, and how these could compromise the project. We then present our findings on the control portfolio.

## Stakeholders

The principal controller comprised the corporate VP and AVP of Logistics and corporate VP and AVP of IT, supported by a small team of corporate IT and logistics personnel. They were responsible for meeting corporate’s goals for the project, which were 60% standardization of processes across the three BUs, and no more than 5% customization of the package. They were also responsible for getting the project to complete on time and within budget, despite the aggressive schedule and tight budget. While the principal controller was experienced in managing vendors in more structured projects, and routinely deployed a set of formal controls, they had never managed an enterprise-wide process standardization project involving multiple BUs.

There were two implementation partners in this project. The PIP was contracted to analyze requirements and provide a standardized design. The SIP was contracted initially to shadow the PIP to understand requirements, and to implement the requirements for one BU. After the completion of the contract, the SIP would have to bid to implement the system for the remaining two BUs. Because of problems mid-way through the requirements phase, the PIP, at the suggestion of the principal controller, invited the SIP to assume a greater role in the project, and to perform substantial work in the requirements analysis and design phase. There were about 50 consultants involved in the project. The PIP was primarily responsible to the principal controller for delivering the design, and the SIP was responsible for implementing the package in the sea unit. The PIP manager and SIP manager worked closely, with each focusing on different aspects of the project, and their consultants worked as an integrated team.

The principal controller believed the implementation partners were committed to the project’s successful implementation, as the organization would serve as a good reference site. However, the principal controller was concerned about the reasonableness of the partners’ cost estimates. The principal controller was also troubled by the range of knowledge and skills of the PIP. The PIP was still building up its Asian operations and had to recruit many consultants from other countries who were new to both the consultant organization as well as to the client’s industry. Hence, BU representatives initially observed that the consultants were more solution-savvy than industryknowledge savvy.

There was also relatively little knowledge of each other’s work practices and norms among the client and implementation partner personnel. Thus, conflicts in work practices arose during the project. For example, the principal controller commented that consultants were not positioning things right, and that deliverables were very high level, not detailed enough. The consultants characterized their style as stating the problem and offering the solution immediately, in contrast to the client’s style which stressed framing and justifying the issues before presenting the problem.

The role of the 45 full-time BU representatives in the project was to define their requirements, and collaborate in the design of the common system – although the expectation was that the consultants would be responsible for coming up with the proposed designs. However, since the BUs had operated autonomously, their representatives were BU-centric in their orientation. They saw their role as ensuring that the operations of their BU were not compromised by process or data standardization. For example, one corporate manager noted that users tend to push their own ideas y at the meetings even after a decision (on a standard process or data) has been taken. BU representatives were knowledgeable about their own processes, but not those of other BUs. They were familiar with the previous BU-specific version of the package software, but had not been involved in a major IT-enabled process standardization project.

## The control portfolio

The principal controller enacted a portfolio of controls to address the distinct attributes of the vendor consultants and BU representatives, and to ensure that the project goals were met. As the project progressed, the controllees also created controls that contributed to the progress of the project. We first present the controls enacted by the principal controller.

## Controls targeted at implementation partner

The principal enacted an initial portfolio of controls that was dominated by outcome controls targeted at the implementation partner. This slew of outcome controls included a number of contractual specifications. These defined the expected scope of the project by identifying the processes to be designed and implemented. To address the risk of exceeding the budget, the controller had the PIP agree on a fixed price contract. Interim milestones and deliverables were stated in a detailed project plan developed soon after the contract was signed. The milestones and deliverables were to be monitored through fortnightly meetings, where the implementation partners were to present the status of work done. The controller would evaluate and accept each deliverable before authorizing progress payments to the implementation partner. These outcome controls were those that the organization routinely used in its other projects. For example, the PIP submitted an initial set of high-level requirements in week 6, as required. This document was rejected by the principal controller as being insufficiently detailed. This deliverable was iteratively revised and rejected until the principal controller finally accepted it in week 12.

There were relatively few behavior controls. One was the contractual assignment of corporate IT personnel to the PIP’s team. The principal controller saw the assignment as a means to gain a better understanding of how the PIP worked.

The principal controller attempted to initiate clan control by creating a strategic relationship with the PIP. The relationship was announced with a ceremony to signal to members of the client and PIP organization that they were in the relationship for the long haul. As part of the strategic relationship, both parties agreed to hold informal, highlevel talks to keep each other informed as to changes in each others’ business. The organization also agreed to future interactions, in terms of serving as a reference site for the PIP, that is, to open its doors and allow other prospective customers of the PIP to review operations associated with the ES.

In the fifth month, to address poor interim performance, the principal controller sought to influence the selection of the new primary implementation partner manager (PIPM), who was to replace the previous PIPM who had left the project. While the principal controller did not have contractual or formal authority over the composition of the PIP team, the PIP was willing to let the principal controller sit in on their selection interviews for the PIPM because of the strong informal relationship developed between senior management of the client and PIP organizations. The principal controller influenced the selection so that the new PIPM was someone who was experienced with the local context. ## Controls targeted at BU representative

The principal controller initially enacted relatively few controls on the user representatives. Outcome controls include corporate articulating to the BUs that at least 60% of the processes were to be standardized across the BUs and that 95% of them were to be based on the standard package. To further motivate the BUs to meet targets, the principal controller created a novel outcome control mechanism. While liquidated damages clauses in contracts usually penalize the implementation partner for late delivery, the liquidated damages clause in this project stated that the cost of delays would be shared equally by both the PIP and the client. The BUs’ share of this cost would be deducted from their budgets.

Despite this control, the schedule slipped significantly in the first few months of the project. The principal controller recognized that delays in deliverables could be linked to insufficient responsiveness on the part of users. They therefore rigorously enforced the interim deadlines and applied sanctions, withholding approval for long vacation leave by BU representatives until progress was made in meeting interim deadlines. This provided BU representatives with a personal incentive to be more engaged and accommodating so as to speed up the work and meet deadlines.

An interesting behavior control enacted across the BUs was a procedure for BU representatives, aided by consultants, to negotiate with each other. A process would be accepted as standard (coded ‘blue’ in the process diagrams) if at least two BUs agreed that it captured their requirements. Standard processes would be paid for by corporate. This behavior control made it much easier for the BU representatives to reach agreement on common processes, so that there were relatively few nonstandard processes. ## Controls targeted at all team members

In addition to controls targeted at one group, the principal controller employed certain controls that applied to all groups simultaneously (see Table 6). Project schedules were the main common outcome control as they helped all stakeholder groups to know when each set of tasks was to be completed. The main common behavior control was the project hierarchy, which specified the governance process for the project. The VP of logistics and VP of IT jointly chaired the steering committee, which was responsible for overseeing the project’s direction and approving any major changes in schedule, budget or scope, and resolving issues that had been escalated from lower levels. The project management committee, comprising representatives of the implementation partner and users from the project team, met at least fortnightly, and reported to the steering committee.

The principal controller also took steps to indirectly control project members by influencing their mindset. One mindset change the controller wanted was to promote the norm of cooperation. The principal controller felt it could do this by increasing the opportunities for informal interaction across stakeholder groups. The work site was located in a remote area, and team members originally drove out in small vendor or user groups to reach public food facilities. Access to food facilities was improved so that team members would find it easier to have lunch together. The principal controller obtained permission from a nearby company to open its cafe´ to project team members. A mobile food vendor was also brought onto the project site. Occasionally, meals were sponsored at nicer restaurants, and the principal controller took pains to ensure that attendees came from all stakeholder groups. Weekly sports matches and karaoke sessions were also arranged after work.

The principal controller also attempted to build commitment to the project by articulating that the project team would sink or swim together. The controller encouraged the promulgation of this view by also not taking long periods of vacation leave themselves, and by visibly putting in long hours on the project.

Controllee-initiated controls

Besides controls initiated by the principal controller, there were also controls initiated by controllees. We observed that most controls were initiated by two individuals – the new PIPM or the secondary implementation partner manager (SIPM). A few controls were also initiated by consultants and users.

The PIPM proposed the use of a single methodology and process modeling tool. Previously, the supply management track used one particular modeling tool, while the other tracks used another. With the principal controller’s approval and support, a behavior control was instituted that all team members were trained to use a consistent modeling approach, and access the standardized documentation database. The PIPM designated one vendor employee to maintain the documentation database and run reports to spot methodological inconsistencies. Team members were able to communicate more effectively across tracks since they were using common terms and process tools.

The PIPM was also concerned about the functional silos in the project team. While he could not completely dismantle the functional team structure, he was able to soften the boundaries by breaking the three large functional teams into over a dozen smaller teams, each working on scenarios that cut across the functional tracks. An example of a scenario would be ‘vehicle breakdown repair.’ The act of replacing of a vehicle’s part consisted of an engineering component, a supply component, and a finance component. Both BU users and consultants found it easier to interact with each other in these smaller teams. This change became a formal behavior control when the principal controller quickly moved to realign the formal accountability structure. The controller designated that each scenario was to be owned by a BU user and a consultant, who were to jointly review, shape, and lobby for decisions in support of the solution. The consultant and user owner of each scenario were jointly accountable for delivering its design on schedule. Senior executives from the principal controller group were appointed the executive owners for specific scenarios so that cross-BU differences could be escalated to them for resolution.

The SIPM worked closely with the PIPM, and organized a series of informal workshops and luncheon meetings involving team members from corporate, the BUs, and the vendors. The idea behind these workshops and meetings was to encourage open communication among team members to surface issues proactively, so that they could be addressed by the team. To follow through on the issues that were brought up, the SIPM introduced an issue log, where issues raised at the meetings were tracked and their status displayed. Users thus could see that their issues were being dealt with and closed. The increased visibility of their work also motivated the vendor consultants to perform.

The SIPM also felt that the user-consultant teams should sit together, instead of each stakeholder group keeping to their own rooms. He arranged to have the principal controller visit one of the SIPM’s other client sites to observe the facilities layout for the large project team. This led the principal controller to upgrade the facilities, enlarge the rooms and put in more workstations. The additional space allowed the consultants and users to sit together to work on their scenarios.

There were also clan controls that appeared to emerge over time from the interaction among group members. For example, the work norm changed over time, so that all team members began to stay back and work longer hours. Many of the PIP consultants were new to the PIP’s organization. The team from the PIP thus had few work norms. In the beginning of the project, the PIP consultants adopted the user work norms of leaving at the end of the official work day. When the SIP consultants came on board, the norms began to shift towards longer working hours. Working overtime was a common practice in the SIP’s culture. When the PIP consultants and BU employees observed the SIP consultants working late, they too began to stay late. The principal controller tacitly encouraged this by providing infrastructure support for those working late.

Another example of an informal control that emerged from the interaction among group members was when user representatives began to give informal feedback to the principal controller about specific consultants who they felt were uncooperative and underperforming. While the principal controller had no contractual or formal authority to have these consultants removed, they did pass feedback to the PIP. The PIP removed a few of these consultants from the project and replaced them with consultants from the SIP. The voluntary removal of a few consultants by the PIP sent strong signals to the consultants that a higher level of cooperation with the users was expected going forward. ## Discussion

The key question in this study was: how does the presence of multiple stakeholders influence the control portfolio? As in prior studies, we found that there were common formal controls such as project schedules and a project hierarchy that were applied to all controllees in the project to facilitate the coordination of their diverse efforts. In addition, we found informal clan controls such as social activities that promoted informal interaction among all team members.

The key contribution of this study, however, lies in three main discoveries. First, controllers employ not only common controls, but also separate controls targeted at each stakeholder group. Second, a closer examination of the controllee-initiated controls suggests that there are subordinate controllers, and that there is a hierarchy of controllers. Third, the principal controller’s support is required for the effective enactment of controls initiated by subordinate controllers and other controllees that cut across stakeholder groups.

## Separate controls targeted at each stakeholder group

Consultant and user controllees differ in their tasks, role expectations, and knowledge and skills. The presence of multiple stakeholders on the project team required the principal controller to enact specific controls for each group that addressed their distinct attributes.

The users’ tasks in the analysis and design phase were to provide information on their requirements, and to collaborate with designers on developing the functional design. However, in this case, the users’ initial view of their role emphasized protecting their BU’s interests and ensuring that their current practices were perpetuated. There was therefore the risk that they would distort information while negotiating their requirements and the system’s design to retain their current practices. The user controls enacted by the principal controller were therefore largely aimed at encouraging users to converge towards standard processes, and to discourage requests for customization. Hence, the controls included the specification of a 60% standardization target, a 5% cap on customization, having BUs bear 50% of the cost for overruns, having a formal process to control the approval of customization requests, and a process that encouraged BUs to negotiate with each other towards a common design.

The consultant’s task in systems analysis and design was to elicit requirements from the user, synthesize the requirements across user groups, and propose a standardized design for the enterprise. In this case, the implementation partners supported the controller’s objective of standardization as this simplified system design and configuration. The principal controller therefore enacted controls to encourage the implementation partners to deliver an appropriate level of quality at a reasonable price, and to discourage them from cutting corners. These controls included carefully specifying the project’s scope, interim milestones and deliverables, regular status reporting, and a process for assessing deliverables before payment. The principal controller also attempted to increase the PIP’s commitment to the project through informal means, such as the creation of a strategic relationship, and to improve the quality of consultants by getting involved in the selection of a new PIPM.

## A hierarchy of controllers

Consistent with prior research, we had originally conceptualized only one controller for the project – the corporate project management team. The implementation partners and BU representatives were controllees, as they were subject to controls enacted by the corporate controller. However, as we reflected on the controls that were enacted by the controllees, we realized that they raised the question of whether there was more than one controller in the project.

Specifically, we observed the PIPM and SIPM initiated a number of influential controls, such as the use of a common process modeling tool, the scenario approach, issue-airing workshops and the co-location of users and consultants. While they were subject to the controls enacted by the principal controller, they had formal authority over their consultants, and also had some responsibility for achieving project goals. This explains their more proactive stance (relative to other controllees such as user representatives and other consultants) in initiating controls.

It is likely that the PIPM and SIPM also had instituted controls over their consultants. However, since we had considered them only as controllees, and our focus was on controls they initiated that cut across stakeholder groups, we do not have systematic data on the controls they may have enacted only over their consultants.

## Principal controller support for controllee-initiated crossstakeholder controls

The PIPM- and SIPM-initiated controls that, from our observations, crossed stakeholder lines. However, the PIPM and SIPM did not have direct formal authority over the BU representatives. There was therefore a need for support from the principal controller for the PIPM- and SIPMinitiated controls to be effectively enacted. The PIPM and SIPM had to ‘borrow’ power from higher up in the project hierarchy to assert control. For example, the PIPM’s initiatives to use a common process modeling tool and to have a scenario approach required the support of the principal controller. The principal controller approved and formally mandated the use of the common process modeling tool recommended by the PIPM, and also revised the accountability and issue escalation hierarchy to support the assignment of user-consultant pairs to specific scenarios. Similarly, the SIPM’s issue-airing workshop had greater weight because of the presence of the principal controller, while the suggestion to co-locate users and consultants could only be enacted with the principal controller’s approval and provision of funds to remove room partitions.

BU representatives and consultants also initiated a few controls that cut across stakeholder lines, and which benefitted from principal controller support. For example, the corporate controller conveyed user feedback on underperforming consultants to the PIPM for his action. The principal controller tacitly encouraged the emerging norm of staying back late to work by extending the hours of server operations and delaying server backup.

## Implications for theory and practice

This study extends prior research on IS project control to the increasingly common enterprise-wide project context, where there are many stakeholders involved in a project. Prior research established that project controllers rely on a portfolio of controls and that contextual factors, such as tasks, controller–controllee knowledge and skills, and expectations, and the organization’s approach to controls, influence the composition of the control portfolio. The implicit assumption has been that the portfolio of controls is applied to all members of the project team.

By explicitly considering the multi-stakeholder context, and recognizing differences in stakeholders’ attributes, this study reveals that a controller also enacts separate controls for each group to address the distinctive attributes of each group, and increase the alignment of their efforts towards the achievement of project goals.

The study also extends the notion of who is the controller. Whereas prior research had gradually expanded the concept of controller from an individual to a group of individuals, our study further expands the concept to suggest that there can be multiple groups of controllers. In our study, the traditional controller role (as conceptualized in prior research) was filled by the corporate project management team comprising senior business and IT executives from corporate headquarters. However, implementation partner managers (PIPM and SIPM) also appeared to form another controller group that had some responsibility for achieving project goals, and who therefore were proactive in initiating controls. There was a hierarchical relationship between the controllers, with the implementation partner managers being subordinate to the principal controller.

Finally, the study sheds light on the understudied area of controllee-initiated controls. While prior research has noted the existence of such controls, our study found that these controls benefitted from support from the principal controller. We theorize that this is because, in a multistakeholder context, controllees from one group usually have no formal authority over controllees in other groups, and often little informal influence as well, as they tend to be separated by cultural and structural differences. Hence, support from the principal controller is needed to help in the acceptance of controllee-initiated controls by other controllee groups.

The study also has implications for practitioners. Most importantly, it highlights that in managing complex, multistakeholder projects, the project controller cannot take a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to project control. He needs to identify the key stakeholder groups, consider whether there are important differences in the tasks, knowledge and skills, expectations, and organizational cultures, and how these are likely to impact project success. He then creates separate controls that address the concerns arising from the distinctive attributes of each group.

The principal controller should also identify members of the stakeholder group who may have a subordinate controller role. The principal controller needs to be aware of the controls that they initiate, and provide support for such controls that need to be enacted across groups to increase the likelihood of project success.

## Limitations and further research

This paper has taken a first cut at examining the issue of managing multiple stakeholders in enterprise projects using control theory. One of the limitations of the study was that since we had originally conceptualized the study as having only the principal controller, and all other project members as controllees, we did not focus on controls that subordinate controllers may have enacted over their own stakeholder groups. Future studies may wish to examine how subordinate controller-initiated controls targeted at their own stakeholder groups (e.g., controls placed by an implementation partner manager on his consultants) contribute to, and interact with controls that cross stakeholder lines, as well as with principal controllerinitiated controls.

A second concern that arises with the use of a single case is the generalizability of our findings. We therefore consider each of our main findings and discuss its generalizability to other multi-stakeholder IS projects. The finding that the controller will enact separate controls for each stakeholder group is likely to be true in all multistakeholder IS projects where stakeholders have diverse priorities and attributes. However, the exact controls that the controller chooses for each stakeholder group may vary from our study, depending on the specific attributes of the controller and each controllee group, as well as the goals of the project. For example, in projects where the consulting organization is not as keen to have the client as a reference site, the client controller may not have the ability to enact the control of influencing the selection of the consultant manager. Further research is needed to establish whether there is a repertoire of controls that are routinely targeted at users, and other controls that are usually targeted at consultants.

The finding that there is more than one controller group in a multi-stakeholder IS project is likely to hold true in large projects and/or projects with external parties. Large project teams are often sub-divided into smaller sub-teams, and there may be a number of individuals who serve as controllers of the smaller teams, while being subject to the controls of the principal controller. Similarly, when there is a significant consultant presence on the project, a consultant manager is likely to also serve as a controller, while being subject to the client controller as well. However, the number of controller groups may differ from project to project. In our case, we found two controllers – the main corporate controller group, and the implementation partner managers. However, in other large, multi-stakeholder project, there may also be one or more controllers from the user groups. Further research may identify projects with more than two controller groups, and further examine how this state of affairs influences the enactment of controls across stakeholder groups.

Finally, the finding that support from the principal controller enables controllee-initiated controls to be effective across different groups is likely to hold true in all multi-stakeholder IS projects. This is because controllees or controllers from a specific stakeholder group have less influence over members of other peer groups, so that the support of the principal controller is needed for effective enactment of controls initiated by controllees. However, the way in which the controller provides such support is likely to vary from one project to another. Our study suggests that the principal controller provided support through approval (e.g., of the common modeling tool), provision of resources (e.g., as in the renovation of the workspace), and participation (e.g., as when the controller showed up for the SIPM arranged issue airing meetings). Further research may surface other ways in which the principal controller can increase the effectiveness of controllee efforts to enact controls that contribute to project success.
