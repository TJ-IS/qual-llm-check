# Forming Interoperability Through Interorganizational Systems Standards

Kexin Zhao and Mu Xia

Kexin Zhao is an assistant professor of management information systems at the Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina at Charlotte. She received a Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign. Her research interests include electronic commerce, IT standardization, and diffusion of IT innovations. Her papers have been published in journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, and Electronic Markets.

Mu Xia is an affiliated researcher at the Key Lab of Financial Intelligence and Financial Engineering, Southwestern University of Finance and Economics in Chengdu, Sichuan, China. He received a Ph.D. in management science and information systems from the University of Texas at Austin’s McCombs School of Business. His research interests include online user behavioral analysis, e-business standardization, electronic commerce, and combinatorial auctions. His work has been published in many scholarly journals, including Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Decision Support Systems.

Abstract: Interoperability is a crucial organizational capability that enables firms to manage information systems (IS) from heterogeneous trading partners in a value network. While interoperability has been discussed conceptually in the IS literature, few comprehensive empirical studies have been conducted to conceptualize this construct and examine it in depth. For instance, it is unclear how interoperability is formed and whether it can improve organizational performance. To fill the gap, we argue that interorganizational systems (IOS) standards are a key information technology infrastructure facilitating formation of interoperability. As an organizational ability to work with external trading partners, interoperability’s development depends not only on capability building within firm boundaries but also on community readiness across firm boundaries. Using data collected from 194 organizations in the geospatial industry, we empirically confirm that interoperability is formed via these two different paths. Furthermore, our results show that interoperability acts as a mediator by enabling firms to achieve performance gains from IOS standards adoption. Our study sheds new light on formation mechanisms as well as the business value of interoperability.

Key words and phrases: business value, geospatial industry, interoperability, interorganizational systems standards, IT standards, network effects, standardization, standardized data infrastructure.

In today’s network econom y [53], firms need to carefully manage interorganizational processes in order to access external resources, mitigate strategic uncertainties, and gain competitive advantage [47]. While these processes have been digitized by information technologies (IT) [49], firms continue to face challenges caused by information systems (IS) from heterogeneous trading partners in a distributed computing environment. Consequently, it is crucial for firms to become interoperable in order to coordinate and co-create value with their partners.

Despite the critical role of interoperability, theoretical and empirical research pertaining to this important organizational capability is limited in the IS field. Our literature review indicates that interoperability has never been formally examined in prior empirical studies of interorganizational systems (IOS) [47, 49, 59, 64]. It is unclear how interoperability should be conceptualized and operationalized in the context of digital value networks. Also underresearched is how interoperability is formed and whether it can lead to organizational performance gains. Our research intends to fill this gap in the literature.

Interoperability is possible only when a common language is used by various IS, despite heterogeneity in software, hardware, and system architecture. Such a common language is defined by IOS standards, technical specifications describing data formats, and communication protocols for computer communications [40, 73]. IOS standards contribute to interoperability by providing “shared business terms, functions, processes, and protocols” [41, p. 467]. The IOS standards we focus on here are XML (Extensible Markup Language)-based and use the Internet as the delivery platform to transport data [73]. They are built on multiple layers of international network standards, such as TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol), HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol), XML, SOAP (Simple Object Access Protocol), and WSDL (Web Service Definition Language) (see [69, 73] for detailed descriptions). Due to rapid technological changes and increased heterogeneity of members involved in standardization, IOS standards are mainly developed and promoted via multilateral collaboration within consortia [20, 68, 69]. To solve industry-wide interconnectivity issues, most consortia-based IOS standards are system-independent, and their potential adopters comprise a large trading community [29, 73].

In this research, we argue that IOS standards adoption enables firms to develop interoperability. Specifically, interoperability is developed via two different paths. The first path is internal capability building. The organizational capability literature suggests viewing capabilities as a hierarchy, noting that simpler capabilities are needed to build more complex ones [19]. Thus, we identify standardized data infrastructure (SDI) as a simpler ability built from IOS standards adoption, which can be used as a basis for developing interoperability. The second path considers community readiness across firm boundaries. If the same standards have been accepted by more trading partners, achieving interoperability in dynamic value networks becomes easier. We also examine the effects of interoperability, as the IT capability literature suggests that organizational capabilities mediate the relationship between IT and firm performance [62].

To empirically validate our model, we collected data from 194 organizations in the geospatial industry. Organizations in the geospatial industry create, communicate, and use spatial information in their commercial or institutional processes. IOS standards developed and promoted by its industry consortium, Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC; www.opengeospatial.org), have been widely adopted worldwide.

We organize the paper as follows. In the next section, a conceptual framework is proposed based on the IOS literature and IT capability research. The model is then tested on users of OGC standards in the Empirical Study section. Finally, we summarize the research by discussing key findings, research implications, as well as limitations and potential directions for future research.

## Theory Development

## Assessment of Prior Literature

Since we explore how interoperability is built and its performance effects, we review and analyze the key literature of IT capability and firm performance related to IOS implementation (summarized in Table 1). Most prior studies examine general IOS applications, with the only exception being Venkatesh and Bala’s study [64], which focused on standards underlying IOS. The current study also explores IOS standards and could enrich our understanding of the role of standards in interorganizational relationships. IOS standards automate interfirm business processes, and thus influences from trading partners are important. Prior research mainly explores typical buyer–supplier relationships in a value network. Some articles consider dyadic buyer– supplier relationships [28, 50, 59, 64], while others study influences from upstream suppliers [47, 48]. Interoperability aims for coordination and collaboration in a value network that is no longer limited to typical buyer–supplier relationships. We study standards facilitating geospatial Internet-based information sharing, and the relevant value networks include upstream suppliers, downstream customers, IT vendors, as well as potential new partners that have adopted the same standards. Therefore, we revise the network effects construct to better represent such an extended value network.

Table 1 illustrates that prior studies have not investigated system adoption, capability building, and performance effects simultaneously in a holistic model. Rai et al. [48] and Venkatesh and Bala [64] have identified drivers and consequences of IOS adoption. While they confirm positive performance effects from IOS adoption, they cannot adequately explain the mechanism through which IOS contributes to firm performance [5]. Others studies propose various IT capabilities that help firms leverage interorganizational relationships [47, 49, 50]. Rai et al. [49] suggest that IT infrastructure integration capability leads to better supply chain process integration capability. Rai and Tang [47] find that two structural IT capabilities help firms achieve competitive process capabilities. Although those IT capabilities have been clearly defined and measured, their formation mechanisms have not been explored. The link between IT usage and IT capabilities is missing and it is unclear how firms develop those capabilities. Zhu and Kraemer [72] include both IT capabilities and technology usage in their model, but treat them as independent antecedents of the business value of a firm. As a result, questions such as whether IOS deployment matters in building IT capabilities still remain unanswered. To fill the gap, our model proposes that IT capabilities mediate the relationship between IOS standards adoption and organizational performance gains. We clearly demonstrate that the development of IT capabilities depends on related technology implementation. In the next subsection, we conceptualize and operationalize interoperability based on the IT capability literature and highlight how it differs from other relevant capabilities discussed in prior research.

## Conceptualizing Interoperability in a Digital Value Network

Integrating both management and economics perspectives, IT capability research focuses on how firms create business value through their abilities to utilize IT resources to execute business processes [50, 63]. Several types of IT capabilities have been identified (see [38] for a good summary; see also [5]). Following the same theoretical lens, we define interoperability as a firm’s ability to manage disparate IS with trading partners in its extended value network. An interoperable firm’s IS can achieve integration and synchronized information sharing with multiple partners. It is built upon industry-wide open standards, whose adoption can lead to lower asset specificity, lower coordination costs, and better fit among partners [13]. Firms with interoperability can coordinate with multiple partners to more effectively manage a changing business environment and new technologies. Consider, for example, a situation in which an insurance company is negotiating with an institutional property insurance policyholder. Interoperability can help the company to quickly integrate its system with partners (e.g., the new policyholder, weather, and geospatial data providers) to collect critical spatial parameters, such as the locational distribution of involved assets and local climate information.

Interoperability differs from other related IT capabilities as follows. First, since interoperability focuses on the ability to exploit external IT resources embedded outside firm boundaries, other organizations’ standards adoption matters. In other words, interoperability is subject to environmental factors. To understand this dependency on other firms’ behaviors, we employ network effects theory to complement IT capability research.

Second, interoperability discussed in our study is built from IOS standards. Unlike supply chain process integration capability that examines financial, physical, and information flows across the supply chain [49], interoperability focuses on standardsenabled IT artifacts underlying those flows. Alliance capability, another similar construct, explores general long-term cooperation among firms at the strategic level [25], while interoperability studies system-centric coordination enabled by IOS standards at the operational level.

Furthermore, interoperability is modeled as a complex capability, since some simpler capabilities are needed before its establishment [7]. Specifically, we posit that effectively managing data flows within and across firm boundaries is a critical prerequisite of interoperability. ## Formation of Interoperability: Internal Capability Building

As noted by Grant [18], the formation of organizational capabilities may require the existence of more simple capabilities, often in a sequential manner. In this research, we propose standardized data infrastructure (SDI) as a more simple capability needed to build interoperability. SDI refers to firms’ ability to manage both data used internally and data prepared for and obtained from external partners. Within the firm, data should be defined consistently and made easily accessible. This is measured by data availability, a notion similar to data consistency discussed by Rai et al. [49]. In addition, a firm must make data available for its partners and must, in turn, acquire and process data from those partners. While a firm cannot control data availability beyond its boundary, it can manage interfirm data exchange activities. Thus, we introduce data connectivity to define the degree to which interfirm data-sharing processes have been standardized.

Figure 1. The Research Model

Mithas et al. [38] suggest that the ability to manage data is a foundational capability that influences the development of other organizational capabilities. Thus, we focus on SDI as the mediator between IOS standards adoption and interoperability. SDI is also an immediate goal of the IOS standards we study here, as the OGC was founded to address the inability to share geospatial data between systems [42].

To build SDI, firms need to adopt the corresponding standards. To capture the extent of IOS standards adoption coherently and comprehensively, we propose two conceptually different dimensions, volume and diversity [21, 57]. Volume refers to the extent to which data exchanges are specified by the standards [57, 73]. However, volume alone cannot explain the nature of data exchanges supported by the standards. Many open standards follow a modular architecture, where complex interorganizational processes are decomposed into subprocesses or activities and each subprocess is described by a standard module [32, 55]. In the case of OGC standards, for instance, there are over 30 standard modules, each specifying a subprocess for geospatial data sharing. Among them, for example, is the Geography Makeup Language, which defines XML grammars for expressing geographical features. Another module is the Keyhole Markup Language, which specifies how to depict geographic annotation and visualization on existing or future Web-based maps. Therefore, we use diversity as another dimension of standards adoption to represent the extent to which different value chain activities specified by IOS standards are actually utilized [36, 57].

Adopting IOS standards is a means to build SDI. Based on industry-wide specifications, firms translate their proprietary data into standardized formats [4]. Standards provide firms a common blueprint to establish consistent data definitions throughout their business processes. When implementing IOS standards, firms need to carefully examine their interorganizational workflows, identify inconsistencies with standard requirements, and undergo extensive process changes or even reengineering [8]. Consequently, standards adopters can more reliably and accurately capture, process, and store data, leading to improved data availability for their internal users. Many IOS standards are developed via consortia through extensive testing and consensus-seeking processes, and technical specifications are often accompanied by implementation guidelines and industry best practices summarized by the developing firms [69, 71]. Standards adopters can learn from experiences accumulated during the development stage to design interfirm data-sharing processes. Standardization also lessens dependency on relationship-specific investments and reduces coordination costs in value networks [13]. As a result, adopting consortia-based standards lowers the barriers for trading partners to reach agreements on information-sharing practices, leading to improved data connectivity. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: Firms’ SDI is positively influenced by their IOS standards adoption.

SDI focuses on technological configurations routinized by deploying IOS standards, while interoperability emphasizes process adaptability needed to manage heterogeneous trading partners in value networks. SDI is a critical enabler of the development of the higher-level capability, interoperability. SDI aims to create streamlined data flows within and across firm boundaries. It lays a key foundation for firms to establish, maintain, and reconfigure digital interfirm links. SDI allows a firm to access and share needed information with its partners in a timely fashion, leading to more effective coordination and joint decision making. SDI also leads to enhanced data reusability and improved visibility, which enable firms to decide with whom they wish to cooperate and integrate systems. As a result, firms can better design agile value networks [16]. Firms with SDI can manage their interfirm systems and procedures in a more synchronized manner than those without SDI. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: Firms’ interoperability is positively influenced by their SDI.

## Formation of Interoperability: External Community Readiness

Interoperability represents a firm’s ability to connect its information systems with its partners. Achieving interoperability becomes easier for the firm if its trading partners have adopted the same IOS standards. Those partners would be more prepared to integrate systems with the focal firm because of their shared understanding of the underlying technology specifications. Therefore, we expect that external community readiness, an environmental factor, could also shape the development of interoperability. Network effects, a well-established concept reflecting technology adoption by other organizations, are used to assess such readiness.

According to Katz and Shapiro [26], network effects can be both direct and indirect. Direct network effects depend on the number of adopters of the same product, and they can be further differentiated into local and global network effects [60]. Local network effects arises from the subset of adopters who have local or close social ties with the focal firm. In our case, a firm’s trading community, including its current upstream suppliers and downstream customers, is the key source of local network effects [73]. Global network effects are caused by the general population, which has weak or no current business ties with the focal firm. We use peer adoption to reflect such effects because it measures the extent of IOS standards diffusion among industry peers across the overall market [73]. Indirect network effects refer to the availability of complementary applications and services, which increases the value of the focal product [3]. The more complementary applications and services available for a particular technology in the market, the greater are its indirect network effects.

Developing organizational capability based on IOS standards is contingent on the status of technology adoption by others in the value network. Stronger network effects indicate the higher probability that a firm can easily connect and share data with its partners who have also followed the same standards. In other words, more adopters of the same standards and more available complementary technologies and services mean that there will be more opportunities for the focal firm to easily integrate with its partners, leading to better interoperability. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a: Firms’ interoperability is positively influenced by network effects.

In addition to the direct effects discussed above, network effects influence interoperability indirectly via their well-documented effects on technology adoption [27, 53]. According to Fichman and Kemerer [15], two perspectives explain adoption of technologies: (1) organizational adoption, that is, incentives and challenges organizations face internally when trying to adopt a new technology, and (2) network effects, that is, how well the technology has been adopted by other organizations. Network effects positively affect organizational adoption, as the adopting organization perceives the value of the technology to be higher and the cost of adoption to be lower if more organizations have adopted it. In other words, the larger the existing adoption base, the more likely a firm will choose to accept the candidate technology. More importantly, network effects are a source of increasing returns to adoption. Such increasing returns come from reduced adoption costs (the more people working with the standard, the lower the cost of using it) and technological interrelatedness (the more adopters, the more technologies supporting the standard) [15, 23]. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 3b: Firms’ IOS standards adoption is positively influenced by network effects.

## Interoperability’s Effect on Firm Performance

The improvement in firms’ performance comes in the form of both operational efficiency and strategic benefits [36, 39]. The former refers to a firm’s efficiency gains in handling day-to-day activities and processes [59] and the latter measures its enhanced competitiveness in the market.

In order to achieve both operational efficiency and strategic benefits in today’s global economy, firms need to manage their value networks efficiently by working closely with their trading partners [51]. An interoperable firm understands data and business processes that need to be shared among partners. Such knowledge is important to in establishing collaboration in a value network. Firms that have the ability to work with diverse systems and organizations can focus more on strategic collaboration than on daily and more mundane transactional details [32]. For instance, in the area of emergency response and disaster management, an up-to-date operational map can be created by integrating imagery data, hazards data, and route data provided by heterogeneous, yet interoperable, government agencies that have adopted OGC standards.

Consequently, organizations operating in different jurisdictions can promote dialogue, collaboration, and innovation concerning crisis management by discovering, sharing, and analyzing location information [9].

Furthermore, as technologies and markets evolve rapidly, interoperability can help firms mitigate disruptions and potential losses due to changes on their partners’ side, such as system upgrades or new system adoption. Such an ability makes it easy to terminate existing connections, should the need arise. Interoperable firms can be less concerned about being locked in, reducing vulnerabilities caused by opportunism of their existing partners [13]. They can also enjoy lower costs of starting new business relationships, which give them access to new markets, skills, and opportunities to share risks and enhance production efficiency [19]. Interoperability enables firms to respond to market fluctuations by adjusting their digital connections with partners, and makes them flexible, agile, and efficient. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4: Firms’ performance is positively influenced by their interoperability.

## The Empirical Study

## Research Context and Survey Sample

We collected data from OGC, which was established in 1994 as a not-for-profit international standard organization to meet the increasing demand for improved and faster geoprocessing. With the recent rapid growth in the adoption of GPS (global positioning system) devices and, correspondingly, in the location-based software and services market, significant needs for the development and adoption of geospatial standards have arisen not only from the industry but also from government agencies and research institutions. Sythoff and Morrison [61] estimated that the global locationbased services market is expected to reach \$10.3 billion in 2015, an increase from \$2.8 billion in 2010.

Our definition of the geospatial industry is broader than the traditional definition in that it encompasses not only the traditional market of geospatial IS software but also vertical sectors in which companies use geospatial data as a critical source of inputs in their business processes. As the examples used earlier indicate, those vertical sectors include insurance, construction, emergency response, geoscience, defense, and transportation, among others. When OGC defines GeoBI on its Web site, it indicates that “traditional geographic information systems (GIS) and image processing software products provide powerful techniques for dealing with geospatial data in geospatialcentric applications, but geospatial data and processing are no longer confined to those products. With the advent of the Web, and partly due to OGC standards, geospatial data has become an enabler for many mass-market applications, such as Web mapping and location based services” [43].

Since our objective is to explore how interoperability enables IOS standards users to realize business benefits, we focus only on firms that need to share geospatial data in value networks, not vendor firms that provide the GIS software to user firms. In this market, there are often multiple types of users that are involved in a business activity requiring location-based data. Some need to use geospatial data to conduct their business, while others provide such data. For example, hydrologic scientists from various research institutions, water companies, and environmental consulting firms utilize data that describe the state and location of water resources, both above and below the ground surface, provided by government agencies from around the world. Later, analyses can be provided as feedback allowing agencies to improve water resource management [42].

We developed a Web-based survey to collect data and distributed survey requests with assistance from OGC management. To maximize the statistical generalizability of our study, a multistep approach was adopted [52]. The first step is to clearly define the population of interest. We targeted all users of OGC standards, since our goal was to empirically examine how firms improve business performance by developing interoperability based on OGC standards. We conducted the survey in two stages in order to reach the target population. At the first stage, we sent a survey request to OGC members, the most likely users of standards, in May and June 2008. One hundred thirty responses were received, representing a 34 percent response rate.<sup>1</sup> At the second stage, from December 2008 to January 2009, to reach other users who were not official members of the consortium, we distributed the survey to an OGC e-mail list open to any individuals and organizations interested in OGC standards. We subsequently received 102 responses from nonmembers. We were unable to calculate the response rate for nonmember users, since we could not determine how many of the registered e-mail list participants were nonmember organizations that had implemented OGC standards. After excluding responses from IT vendors, the total number of usable responses was 194.

The second step is to understand the representativeness of the sample. Table 2 lists the key sample characteristics. Most of the respondents were from North America and Europe, as the OGC managers later confirmed that the consortium had well-established customer bases in these two regions. Our sample covered all vertical sectors for which the OGC intended to develop standards that would help them to “solve problems related to the creation, communication, and use of spatial information” [42]. The distribution of respondents indicated a balance between member and nonmember organizations, between large and small organizations, as well as between experienced and new users, three noticeable features of OGC standards users suggested by industry experts.

To further assess representativeness, we compared all the key variables used in the study between the early and late respondents [1, 52]. Since the survey was administered to members and nonmembers separately, we tested for bias separately as well. The analysis revealed no statistically significant differences. In addition, for members, we compared the respondents and nonrespondents in terms of membership level, geographic region, organizational type, vertical sector, and size, finding no statistically significant differences in any classification. Therefore, inferential statistics obtained from our sample can be applied to guide inferences about the target population [52].

## Instrument Development

We operationalized all the constructs, other than diversity, by multiple items measured with a seven-point Likert scale (see the Appendix for details). Diversity is a single-item construct measured by the number of standard modules an organization has adopted. We defined the term organization to mean any group (e.g., research centers, firms, or government agencies) to which the respondent belonged. To clarify the boundary of the survey, we specified in the cover letter that the survey concerned the OGC and its standards and defined partners as both suppliers and customers who worked with the respondent’s organization in areas where OGC standards were relevant.

Five first-order constructs—Trading Community Influence, Peer Adoption, Complementary Technologies and Services, Operational Efficiency, and Strategic Benefits— were specified as formative constructs due to their direction of causality from items to construct, as well as uninterchangeable and noncovariance items [17, 45].

Prior literature suggests that the overall network effects are a combination of local network effects, global network effects, and indirect network effects [26, 60]. Therefore, we model network effects as a formative second-order construct. To better reflect our research context, we rename these subdimensions as Trading Community Influence (i.e., local network effects), Peer Adoption (i.e., global network effects), and Complementary Technologies and Services (i.e., indirect network effects). The first two subdimensions were examined in Zhu et al.’s IOS study [73], whose measurement items have been adapted here. Complementary Technologies and Services are defined as the availability of various compatible solutions and supporting services, such as consulting and training. We developed five new items to measure this construct.

Firm performance is conceptualized as two subconstructs: operational efficiency and strategic benefits [32, 59]. We used subjective measures, a commonly adopted approach in the IS field (e.g., [49, 59]). Operational efficiency is composed of productivity, reduced cost, reduced integration time, responsiveness, and flexibility in adding new technology components [45, 49]. IOS standards enable firms to more quickly integrate new applications and components into their existing systems, because these standards typically serve as an overarching framework for existing, ongoing, and future standard modules. For example, the OGC provides the OGC reference model to describe “the relationships between the OGC standards” [42]. Strategic benefits include better customer service, better understanding of market segments and customer needs, new business opportunities, new products, and enhanced partner relationships [59].

Other multi-item first-order constructs are reflective because they are manifestations of the construct, interchangeable, and covary [17, 45]. Data availability and data connectivity are two subdimensions forming SDI. The former was developed based on the data consistency notion in Rai et al. [49]. The latter refers to how well interfirm data exchange processes have been established, and is measured by two newly developed items. Interoperability, another newly developed construct, is defined as a firm’s ability to integrate with heterogeneous partners’ systems.

Three newly proposed multi-item constructs were developed based on procedures suggested by MacKenzie et al. [31]. After clearly defining the conceptual definition of the constructs, two authors proposed items to represent them. To further clarify the measurement items and evaluate their content validity, we employed two methods: (1) we incorporated suggestions from experts that were obtained via multiple rounds of teleconferencing and e-mail exchanges with OGC executives, and (2) we interviewed people from seven OGC user organizations who represent different organization types and geographic regions. The face-to-face interviews were conducted at a week-long OGC technical committee meeting in March 2008. Those representatives were in charge of their organizations’ standards adoption. They went through the entire questionnaire with us, assessed content adequacy, and provided valuable feedback to improve the survey design.

## Control Variables

We included several control variables to account for other heterogeneities among our survey respondents. Firms’ performance may depend on size, since larger organizations may have superior abilities to leverage scale efficiencies and reap further benefits from implementing IOS standards [22]. Therefore, we added firm size as a control variable. Firms with more adoption experiences may be in a stronger position to fully realize the business value of adopting the standards. We thus added years of usage as a second control variable. In our case, IOS standards are developed by the OGC, an open consortium, and firms have the option to get involved in the standards development process [68, 69, 71]. Firms may perceive more value from the standards if they can gear the specifications toward their proprietary needs and preferences by working in the consortium. Therefore, we controlled for OGC membership status with a dichotomous variable, where 1 represented a member organization and 0 otherwise. In addition, we added several dummy variables in order to differentiate organization types, regions, and vertical sectors.

## Measurement Validation

Our measurement model includes both formative and reflective constructs as well as formative second-order constructs. To assess content validity, we either used validated items from prior literature or asked domain experts to refine items to achieve representativeness [58]. We then used the survey data to examine the psychometric properties of the scale.

## Common Method Bias

We follow guidelines suggested by Podsakoff et al. [46] to address common method bias, to make accommodations for the usage of the single survey method. At the design stage, OGC managers and representatives from seven user organizations reviewed the survey. Revision based on their comments reduced common method variance by addressing item complexity and ambiguity. At the reporting stage, we told the respondents that the survey was completely anonymous and that no individual data would be disclosed at any time. Doing so may alleviate the method effects by common raters. In addition, we applied three different statistical techniques to control for common method variance [46]. First, we ran the Harman’s one-factor test, and the results indicated that there was more than one factor that accounted for the majority of covariance. Second, we adopted partial correlation procedures to include a general factor and link it to all of the constructs in the model. The general factor was identified from the first unrotated factor [58]. After adding the general factor into the model, it was found that the structural model was not affected greatly. The increase level of R<sup>2</sup> is much lower than the level suggested by Williams et al. [67]. Third, we followed the procedures of the marker-variable technique to assess common method variance [33]. We selected the smallest correlation in the correlation matrix as the marker variable, and only 19 percent of significant correlations became nonsignificant when adjusted for common method variance. The result suggested that common method bias was not a concern in our research design [33].

## Results

We used PLS to test the structural model (Table 8). A bootstrapping procedure with 500 iterations was performed to examine the statistical significance of the path coefficients [10]. $R ^ { 2 }$ was used to demonstrate how well the endogenous variables were explained by their antecedents. It was found that 33.5 percent of the variance in IOS standards adoption was explained by network effects, 12.4 percent of the variance in SDI was explained by standards adoption, and 34.0 percent of the variance in interoperability was explained by SDI and network effects. Our model explained 23.3 percent of the variance in firm performance. These values are comparable to previous IS research [49, 59]. The magnitude and significance of the structural paths, strong loadings of reflective constructs, significant weights of formative constructs, and adequate $R ^ { 2 }$ demonstrate a good overall model fit [10].

Table 8 indicates that all our hypotheses are supported. As we expected, IOS standards adoption leads to SDI $( \beta = 0 . 3 5 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which lays a foundation for interoperability $( \beta > 0 . 5 0 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Network effects influence both standards adoption $( \beta = 0 . 5 7 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and interoperability $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Finally, interoperability, an organizational capability developed from implementing IOS standards, positively and significantly affects firm performance $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 0 , p < 0 . 1 )$

Interestingly, none of the control variables have significant effects. A firm’s size has little impact on its performance. It could be that as XML-based IOS standards reduce the costs and complexity of system implementation, both large and small organizations can adopt them to obtain related business benefits [35, 73]. A longer history of using OGC standards does not lead to more benefits. While earlier adopters might gain more experience, they could face a lower-quality product than later adopters [14] because standards development is an iterative and continuous process. Furthermore, users obtain similar business value from OGC standards adoption no matter what their organization types are, which geographic region they come from, which vertical sector they operate in, and whether they are OGC members or not.

Having proposed two mediators in the model [49, 59], we tested the mediation effect in two ways. First, we did nested models comparison, where the proposed fully mediated model was compared to a competing partially mediated model. When the direct effect from standards adoption to interoperability was added, the $R ^ { 2 }$ of interoperability increased from 0.34 in the fully mediated model to 0.344 in the partially mediated model. Applying a procedure similar to the one used to test the nested model in stepwise linear regression, the pseudo $F ( 1 , 1 7 2 )$ statistic was 0.003, which was nonsignificant. The result suggests that SDI fully mediates the relationship between standards adoption and interoperability. After adding the direct effect from SDI to firm performance, the $R ^ { 2 }$ of performance increased from 0.233 in the fully mediated model to 0.269 in the partially mediated model. Consequently, the pseudo F (1, 172) was 0.019, indicating that interoperability fully mediates the relationship between SDI and firm performance. When running a series of regression models, we also confirmed that mediation conditions suggested by Baron and Kenny [2] were also satisfied, since adding mediators reduced the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. Second, we employed Sobel’s [56] approach to conduct a significance test for individual mediated paths. We calculated both the magnitude of the mediation effects and the standardized deviation of the mediated paths. The resulting z-values were 107.74 for SDI and 18.63 for interoperability, which were significant at $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . These findings further confirmed the statistical significance of the mediation effects originally proposed by our model.

## Discussion

In this research, we investigate interoperability, an important organizational capability built from IOS standards. Interoperability focuses on the ability to coordinate and reconfigure digital connections with multiple partners. It is critical in the interorganizational setting, because it enables firms to become agile and adaptive, and create synergies and new value-generating opportunities with trading partners. Similar to other IT capabilities, interoperability drives operational excellence and firm competitiveness [38]. For example, in order to optimize flight scheduling, airlines need to combine air traffic data, weather data, and flight location and status data. Such aeronautical data and weather information are remotely captured by various sensors owned by different organizations. To effectively manage the multitude of flights in numerous locations on a daily basis, airlines need to have the ability to access, process, and analyze geospatial data from multiple sources using heterogeneous systems. With the support of OGC standards, airlines can establish a consistent interface for interfirm data exchanges and more efficiently integrate their internal systems with their partners’ systems. Such interoperable geoprocessing and coordinated efforts can lead to reduced operational costs, shorter waiting time, and improved customer satisfaction and loyalty.

While standards adoption lays the foundation for firms to develop interoperability, its impact is fully mediated by SDI. SDI considers managing data flows within (internal data availability) and across firm boundaries (external data connectivity). However, having streamlined data flows does not mean a firm is interoperable, since true interoperability requires firms to be agile and ready for more radical process and institutional changes. Interoperable firms should be willing to constantly examine their value network relationships, be open to new partnership opportunities, and actively manage their external networks. Furthermore, our findings suggest that interoperability is not only dependent on firms’ internal IT resources but also the external business environment.

## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes several important contributions to both IT capability studies and the IT standardization literature. First, we conceptualize interoperability as a managerial IT skill, a key attribute of IT leading to firms’ success [37]. Interoperability focuses “less on IT, per se, and more on the process of organizing and managing IT” [37, p. 500]. It requires managers to have socially complex abilities to enhance the breadth and depth of value network relationships. Specifically, managers need to understand the potential of IOS standards, modify business processes to establish a standardized data infrastructure, and anticipate and support IT needs to coordinate with trading partners.

Second, we provide a deep understanding of network effects associated with IOS standards. The goal of IOS standards is to promote industry-wide connectivity [35]. Thus, their adoption decisions and business effects are not limited to the traditional dyadic business relationship examined in prior literature [29, 59]. To capture this characteristic, this study is one of the first that empirically examines three types of network effects: direct local network effects, direct global network effects, and indirect network effects. We find that, somewhat surprisingly, peer adoption (i.e., direct global network effects) is more important than trading community adoption (i.e., direct local network effects). While firms still care about the adoption of their immediate partners, they are more concerned with their peers’ adoption. One potential explanation is that the ultimate success of IOS standards depends on wide diffusion among peers, not just firms’ current partners. Peer adoption also expands IOS standards market reach and enables firms to establish new partnerships beyond their preexisting relationships.

Third, we reveal that network effects influence not only standards adoption but also standards-related capability building. To achieve interoperability, firms’ unilateral efforts are necessary but not sufficient. Involvement from all related parties in value networks is crucial, and network effects capture such important community-wide standards adoption.

Fourth, our research examines standards underlying GIS, an underrepresented area in the mainstream IS literature [65]. GIS is multidisciplinary in nature because it integrates a wide range of geospatial data related to location, climate, infrastructure, and natural resources. Consequently, the geospatial industry is a diverse and information-intensive industry. Providers and users of geospatial data represent different organizational types, vertical sectors, and geographic regions. Yet all of them can benefit from geospatial interoperability enabled by OGC standards.

## Research Implications

Our study offers several implications for both standards-adopting firms and consortia managers. Our findings suggest the importance of developing a key organizational capability to realize superior firm performance from standards adoption. Such capability enables firms to respond to changing technologies and market conditions, manage evolving business interactions more effectively, and focus on in-depth collaboration with a large set of partners. Firms also need to closely monitor the adoption decisions of others. It is challenging for firms to establish interoperability based on a given standard if its market is not very mature and other firms have not committed to it.

Our findings suggest that organizations’ incentives are generally aligned with the mission of industry consortia, since adopting industry-wide standards is beneficial to firms. Thus, consortia managers should actively promote the diffusion of their standards in addition to developing them. Network effects are a salient driver of standards adoption and affect how firms build interoperability. Consortium managers need to promote standards to general audiences outside the consortium. They can facilitate development of complementary technologies and services to encourage new user adoption. For example, consortia can create online tutorials or organize workshops to demonstrate step-by-step implementation guidelines to new users.

Although our model was tested on standards adopters in the geospatial industry, the findings are not limited to that industry. Changing technologies and business conditions occur in many industries and interoperability will assist them in managing value network dynamics. For many industries that actively develop and promote open IOS standards [40, 69], firms are able to develop interoperability based on industry-wide connectivity. However, in industries that fail to develop an IOS standard [35], developing interoperability is hindered by the need to deal with multiple rival/incompatible standards with diverse partners. Furthermore, given the generalizable nature of our survey instruments, they can be applied to other industries with only minor changes.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are several notable limitations of our study that offer opportunities for future research. First, we examined the business benefits of IOS standards from the users perspective. However, IT vendors represent another type of standard adopters. Vendors and users have different interests in standards, as users implement them for improved cooperation with partners, while vendors adopt them with the goal of selling standardcompliant products to users [34, 70, 71]. It would be interesting to investigate whether vendors can benefit from IOS standards and, if so, how.

Second, we validated our model using data collected from the geospatial industry. Future research might test the model in other areas that actively develop industrywide IOS standards.

Third, our proposed measures for several new constructs, such as interoperability and complementary technologies and services, are open to further validation. For example, SDI and network effects explained 34 percent of the variance of interoperability. Exploring whether other complementary organizational resources, such as managerial support and interorganizational trust, could lead to better interoperability would be interesting. More rigorous scale development techniques, such as rater matrix and MIMIC (multiple indicators, multiple causes), might improve the validity of these newly proposed constructs [31]. Furthermore, data collected from new samples could be utilized to cross-validate the scales in order to develop a norm for them [31].

Fourth, this study focused on creating business value from the focal firm’s perspective. It is unclear how a value network might benefit from IOS standards. In addition, we relied on subjective measures for firm performance because of our interest in performance gains specifically created by IOS standards, and accounting-based measures were not available at the time. Furthermore, many organizations in our sample were private firms and not-for-profit organizations that, in general, did not have publicly accessible accounting-based performance measures. Future research efforts may thus take a different approach. For instance, longitudinal case studies could be conducted to track objective performance gains contributed by standards adoption.

Finally, future studies can apply a process-oriented paradigm to address the limitations of our variance-based model [34]. For instance, the process-oriented paradigm might examine the evolving interactions between standards development and adoption [34]. Our cross-sectional data demonstrated that more standards adoption leads to improved firm performance via enhanced interoperability. The process-oriented model can further test for causality suggested by our model.

## Conclusion

In the current com petitive environm ent, firms appear to be adopting IOS standards at an increasing rate to improve interorganizational information sharing and coordination, and ultimately achieve better firm performance. To do so, our study suggests that IOS standards adopters need to establish streamlined data flows in order to achieve interoperability, a valuable organizational capability. We hope our work will encourage researchers to further explore issues related to IOS standards and interoperability, which are critical to the management of digital value networks.
