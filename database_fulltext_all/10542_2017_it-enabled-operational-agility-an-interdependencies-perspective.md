## Abstract

Firms make large investments in enterprise information technology (IT) expecting positive impacts on their supply chain operations. Toward IT-enabled operational agility, an enterprise system facilitates the responsibilities of multiple stakeholders; however, its success is not determined merely by its adoption. Firms must contend with information asymmetries and resource interdependencies simultaneously. To this end, this study presents a model that illustrates how IT can be used to accomplish operational agility at a firm. The stagewise model suggests that toward IT-enabled operational agility, new capabilities are enacted to manage resource interdependencies, the process of negotiation and managerial practices that shape IT use in supply chain tasks. Through our findings, our study extends existing prescriptions on firm interdependencies and presents a set of actionable guidelines to help managers better engage with technology toward the attainment of IT-enabled operational agility.

Keywords: Operational agility, resource interdependencies, IT capabilities, enterprise systems, case study

## 2 Introduction

Operational agility refers to the ability to sense and execute the identification and implementation of business opportunities quickly, accurately, and cost-efficiently, and is an important determinant for the success of the firm [1]. This is an essential capability that, when leveraged, allows a firm to sense environmental changes and respond effectively, especially within emerging dynamic markets [2]. Enterprise-wide information technology (IT) plays an important role in enabling operational agility in the firms and in supply chains. Accordingly, many scholars have contributed to the knowledge of IT-enabled operational agility in this context [3-5]. In practice, however, although often carefully rationalized in advance, firms fail to realize all the expected benefits immediately post IT implementation, such that expectations of packaged solutions like enterprise systems are often disassociated from their continuance [6, 7]. An understanding of the developmental and management aspects of ITenabled operational agility continues to elude research and practice [3].

Management by objective works - if you know the objectives. Ninety percent of the time you don't...most of what we call management consists of making it difficult for people to get their work done − Peter Drucker

Organizations can take years to realize the full potential of IT projects [8, 9]. Hence, they are facing ever-increasing pressure to justify IT investments [10]. Practitioners are increasingly aware of the role of IT systems in supporting rapid responses to immediate business challenges. For example, the annual Operational Agility Forum, which was convened in 2009 by senior business and IT professionals, focuses on exploring new ways of empowering business operations to deliver operational agility with IT tools. The above quote from Peter Drucker, a renowned management guru, highlights both the information asymmetries and the management practices (or lack of) in a firm that can impede the completion of firm tasks. Often, information asymmetries in the requirements necessary to facilitate workflow coordination, overcoming uncertainty in decision-making [11], and resolving organizational conflicts [12] between departments (e.g., in resource allocation) can pose a threat to cooperative processes [13]. Furthermore, complex IT tools such as enterprise systems often induce a cultural shift that unites and coordinates core organizational entities. Research highlights that the ability of such systems to integrate and automate business process would

ultimately lead to synchronous coordination of organizational entities. Furthermore, these

systems provide the potential to continuously innovate and support agility within the

organization [14]. Hence, in recent times, scholars have called for research across IT

capability profiles with varying levels of uncertainty, and also for research looking beyond

technology to structures, processes, or people who interact with IT in enabling agility [15].

Against this backdrop, our research question is: How do firms use IT to achieve operational

agility? Addressing this research question is important because despite ample conceptual

evidence corroborating the role of IT in enhancing agility, we still know surprisingly little

about the development of IT-enabled operational agility [15, 16]. Operational agility requires

new management practices, processes, structures, or techniques at the operational level that

provide insights into what managers do and how they influence and negotiate [17] to achieve

the competitive advantage they seek [18]. However, the empirical research to date has not

examined the influence of organizational capabilities and IT in the accomplishment of

operational agility [19]. This knowledge gap severely undermines the realization of benefits in

supply chain-enabling commercial packages, such as advanced planning systems [20] as well

as scheduling and controlling systems [21].

By using a multicase study design, this study examines how enterprise-wide IT systems are

used to enable supply chains in firms in India and Australia in an attempt to address the above

challenges. We seek to extend the literature on IT-enabled management strategies by

addressing a significant gap in the literature regarding how the approach to managing

interdependencies may change over time as a function of IT-enabled business processes in

order to achieve operational agility and arrive at the desired resource-based decisions. From

our analysis of the empirical data, we derived a model to illustrate how firms use IT to

accomplish supply chain operational agility. In our proposed model, operational agility is achieved through the development of coordination loops across planning, production, and order fulfillment.

## 3 2 Literature Review: IT-Enabled Operational Agility

Agility, the capability of a firm to rapidly sense and adapt to changes through a dynamic network of linkages in different processes [22-24], is a critical determinant of a firm’s success in responding to customer demands and producing product and service value for consumers [3-5]. The notion of agility has emerged as a key business imperative as, increasingly, unexpected market changes and uncertainty compel firms to consider their ability to respond to change. Conceptually, this is similar to achieving operational agility, which is defined as the ability to execute the identification and implementation of business opportunities quickly, accurately, and cost-efficiently [in 1]. A growing body of the literature suggests that IT systems play an important enabling role for operational agility in the firms and for their supply chains as they allow the firm to sense environmental changes and to respond effectively [2, 16]. The undertaking of (IT and business) alignment can be thought of as a sensing capability, while changing structures through IT mechanisms could be viewed as a response capability [15, 16].

Our review of the strategic management, organizational studies, and information system (IS) literature suggests that, in practice, humans are not merely tools, and their coordination plays an essential role in defining firms’ operational agility and supply chains. At the outset, it is argued in the literature that the knowledge about hierarchical planning, goods, structures, and algorithms is still insufficiently developed to allow for easy implementation using IT tools [25]. Management and IS studies are generally in agreement that the implementation and use of IT must be accompanied by new practices to manage the levels of task and resource interdependence that exist between business units [26-28] because the benefits of new

#

practices are partially offset by the learning curves associated with them [29, 30]. Conversely,

the bulk of supply chain management (SCM)-related literature focuses less on the nature of

goods supplied and organizations’ operating mantras and more on planning systems and

supply chain process improvement. For instance, Stadtler [31] looked at the main

shortcomings of the software for advanced planning in the supply chain; Meyr et al. [32]

looked at the software modules covering the supply chain; while Lodree [33] investigated a

supply chain system solution to backlogged items, lost sales, and contractual disagreements.

Although these studies make important contributions to the understanding of how processes

are optimized using such IT platforms, they also reveal the significance of sociotechnical

issues related to the complex organizational mechanisms that underpin their performance.

Nevertheless, the challenge is that these issues remain understudied [34, 35]. Recent work

suggests that operational agility not only requires at least the construction of an IT-enabled

information processing network but also the implementation of organizational controls to

enhance the right information processing capability [4]. Despite a general awareness, we are

in agreement with other scholars [3, 15, 16] that an adequate understanding of the

development of operational agility still eludes research and practice.

Recent IS literature reveals a relationship among agility, alignment, and resources, such that the alignment of IT capabilities and business strategy is necessary to foster agility, and is positively associated with firm performance [15, 16]. For example, it has been argued that the organizational capabilities that create alignment are the same capabilities that allow alignment to foster agility [16]. Hence, given that a true test of agility and its implications for performance lies in how easily and quickly firms can revise their behaviors, another relevant challenge is understanding how business leaders can create an environment for organizational agility [36], especially when different business units may use a system differently. Moreover, when the business units themselves are working in tandem, new structures and practices must exist in order for the system to understand operational goals. For this purpose, managers must know how to configure their operations. Alignment is crucial to direct key resources enabling firms to shift and pursue new strategies. This creates a trade-off between resource commitment to current goals and the ability to adapt those goals based on shifting market needs [16]. Hence, from an alignment viewpoint, it is reasonable to accept that for complex enterprise systems, which require substantial resources and coordination, further theoretical scrutiny of how alignment enables agility from a resource perspective would be useful. This aligns with calls for further research into different IT and business profiles, their varying levels of uncertainty, structures, path dependencies, and routines associated with the accomplishment of IT-enabled agility [15, 16]. On this basis, we turn to the literature on resource interdependencies as the starting point of our enquiry.

## 4 3 Theoretical Foundation: Organizational Interdependencies

All organizations develop interdependencies and the more complex ones can consist of several types [37], such that different task and resource interdependencies would demand different coordination methods in terms of rules and operating procedures, adaptive planning and scheduling, and mutual adjustments at the very least [38]. A low level of interdependence typically implies that the tasks performed between departments and/or workers are fairly repetitive and familiar, which means that the exceptions that can occur during task execution are relatively few. In lower levels of interdependence, the relative certainty about objectives also implies highly standardized processes where the decision-making process is simple and the outcome is fairly well predicted [11]. However, a high level of interdependence typically implies that the tasks are likely to create an increasing level of uncertainty in the decisionmaking context. In higher levels of interdependence, products or projects tend to be highly

#

customized and the absence of standardization makes it difficult to anticipate which problems will be encountered during task execution [11]. The effect of interdependencies in firms is a research concept that has garnered a significant amount of research attention [38-41] because it facilitates an understanding of both intrafirm and interfirm decisions on performance [42]. Hence, interdependence dictates how departments and/or workers depend upon each other for resources to accomplish their tasks [11, 43]. Increasing the interdependence between departments and workers poses significant challenges as it creates additional information requirements to facilitate workflow coordination and creates higher levels of uncertainty in decision-making between departments [11]. Hence, high or low levels of interdependence pose acute challenges when deciding the appropriate configurations of a firm’s activities that would facilitate the attainment of competitive advantage [44, 45].

The extent of work interdependence can range from autonomy (for the lowest form of pooling interdependency), to the linkage of organizational departments in a serial manner (for sequential interdependence), to project and mutual adjustment among specialized departments (the highest form of reciprocal interdependence) [11, 43, 46]. As interdependence grows, the information needed must correspondingly become more current, timely, and pertain to unpredictable events. For instance, as tasks become increasingly interdependent (such as data mining and coordination), the exchange of current information in a timely manner is necessary to ensure that activities are on schedule, and that resources are used for analytical and strategic use. IT systems play a strategic role in network coordination and as a control mechanism [17] of the interdependent relationships in a firm [12]. The premise is that uncertainty and organizational conflicts, typically over resources and decision-making, can pose a threat to cooperative processes, and the use of IT may potentially reduce the risk of loss of resource control [12]. When relationships between business units are highly

#

interdependent, the need for structure is low. A less structured relationship requires more

complex and ambiguous mechanisms, and is not likely to fully account for the contingencies

in the relationships [12].

Commercial software products are accompanied by vendor hype and stories of Commercial software products are accompanied by vendor hype and stories of implementation failure. Hence, the benefits of new practices are partially offset by the learning curves associated with them [29] and organizational lag [47]. Hence, new management practices and capabilities have to be developed [19] to alleviate the disappointment (despite the purported benefits) when firms latch onto a single new practice o technology and expect it to solve their problems. These strategic management practices, processes, structures, or techniques intended to further organizational goals that can be generated and implemented without known precedent [19]. Despite the existence of knowledge of new management practices and capabilities to achieve IT-enabled operational agility, few studies have looked at how they may be applied [4]. Although this form of innovation is posited as the most important determinant of firm performance [19], the research to date has mainly focused on innovation as an outcome [48]. One way to examine this process of innovation is to look at the actions of the managers who champion IT use to drive organizational change [49, 50]. Leadership in innovation is linked with organizational and contextual factors, and is effected by managers who play direct and indirect roles in coordinating innovation processes and relationships [48]. Incidentally, in the IS literature, Nah et al. [51] and Shang and Seddon [52] have illustrated the value and appropriateness of managers’ inputs for software implementation and adoption success. Furthermore, managers implement deductive innovation structures through their decisions and actions to deliver innovation [53]. In addition, we posit that the resource interdependencies and tasks that exist in firms exert managerial influences on the firms’ capabilities to rapidly adapt and respond to changes.

## 5 4 Research Method

The present study adopts a case study design [54], where we focus on particular cases [55] of IT system use by managers to support tasks across the supply chain. We adopt a multicase approach because it allows for comparison, and addresses, to some extent, the limitations associated with generalizability and observer bias in single-case studies [56, 57]. Given that research into the developmental aspects of resource interdependencies is still nascent and our research question is a “how” question [58], a case study methodology is especially appropriate. This design emphasizes a systematic way of looking at the events, collecting data, analyzing information, and reporting the results. As such, the case approach is appropriate because we seek in-depth answers to an exploratory and investigative set of “how” questions. We analyzed the breadth and depth of managers’ interactions with the IT systems, focusing on reflections on the manner of completing or the actual activities completed in the supply chain. We also analyzed the development of cognitive states when a manager interacted with the IT systems, from tentative and transactional to more fluent and mature. Hence, we covered the depth and proficiency demonstrated by a manager when interacting with the system at that level at a point in time.

The selection of our case studies and the interviewees was determined by a number of criteria. First, the use of enterprise IT systems should be extensive in the firm or across firms, and for a variety of work processes for several years. This is because the extensiveness of IT use across work processes and for a considerable amount of time would allow us to examine the IT-enabled relationships among resources, structure, and routines and their effect on operational agility in depth. Second, all the interviewees must have held managerial positions in their organizations for at least 1 year, which ensured that all interviewees were experienced in long-term postimplementation enterprise system use. Furthermore, all the interviewees must have participated in system adoption and used the system for a period of time. Lastly, the firms should be largely successful, enjoying robust growth, and known to be responsive to changing market demands and conditions. The firms should also be sufficiently large, so that complex supply chain processes and resource interdependencies can be studied. On the basis of our selection criteria, our first case study is Aus-P, a major milk and dairy supplier for large and small distributors and retailers in Australia. The case centers on how Aus-P managed the expectations of its IT system (SCMS) in facilitating its supply chain, against the backdrop of its operating mantra as well as the nature of its business, namely, fast-moving consumer goods (FMCGs) – highly perishable foods with a very limited shelf-life such as milk and dairy products [69]. Our second case study is AM Province, a group of four large utility firms operating in a provincial state in India. The case centers on how the firms operated to provide agri-service, fuel, utilities, and medical products for the provincial state. Both cases represent established manufacturers with complex supply chain processes and have experienced uncertainty in their IT-enabled transformations.

All the potential interviewees were initially contacted by e-mail. From the original pool of approximately 30 candidates contacted, 14 interviewees across the two studies confirmed their participation and were subsequently contacted to arrange interview timings and to obtain their working-profile details. The demographics and profile descriptions of each of the 14 interviewees and their organizations are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

## 5.1 4.1 Data Collection and Analysis

Data collection was performed in line with the principles for conducting interpretive field studies [59]. Data were collected for a period of 2 years, that is, from 2010 to 2012. Data from the interviews were supplemented with data gathered from company publications and corporate websites to provide a more holistic picture of the cases. The multicase synthesis adopted in this research design provides in-depth insights into how firms accomplish ITenabled operational agility. Primary data collection process was conducted through face-toface and telephone interviews. Each interview lasted 60–90 min. We used an interview protocol [60] to facilitate the interview [61]. The sample questions in the protocol are attache in Appendix 1. We did not maintain strict adherence to the protocol [62] and included a mixture of probing questions [63], as we sought to retain openness and variability during the interviews [64]. The protocol did not indicate the order of the questions during the interview, as the order was adapted according to the flow and direction of each interview. When new themes were identified in the initial interviews, new questions were added to the protocol to enable the emergent themes to be explored in subsequent interviews. The interviews were recorded and transcribed to ensure that a complete record was maintained [65, 66], and data analysis was performed simultaneously with data gathering. We analyzed the statements from one manager and compared other details of the interview with our theoretical underpinnings. We continued to compare the statements with the second, third, and subsequent interview notes. Constantly moving back and forth between the initial statements, the first sets of statements from the interview data and theoretical perspectives while consulting the relevant literature became the foundation of coding and data analysis.

We performed open, axial, and selective coding on the collected data and notes [67] and documents from the secondary data collection process. During open coding, we assigned conceptual labels to commonly occurring data such as “system standardization” and “coding structure” that captured interfirm activities and operational processes. We present the corroborating evidence from our interviews for these labels in Tables 3, 4, and 5. The purpose

#

of open coding was to derive a manageable set of categories from the voluminous amount of data we collected [65]. Next, we identified commonalities and variations in the data assigned to each theme in a process of axial coding [67]. We recorded them as subthemes, as presented in Tables 3, 4, and 5. The purpose of axial coding was to abstract a set of conditions, interactions, and processes. The subthemes “develop network” and “IT coordination,” for instance, were processes for developing resource sensing in planning between sides. We compared the data on each theme and subtheme from case to case in line with the process described by Merriam [68]. While doing this, the similarities and differences among the managers’ experiences in IT use for supply chain operations became evident.

From the emerging data, we discovered patterns and developed further mappings of the coded responses. We discovered diverse IT capability profiles [e.g. 15] with varying levels of uncertainty, as well as their interdependencies and implications. Axial coding was succeeded by selective coding to generate theory, whereby explanations of phenomena (e.g., actions and processes) emerged. We focused on the multicase synthesis of firms’ processes [17] in the supply chain as a vehicle for the integration of the major themes, thereby developing and refining our theoretical claims. To organize our findings, we adopted a pluralistic approach [17] to ascertain the assumptions of interaction between interdependent sides, the ways in which managers negotiated the technologies, and the outcomes of the negotiation. While analyzing the cases, by following the theoretical themes identified and for each process category, we first excavated the independence between constituents in completing a supply chain task, highlighting the assumptions, expectations, and interests of all sides. We then synthesized the data into several themes about resource interdependencies, firm capabilities, and IT-enabled agility in which the content, process, structure, and context differed. This revealed the challenges confronting the firm, as well as how actions and mechanisms were

executed with the implementation of the enterprise IT systems. Finally, we compared the mappings to shape our conceptualization of the phenomenon of interest, and inductively derived an emergent model that consolidates the different patterns of exercising resource interdependencies amidst various firm responses.

## 6 5 Findings

As explained in the preceding section, theoretical themes were identified from our case data and grouped with their representative quotes from both sets of the case study interviews. According to the sequence of supply chain processes, we further divided our data into three process categories: planning (including procurement) tasks, production tasks, and distribution tasks. We present our findings in this section. Across both cases, we examined how capabilities were developed to promote IT-enabled operational agility in the supply chain. The analysis is based on the recognition that an agile supply chain is a critical determinant of firms success in responding to customer demands and producing product and service value for consumers [3-5]. Supply chain performance can be measured by how well a system’s modules support the cash-to-cash cycle – in other words, how well the modules support the material flow across a supply chain and related value-adding business functions such as procurement, production, distribution, and sales. Herein, the planning process (short term to long term) has a major impact on all the processes [31]. As discussed in our literature review, the undertaking of (IT and business) alignment can be thought of as a sensing capability, and changing structures through IT mechanisms could be viewed as response capability [15, 16]. Finally, the outcomes related to operational agility in the supply chain processes, resulting from the negotiating of capabilities, were identified.

## 6.1 5.1 Planning Tasks

Achieving operational agility in planning and procurement is critical to develop integration, coordination, and unison between business units, and ultimately efficiency at both case organizations. Particularly in the case of Aus-P, the nature of FMCGs therefore poses many challenges for a firm’s cash-to-cash cycle and its planning systems. FMCGs tend to be sold quickly at varying, often relatively low cost; the absolute profits made on FMCGs are relative and FMCGs, in general, are sold in large quantities. Price elasticity – the fluctuating consumer demand caused by price movements – is a key consideration in forecasting future demand and determining what the company should produce and in what quantity. Moreover, seasonality requirements affect the development of ANY detailed production schedules following demand planning, influencing the determination of the amount, and timing of raw material orders (material requirement planning) further. This implies a low interdependence between business units on staffing and production matters. FMCG businesses produce good that must be delivered and stored in warehouses under extreme controls, and require systems that will not only execute the processes but also make rapid adjustments when customer demand differs from the forecast.

Before implementation of the enterprise IT system, some Aus-P staff had been working for several years and had become accustomed to manual calculations in their planning reports. At the early stage of system implementation in 2003, some of those staff were reluctant to change their work routines. Reluctance from the employees was a significant challenge to the management. Therefore, the IT staff and senior managers spent time educating the planning, procurement, warehouse, sales, and branch staff and persuading them to use the new system. As the Production Planner explains: “Once people started to see the benefits financially, then they started accepting it.” After 1 year, through this “door-knocking” strategy, all branches

and depots formed an interdependent network bounded by the new system. Similarly, when managers of the firms in the AM province initiated planning with little resource interdependence following the introduction of a new enterprise system, they did so by explicitly gathering knowledge through socialization and experimentation. During this time, they would relate and refer their use of enterprise systems with that of others, or with their previous experience. Empowered by the new system, business units were integrated and began to share timely, accurate, and consistent planning information. Moreover, the standardization of the enterprise IT system minimized the difficulty of merging other firms. For example, in late 2009, Aus-P successfully completed the acquisition of a fresh milk business from another large food company. Because the acquired company had adopted the same enterprise IT product, the acquisition process was smooth and efficient. With the integration of the system, the business units began to coordinate closely, which created operational agility in executing the planning and procurement tasks. ## 6.2 5.2 Production Tasks

For manufacturers, production is the core business process, in that the process is directly related to the volume of products and their supply efficiency. Before the implementation of the enterprise system, the Aus-P production staff frequently needed to answer calls from sales, planning, financials, procurement, and warehouses. Unfortunately, the production process was often disrupted on multiple sides and it was common for customers’ orders to be forgotten or delayed. The Production Manager complained: “What we did before the system? Well, a lot of confusions. For example, we are producing a key customer order in our assembly line.

Suddenly, we got a call from the sales guy, saying that we have [a] new extra order. What we should do? Continue the current production schedule or change it immediately? For this, we have to call other people, like warehouse, financial, and procurement…to negotiate it.” Moreover, because of the isolated working style of the business units, people could not share information in a timely manner. Such information asymmetry typically leads to mistakes in production plans.

To overcome these challenges and achieve operational agility in the production process, the production managers in Aus-P took full advantage of the new system to establish their priority along the financial chain and expanded the production network. Owing to the powerful functions of the system, accurate production planning and detailed scheduling were formulated. The supply planning manager at the head office could assign customer orders to different regional subsidiaries and create detailed production schedules for each factory. This greatly improved the efficiency of production and the interlacing of processes in Aus-P. Similarly, in the case of TPA limited at the AM province, when conditions are new or when they change over the course of time, managers make necessary hierarchical adjustments during linked operations. The coding structure is the master data of the system, and is also the foundation of coordination between the business units. Both Aus-P and some firms in AM province designed unique goods’ coding structures to enhance internal coordination, as they sought to differentiate their goods from those produced by outside partners. Through such use of IT for coordination, the production process achieved operational agility and a sequence of interrelated benefits. The Aus-P Logistics and Supply Chain Manager explained: “The satisfaction and service is the end-in, and financial performance is obviously critical. You will have more money coming in or your cash flow will improve once you have got tight in your

core business…when we are trying to achieve this, the most important thing is that it has to be linked, it has to be done efficiently, so I would rate efficiency as the second most important thing…you’re trying to achieve high levels of customer satisfaction, you have to achieve in an efficient manner. And the quality control of efficiency will lead to, you know, productivity improvements and all that.” A summary of the theoretical themes, subthemes, and representative quotes of how IT-enabled production agility was achieved in the case organizations is provided in Table 4.

## 6.3 5.3 Distribution Tasks

Our findings reveal that some exceptions and contradictions occur when deep crossdepartment coordination is conducted during distribution tasks. Before the introduction of the IT system in the case organizations, the sales and distribution staff faced a number of issues in their routine work because of knowledge constraints. The sales staff attended weekly meetings with the planning and financial departments to produce detailed demand planning, supply network planning, production planning, and scheduling through the provision of sales history reports and sales order forecasts. The sales staff needed to spend a large amount of time analyzing the sales history and forecasts. When new orders came from customers, the sales staff were responsible for coordinating with the warehouse, production, procurement, and financial functions to check if the warehouses and factories had the ability to fulfill the new order on time. Exceptions and contradictions occurred when the cross-department coordination was conducted. The immediate consequence was lower customer satisfaction. Furthermore, because of knowledge constraints on the most effective means of transportation for the distribution of goods to customers, increased transportation costs and customer complaints were common.

To relieve these kinds of unsatisfactory situations, the managers in Aus-P and the AM province were keen to deploy new enterprise IT system to improve the performance of national sales and distribution. Whenever obstacles and exceptions occurred, the sales and distribution staff first checked the latest financial conditions and budgets and then viewed the real-time information from other departments. Through systems integration, the sales and distribution staff could provide rapid replies to customers and adjust their sales report and transportation strategy. Furthermore, the system gave the firms a powerful reporting capability. Sales reports were no longer the “bible” of sales staff only, but merely a “compass” for the entire company. According to different requirements and by filtering the properties and formats, the systems can generate a variety of standardized sales reports, which can be utilized by staff around the company, especially staff in the financial department and senior managers (i.e., to make strategic decisions). Over time, and based on our findings on managers in AM province firms as well, making adjustments to the context of IT use, being able to negotiate and understand routine tasks, and offering feedback to a collective become a natural part of their role and the organization’s socioeconomic system. Through the improved process and flexible reporting in sales and distribution, as enabled by the new system, operational efficiency and customer satisfaction were derived. The National Demand and Supply Manager in Aus-P revealed: “You know, the productivity achievement, process improvement…that’s sort of the basic and very top line benefit, but that really happens, really happens in terms of, at the end of it, the customer satisfaction.” A summary of the theoretical themes, subthemes, and representative quotes illustrating how IT-enabled sales and distribution agility was achieved in the case organizations is provided in Table 5.

## 7 6 Discussion

Building on both the strategic management and IS literature, our findings reveal how firms accomplish IT-enabled operational agility. From a resource viewpoint, our study proposes that accomplishing IT-enabled agility requires extending a firm’s capabilities to manage uncertainty in resource interdependencies. We extend and deconstruct the notion of agility in strategic management to refer to the alignment of processes through influence, the integration of organizational units through interdependent networks, and the coordination of materials, information, and financial flows through IT to improve competitiveness. On the basis of integrating the interdependencies lens adopted in the research and our case study findings (summarized in Tables 3, 4, and 5), a stagewise model of how firms achieve supply chain operational agility through the development of IT-enabled capabilities from a resource perspective is proposed. The model is illustrated in Fig. 1. Our model suggests that toward the attainment of operational agility, interdependencies affect the process of negotiation and influence how managers use IT to support the supply chain, with implications for resources. In other words, resource interdependencies and tasks exert managerial influences on a firm’s capabilities to rapidly adapt and respond to changes. We posit that resource sensing, resource acting, and resource responding capabilities are enacted in the accomplishment of agility. These implications for resources can be broken down into three tasks, namely, planning, production, and sales fulfillment. Given that our model was inductively derived from our case study data, the following discussion first explains how the literature corroborates our model and each of the identified tasks. Subsequently, from the process model and further analysis, a five-step practical guide to develop operational agility is presented.

7.1 6.1 Developing Resource-Sensing Capabilities in the Planning Phase Planning tasks are often the first phase in a supply chain and require the consolidation of information from multiple, often autonomous business units [31]. These units share lowresource interdependence with one another. This creates fluctuations in the information and resistance to conform with protocols [70]. This is in line with the observation that management persuasion is normally needed because of the employees’ resistance to new IT adoption [71]. To counter these challenges, managers can, for example, adopt a doorknocking approach to generate units’ awareness of each other’s resources and to establish rules of communication. In addition, to facilitate the protocols established in the previous step, the standardization of IT systems is required. This mitigates the fluctuations and resistance [72] encountered in the sensing phase of the supply chain. This coordination among interdependent units for planning tasks and resource allocations occurs in a loop. This is in line with the assertions in the literature that IT-enabled capabilities can never be “once and for all” but, rather, is a continual improvement process [73]. Alignment brings support to existing business needs and ensures existing IT capabilities are applied to discover new opportunities. Through continuous evolution in the coordination loop for planning, especially with enhanced collaborative communication among multiple units and IT-enabled standardization, agility in the form of information sharing and collaborative networks [74, 75] can be derived in the first task of sensing in the supply chain.

7.2 6.2 Developing Resource-Acting Capabilities in the Production Phase Production tasks are often the second phase in a supply chain and require coordination between departments such as the procurement, outsourcing, financial, and warehousing areas [31]. These units’ tasks are interdependent in a serial manner. However, without the integration supported by enterprise IT systems, the linkage of interdependencies between those units is constrained to a certain extent. For example, our case study data reveal that before the integration of IT infrastructure, the business units in the case organizations used their own systems and had difficulties “talking” to the production network. Such partially linked interdependence creates disparate units either due to system inconsistencies or due to departments’ safeguarding of their interests. This is in line with the proposition in the literature that the level of interdependence between organizational units is affected both by technical and social aspects [70, 76]. To counter these challenges and to generate adhesion between business units, managers must construct the priority chain in which the financial goa normally serves as the first priority [77]. This also requires a consistent IT system across multiple units to integrate the operations as a whole [78]. Another measure which production management can use is a coding structure for raw materials, semiproducts, and finished good to more effectively manage the material procurement and outsourcing. This is in line with the observation in the literature that the bill of the material is an effective method of communication between manufacturing partners or between plants in a single manufacturing plant [79]. The coordination of sequential IT and coding structures in the acting phase of the supply chain can further mitigate the problem of disparate units [80]. This coordination among interdependent units for production tasks occurs in a loop. Through continuous evolution in the coordination loop for production, agility in the form of process interlacing and efficiencies [81] can be derived in the acting task in the supply chain.

## 7.3 6.3 Developing Responding Capabilities in the Sales Fulfillment Phase

Sales fulfillment and distribution tasks are often the last phase of a supply chain [31] and require the consolidation of information from multiple units. These units’ tasks are deeply interdependent and share an integral role in achieving high-level customer satisfaction. As noted in the literature, high levels of interdependence typically create an increased level of uncertainty in the decision-making context; in addition, the absence of standardization at high levels of interdependence makes it difficult to anticipate which problems will be encountered during task execution [11]. Therefore, high-level interdependence requires high-level knowledge of skills [70]. This leads to knowledge constraints in sensing the fluctuating customer requirements and rapidly changeable market [11], and causes difficulty for knowledge retention in the sales fulfillment and distribution tasks [82]. To counter these challenges, firms initiate standardized protocols among those highly interdependent units, and whenever exceptions occur, flexible revisions of the protocols are allowed. Furthermore, to handle instant customer orders and variable reporting requirements, continual adjustments to reporting and requirements are necessary. This is in line with the recommendation in the literature that when confronting dynamic environments, management should always leave certain spaces open for strategy adjustment and revision [70, 83]. The approach of allowing flexible protocols and requirement adjustments will mitigate the knowledge constraints in the responding phase of the supply chain [84]. This coordination among highly interdependent units for sales fulfillment and distribution tasks occurs in a loop. Through continuous evolution in the coordination loop for sales fulfillment and distribution, agility in the form of flexibility in process structure and information sharing [4] can be derived in the third task of responding in the supply chain.

## 8 7 Research Implications

Our research has several implications for strategic management and practice. First, the roadmap we constructed for establishing effective interdependencies for IT-enabled operational agility is a conceptual innovation. On the basis of our empirical model (Fig. 1), we present a five-step practical guide to aid managers when attempting to coordinate departmental interdependencies for operational agility. Each of the five steps is explained in Table 6. This roadmap deconstructs the nature of operational agility and an organization’s operating mantra with a focus on planning systems and supply chain process improvement. Our research adds to a holistic understanding of the complexity of structures through understanding a firm’s setup, the structures across cooperating units, the coordination mechanisms across units during cooperation, and the appropriateness of IT [37], especially for large and complex firms with diversified operations and processes.

Second, our study clarifies the view that the role of organizational resources is greater than the role of IT resources in generating agility. We show that resource interdependencies across key processes must be effectively coordinated through identifying the dependencies that arise (from joint tasks) and the resources available to inform the coordination mechanisms that manage those dependencies simultaneously. We found that asset orchestration in a firm is contingent on these interdependencies such that different interdependence types demand different coordination methods in terms of rules and operating procedures, adaptive planning and scheduling, and mutual adjustments [38]. Concomitantly, we have established that resources must be carefully orchestrated in the successful adoption of enterprise IT systems. We showed that the predominant competitive environment of each phase of platform

#

development gives rise to a specific focal capability that is developed through resourcefocused actions. We acknowledge that different cohorts use systems differently and that cohorts themselves work in tandem for the systems to realize the identification and implementation of business opportunities quickly and cost-efficiently.

Third, this research untangles the complexity of interdependency structures in IT-enabled firms. Our research addresses this gap, adding to the extant studies that focus on the integration of IT systems to support supply chains, which generally assume that operational agility is automatically enabled by IT systems and that cooperation among departments would naturally ensue. In an increasingly digital and increasingly competitive market, this research adds to the fundamental understanding of the positive synergies behind the multiple constituents of firm. Increasingly, businesses must function as platforms that operate in an ITenabled commercial network of suppliers, intermediaries, and customers, such that different groups of firms transact with each other for sustainable growth [85]. The rapid emergence of the “platforms” notion presents critical research opportunities for researchers [86] as such logic reshapes interpretations of how firms cooperate and potentially harness web technologies to bring about business synergies and ultimately operational agility. Without an understanding of how synergies between interdependent constituents are developed, we cannot understand how contextual conditions such as technological advances, national and organizational cultures, social and political forces, and competitive strategies [87], shape cooperation within and among platforms, competitive strategies, and their related trade-offs.

## 9 8 Conclusion

Despite knowledge of resource interdependencies, few studies have suggested how it can be applied to achieve IT-enabled operational agility. The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, on the basis of our empirical study, we propose a stage-wise model of how firms

achieve operational agility. The model reflects our conceptualization that to achieve ITenabled operational agility, firms must develop capabilities through the establishment of firm structures and interdependencies to help managers negotiate and influence IT use. The model suggests that the achievement of operational agility in the supply chain can be segregated into a series of tasks in the coordination of planning, production, and sales fulfillment. Second, the study prescribes a set of actionable guidelines to help managers classify and manage innovative firm management practices. We propose a step-wise guide to manage and reinforce coordination mechanisms. Because of the limitations of the study, namely, the inclusion of only two case studies for comparison and the setting of the study in the context of manufacturers and service providers in Australia and India, we sought to reach further theoretical saturation by conducting a comprehensive multicase analysis and triangulating our study findings against the extant literature.

In summary, this paper presents a study on the relationships between interdependencies and IT-enabled operational agility. The study builds on the literature on these topics to further develop the understanding of how firms sense environmental changes and respond effectively in an increasingly competitive and digital landscape. There are specific implications for management practice. First, managers must classify and manage innovative firm management practices. Managers will not expect immediate impacts on their supply chain operations with an IT investment but, toward IT-enabled operational agility, multiple stakeholders must contend with information asymmetries and resource interdependencies simultaneously. Managers must recognize the interdependence context that affects the process of negotiation and use this means to practice IT use in supply chain tasks.
