## Abstract

This study assesses the job involvement of 464 professionals and managers in the information systems (IS) field and investigates the role of involvement in influencing the quality of work life. Results show significant variation in the level of job involvement displayed by IS employees and differential patterns of relationships among the study variables for IS personnel with low, moderate, and high levels of job involvement. The findings indicate that involvement serves as a complex moderator role in the pattern of relationships of work experiences, and of job characteristics with career expectations and career outcomes. It has both linear and non-linear relationships with several of the study variables. While in some cases, high levels of job involvement tend to enhance the beneficial effects of work experiences on the quality of work life, in others such involvement tends to heighten the negative effects of role stressors. Implications of the findings and directions for future research are discussed.

Keywords: IS personnel, career management, job involvement
ISRL Categories: EB, EH02

## Introduction

Researchers and managers in the information systems (IS) field have increasingly recognized the vital importance of qualified information systems employees to the successful implementation and use of sophisticated information technology in organizations (Amoroso, et al., 1989; Baroudi, 1985; Keen, 1991; Niederman, et al., 1991; Watson, 1990). A number of researchers have identified effective human resource management as a critical issue facing the IS field and have called attention to the need for improving human resource planning, recruitment, and development of IS employees (Champy, 1992; Niederman, et al., 1991; Watson, 1990). This appreciation has been accompanied by heightened interest in assessing the effect of human resource management practices and policies on the work experiences, job attitudes, performance, and overall quality of work life of IS professionals (Baroudi, 1985; Couger, 1988; 1990; Goldstein and Rockart, 1984; Igbaria, et al., 1991).

The concept of the "quality of work life" encompasses a wide range of organizational phenomena (Campbell, et al., 1976; Davis and Cherns, 1975; Loscocco and Roschelle, 1991;

Walton, 1974), but most relevant to this study is the view that quality of work life is “the degree to which members of a work organization are able to satisfy important personal needs through their experiences in the organization” (Suttle, 1977, p. 4). Thus, the quality of work life of an individual is defined by the individual’s affective reactions to both objective and experienced characteristics of the work organization.

Prior research on human resource management as related to IS employees has focused on such issues as the motivational properties of IS jobs (Baroudi, 1985; Couger, 1988; Goldstein and Rockart, 1984) and external indicators of career success such as salary, organizational level, and hierarchical advancement. It is only recently that IS researchers have recognized the importance of the internal career, i.e., the personally defined and valued characteristics and outcomes of work, and the fit between these and individuals' actual job experiences, in determining the quality of work life of IS employees (Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988; Igbaria, et al., 1991). For example, Igbaria, et al. (1991) found that employees whose work setting was compatible with their career orientation reported higher levels of job and career satisfaction than employees whose career orientation did not match attributes of their work setting.

While job satisfaction is generally considered to be the primary indicator of the quality of work life, job involvement and organizational commitment have also been identified as important attitudinal outcomes reflective of the internal career and of the "goodness" of the quality of work life (Davis and Cherns, 1975; Loscocco and Roschelle, 1991). Job involvement is an affective reaction to the job that is related to but is both conceptually and empirically distinct from job satisfaction, as well as forms of work commitment such as career commitment and organizational commitment (Blau, 1985a; 1985b; 1987; Brooks, et al., 1988; Gorn and Kanungo, 1980; Kanungo, 1979; 1982).

Although the concept of job involvement, its antecedents, and outcomes have been researched extensively by organizational researchers (for reviews, see Blau, 1985a; 1985b; Kanungo, 1979; 1982; Morrow, 1983; Rabincwitz and Hall, 1977), little attention has been devoted to exploring job involvement and its relationship to the work experiences and job attitudes of IS personnel. The dearth of research in this area is surprising in light of prior findings that IS professionals have high need for achievement and growth, and place a high value on interesting work, job challenge, and growth opportunities (Baroudi, 1985; Couger, 1988; Couger and Zawacki, 1980). The job design literature provides extensive evidence that job involvement is associated positively with the five “core” job characteristics or job scope (Hackman and Oldham, 1980; Saal, 1978; 1981), need for achievement and growth (Dreher, 1980; Rabinowitz, et al., 1977), as well as job satisfaction, performance (Batlis, 1980; Saal, 1978; 1981), and organizational commitment (Morrow, 1983). These factors underscore the importance of examining the work experiences and other factors related to the job involvement of IS employees and the relationship of involvement with favorable job and organizational outcomes, reflecting their quality of work life.

Perhaps the most compelling argument for investigating the nature, determinants, and outcomes of job involvement of IS employees derives from the identification of IS workers as a distinct occupational group, which impacts their “identity, attitudes, interests, colleagueship, collective action, power, status and work consciousness” (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1989, p. 23). Given the explosive growth of the IS field over the last three decades, IS employees constitute an increasingly important component of the work force of many organizations. The design and implementation of new technology by IS workers directly and indirectly impacts the activities of other employees. Thus, IS employees have the potential to influence the consciousness and practices of other workers in the organization. Recognizing this, Orlikowski and Baroudi (1989) have called for increased research on IS as an occupation, especially the role and status of IS workers, including their career orientations, socialization, world views, and life interests.

According to the social information processing view of job attitudes (Salancik and Pfeffer, 1978), individuals' perceptions and interpretations of the work environment and their reactions to various aspects of their job or task environment are importantly influenced by the social context in which they work, particularly cues given by the work group. Gomez-Mejia's (1984) findings that occupational membership was the single most important determinant of individuals' job involvement provides empirical support for this perspective. Thus, it is possible that the level of job involvement of IS personnel and its determinants and outcomes differ from those found in other occupational groups.

A recent meta-analysis of the antecedents of commitment (Cohen, 1992) shows that job involvement and its relationship to organizational commitment differ significantly across occupational groups. Viewed in conjunction with previous findings (Igbaria and Greenhaus, 1992) that organizational commitment is the most proximal determinant of turnover intentions of MIS employees, these studies reinforce the importance of investigating the job involvement of IS employees and the role of involvement in influencing the quality of work life. Thus, the purpose of this study is to examine (1) the relationship of personal/demographic variables, job characteristics, and work experiences with variations in the level of job involvement; (2) the relationship of job characteristics and work experiences with career expectations, job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment; and (3) the role played by job involvement in influencing the relationships among these variables.

## Theoretical Framework and Research Focus

## The meaning of job involvement

Job involvement has been defined and operationalized in a variety of ways across studies (for reviews, see Kanungo, 1982; Morrow, 1983; Rabinowitz and Hall, 1977). These include the view that involvement refers to: an individual's ego-involvement with work or psychological identification with the job; the psychological importance of work or the centrality of work to the individual; the importance of performance to an individual's self image (Lawler and Hall, 1970; Lodahl and Kejner, 1965). In response to concerns about concept redundancy (Morrow, 1983), and the dimensionality and discriminant validity of the construct (Saal, 1981), Kanungo (1979; 1982) used a motivational perspective to reconceptualize job involvement as a unidimensional construct. Distinguishing job involvement from work involvement, he defined job involvement as the psychological identification of an individual with a specific job. Thus, while work involvement is historically determined, job involvement is situationally determined and is based on the need-satisfying properties of a particular job. Given our interest in assessing job involvement as the affective reaction of a person to his or her job and its motivational properties, this study adopts Kanungo's definition of job involvement as the cognitive state of an individual's psychological identification with the job.

## Conceptual model of work experiences, job involvement, and quality of work life

Figure 1 presents the conceptual model of work experiences, job involvement, and quality of work life of IS personnel that guided this study. The model posits that demographic variables and work experiences are directly related to career expectations, which in turn is directly related to the three indicators of quality of work life examined, i.e., job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment. The model also posits that demographic and work experience variables can be directly related to these outcome variables. Moreover, the relationships of the antecedent demographic and work experience variables with career expectations and with job outcomes are hypothesized to be moderated by the level of job involvement. Finally, job involvement is posited to moderate the relationships of career expectations with job outcomes.

The model depicted in Figure 1 is similar in some respects to the structural model of turnover intentions proposed and tested by lgbaria and Greenhaus (1992), and there is some overlap in the variables included in the two studies. However, the central foci of the two models differ considerably. The model tested by lgbaria and Greenhaus (1992) examined the causal linkages of demographic variables, role stressors, and career-related variables with MIS employees' turnover intentions and the role of job attitudes in mediating these relationships. A structural model assumes the existence of linear, additive relationships among the study variables, whereas the model proposed in this study explicitly recognizes and tests for non-linear relationships, especially with job involvement. The central objective of this study is to examine the relationship of job involvement with the model variables in general and to examine the moderating role of involvement on the relationships of work experiences and career expectations with indicators of the quality of work life. Moreover, this study goes beyond the Igbaria and Greenhaus (1992) model by including job characteristics, boundary-spanning activities, and developmental prospects as antecedent variables that potentially influence the three indicators of quality of life, while career expectations are expected to play a mediating role in these relationships. The specific variables included in the model, the rationale for their selection, and the hypothesized linkages among them are discussed in the following sections.

## Demographic Variables

Given the perspective that job involvement is influenced by individual as well as situational characteristics, three demographic variables were examined as individual antecedent correlates of job involvement, i.e., age, job tenure, and tenure in the MIS field. There is extensive evidence of the positive relationship between age and job involvement (Lorence, 1987; Morrow and McElroy, 1987). A number of studies have found job involvement to be positively related to job tenure and organizational tenure (Morrow and McElroy, 1987; Rabinowitz, et al., 1977), while others indicate non-significant relationships (Blau, 1985a; 1985b; Saal, 1978; 1981). In view of our interest in IS as an occupational field, we selected number of years in IS rather than organizational tenure to gauge the influence of experience and investment of time on involvement.

There is growing evidence that the relationships of demographic variables with job involvement may not be linear. Wagner (1987) documents the existence of a curvilinear relationship between organizational tenure and job involvement, with involvement increasing progressively with increased tenure until mid-career, and then tapering off with long tenure. Raelin (1985) found a different form of curvilinearity in the relationship between career stage and job involvement, with involvement being high in the early and late career stages and low in the middle career stage. Job involvement has also been found to have non-linear relationships with job characteristics and job outcomes, as well as non-linear interactive effects with demographic, personal, and job characteristic variables on job satisfaction and organizational commitment (Batlis, 1978; 1980; Gould and Werbel, 1983; Orpen, 1979). Based on these studies, we hypothesize that:

H1. Job involvement will show non-linear relationships with demographic characteristics of IS employees, job characteristics and work experiences, career expectations, and job outcome variables.

## Work Experiences

There is extensive evidence that jobs that provide intrinsic rewards such as challenge, variety, and meaning are reported to be the most involving and satisfying (Hackman and Oldham, 1980; Kalleberg, 1977; Lawler and Hall, 1970; Lorence, 1987; Loscocco, 1989; Saal, 1978). As documented earlier, IS employees are high on achievement orientation and value intrinsic job characteristics such as interesting work, job challenge, and growth (Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980). Thus, we investigated the job characteristics of IS employees in terms of the extrinsic and intrinsic rewards they provided as salient variables potentially related to their job involvement.

IS researchers (Ahituv and Neumann, 1990; Davis and Olson, 1985; Lucas, 1989) have identified boundary-spanning activities as representing an important feature of jobs in systems analysis and design. Boundary-spanning activities reflect the extent to which a job involves communication and interaction with individuals outside the person's own department in the form of oral and written communication, formal meetings, and informal contact. The frequent interaction with individuals both internal and external to the IS department provides increased visibility for the incumbent of such a role and may contribute to heightened career expectations. Boundary-spanning activities have also been found to be related to IS professionals' job satisfaction and organizational commitment (Baroudi, 1985; Keller and Holland, 1975; Keller, et al., 1976; Lysonski and Woodside, 1989).

Role conflict and role ambiguity represent two additional work experiences that can affect the quality of work life adversely (for reviews, see

Jackson and Schuler, 1985; Van Sell, et al., 1981). These two role stressors, which are pervasive across different organizations and occupations, have been found to be associated with decreased job involvement and job satisfaction (Jackson and Schuler, 1985; Van Sell, et al., 1981). Empirical studies in IS also provide confirmatory evidence that role conflict and role ambiguity are negatively related with job satisfaction and organizational commitment (Baroudi, 1985; Lysonski and Woodside, 1989).

## Career Expectations

Other research suggests that quality of work life is a function not only of current work experiences, but also of anticipated or expected future career experiences (Chatman, 1989; Hodson, 1985), such as perceived opportunities for advancement. The findings that promotion opportunity and mobility potential are positively related to satisfaction and negatively to organizational commitment (Anderson, et al., 1981; Mowday, et al., 1982) suggest the potential relevance of career expectations in explaining job involvement as well as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and career satisfaction. Thus, individuals who are in jobs or categories of jobs that have limited potential for advancement may be less involved in their job, experience decreased satisfaction and have lower commitment to the organization (Kanter, 1977).

Two types of career expectations were examined: (1) expectations regarding the likelihood of promotability, which were labeled advancement prospects; and (2) expectations regarding internal job opportunities, e.g., job transfers, that may be developmental in that they entail challenging job assignments and the potential for recognition (King, 1991). Expectations regarding internal job alternatives were labeled developmental prospects. Both advancement prospects and developmental prospects have been found to be positively related to job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to stay among IS employees and others (Igbaria and Greenhaus, 1992; King, 1991; Liou, et al., 1990; Mottaz, 1988).

## Job Outcomes

Job satisfaction refers to the affective reaction of individuals to various features of the job. As noted earlier, job satisfaction is a primary outcome of work experiences that meet valued needs of individuals and thus represents a key indicator of the quality of work life. Empirical studies have also provided extensive evidence of the positive effects of involvement on job satisfaction and organizational commitment (BenPorat, 1980; Blau, 1985a; 1985b; 1987; Kanungo, 1982; Lodahl and Kejner, 1965; Morrow and McElroy, 1987; Parasuraman and Alutto, 1984; Parasuraman and Nachman, 1987; Rabinowitz and Hall, 1981; Saal, 1978; 1981). Career satisfaction, which refers to the overall affective reaction of individuals to their careers, i.e. pattern of work experiences over time, was examined as another (long term) indicator of the work-related well-being of individuals. Igbaria, et al. (1991) found that congruence between the type of job held by an individual and his or her career orientation was associated with increased career satisfaction.

The findings of the studies reviewed in the foregoing sections suggest the following hypotheses concerning the expected relationships among task and work experience variables, career expectations, and job outcomes.

H2a. Task characteristics and boundary-spanning activities will be positively related to advancement prospects and developmental prospects.

H2b. Role conflict and role ambiguity will be negatively related to advancement prospects and developmental prospects.

H3a. Task characteristics and boundary-spanning activities will be positively related to the quality of work life of IS employees as indexed by job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment.

H3b. Role conflict and role ambiguity will be negatively related to job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment.

H3c. Developmental prospects and advancement prospects will be positively related to job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment.

## Job involvement and the pattern of relationships among the study variables

Prior empirical research reveals varied and inconsistent results concerning the role and place occupied by job involvement in the network of relationships among demographic and work experience variables, and job attitudes. While some studies indicate that job involvement is an index of well-being along with job satisfaction and organizational commitment (Blau, 1987; Dreher, 1980; Morris and Koch, 1979; Morrow and McElroy, 1987; Sekaran and Mowday, 1981), others suggest that job involvement may be a predictor of job satisfaction, work commitment, and performance (Ben-Porat, 1980; Rabinowitz, 1985; Wiener and Gechman, 1977). A few studies provide limited support for job involvement as a mediator of the relationships of individual and job situation variables with job satisfaction, absenteeism, and performance (Dailey and Morgan, 1978; Saal, 1978; 1981). At least one study reports that job involvement is an outcome of job satisfaction (Rabinowitz and Hall, 1977).

A growing number of studies indicate the possibility that job involvement serves a moderator role by attenuating or enhancing the relationships of job characteristics and work experiences with job outcomes. According to James and Brett (1984), a variable z (job involvement in this study) is a moderator if the relationship between two variables, say x and y, differs as a function of the level of z. Batlis (1978) found that the relationships of several dimensions of organizational climate with job satisfaction are significant and stronger among individuals with high job involvement than those with low job involvement. Moreover, job involvement serves to attenuate the positive relationships between role stressors and propensity to leave (Batlis, 1980). In other studies, job involvement has been found to moderate the relationships of employment status and life changes with indicators of psychological well-being (Innes and Clarke, 1985; Shamir, 1986; Stafford, et al., 1980).

Viewed collectively, these studies provide fairly strong support for the treatment and examination of job involvement as a moderator variable likely to influence the nature and pattern of linkages proposed among the demographic and work experience variables, career expectation variables, as well as job attitudes. Thus, the following general hypotheses are proposed concerning the potential moderating effect of job involvement on the relationships among the study variables depicted in Figure 1.

H4a. Job involvement will moderate the relationships of task characteristics, boundary-spanning activities, role conflict, and role ambiguity with advancement prospects, developmental prospects, job and career satisfaction, and organizational commitment.

H4b. Job Involvement will moderate the relationships of advancement prospects and developmental prospects with job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment.

In summary, this study can make a contribution to the IS literature by examining an important, but hitherto unexplored, variable indicative of the quality of work life of IS employees, i.e., job involvement. The study should provide interesting insights into the job involvement of IS workers, the individual and work experience variables that explain variations in involvement, and other indicators of the quality of work life of individuals who pursue careers in information systems. The results of the study can also serve to explain the nature of the role played by job involvement in influencing worker reactions to different work experiences and specifically, whether involvement moderates the relationships of work experiences with job attitudes. A major contribution of this study, therefore, is the level of sophistication in the analyses of relationships of a key variable, i.e., job involvement, and its complex effects on various organizational phenomena and on the quality of work life.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

The data used in this study were gathered as part of a wide-ranging survey (Career Attitude Survey) of IS employees designed to investigate their job perceptions, challenges, and problems, and the impact of different work experiences on their job attitudes, work involvement, performance, and intention to stay with the employing organization. Portions of the data used in this study were examined previously in Igbaria, et al. (1991) and Igbaria and Greenhaus (1992). The focus of these studies was on assessing the network of variables that contribute to the propensity to leave the employing organization and the effect of incompatibility between an individual's career orientation and work setting on the internal career of IS employees. In contrast, this study assesses IS employees' work experiences and their relationships with job involvement, as well as the role of job involvement in influencing their affective reactions to these experiences and career expectations, reflecting the quality of work life.

The Career Attitude Survey was distributed to the membership of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) in three states—Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Southern New Jersey. Since the members of ACM represent a wide variety of jobs and organizations, it was expected that the sample would be fairly representative of the spectrum of jobs and types of organizations found in the IS field. Of the 2,548 surveys distributed, 517 were returned, for a response rate of 20.3 percent. This response rate appears to be consistent with other mail surveys (George and Barksdale, 1974; McNamara, 1972). Surveys with incomplete data and/or those received from retired employees and others not in the computer field (e.g., faculty in academic institutions) were excluded from the study. This resulted in a final sample of 464, of whom 371 (80 percent) were men, and 93 (20 percent) were women. The respondents held a variety of positions within the computer field; they included systems programmers (35), applications programmers (79), systems analysts (63), project leaders (60), and IS managers (93); the rest included consultants and software engineers. Approximately 54 percent of the men and women in the sample held professional positions, while 46 percent were in managerial positions. A majority of the respondents (59 percent) worked in large IS departments, i.e., those with more than 30 employees. The rest were distributed among departments with fewer IS employees, e.g., 1-5 (13 percent), 6-10 (10 percent), 11-20 (11 percent), and 21-30 (7 percent).

In view of the somewhat low response rate to the survey, we compared the characteristics of our sample with selected demographic characteristics of the ACM membership as a whole (Fletcher, et al., 1986) in order to determine the representativeness of the sample to the population of ACM members. Thus, we found that men comprised 80 percent of our sample compared to 85 percent of ACM members. The age and educational level of the respondents in our sample were also similar to ACM members in a study reported by Fletcher, et al. (1986). In our sample, 93 percent were 55 years old or younger, compared with 94 percent for ACM. Similarly, 51 percent of our sample (versus 56 percent of ACM members) had a master's degree, and 94 percent had at least a bachelor's degree (compared to 92 percent in ACM). We found that 49.5 percent of respondents in our study had fewer than 9 years of computer experience (versus 48 percent in the ACM report), 67.7 percent had fewer than 14 years of computer experience (versus 68 percent in the ACM report), and 10 percent of the participants in our sample had more than 25 years of experience in computers (versus 9 percent in the ACM report). These data provide substantial evidence that the demographic characteristics of our sample are quite similar to those of the total ACM population.

## Measures

Data regarding demographic characteristics of the sample were assessed from responses to questions included in the survey under the section heading “Background Information.” Tenure in the current job and organization were reported as the number of years an individual had been employed in his/her job and organization, respectively. Participants also indicated the number of years they had been in the IS field. Salary consisted of six tiers from (1) below \$25,000 to (6) \$65,000 or above.

The instruments used to measure the other variables in the study included items and scales derived from the literature and adapted for use in this study, as well as new measures developed expressly for this study. The Appendix presents details regarding the source, number of items, and other features of the scales used to measure boundary-spanning activities, role stressors, job characteristics, job involvement, career expectations, job and career satisfaction, and organizational commitment. The psychometric properties of the scales in terms of their internal consistency reliability and convergent and discriminant validity for use in this study were assessed by means of Partial Least Squares (PLS), which is described in the next section.

## Data analyses

The technique of partial least squares analysis (PLS) was applied to test the measurement model to determine the internal consistency reliability and construct validity of the multiple item scales used to operationalize the study variables. PLS is a second-generation multivariate technique that facilitates concurrent testing of the psychometric properties of the scales used to measure a variable and estimating the parameters of a structural model, i.e., the strength and direction of the relationships among the model variables (Fornell, 1982; 1987; Lohmoller, 1989; Wold, 1982). PLS embodies two sets of equations. The structural equations represent the paths among the constructs, and measurement equations represent the relationships between the indicators and the constructs that they measure. The model describes the relationships or paths among theoretical constructs. For each construct included in Figure 1, there is a related measurement model, which links the construct to a set of items. The test of the measurement model also involves examination of the convergent and discriminant validity of the research instruments, which indicates the strength of the measures used to test the proposed model. The structural model assesses the explanatory power of the independent variables and examines the size and the significance of the path coefficients. Together, the measurement and structural models form a network of measures and constructs (Fornell, 1982; 1987; Fornell and Bookstein, 1982).

The test of the measurement model includes estimation of the reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha) of the items that comprise a scale, as well as an examination of the convergent and discriminant validity of the research instrument. In determining the appropriate minimum loadings required for the inclusion of an item within a scale, we used Fornell's (1982) criterion of a loading of .70 or higher and an average extracted variance equal to or greater than .50 for measures of established constructs (e.g., job satisfaction and organizational commitment in this study). We also used the guidelines recommended by Hair, et al. (1992) in determining the relative importance and significance of the factor loading of each item, i.e., loadings greater than .30 are considered significant; loadings of .40 are considered more important; and loadings .50 or greater are considered to be very significant. Finally, the criteria suggested by Nunnally (1978) were applied to determine the adequacy of the reliability coefficients obtained for each measure.

To assess discriminant validity of the measures, i. e., the extent to which items differentiate among constructs or measure distinct concepts, we examined the correlations between the measures of potentially overlapping constructs (Grant, 1989). If the items comprising an instrument that measures a construct correlate more highly with each other than with items measuring other constructs in the model (Fornell, et al., 1982; Grant, 1989), the measure is judged to have adequate discriminant validity. It has also been suggested that the variance shared between measures of two different constructs (the squared correlation) should be lower than the average variance extracted by the items measuring each construct. In other words, measures of constructs should correlate more highly with their own items than with measures of other constructs in the model (Grant, 1989).

To test the measurement model, the sample was split into two subgroups by assigning all questionnaires with even identification numbers into one sample (S1) and all the responses with odd identification numbers into a second sample (S2). The resulting sample sizes were 224 observations for S1 and 214 observations for S2. The initial model was tested with S1, and based on these results the model was revised. The holdout sample (S2) was used to test the revised model.

Analyses of the data to assess the relationships among the study variables included several steps. Based on the findings of Raelin (1985) and Wagner (1987), we first examined whether job involvement had non-linear relationships with the study variables. We inspected the distribution of respondents' scores on job involvement and assigned them to three categories denoting low, moderate, and high involvement respectively. Individuals with involvement scores in the upper and lower quartiles were classified as high and low in involvement, while those in the middle second and third quartiles were classified as being moderate in job involvement. Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was then performed to assess whether variations in the level of involvement were related to demographic and background characteristics of the sample. Based on the results of this initial analysis, Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA) was performed to determine whether the work experiences, career expectations, and job attitudes of IS employees varied as a function of their level of job involvement. The covariates to be controlled were selected on the basis of the initial MANOVA results, which are discussed later.

The hypothesized multivariate network of relationships among the study variables depicted in Figure 1 was tested by means of Partial Least Squares (PLS). PLS is a powerful analytical technique in testing structural equation models and is particularly applicable in research areas where theory is not as well developed as that demanded by LISREL (Fornell and Bookstein, 1982). PLS makes minimal demands about measurement scales, sample size, or the distribution of the residuals. Of particular relevance to this study is the fact that PLS does not depend on having multivariate normally distributed data (distribution-free). Finally, it can be used with non-interval-scaled data and importantly, with small samples. Fornell and Bookstein (1982) state that PLS “avoids many of the restrictive assumptions underlying maximum likelihood techniques and ensures against improper solutions and factor indeterminacy” (p. 440).

The initial evaluation of the structural model was conducted with the original S1 sample. To test the estimated path coefficients, t-statistics were calculated using a non-parametric test of significance known as jackknifing (Tukey, 1958; Wildt, et al., 1982). The hypothesized moderator effects of job involvement on the relationships of demographic and job situation variables with job attitudes would ordinarily be tested by means of moderated multiple regression analysis (Cohen and Cohen, 1983). However, this technique was considered to be inappropriate in this study because it assumes symmetrical or monotonic and uniform changes in the relationship between the independent and dependent variable. In situations where the effect of the proposed moderator variable is expected to be non-linear, three techniques have been suggested to assess the interaction effects: (1) the use of quadratic or higher terms to explore non-linear relationships; (2) the use of dummy coded variables to represent different levels of the moderator variable; and (3) regression analysis within subgroups that are identified by scores on the proposed moderator variable (Finney, et al., 1984).

Since subgroup analysis facilitates interpretation of interactions and non-linear effects, the structural model was tested separately for each of three subgroups identified earlier based on their level of job involvement, i.e., low (n = 114), moderate (n = 233), and high (n = 110). Thus, separate regression equations were tested to assess the adequacy of the conceptual model presented in Figure 1 for each of the three levels of job involvement. To determine whether the relationships of demographic variables, work experiences, and work expectations with quality of work life varied as a function of job involvement, we examined the significance of the differences among the respective standardized path coefficients obtained for the three categories of individuals, applying the formula proposed by Cohen and Cohen (1983).

Average variance extracted for all constructs exceeds .50 as recommended by Fornell and Larcker (1981), and the composite reliability of all scales exceeds .80. The item reliabilities of all the constructs (not presented here) are also significant.

The results of the test conducted to assess discriminant validity of the measures are presented in Table 2. An examination of the matrix in Table 2 reveals no violation of the criteria for discriminant validity. In all of the 136 entries examined, the squared correlations, representing the shared variance among variables, were found not to exceed the average variance explained. In summary, the convergent and discriminant validity of our instruments using both S1 and S2 samples is satisfactory.

## Correlates of job involvement

Table 3 presents the results of the ANOVA conducted to determine the work life experience correlates of IS employees with different levels of employment. Inspection of the frequency distribution of job involvement scores across IS employees shows that they could be classified into three groups reflecting low, moderate, and high levels of job involvement respectively. The appropriateness of this classification was determined by means of T-tests of the differences in the involvement scores of IS personnel assigned to the three groups. The results show that the mean involvement score of employees in the low group (X=1.63, median=1.63, S.D.=.29) is significantly lower than that of employees in the moderate (X=2.67, median=2.63, S.D.=.35) and high groups (X=3.92, median=3.88, S.D.=.44). The ANOVA results show also that number of years in the IS field is the lone variable related to the differential levels of job involvement displayed by IS employees. Thus, the results provide only partial support for Hypothesis 1. Number of years in the IS field shows a U-shaped relationship with involvement. Individuals with both high and low job involvement are likely to have spent more years in the IS field (X=13.69 and 12.01, respectively) than those with medium involvement (X=11.16 years).

Based on these results, it was decided that number of years in the IS field should be controlled in all subsequent analyses. Table 4 presents the results of MANCOVA, controlling for number of years in IS as covariates, conducted to assess variations in role characteristics, job characteristics, career expectations, and job attitudes associated with different levels of job involvement.

The results provide moderate support for H1 and show that job involvement has a main effect on five of the variables examined: boundary-spanning activities (F=3.65, p≤.05); role conflict (F=3.08, p≤.05); job characteristics (F=3.08, p≤.01); salary (F=3.01, p≤.05); and organizational commitment (F=7.44, p≤.001). Inspection of the cell means reveals some evidence of non-linearity in the relationships of boundary-spanning activities and the three levels of job involvement. Thus, IS employees with high levels of job involvement were found to engage in interactions with other departments and organizations more extensively (X=3.21) than those with low levels of involvement (X=3.04), but the latter report higher levels of boundary spanning than the moderate involvement group (X=2.86). Similarly, IS personnel with both low and high levels of job involvement report greater role conflict (X=3.17 and X=3.20 respectively) than those with moderate levels of job involvement (X=2.96). Additionally, salary has a nonlinear relationship with job involvement; IS employees with high levels of job involvement earn a higher salary (X=4.09) than those with low levels of involvement (X=3.89), but the latter report a higher level of salary than the moderate involvement group (X=3.69). The results reveal a linear relationship between job characteristics and job involvement, such that the greater the perceived opportunities for rewards in their jobs, the higher the job involvement of IS employees. Finally, a linear relationship was also found between organizational commitment and job involvement, where highly job-involved employees were found to have significantly higher commitment (X=3.42) to the employing organization than those with moderate and low levels of involvement (X=3.31 and X=2.97), respectively.

## Tests of the moderating role of job involvement

In view of the differences found in demographic characteristics, work experiences, job characteristics, and job attitudes of IS employees with low, moderate, and high levels of job involvement, the test of the structural model was conducted separately for the three involvement groups. This enabled assessment of possible differences in the pattern of relationships among the model variables across the three involvement groups. The results showing the direct effects of the demographic variables, work experiences, and task characteristics on career expectations, job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment are presented in Tables 5, 6, 7 and 8, respectively. The direct effects are presented by the symbols $\beta$ and $\gamma$ , which are based on the convention of PLS as a data analysis technique. Accordingly, $\gamma$ values reflect the direct effect of the independent variables on the mediating variables in the model, as well as the outcome variables. On the other hand, $\beta$ represents the direct effect of the mediating variables on the outcome variables. Since the model includes a large number of variables, numerous direct and indirect paths from the exogenous variables to successive dependent variables, and moderator effects, we decided to highlight only the most salient and strongest relationships within the text in this section in order to avoid confusing the reader. Moreover, although some of the paths between variables were statistically significant, they did not meet the criterion of practical significance suggested by Kerlinger and Pedhazur (1973) for inclusion in a path diagram. Therefore, in this section, only betas with values of .05 or higher, and which are significant at the .05 level or better, are reported.

## Determinants of Career Satisfaction

The structural model (Hypothesis 3c) also posited direct positive relationships of career expectations with career satisfaction. Table 7 presents the results of the test of the model pertaining to career satisfaction. Data show that the model variables explain 35 percent, 40 percent, and 48 percent respectively of the variance $(p \leq .05)$ for the low, medium, and high involvement groups. Consistent with expectations, advancement prospects are positively related to career satisfaction across all three levels of job involvement. The effect of developmental prospects on career satisfaction is stronger $(p \leq .05)$ among individuals with moderate $(\beta = .12)$ involvement than among other groups. Table 7 data indicate the presence of moderator effects of job involvement on the relationships of all work experience variables on career satisfaction. Thus, the positive relationship between work life experiences and career satisfaction among employees with medium involvement ( $\gamma = .10$ ) is opposite to the work life experience/career satisfaction relationship noted for the low and high involvement group ( $\gamma = -.10$ and -.17, respectively). Job characteristics demonstrate increasingly strong positive effects on career satisfaction from the low ( $\gamma = .33$ ) to moderate ( $\gamma = .47$ ) to high ( $\gamma = .61$ ) involvement groups. The negative effect of role ambiguity on career satisfaction is markedly higher ( $p \leq .05$ ) among individuals with moderate and low ( $\gamma = -.16$ and -.18, respectively) involvement than among those with high ( $\gamma = -.06$ ) involvement. On the other hand, role conflict is differentially related to career satisfaction for those with high job involvement ( $\gamma = -.09$ ) compared to those with low and moderate involvement ( $\gamma = -.05$ and -.04, respectively). Finally, the positive effect of salary on career satisfaction for the low job involvement is markedly higher ( $\gamma = .25$ ) than that found for the moderate job involvement group ( $\gamma = .07$ ) and the high job involvement ( $\gamma = .11$ ).

## Determinants of Organizational Commitment

Table 8 reports the test results pertaining to organizational commitment. The data show that 47 percent, 44 percent, and 53 percent of the variance in organizational commitment is explained by the model variables for low, moderate, and high job involvement groups, respectively. As predicted in Hypothesis 3c, developmental prospects are more strongly related to commitment among those with low job involvement ( $\beta = .17$ ) than those with high involvement ( $\beta = .11$ ). Advancement prospects have a positive effect on organizational commitment only in the moderate ( $\beta = .06$ ) job involvement groups.

Boundary-spanning activities are positively related to organizational commitment—the effects are stronger among employees with moderate and high job involvement ( $\gamma = .14$ and .15, respectively) than those with low job involvement. Job characteristics also demonstrate strong direct and positive relationships with organizational commitment for low, moderate, and high job involvement ( $\gamma = .42$ , .39 and .38, respectively). Furthermore, both role stressors have negative effects on organizational commitment. The adverse effect of role conflict on commitment is markedly higher for the high ( $\gamma = -.40$ ) involvement group than in the moderate ( $\gamma = -.21$ ) and low ( $\gamma = -.18$ ) involvement groups. Again, the negative relationship of role ambiguity with organizational commitment is stronger for those with low ( $\gamma = -.15$ ) as compared to the moderate and high involvement ( $\gamma = -.10$ and -.03, respectively) group. Further, while the effect of salary on commitment for the low job-involvement group is negative ( $\gamma = -.11$ ), its effect for the medium and high job-involvement groups is positive ( $\gamma = .04$ and .11, respectively). Further, the effect of salary for the high involvement group is greater than its effect on the medium and low job-involvement groups. Finally, the effect of work life experiences on organizational commitment is stronger among low job involvement ( $\gamma = .18$ ) than medium and high job involvement ( $\gamma = .02$ and .06, respectively). Overall, job involvement moderates all the relationships of the antecedent variables with organizational commitment.

## Discussion

The results of this study indicate that IS personnel can be classified into three categories based on their scores on job involvement—low, moderate, and high job involvement, reflecting differential levels of psychological identification with their job. The work experiences and job attitudes of individuals who display high job involvement differ significantly from those with low and/or moderate involvement, which supports the findings of non-linear relationships reported in previous research (Innes and Clarke, 1985; Saleh and Desai, 1990; Wiley, 1987). The results show that boundary-spanning activities, role stressors, and job characteristics of IS employees differ significantly among those who display low, moderate, and high levels of job involvement respectively. The profile of high-involvement IS employees that emerges from these findings is that their jobs are higher in intrinsic and extrinsic rewards including salary, and entail more extensive boundary-spanning activities and greater role stress than the jobs held by employees with low and/or moderate involvement. These experiences are also differentially related to career expectations and indicators of the quality of work life among individuals in the three involvement groups. Moreover, high-involvement IS employees also display greater commitment to their employing organizations.

A key point of departure of this study relates to the differential pattern of relationships among work experience variables, career expectations, and quality of work life found for individuals with different levels of job involvement. Moderator effects are usually assessed by examining differences in the form and/or strength of the relationship of the independent variable with the dependent variable at low and high levels of the presumed moderator variable. The moderator effects found in this study are far more complex and variable than previously thought. Thus, the relationships of boundary-spanning activities with advancement prospects and developmental prospects are significantly different for the three levels of involvement—the strength of the relationships is lowest for those displaying moderate levels of job involvement. Similarly, the effect of job characteristics on developmental prospects is significantly greater for IS employees with both low and high involvement than those with moderate involvement. On the other hand, the job characteristics-advancement prospects relationship is stronger among individuals in the low job-involvement group than among those with moderate or high levels of involvement. Furthermore, jobs high in both intrinsic (e.g., autonomy, challenge) and extrinsic (e.g., opportunities for recognition) rewards have progressively stronger positive effects on the job satisfaction and career satisfaction of individuals with low, moderate, and high job involvement, respectively. The results also show differential effects of salary on job and career satisfaction, and commitment to the organization. The relationship of salary to career satisfaction is markedly higher for individuals with low job involvement than those with moderate or high levels of involvement.

The differential relationships found for role conflict and role ambiguity with advancement prospects and developmental prospects provide further evidence of the moderating role of job involvement. Role ambiguity demonstrates a positive relationship with advancement prospects among individuals with low job involvement, but has negative effects among those with high job involvement. Additionally, the negative effect of role conflict on organizational commitment is stronger among individuals in the high involvement group than among those who display low and moderate involvement respectively. Thus, it appears that high levels of job involvement can exacerbate the negative effects of role conflict on quality of work life. These findings are analogous to those documented in the work-family literature (Greenhaus and Beutell, 1985) that high levels of job involvement and family involvement heighten the level of work-family conflict experienced by individuals and its aversive effects on individuals' well-being.

The prominent role of boundary-spanning activities and of job involvement in influencing the job attitudes of IS employees is demonstrated by the finding that such activities have a stronger effect on the organizational commitment of individuals with moderate and high levels of job involvement than those with low job involvement. Boundary-spanning activities show a different pattern of relationships with job and career satisfaction among the moderate job-involvement groups, where they are negatively related to both job and career satisfaction.

Overall, the findings indicate that job involvement plays a varied and complex role in influencing the reactions of IS personnel to different experiences at work and consequently, their quality of work life. While these findings complement the results reported in prior studies (Batlis, 1978; Blau and Boal, 1987) concerning the beneficial effects of job involvement on the quality of work life, they also indicate that high job involvement may be detrimental to the individual because it exacerbates the negative effects of role stressors. The observation that high levels of psychological identification with the job can have both positive and negative consequences for the individual suggests the need to reconsider traditional normative assumptions about the universal desirability of high job involvement.

## Implications and Directions for Future Research

The findings of this study demonstrate the importance of job involvement as a salient variable related to the work experiences of IS employees, their affective reactions to their job, and the quality of work life. Job involvement shows both linear and non-linear relationships with several of the study variables. While in some cases, high levels of job involvement tend to enhance the beneficial effects of work experiences on the quality of work life (e.g., job characteristics, job/career satisfaction relationships), in others such involvement tends to heighten the negative effects of role stressors (e.g., the relationships of role conflict with career expectations, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment). These findings parallel the results documented in the job design literature (Hackman and Oldham, 1980) concerning the positive moderating role of “growth need strength” (which encompasses need for achievement and job involvement) on individuals’ affective reactions to jobs with high job scope, i.e., with high levels of skill, variety, autonomy and task significance. These findings are also consistent with those reported in the work-family literature that high levels of work and family involvement tend to intensify the experience of work-family conflict (a form of inter-role conflict) and exacerbate its adverse effects on individuals’ quality of life in the work and family domains (for review, see Greenhaus and Beutell, 1985). In other words, high job involvement seems to be a mixed blessing.

The theoretical implications of the findings are that job involvement should be included as a critical variable in models of IS employees' job attitudes in response to selected characteristics and other work experiences. Moreover, future research exploring other aspects of IS employees' psychological identification with their job should test whether the relationships of job involvement with its antecedents and outcomes are linear or non-linear. The results of this study do not permit any conclusions regarding the direction of influence of job involvement with the study variables, i.e., whether variations in levels of involvement contribute to variations in work experiences and outcomes, or whether differential work experiences account for the differences in involvement. Thus, longitudinal research is needed to clarify the nature and direction of causal connections among the model variables.

From an organizational and managerial perspective, the findings suggest the need for focusing on different variables as the leverage point for influencing the job attitudes of IS employees with low, moderate, and high levels of job involvement, respectively. The differential strength of these relationships among those with low, moderate, and high job involvement suggests that job characteristics play a major role in IS employees' career expectations and quality of work life. Thus, while salary raises might be useful in enhancing the career expectations and quality of work life of IS personnel with low job involvement, strengthening the intrinsic characteristics of the job through increased autonomy, recognition, challenge, etc. may be more effective in enhancing the career expectations of IS employees who have high job involvement. The strong effect of job characteristics on QWL suggests that IS employees are more affected by the intrinsic aspects of job contexts than salary and extrinsic aspects of job contexts. It is reasonable to assume that IS employees, particularly the high job-involvement group, who are given more challenging assignments and other intrinsic rewards may develop higher degrees of satisfaction and stronger bonds to the organization. This suggests that IS employees should focus attention on providing sufficient challenge and autonomy on the job as well as on permitting freedom and autonomy to be creative and to pursue their own ideas. This implies that where possible the job should be redesigned to incorporate those motivators (autonomy and challenge) in order to enrich it and eventually improve the quality of work life.

In a similar vein, the findings that the relationships of boundary-spanning activities with organizational commitment are stronger for those in the moderate and high involvement groups suggest that IS managers should institute planned job rotations or transfers that will provide these individuals broader job experiences and opportunities to work on tasks or projects involving boundary-spanning activities. However, a note of caution is indicated because as there is strong evidence in the stress literature (Jackson and Schuler, 1985) that individuals in boundary-spanning roles experience increased role conflict and role ambiguity, with attendant negative effects on job attitudes. Therefore, efforts to provide job assignments that entail boundary-spanning should be accompanied by mechanisms for clarifying role expectations and minimizing the likelihood of conflicting role demands. The results also show that both role stressors have negative effects on satisfaction and commitment among the low, moderate, and high job involvement. It is noted that the effect of role conflict on satisfaction and commitment among the low-involvement group is the lowest. These findings are consistent with the findings of work-family literature that the highest levels of stress are experienced by those who are highly involved in both family and work (Greenhaus and Beutell, 1985). This may suggest that organizations should ensure that the level of role stressors is not raised unnecessarily as a result of poor management practices and policies within the IS and non-IS departments.

Although the results of this study may be generalized to other IS personnel, it would be useful to test whether the observed relationships hold for different types of technical and professional personnel (e.g., engineers and R & D professionals) in different organizations. Moreover, since this study examines only two levels of IS employees, future research needs to examine whether job involvement and its effects in moderating the affective reactions of individuals to attributes of their work setting differ among IS personnel representing a larger variety of positions both horizontally and vertically. It is also possible that selected demographic variables (e.g., gender, number of years in IS field, hierarchical level), life style variables (e.g., single-earner vs. dual-earner, parenthood) and job involvement, as well as boundary-spanning jobs and role stressors jointly moderate the relationships of work experiences with job attitudes and intention to stay. Therefore, future research should include other salient variables and also examine more complex interactions of job involvement with demographic and other variables in influencing the pattern of relationships between work experiences and job outcomes.

In conclusion, this study makes a contribution to the growing IS literature on careers (Baroudi, 1985; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988; Igbaria and

Greenhaus, 1992) by demonstrating the salience of job involvement in influencing the quality of work life of IS employees. More specifically, the findings indicate the key role played by job involvement both in promoting and detracting from the quality of work life. We hope that the initial insights gained in this study will stimulate additional research toward a more complete understanding of the complex role of job involvement in models of career development and job attitudes of IS personnel.
