# The complementarity of autonomy and control in mobile work

Amanda J. Porter and Bart van den Hoo<sup>f</sup>

KIN Center for Digital Innovation, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Amsterdam, Netherlands

## ABSTRACT

In this paper, we investigate how the use of mobile technologies contributes to the emergence of a complementary control system, in which both employee autonomy and management control are enhanced. We apply an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens to understand the a<sup>f</sup>ordances (and constraints) for complementary control that emerge in the use of mobile technologies by both employees and managers. Based on a qualitative case study of the implementation and use of a mobile Sales Force Automation tool at a multinational company, we identify three informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances for control and analyse how these were actualised to enhance both management control and employee autonomy. Our study contributes to extant literature on the relation between mobile technologies and autonomy and control by showing the central role of mobile technologies’ a<sup>f</sup>ordances in redistributing informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control in the interaction between managers, employees, and mobile devices.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing adoption and use of digital technologies in the workplace is rapidly changing the way work is organised and takes place in practice. One of the most prominent changes we have witnessed over the past years is the increasingly mobile nature of work (Chatterjee, Sarker, & Siponen, 2017; Yuan, Archer, Connelly, & Zheng, 2010). Today’s workplace is dominated by laptops, smartphones, and tablets that enable employees to access, create, process and exchange work-relevant information wherever they are, and whenever they have the opportunity or need to do so (Leonardi, Treem, & Jackson, 2010). These mobile technologies increasingly facilitate mobile work, or “work requiring physical movement from one place to another and/or temporary activity outside a <sup>fi</sup>xed workplace” (Yuan et al., 2010, p. 125). Obviously, this rise of mobile work is not a merely technology-driven phenomenon: organisations are rapidly changing, becoming more <sup>fl</sup>exible, agile and <sup>fl</sup>uid in response to the increasingly dynamic and unpredictable character of modern-day business environments (Chatterjee et al., 2017).

This increasingly mobile nature of work and the technologies enabling this development raise many possibilities and challenges for today’s workforce (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Cohen, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2013; Yuan et al., 2010). One particular theme that garners increasing attention in both practice and academia concerns the complex implications of mobile work for control and autonomy in organisations. Previous research on mobile technologies has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of paradoxes (Arnold, 2003; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Mazmanian, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2013), dualities (Wiredu, 2007), contradictions (Karanasios & Allen, 2014) or dialectics (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, Isaac, & Kalika, 2014; Middleton & Cukier, 2006) related to control and autonomy in organisations. On the one hand, mobile technologies are seen as empowering workers by liberating their work from constraints in terms of time and space (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Cousins & Robey, 2005), whereas on the other hand, these same technologies are found to increase managerial control over work, through constant connectivity and remote control mechanisms (Mazmanian et al., 2013; Shklovski, Troshynski, & Dourish, 2015; Wiredu & Sørensen, 2006).

Previous research thus indicates that the use of mobile technologies enhances both autonomy and control, but does not yet provide a systematic insight into the mechanisms that could explain this dual role of mobile technologies. Our study aims to contribute to such an explanation. In this paper, we examine how the use of mobile technologies may contribute to the emergence of a complementary control system, a form of control that enhances both the legitimate authority of management and the autonomy of employees (Weibel, 2010). In our analysis, we focus on the afordances, in terms of control and autonomy, that emerge in the use of mobile technologies by both employees and managers. Based on these insights, the research question guiding our study is: What afordances related to autonomy and control emerge in the use of mobile technologies by employees and managers, and how do these afordances contribute to the emergence of a complementary control system?

Based on a multi-year qualitative case study of the implementation and use of a mobile Sales Force

Automation tool at a multinational company, we identify the various a<sup>f</sup>ordances (and constraints) that emerged in relation to employee autonomy and management control. We compare these a<sup>f</sup>ordances and constraints with the technologies that preceded the mobile SFA tool, and discuss how, over time, a<sup>f</sup>ordances were actualised in a way that contributed to a complementary control system. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the actualisation of informational afordances, which allow for a redistribution of informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in establishing control, created a control system in which increased organisational control emerged in concert with increases in sales agents’ autonomy and ability to improve their performance.

This paper o<sup>f</sup>ers three main contributions. First, by identifying the informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances that contribute to increasing both professional autonomy and managerial control through the use of mobile devices, our study extends the literature on complementary control (Weibel, 2010) to the setting of mobile work. Second, by theorising how these informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances distribute informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control in interaction between managers, employees and mobile devices, our study o<sup>f</sup>ers novel insight into the mechanisms by which use of mobile devices increases both professional autonomy and management control, extending existing research on the relation between mobile devices and autonomy and control.

Third, our a<sup>f</sup>ordance perspective on autonomy and control usefully accounts for the fact that di<sup>f</sup>erent actors and technologies, with di<sup>f</sup>erent abilities, goals and requirements, are mutually engaged in the enactment of complementary control – a more nuanced perspective on control that goes beyond more dichotomised views of control, and that answers calls for new conceptual tools to interrogate and theorise the nature of “<sup>fl</sup>uid” or “free” forms of control in the mobile age (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we present our literature review, in which the literature on mobile technologies, autonomy and control is related to literature on organisational control and literature on a<sup>f</sup>ordances. Next, we discuss the setting of our qualitative case study, and explain how we collected and analysed our data. Our empirical analysis focuses on the a<sup>f</sup>ordances (and constraints) for control that emerged over the course of the implementation and use of the mobile Sales Force Automation tool within our case study organisation. The paper concludes with a discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Literature review

In order to obtain insight into the current state of research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we surveyed the literature on mobile technology use in organisations with a focus on organisational control and autonomy. We will <sup>fi</sup>rst present the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this literature review, and then further conceptualise control and autonomy. Finally, we outline the a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens on which we will base our empirical enquiry into this phenomenon.

## 2.1. Mobile technologies, autonomy and control

The rapid rise in the adoption and use of mobile information and communication technologies has evoked a broad interest in the consequences of these technologies, both in practice and in academia. In order to collect the most important insights from the academic work in this area, we conducted an analysis of recently published papers that addressed the relationship between mobile technologies and notions of control and autonomy. As a starting point for our literature review, we studied the papers in two special issues of the European Journal of Information Systems (Special Issue on Mobile User Behaviour, 2006; Special Issue on Mobile Information Systems and Mobility, 2014). Next, we extended our search through backward and forward searches (Webster & Watson, 2002): we studied the reference lists of the articles in the Special Issues for possibly relevant papers on the subject, and then used Google Scholar and Web of Science to identify papers that were citing these papers (both papers in the special issue, and those resulting from our backward search). Next to that, we also conducted a focused search on the combined keywords “mobile technology” or “mobile devices”, “control” and “autonomy” in Google Scholar and Web of Science. Together, these steps led to a collection of 23 papers that addressed (in some form or other) the role of mobile technology in relation to autonomy and control. Table 1 summarises our thematic analysis of these papers, in which we focused on the primary role of mobile technology as described in the papers, the conceptualisation of control and autonomy, and the relationship between these two concepts.

When we consider the role of mobile technology, we see a dominant view in the literature of a paradoxical relationship with autonomy and control (Arnold, 2003). Jarvenpaa and Lang (2005) for instance, list eight central paradoxes of mobile technology that users encounter. At least two of these paradoxes (empowerment/enslavement and independence/dependence) relate to an interesting duality in terms of autonomy versus control (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Karanasios & Allen, 2014; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Mazmanian et al., 2013). As Table 1 shows, the majority of the studies we analysed addressed this paradoxical nature of the relationship between autonomy and control in relation to mobile technology. A prominent paradox in the literature is the one between two roles of mobile technology: mobile devices on the one hand create norms for employees to have constant connectivity to work, but at the same time, devices can empower employees to manage the boundaries between work and home. This “autonomy paradox” (Mazmanian et al., 2013) is central to a majority of the studies we analysed.

Next to this paradoxical role, there is also a speci<sup>fi</sup>c subset of the literature that conceptualises autonomy and control as opposites in relation to mobile technology use (Anderson & Dourish, 2005; Shklovski et al., 2015; Wiredu, 2007; Wiredu & Sørensen, 2006). This is often connected to a role of mobile technology as a means of remote monitoring and control: when employees are being monitored or their activities determined by the organisation through mobile devices, they will be less autonomous. Conversely, the less monitoring by the organisation that there is through the mobile device, the more autonomous employees will become.

Finally, we also found in the literature the conceptualisation of mobile technology as an enabler of increased work e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and mobility (Karanasios & Allen, 2014; Kietzmann et al., 2013; Yuan et al., 2010). Studies focusing on this role of mobile technology typically do not pay much explicit attention to autonomy and control, although there is an implicit assumption in this literature that the mobile device is connected to user autonomy in terms of enabling increased <sup>fl</sup>exibility in where and how users work.

Comparing and contrasting the three conceptualisations of the role of mobile technology above, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that there are two (related) approaches to autonomy. First, mobile technologies are welcomed by many professionals because they feel that these technologies empower them in terms of increased autonomy and <sup>fl</sup>exibility in how they arrange their work, allowing them to work from any place, at any given time, and to improve the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of their work (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Cousins & Robey, 2005, 2015; Dery et al., 2014; Wajcman & Rose, 2011). In general, mobile technologies are found to allow for greater personal freedom, enabling employees to work away from traditional workplaces, with improved <sup>fl</sup>exibility in terms of time and space, and providing them with improved access to needed work artefacts (Leonardi et al., 2010), thus increasing employees’ autonomy. Second, a more speci<sup>fi</sup>c, related approach to autonomy is where mobile devices are seen as tools for boundary management: they enable users to actively manage the boundaries between work and private time, enabling them to become more e<sup>f</sup>ective and autonomous (Cousins & Robey, 2005, 2015; Dery et al., 2014; Hislop & Axtell, 2011).

Comparing and contrasting the three conceptualisations of the role of mobile technology above, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that there are two (related) approaches to control. First, there is an emphasis on feelings of being controlled as a consequence of the norms and expectations of “constant connectivity” implied by mobile technologies: employees feel they always have to be “on call” and supposedly available. This form of control is found in a number of empirical studies, where workers equipped with mobile devices were found to limit their own autonomy (Cavazotte et al., 2014; Hislop & Axtell, 2011; Mazmanian, 2013; Prasopoulou et al., 2006), to “reinforce a view of themselves as ‘work warriors’ and high achievers” (Mazmanian et al., 2013, p. 1351). Next to this, there is also a subset of studies that explicitly address organisational control as the monitoring, measuring, and evaluating of employees’ behaviour (Anderson & Dourish, 2005; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Shklovski et al., 2015). Mobile devices incorporate various technologies that enable the tracking of locations where they are used, as well as the functions for which they are used, which enable managers to keep track of where employees are, what they do, and how much time they spend at certain locations doing certain things. Also, controlling actions (like instructions and performance measures) are “inscribed” into technologies, enabling managers to act as a “distant authority” that exerts control over an increasingly dispersed workforce through a “mediated form of copresence” (Wiredu & Sørensen, 2006, p. 316).

Our analysis of the literature shows that autonomy and control are relational concepts that emerge in the relationship between employees, managers and technologies, and that interact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence one another. Yet the majority of the studies in our review tend to examine mobile technology in relation to either user autonomy or management control. On the one hand, many studies tend to focus on how employees interact with their mobile devices, and how this interaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences how they experience and manage boundaries between work and life – with little to no attention for the managerial perspective on this. On the other hand, the few studies that examine managerial control tend to look at how managers engage in remote control and monitoring – with little or no attention to the employee perspective in relation to this. In sum, our analysis of the literature shows that what is generally lacking is an approach to the phenomenon that encompasses both the perspectives of the user – the employees, as well as their managers – and how these perspectives interact and relate to each other. In order to come to such an approach, in the next section we will <sup>fi</sup>rst use the literature on organisational control to further conceptualise control, autonomy, and their interrelatedness. Subsequently, we will argue how an a<sup>f</sup>ordance perspective may help in understanding how control and autonomy emerge in practice through the interaction between employees, managers, and mobile technologies.

## 2.2. Control and autonomy

Organisational control is recognised as one of the central activities of management (Fayol, 1949). De<sup>fi</sup>ned as “any process whereby managers direct attention, motivate, and encourage organizational members to act in ways desirable to achieving the organization‘s objectives” (Cardinal, Sitkin, & Long, 2010, p. 56), organisational control aligns employees behaviour with the organisational goals. In order to achieve this alignment, systems of organisational control combine di<sup>f</sup>erent control mechanisms aimed at particular control targets. Control mechanisms can be either formal (rules, procedures, monitoring) or informal (norms, culture, socialisation). Control targets can be (1) the input to a system, for example, to screen individual skills or credentials of employees, (2) behaviours, for example, the way employees conduct their tasks, or (3) output, for example, the <sup>fi</sup>nal product that an employee produces (Cardinal et al., 2010).

Importantly, the fact that control is aimed at alignment of individuals’ behaviour with organisational goals does not mean that control is the direct opposite of autonomy – or that there is necessarily a paradoxical relationship between the two. Mazmanian et al. (2013) de<sup>fi</sup>ne autonomy in terms of “the ability to exercise a degree of control over the content, timing, location, and performance of activities” (p. 1337). As Weibel (2010) argues, this ability is an important element of individuals’ intrinsic motivation, and this intrinsic motivation in turn is a prerequisite for e<sup>f</sup>ective organisational control: such control is likely to be more e<sup>f</sup>ec tive in terms of reaching organisational objectives when employees are not only measured, monitored and rewarded for behaviour that contributes to such objectives, but when it also facilitates the intrinsic motivation for individuals to contribute to these objectives. Intrinsic motivation is stimulated in environments that enable individual self-determination, which hap pens when psychological needs of individuals are satis-<sup>fi</sup>ed. These include the need for autonomy (feeling that behaviour is self-chosen and not imposed by others), competence (feeling good about one’s professional abilities and development), and relatedness (feeling connected to others, based on mutual understanding and respect) (Ryan, 1995; Weibel, 2010). According to this argument, combining organisational control mechanisms and control targets in a way that leaves room for employees to feel empowered, to participate, and to enact and increase their professional competence will establish a complementary control system that reinforces the e<sup>f</sup>ects of control and intrinsic motivation in aligning organisational members’ activities with organisational objectives (Weibel, 2010). A complementary control system is a relational concept, in the sense that it is created in interaction between management and employees, and that the interests of both parties should be aligned for e<sup>f</sup>ective control to emerge.

Now that we have sketched out our theoretical conception of autonomy and control, in the next section we explain how we approach the study of this phenomenon empirically. In order to relate the use of mobile technologies to this theoretical conception, and study how these technologies may be related to complementary control, we need a perspective on mobile technology use that avoids ascribing the technology a single, deterministic role, such as an enabler or a remote controller. Such deterministic views “tilt” towards explaining either user autonomy or management control, but not both in conjunction with one another. Further, to interpret how control mechanisms increase or decrease users’ intrinsic motivations, we need detailed knowledge of the context of work – for example, knowledge of how control mechanisms are created and deployed and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks and practices that comprise “work” in the setting.

Therefore, in the next section we present an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens that we apply to our research. Such a lens can help provide a deeper insight into how autonomy and control emerge in the actual interactions between various actors (employees as well as managers) and mobile devices, because it is an inherently relational lens: a<sup>f</sup>ordances exist in the relationship between technologies and actors. Also, this lens focuses on the context within which these interactions take place, providing insight into how di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ordances emerge in interactions that take place in di<sup>f</sup>erent organisational or social contexts. In other words, given that the very same features of mobile devices used by di<sup>f</sup>erent users in di<sup>f</sup>erent organisational settings can result in very di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects in terms of autonomy and control, mobile devices aford di<sup>f</sup>erent actors di<sup>f</sup>erent possibilities for action in a given context.

## 2.3. A<sup>f</sup>ordances for autonomy and control

The concept of a<sup>f</sup>ordance originated in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of ecological psychology and has been adopted in the IS literature as a possible lens to analyse how human – technology interaction gives rise to changes in organisational arrangements and practices. In our discussion of a<sup>f</sup>ordances, we follow Volko<sup>f</sup> and Strong (2013) who de<sup>fi</sup>ne a<sup>f</sup>ordances as “the potential for behaviours associated with achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relation between an object (e.g., an IT artefact) and a goal-oriented actor or actors” (p. 823).

Our goal with applying an a<sup>f</sup>ordance perspective is to concretely analyse how di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ordances related to autonomy and control emerge in the interaction between employees, managers, and mobile technologies. In line with authors like Cousins and Robey (2015) and Volko<sup>f</sup> and Strong (2013), we emphasise the relational nature of a<sup>f</sup>ordances. Although an a<sup>f</sup>ordance can be conceptualised as a property of an object that exists independently of whether an actor perceives it, it is only when an a<sup>f</sup>ordance is actualised by an actor that we can empirically perceive it: an actor needs to perceive an a<sup>f</sup>ordance and then act on that perception in order for the a<sup>f</sup>ordance to be brought into “the domain of the actual” (Volko<sup>f</sup> & Strong, 2013). This requires actors that have the capabilities to perceive and actualise the a<sup>f</sup>ordance, and have a goal that is served by actualising this a<sup>f</sup>ordance. This means that an a<sup>f</sup>ordance exists in the relationship between an object and an actor: an a<sup>f</sup>ordance may be a possibility in terms of an inherent property of an object, but it is not de<sup>fi</sup>ned unless it is actualised by an actor with matching capabilities and goals.

Furthermore, a<sup>f</sup>ordances are contextual – di<sup>f</sup>erent action possibilities are made available (or unavailable) to speci<sup>fi</sup>c actors in speci<sup>fi</sup>c circumstances (Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld, Latham, & Vurdubakis, 2010). This emphasises the importance of the broader context (organisational, social, task-related, etc.) in which actors and objects relate to one another. This means that the relationship between actor and artefact is embedded in a larger set of relationships within the social (or organisational) context – both other actors (e.g., employees and managers), and other technologies. In other words, a<sup>f</sup>ordances emerge not only in the relationship between a user and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology, but in the relationship between a user and a bundle of available technologies, which the user (explicitly or implicitly) compares (Fayard & Weeks, 2014), embedded in a larger social context involving other actors as well.

A concept that is strongly related to a<sup>f</sup>ordance is the concept of constraint. As Leonardi (2011) explains, an actor will perceive constraints when they see a technology not as o<sup>f</sup>ering possibilities for achieving their goals, but as constraining them in pursuing these goals. Volko<sup>f</sup> and Strong (2013) explain that a<sup>f</sup>ordances are inherently both enabling and constraining, and that whether an a<sup>f</sup>ordance or a constraint is perceived is very much dependent on the actor and his or her goals and context.

In this paper, we use the a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens in order to provide insight into how mobile technology can be related to the emergence of a complementary control system. We are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interested in the a<sup>f</sup>ordances for autonomy and control (and the related constraints) that emerge in the use of mobile technologies by both employees and managers – i.e., the potential for behaviours associated with both autonomy and control that arise from the interaction between users (employee or manager) and mobile technologies. ## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research context

Our research was conducted at a multi-national beverage company based in The Netherlands called BeverageCo.<sup>1</sup> Currently, BeverageCo employs around 23,000 sales agents on a global scale across more than 70 countries. The company’s beverage portfolio is sold to “outlets,” stores that sell products to wholesalers or customers directly. The main selling strategy of BeverageCo is based on the work of sales agents, who build relationships with outlets in order to continually sell products and stay ahead of competitor brands.

BeverageCo is structured with one central “global o<sup>fi</sup>ce” that oversees the activity of operational companies, called “OpCo’s”, which operate locally in each country. In 2013, the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce started to build a global solution in order to realise a single selling strategy for all company OpCo’s. The global strategy was to drive excellence in outlets by ensuring availability, visibility and quality of the brands to win at the point of sale and contribute to sustainable growth. To realise this global selling strategy, a mobile Sales Force Automation tool, which we will call Mobile Sales Force (MSF), was implemented by the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce at participating OpCo’s. MSF is an application that runs on tablets using Microsoft software, communicating with the CRM back-end system, Microsoft CRM Dynamics. MSF was used by management to institute behavioural control through a “checklist” that sales agents had to perform during customer visits. MSF was also used by management for output control by measuring each individual sales agent’s sales performance.

Our research focused on the implementation of MSF across BeverageCo’s leading OpCo’s in seven countries. Though each country had di<sup>f</sup>erent market structures, the basic tasks of the sales agents were the same across all OpCo’s. The primary task of BeverageCo’s sales agents was to visit customer outlets, ranging from 8 to 15 outlet visits each day, organised according to the same set of tasks: pre-call, incall, and post-call activity. In pre-call, the sales agent planned his or her route and the content of his or her visits, looking up client information to prepare and plan brand activations and promotions. During incall, the sales agent was inside the outlets, checking products to ensure consistency with customer contracts and contacting the customer about new sales. Finally, in post-call, sales agents reported their in-call activity to the OpCo and the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce, where this information was stored in a CRM system. Every sales agent regularly reported to a sales manager in the local OpCo who oversaw their activities. One of the main aims of MSF was to enable the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce to further standardise and automate pre-call, in-call, and postcall activities through use of mobile devices.

## 3.2. Data collection

We employed a qualitative case study research design (Yin, 2014). In total, we collected 46 in-depth interviews with BeverageCo sales agents (N = 37) and sales managers (N = 9). BeverageCo phased the implementation: the MSF solution was rolled out slowly, in di<sup>f</sup>erent OpCo’s, across a span of many years. We conducted in-depth interviews with sales agents and managers after the rollouts that took place at OpCo’s in 2014, 2015, and 2016. All interviews were conducted within two to six months after MSF was implemented, which enabled participants to recall their work practices before the system, but still have the experience to judge how MSF was changing their work practices. See Table 3 for an overview of the data collection.

Our investigation began in an exploratory manner, in which we were initially interested in exploring more broadly how a<sup>f</sup>ordances were actualised in the use of MSF at BeverageCo, and how this compared to the situation before MSF was implemented. Such comparisons o<sup>f</sup>er important insights given that a<sup>f</sup>ordances emerge in the relationship between a user and a bundle of available technologies, which the user (explicitly or implicitly) compares when choosing one technology over another (Fayard & Weeks, 2014). In the initial interviews after the rollout of MSF in 2014 (N = 20), we discovered that sales agents and sales managers were overwhelmingly positive about the new way of working; agents reported increases in autonomy and managers reported increases in control.

In line with an iterative qualitative research approach (Wiesche, Jurisch, Yetton, & Krcmar, 2017), we sought to deepen our initial <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the second round of interviews that took place after the rollouts in 2015 (N = 14). Reviewing the literature on technology and control, we found that paradoxes and inconsistencies often arise between employees and managers, so we added more questions to our interview protocol to probe if any potential contradictions or paradoxes resulted from the use of MSF devices. We expected that this inquiry would reveal a “dark side” to the overwhelmingly positive responses to the MSF thus far. However, this second round of data collection largely con<sup>fi</sup>rmed our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews.

In our third round of interviews, after the rollouts in 2016 (N = 12), we again used theoretical sampling (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) to further understand how and why the a<sup>f</sup>ordances enabled by MSF allowed managers and sales agents to achieve greater control and autonomy without apparent contradictions or paradoxes. Following an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens, we knew that this pattern in our data could be related to the way in which MSF a<sup>f</sup>orded new opportunities for action and interaction. Thus, we added some questions to our interview protocol, probing into speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions that MSF a<sup>f</sup>orded both sales agents and managers regarding autonomy and control. We found that a<sup>f</sup>ordances were largely related to information processing that made their jobs easier and enhanced the knowledge needed to do their jobs well. We also found that these informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances contributed to changing the relationship between managers and sales agents in a positive way. We therefore further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed earlier patterns found, reaching “saturation” in our data collection. See our interview protocols in Appendix A.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Transcribed interviews were all analysed with the help of AtlasTI. First, after each round of data collection, the data were openly coded using actors’ <sup>fi</sup>rst order meanings. We used the constant comparative method, in which each new code was compared to previous codes, to ensure that new codes represented new meanings (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Second, we organised codes to further probe the main categories that constituted the core phenomenon of our investigation (Glaser, 1978). We grouped codes by actor (sales agent vs. sales manager), by time (before vs. after MSF implementation), by types of a<sup>f</sup>ordances and constraints (automation of standards vs. time consuming manual reporting), and by dimensions of employee autonomy (control over content, timing, location and performance of work) and management control (monitoring sales activity, enforcing performance targets, etc.). This process allowed us to identify patterns in the data, for example, that constraints were isolated largely to the time period prior to MSF and a<sup>f</sup>ordances primarily emerged after the implementation of MSF. This process also revealed three main “informational” a<sup>f</sup>ordances related to autonomy and control. Finally, we sought to formalise our categories into an analysis of theoretical relevance by analysing how and why these a<sup>f</sup>ordances increased both employee autonomy and management control. Tacking back and forth between data and theory (Glaser, 1978), we found that these a<sup>f</sup>ordances contributed to the emergence of a complementary control system by facilitating a positive relationship between managers and sales agents, one that was characterised by fairness, relatedness, and performance competency. See Appendix B for a diagram depicting our coding scheme. In the following section, we present the detailed analysis of our results.

## 4. Findings

Our investigation revealed that the use of mobile devices at BeverageCo resulted in three main a<sup>f</sup>ordances: automation of sales information, real-time access/visibility of sales and customer information, and auditable performance information. These informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances independently shaped management’s ability to impose and ensure standards of sales activity while enabling sales agents’ greater autonomy over the content, timing, location, and performance of their work. In order to understand how these informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances supported increased autonomy of sales agents and increased control of sales managers, we explain the practices of sales managers and sales agents before and after the implementation of MSF.

## 4.1. Phase one: informational constraints before MSF

Prior to the implementation of the MSF, sales agents worked independently in their own area, serving outlets for which they were solely responsible. Acting as “lone wolves”, sales agents enjoyed a high degree of autonomy over the timing and content of their work. As one sales agent, Jane, explained: “I manage my region. I make sure customers are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, sort out issues, and increase sales volume.” What sales agents did at outlets during customer visits was largely left to their own judgement, dependent on each individual sales agent’s knowledge of the customer and the agreements made on site. To keep their own records on each customer, most sales agents carried around large stacks of paper. Using a paper “call card”, sales agents wrote an unstructured report of what they sold at each customer. The sales agent wrote this information down manually each day, and later reported the information to a sales analyst in the local management o<sup>fi</sup>ce.

Though sales agents enjoyed working in an autonomous and independent manner, the management at BeverageCo had ambitions to create a system that would standardise all sales visits. The global o<sup>fi</sup>ce devised what they called “Outlet Standards,” which prescribed a series of checks that sales agents should perform regarding four dimensions: visibility, a<sup>f</sup>ordability, quality, and availability of the product. A sales manager, Derrick, explained, “Global wants to create a system with the same standards over the world and that customer service also works with one universal system.” The outlet standards were formatted into a paper checklist that sales agents completed while on-site during a customer visit. Outlet Standards through the checklist became a mechanism for management to have greater control over the sales agents’ behaviour while on site, which had previously been determined by the sales agents themselves.

We initially expected that sales agents would view the checklist negatively, since it required certain actions to be performed, taking away their autonomy to organise their own sales visits. Instead, we found that the Outlet Standards were generally well-received as guides for organising tasks. A sales agent explains:

Yes, the checklist makes it easier. Now, you’re focused. You know what you are looking for in an outlet. Before, with the call card, you just go to the outlet visit without knowing what to do [. . .] now you know the drivers: availability, a<sup>f</sup>ordability, visibility and quality. So you know if this is good or not.

Outlet Standards were a control mechanism that increased the sales agents’ task competency, and agents were willing to give up some of their autonomy in return for this increase.

However, while the Outlet Standards were helpful for sales agents, they struggled to keep up with the Outlet Standards checklist and the call cards combined; <sup>fi</sup>lling out both of these forms manually and reporting this to the management o<sup>fi</sup>ce was a time-consuming task that often had to be completed after hours. Because of di<sup>fi</sup>culty getting an Internet signal on the road, sales agents with laptops would often need to wait until the end of the day to enter their call cards into the CRM system at home. A sales agent, Kevin, explained:

I had this leather bag in which I had inside a laptop and all the hardcopies that I wanted. I had to check all my data on paper and when I found good signal or got home I would pass through all the data using my laptop.

Transferring this information from paper into the computer system was an extra task at the end of each day. “You had to write down things in the outlet and then go back to the o<sup>fi</sup>ce or home and spend more than an hour to an hour and a half a day to make data input for the system” (Sam, Sales Manager). Sales agents’ autonomy was diminished as they lost the ability to control the timing of their work.

We also found other constraints stemming from sales agents spending more time preparing reports for management. Sales agents reported that one major drawback of this system was that time spent with their customers during a sales visit was becoming less and less. A sales agent, Dan, explained, “We spent about 1 or 2 hours a day creating input for the CRM. That is what we lost in time to spend on the customers.” Ultimately, sales agents’ autonomy was further diminished as they lost the time to develop relationships with their customers. While the system of management control through the Outlet Standards checklist increased their task competency, when combined with the call cards, it was at odds with the sales agents’ need for relatedness with their customers. The combination of these two control mechanisms therefore reduced the sales agents’ overall autonomy.

Management at BeverageCo realised these constraints and also reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problems with checklists and call cards. A sales manager, Sam, explained “When the sales rep went back to the o<sup>fi</sup>ce or home to make the input [call cards and checklist] that meant we only got access to the data a day later.” Managers were therefore unable to direct sales agents towards certain goals because they lacked access to realtime information. In addition to delays, the data reported often had inaccuracies because of errors in the manual data entry. This made auditing the work of their sales agents di<sup>fi</sup>cult due to the lack of structured and timely information. Without a timely overview of the sales activity, managers also had to spend time manually calculating <sup>fi</sup>gures in excel in order to get a picture of the sales activity in their region. Ultimately, managers at BeverageCo achieved only minimal control via the checklist and call cards; control which came at a high cost considering how these mechanisms of control constrained sales agents’ autonomy. Such constraints were part of the impetus for the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce to change sales processes into a more structured and consistent approach through MSF.

## 4.2. Phase two: informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances after MSF

After the MSF was implemented, the <sup>fi</sup>rst noticeable change that sales agents experienced was relief from time-consuming reporting tasks. One of the main features of MSF was the automation of the Outlet Standards into a digital checklist. The digital checklist directed sales agents through the steps of the visit using their tablets. “MSF wants you to do things in a very strict way, like the digital checklist and opening and closing the visit” (Gerhard, Sales Agent). A sales agent, Dan, elaborated, “In MSF, the whole visit is step-bystep, allowing us not to forget anything. You have ten things that you need to check during the visit like distribution or assets. You can see the process of the visit and see what you speci<sup>fi</sup>cally still need to do.” Much like the paper checklist, the digital version a<sup>f</sup>orded sales agents greater competency in what tasks they needed to perform on site. However, instead of burdening sales agents with lengthy paper work after the sales visit, MSF devices automatically reported the results of the checklist in real time to the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce. By automatically reporting sales activity, agents could perform their work on the road, which was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time saver. A sales manager, Sam, explained:

The main di<sup>f</sup>erence with this system is that you can work inside the outlet. In the past you had to write down things in the outlet and then go back to the o<sup>fi</sup>ce or home and spend more than an hour to an hour and a half a day to make data input for the system. Now they [sales agents] do all that in the outlet with the system and they save all that time by doing it in the outlet. We could say they even earn time.

Automated reporting was an important a<sup>f</sup>ordance because sales agents used the time that they earned to interact with their customers. A sales agent, Dan, explained, “It quickens the <sup>fl</sup>ow of the visit and that means that you have more useful time for negotiations and really talking with the customer. It makes us more <sup>fl</sup>exible and quicker in the market.” A sales manager, George, explained: “They [sales agents] are now spending more time on the true job [rather] than having to do the system job [enter data] in front of the customer.” Sales agents had time for the activity that they were intrinsically motivated to perform: interacting and building relationships with the customer, which was an important <sup>fi</sup>rst step towards management control that was not at odds with sales agents’ autonomy.

Sales agents also used the MSF to improve how they sold to their customers. A sales agent, Chris, explained how customer information was used on site through the tablets, “We have the total control and the total image of our customers through the tablet. It is more accurate than the previous one.” For example, sales agents could bring up information on the history of each customer during their sales visits, which provided knowledge to support a sales approach tailored to the customer’s needs. A sales agent explained, “I don’t need to crack my head about what am I going to do this in this outlet. By looking at my tablet, I know my last conversation, my last discussion. I can quickly make my [sales] decision based on my last discussion, conversation and the evolution of the outlet.” A manager also elaborated: “They [sales agents] can focus on the conversation with the customer and the negotiation, the deals you make. Focus on how to get your visibility bigger and how to get a promotion sold and all these things” (Harold, Sales Manager). Accessibility to valuable customer information and history not only gave sales agents control again over the content of their work, it also enabled them to enhance their performance.

The new digital checklist also a<sup>f</sup>orded managers in the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce the opportunity to inscribe corporate standards for sales activity into the system. However, instead of error-prone and delayed reporting, automated reporting enabled the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce to better monitor sales agents’ behaviour in real-time, ensuring greater accuracy of standards. Through the MSF devices, Outlet Standards were linked to individual sales agents, so when a sales agent performed the digital checklist on MSF, management received real-time information on an agent’s activity for each of the standards.

Managers eventually used this information to create personalised performance targets for sales agents. Using an audit tool on the MSF, sales managers could check whether sales agents’ activity met the targets. The audit tool also displayed all of the targets to the managers through a dashboard, providing real-time visibility of the overall sales activities for the region, “So if I create a dashboard of the sales rep in the last four weeks, it actualises every day, every hour.” (Mike, Sales Manager). Sales managers used the audit tool in order to make decisions on new targets for the region. Abby, a sales support sta<sup>f</sup>, explained: “Most of the data will go to the manager who will analyse it and they will implement new targets and take decisions, depending on the results.” New targets were added because the system provided data on a larger range of activities than what was available before. As a sales manager, George, explained, “MSF is a measurement tool.” Performance numbers quickly conveyed the information necessary to support sales managers’ decision-making on setting dynamic performance targets. Accordingly, the information provisioned by the use of MSF marked an important shift in management control at BeverageCo: from minimal control that monitored sales information post – hoc, to pro-active alignment of sales agents performance with management targets.

Sales agents themselves also used the targets displayed through the MSF devices to actively compare their performance to the standards set by the global o<sup>fi</sup>ce in real time. A sales agent, Tim, explains:

We have two targets. Targets per outlet and the targets for the market. We can see both of them when we visit an outlet. I can see my performance per visit and my performance per target, that helps me a lot. Because <sup>fi</sup>rst of all, my targets are the most important thing I have. I think it helps me a lot to see the targets every time that I want.

A sales agent, Kevin, elaborated, “A new target for MSF is that I’m measuring my percentage of shelf and displays. I’m passing all the data through to

MSF, and if my data shows that something is missing, I will make the order that the store needs and realise a good share of space for my target.” While sales agents also had performance targets with the former paper-based system, they did not have knowledge of their actual performance at any given moment in time. Real-time audits of their performance enabled sales agents to better plan the sales visit, because when targets were not met, this ultimately a<sup>f</sup>ected the sales agent’s commissions. A sales agent, Dan, explained, “MSF always projects what are the main focus areas per outlet. What do you have to install and what should there be in an outlet? You push the client towards those targets.” Sales agents could closely monitor how they were performing:

I know from the start of the month my objectives, I plan my goals for this month and when I look at my results I open this platform and see everything I want to know. I see my objectives and I know the things I must do for the next days (Kristo<sup>f</sup>, Sales Agent).

Interestingly, sales agents did report several technical errors during the pilot phase of MSF, and notably, the inability to view their targets was perceived as one of the most problematic issues for sales agents. This breakdown illuminated how important auditing performance had become for sales agents. Improving sales agents’ performance competency was an important step towards management control that also supported employees’ need for self-determination.

## 4.3. The interaction between sales agents and managers

Our analysis so far has explained how the use of MSF at BeverageCo a<sup>f</sup>orded sales agents greater autonomy and managers increased control. Individually, both parties made important gains such as more accurate monitoring on the part of managers and improving performance competency for sales agents. Yet, we also found these a<sup>f</sup>ordances positively reinforced the relationship between sales agents and sales managers by increasing perceptions of fairness, trust, and relatedness. See Figure 1 for a depiction of how this positive reinforcement between sales agents and managers created and sustained a system of complementary control.

One of the important a<sup>f</sup>ordances actualised through the use of MSF was the automation of sales information. Importantly, this increased the accuracy of sales reporting and monitoring, which had an important impact on the sales agent’s and sales manager’s relationship. Namely, sales agents felt that due to automation of sales information, their managers had a more objective view of their work. Ada, a sales agent explains, “Performance checking has changed. If you have done your work, your boss can know objectively what you have done.” Consequently, sales agents perceived close monitoring of performance, because it was more accurate, as more fair than monitoring prior to MSF.

In addition to increased perceptions of fairness, we also found that real-time access to sales information impacted their relationship positively by increasing managers’ understanding of sales agents’ work. Namely, managers could see immediately if the outlet standards were not met by a particular agent, and could make a quick phone call to get the sales agent back on track. This open line of communication helped managers at BeverageCo to gain a better understanding of what sales agents’ work entailed on a daily basis, as one sales agent re<sup>fl</sup>ected, “There is a change in relationship. The auditable performance information a<sup>f</sup>orded by use of the MSF also created a greater sense of trust, as one sales agent described, “The centre evaluation is <sup>fi</sup>ne. Ultimately, it was the combination of these three informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances that improved fairness, relatedness and trust, creating a positive interdependence between increased employee autonomy and greater management control.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The aim of this study was to explore how complementary control emerges in the use of mobile technologies. Using an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens, we investigated how a<sup>f</sup>ordances evolved over time in the case of BeverageCo. Our analysis showed how sales agents and managers using mobile devices actualised informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances that both alleviated constraints and enhanced their work. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the use of mobile devices automated time consuming information processing tasks and provisioned valuable information that sales agents and managers used to increase both employee autonomy and organisational control. Increased autonomy and control positively reinforced one another as the relationship between sales agents and managers was improved as a result. In this section, we discuss the contributions of our analysis for research on mobile technologies and the relation between employee autonomy and organisational control.

## 5.1. Distributing the informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control

The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution of our study is to identify a<sup>f</sup>ordances of mobile technologies contributing to the emergence of a complementary control system. Weibel (2010) theorised that by supporting individuals’ intrinsic motivation, management control mechanisms could actually enhance employees’ selfdetermination. Yet Weibel’s theory of complementary control lacked explanation of how exactly this occurred, and is not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally aimed at mobile work. Our study elucidates three informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances of mobile devices – automation, real-time access/visibility, and auditable information – that were actualised by users to enhance employees’ motivation as well as to align their activities with organisational objectives (Weibel, 2010), extending complementary control to the setting of mobile work. Further, our study theorises how these informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances collectively enact a complementary control system in the interaction between managers, employees, and mobile devices. In the following sections, we elaborate on how this occurred as informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances distributed the informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control in such a way that both parties, managers and employees, were empowered to exercise their preferred forms of control. Mobile devices play a dual role in relation to autonomy and control: the same technology facilitates both employee autonomy and organisational control.

One of the critical informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances contributing to complimentary control in our study was automation that relieved employees and managers of the demands of information processing. Information processing is an inherent aspect of any system of control, as Beniger (1986) argues, “inseparable from the concept of control are the twin activities of information processing and reciprocal communication, complementary factors in any form of control” (p. 8). Accordingly, mechanisms of organisational control (whether formal or informal, and regardless of the target of control) have informational requirements that need to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled (Ouchi, 1979). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that use of mobile technologies a<sup>f</sup>ording automation supported the informational dimension of organisational control by storing and processing critical information (Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1988), such as sales activity and sales output. In phase one of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, before the implementation of MSF, paper-based reporting demanded that sales agents manually record, store, and transfer information on sales and Outlet Standards to the organisation. This impacted the sales agents’ autonomy negatively, not because sales agents opposed the organisational standards per se, but because sales agents were responsible for ful<sup>fi</sup>ling these informational demands themselves, which constrained their ability to control the location, timing, and performance of their work. Not surprisingly, when employees shoulder the burden of processing such information, management control and employee autonomy become at odds with one another, in the sense that more organisational control means less employee autonomy. However, in phase two of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, after the implementation of MSF, sales agents were relieved of these information processing burdens, shifting their time and attention away from lengthy paperwork at the end of the day to spending more time with the customer during the sales visit. Automated information processing also impacted management positively by creating more accurate information to monitor sales activity. Automation was therefore a critical a<sup>f</sup>ordance contributing to complementary control, as mobile devices are optimally con<sup>fi</sup>gured to handle large volumes of information and take over time consuming information processing tasks (Beniger, 1990).

The use of mobile devices also a<sup>f</sup>orded a number of informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that contributed to complementary control by enabling sales agents and managers to further exercise their own preferred forms of control. Scholars of information technology and control have argued that while technologies are good at processing large volumes of information, as described above, human agents are particularly good at processing information in a personalised manner, while organisations are needed to process information towards explicit and impersonal goals (Beniger, 1990). An informational bene<sup>fi</sup>t realised through use of mobile devices in our case was real-time access to customer and sales information, which bene<sup>fi</sup>ted sales agents by enabling them to customise their sales pitches and therefore give them greater control over the aspects of their work that they valued the most: selling to the customer. Similarly, managers bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from real-time access to sales information because this enabled them to better ensure that the organisational standards were being met. Another informational bene<sup>fi</sup>t realised through use of mobile devices in our case was auditable performance information. Auditable, personalised sales information (Chatterjee et al., 2017) contributed to increasing employee autonomy by enabling sales agents to further hone and boost their performance. Auditable performance information also enabled managers to have greater control over the overall sales performance, helping to safeguard the organisation’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial position. The ability of both sales agents and managers to develop their preferred forms of control was a “win-win” for both employees and managers.

These informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances combine to enable a practice of distributing the informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control in such a way that managers goals for controlling behaviour and outputs of employees actually support employees’ needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Weibel, 2010). For example, auditable performance information, based on automated information, was perceived by sales agents as more fair than previous systems of paper-based control that were considered error-prone and not “objective”. Increased perceptions of fairness are directly related to employees’ enhanced sense of autonomy (Long, Bendersky, & Morrill, 2011). Further, real time access to sales agents’ activities enabled managers to gain a greater understanding of the work performed by sales agents, enabling them to create more useful performance targets. Appropriately distributing the informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of control creates a positive, self-reinforcing loop between managerial control and employee self-determination.

## 5.2. New forms of control emerging in the digital age

Our study also contributes to existing research on mobile technologies in relation to autonomy and control. By o<sup>f</sup>ering an afordance perspective on the role of mobile devices in establishing a complementary control system, our study answers calls for new conceptual tools to interrogate and theorise the nature of control in the mobile age (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014). The perspective of a<sup>f</sup>ordances accounts for the fact that di<sup>f</sup>erent actors, with di<sup>f</sup>erent abilities, goals and requirements, are mutually engaged in the enactment of complementary control. We propose that this more nuanced, less dichotomised view on control can be an important conceptual shift in the debate on mobile devices and their impact on the balance between autonomy and control by providing a basis for new kinds of empirical questions that facilitate deeper exploration of the autonomy-control relationship. Instead of inquiring into the “balance” between autonomy and control, this perspective proposes a more fundamental question: what kind(s) of control are a<sup>f</sup>orded, and control between whom? Second, the a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens shows how these kinds of control are always relational, distributed among multiple agents; this shifts the focus from examining mobile devices’ impact on work to examining how control shifts over time in the interplay between the agents in the control system. We believe that this perspective can facilitate further research aimed at a more nuanced understanding how “<sup>fl</sup>uid” or “free” forms of control (Chatterjee et al., 2017; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014) emerge in the use of mobile technologies, and provide more insight into the dual role that mobile technologies play in relation to autonomy and control.

The concept of an information network is central to free or <sup>fl</sup>uid control. Leclercq-Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al. (2014, p. 543) outline how an information network keeps consultants in a position of “allowed subjection” – although they are mobile, <sup>fl</sup>exible and autonomous, the information network that connects them to the organisation “even leads them to build their own control through the co-construction of a norm of permanent availability” (p. 554). Chatterjee et al.’s (2017) conception of <sup>fl</sup>uid control builds on this idea. In essence, what is described here is very similar to the complementary control system that is central to our study, and clearly connects to our idea of informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances. However, our study contributes to the concept of control <sup>fl</sup>uidity by showing how free or <sup>fl</sup>uid control can be con<sup>fi</sup>gured to become complementary through informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the distribution of informational demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is a key mechanism for ensuring that professionals are able to improve their autonomy, performance competency and relatedness with others while they at the same time willingly co-create an information network that enables a form of management control that they perceive as bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to themselves as well as to the larger organisation. Rather than subjecting the employee to a disguised form of control (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014), connection to the organisation’s information network can become an asset by strengthening professionals’ ability to self-initiate behaviours that are important to them (Sitkin, Cardinal, & Bijlsma-Frankema, 2010) and increase their motivation (Weibel, 2010).

We argue that informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances that emerge in the use of mobile devices are relevant to understand ing mobile work in other contexts. Previous studies <sup>fi</sup>nd similar bene<sup>fi</sup>ts emerging in the use of mobile devices by police o<sup>fi</sup>cers (Karanasios & Allen, 2014), o<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment service engineers (Hislop & Axtell, 2011), footwear sales people (Mazmanian, 2013), law professionals (Cavazotte et al., 2014), and consultants (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014). Although these studies also report bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as less paperwork, easier access to relevant information, and a reduced need to return to the o<sup>fi</sup>ce as a result of mobile device use, they do not conceptualise these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as a<sup>f</sup>ordances that emerge in the interaction between managers, employees, and technologies; instead, these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are seen as the outcome of employees’ mobile technology use. Also, with the exception of Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al.’s study, these informational bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts are not related to the emergence of a complementary control system. Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al.’s (2014) analysis does relate the informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the consultants experienced to the emergence of “free” control, but (similarly to the other studies mentioned above) their focus is exclusively on the consultants’ use of the devices, thus contributing to an information network through which they “build the means of their own control” (p. 554). Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are relevant to other contexts in which mobile work takes place and connection to an information network is critical. Yet our study contributes to earlier work that has been conducted in these contexts by identifying the informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances that emerge in the use of mobile technologies by both employees and managers, and outlining how these a<sup>f</sup>ordances contribute to the emergence of a complementary control system.

## 5.3. Limitations and directions for future research

This paper reports on a single case study, which has several limitations. First, while our analysis o<sup>f</sup>ers a rich account of a<sup>f</sup>ordances for control and autonomy, these results have limited generalisability in the statistical sense. Despite this limitation, our in-depth analysis of comple mentary control is likely to ring true in other settings beyond our case, and as discussed above, is transferable to settings with similar circumstances (Lincoln, 1995). These circumstances are characterised by the presence of highly mobile workers who are connected to their organisation through a network in which a large amoun and diversity of information is exchanged: police o<sup>fi</sup>cers, service engineers, consultants, sales people, health professionals, et cetera. Under these circumstances, similar informational a<sup>f</sup>ordances as found in our case study are likely to arise, with similar possible consequences in terms of complementary control. A related limitation of our study is that we selected a case that focuses on mobile use by sales agents and sales managers, in which performance and remuneration is tied to sales output. Since both sales agents and sales managers are similarly motivated to increase this output, they have many aligned interests. While this feature of our case o<sup>f</sup>ers a unique opportunity to understand and theorise complementary control, it also should also be balanced with other case studies that examine a<sup>f</sup>ordances of control in a range of di<sup>f</sup>erent organisational settings. Future research on a<sup>f</sup>or dances for control and autonomy is needed to better understand the contextual conditions that facilitate the adoption of mobile devices for complementary control, and in particular how complementary control is sustained or subverted over time. For example, while we did not find any evidence of sales agents at BeverageCo “gaming the system” to manipulate their performance numbers, it would be interesting for future research to examine if and how complementary control transforms into other types of control over time. Further, since complementary control relies heavily on mobile devices to ful<sup>fi</sup>l informational demands and distribute informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, it is likely that technological breakdowns will have far reaching consequences. Such technical problems were experienced brie<sup>fl</sup>y at BeverageCo, but were particularly troublesome; without instant access to their performance data, sales agents were jolted back to old work practices that involved taking action based on limited information and themselves falling back into the role of information processors. When mobile devices o<sup>f</sup>er new competencies, their breakdowns can have an opposite e<sup>f</sup>ect, suggesting that the line between independence and dependence may be quick to dissolve (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005). Future research should examine breakdowns in systems of complementary control to better understand the far-reaching consequences of mobile devices in shaping employee autonomy and organisational control.
