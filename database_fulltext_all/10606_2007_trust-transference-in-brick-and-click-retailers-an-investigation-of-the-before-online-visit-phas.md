# Trust transference in brick and click retailers: An investigation of the before-online-visit phase

Huei-Huang Kuan <sup>a,\*</sup>, Gee-Woo Bock <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> National University of Singapore, Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, 3 Science Drive 2, Singapore 117543, Singapore

<sup>b</sup> Sungkyunkwan University, School of Business Administration, 53 Myeongnyn-dong 3-ga, Jongno-gu, Seoul 110-745, South Korea Received 21 February 2006; received in revised form 27 October 2006; accepted 13 December 2006 Available online 17 December 2006

## Abstract

We examined the formation of online trust encountered by potential customers of a brick and click retailer before they visit its online website; this phase of the purchasing experience has been under-investigated in trust literature. Since a brick and click retailer is eager to attract offline customers to its website based on trust in the brick and mortar stores, our exploratory study investigated, (1) Can customers’ offline trust in a retailer affect their online trust (in the website operations of the retailer)?; (2) What other factors might be influential on online trust? A social relations and networks perspective was adopted to explain customers’ online trust formation during the before-online-visit phase. Findings from a sample of 246 offline customers who had never visited a supermarket’s website revealed that word-of-mouth, offline trust, and expected sanctioning power were significant in forming online trust. Furthermore, findings showed that word-of-mouth was more influential than offline trust based on a customers personal experience with the supermarket’s physical stores. This provided empirical evidence on how and why some pure online retailers outperformed brick and click retailers.

Keywords: Brick and click retailers; Social relations and networks; Offline presence; Online presence; Online trust; Before-online-visit phase; Social capital theory

## 1. Introduction

A Gartner Consulting survey of brick and click retailing [18] reported that 33% of their respondents had an adoption strategy in place, 27% were in an internaldiscussion phase, and 14% had initiated discussions with technology vendors. However, the implementation of this form of retailing did not necessarily warrant online success. It was puzzling why customers felt more comfortable transacting with certain pure online retailers (such as Amazon.com) as opposed to wellknown brick and click retailers (such as Barnes and Noble) which had a longer history and possessed a stronger brand image [30]. Other well-known brick and click retailers such as Sears are not experiencing levels of online sales they originally expected either [20].

If the formation of online trust through the offline presence is sufficient for online success, brick and click retailers should excel in their online sales and outperform pure online retailers. However, the examples show that this is not always the case. Therefore, we decided to examine how a customer’s trust in the brick and click retailer’s physical stores (offline trust) affected the customers’ trust of the retailer’s online operations (online trust) and to determine what other factors might influence the formation of online trust in the context of brick and click retailers before the customer has visited the retailer’s website.

These two research questions could contribute to trust research in two major ways: First, despite studies citing trust to be central to online purchases [61,63], many previous studies have identified antecedents of trust, mainly in the context of pure online retailers. Thus, our study bridges the gap of trust research by examining it in the brick and click retailers’ context. Secondly, there has been no study examining online trust of customers towards brick and click retailers before they visit the retailer’s website. This is important because it is very likely that a substantial proportion of customers have already formed offline trust (based on their interactions with the retailers’ physical stores) before they visit the retailers’ websites. In addition, by eliminating the effects of any online visits in examining online trust, we can effectively examine whether trust stemming from customers’ interactions with the retailer’s physical stores would lead to the formation of online trust.

In order to address these questions, we used the social relations and networks perspective based on social capital theory, since trust is developed through the social relations and networks of an individual especially when he or she has had no interaction with the trustee [28]. Research in other disciplines has shown that trust within the social relations and networks of individuals facilitate economic transactions.

## 2. Literature review

Trust has been defined in many ways and there is no universally accepted definition of it [54]. In our study, we consider it to depend on the customer’s beliefs about the vendor’s attributes of integrity, benevolence, and competence [46], because this articulates the cognitive basis on which people build their trust. We adapted two tables of Komiak and Benbasat’s [37] study to provide a systematic way of classifying previous studies on trust (see Table 1). In this, the horizontal axis refers to these authors’ interaction phases: before-, initial- and repeated-, while the vertical axis refer to three types of commerce: conventional, pure online, and brick and click. We thus extended the original tables by differentiating web-mediated commerce, into pure online and brick and click commerce.

From our literature review, two areas of online trust research seemed to be missing: firstly, although online trust is paramount for brick and click retailers’ online success, most research had only considered the context of pure online retailers with only online presence [65]; secondly, studies on brick and click retailers had focused on examining trust during the initial online visit, thus ignoring the process of formation of online trust before a customer makes any interaction with the website. Examining these two areas was the main focus of our study.

## 2.1. Context of trust: brick and click retailers

Brick and click retailers work by exploiting synergies between their offline and online presence to provide better customer service, especially through the transfer of customers’ trust of the offline to the online presence [57]. However, a review of online trust research on brick and click retailers revealed that the relationship between offline and online trust has not been fully investigated. Stewart’s study showed that the brick and click retailer’s physical presence together with its address enhanced online trust. However, it was not clear whether customers regarded this as just another online artifact that assured safe transactions (similar to a third party’s seal) or whether it actually represented their trust in the offline stores of a retailer which ultimately translated into online trust of the brick and click retailer. Likewise, Xu et al.’s investigation of the online trust of a brick and click retailers did not empirically examine the relationship between offline trust and online trust. Hence, it was not determined whether customers offline trust resulting from their actual interaction with the physical stores increased online trust in the same brick and click retailer, and, if so, to what extent.

## 2.2. Interaction dimension of trust: before-onlinevisit phase

Trust develops through interactions [5]. Based on Komiak and Benbasat’s classification, the beforeonline-visit phase refers to the period before a customer has made any online visit to the retailer’s website. Studies on trust during this phase in the context of pure online commerce have emphasized the importance of perceived reputation [1] and control mechanisms [60], such as technological measures to build trust to engage in a transaction. However, apparently, there has been no study to investigate online trust during the beforeonline-visit phase for brick and click retailers. Although Stewart’s study suggested that the offline presence could stimulate online trust of a brick and click retailer during the initial-interaction stage, it did not control for the effects of the first online visit on online trust (i.e. website quality, structural assurance, etc.). Since the initial online visit with the website can help to build trust through the online navigation experience [38], it was impossible to tell whether the online trust formed was due to the online navigation experience or through the customer’s interactions with the offline presence (physical stores).

Thus, in order to examine the effect of a customer’s trust in the physical stores on the online trust without any effect of online visits as well as to address the lack of online trust research for brick and click retailers in the before-online-visit phase, we focused on the formation of online trust during the before-online-visit phase in the context of brick and click retailers.

## 3. Theoretical perspective

Granovetter asserted that trust was generated through the social relations and networks of an individual, especially when he or she has had had no prior interaction with the trustee. Social capital inheres in the structure of relations among actors and was defined as the value of any aspect of an informal social organization that constituted a productive resource for one or more actor [13–15] that could be accessed and mobilized in a purposive action [44]. Coleman reasoned that other individuals’ trust in the online operations of a brick and click retailer within the social network were resources to be mobilized to influence an individual’s economic action and that the mobilization of such resources ultimately lead to the formation of an individual’s trust of the actor. This is consistent with Lewis and Weigert [42] who argued that an individual would form online trust of a brick and click retailer because he or she knew others who also trusted the online operations of the brick and click retailer. Drawing on social capital theory, two factors help generate individual online trust: information channels and effective sanctions.

Information channels provide important information via social relations through which a customer’s online trust in a brick and click retailer can be formed: it can be due to other individuals’ experiences with the retailer as well as any personal experience (offline or online) with the retailer that help the customer infer the characteristics of the brick and click retailer’s online operations (e.g., competence, benevolence and integrity). Since customers do not have any direct online experience with the retailer during the before-online-visit phase, the information channels are informal and due to social contacts and offline interactions with the physical stores of the retailer. Through these information channels, online trust can be formed through Doney and Cannon’s [19] transference process.

As another resource of trust, sanctions can provide the means of limiting untrustworthy and deviant behavior through deterrence: effective sanctions can facilitate and safeguard economic transactions, as they define and reinforce the parameters of acceptable behavior by stating the consequences of violating norms and values. This involves customers in punishing retailers who violate norms, values or goals, by adopting mechanisms such as gossip and rumors or even ostracism (such as exclusion from the network for short periods). Researchers such as Singh [56] found that customers can enforce sanctions on retailers through complaint measures between customers and retailers; if customers think that these are effective in influencing the retailer, they will believe that brick and click retailers are constrained to behave in a trustworthy manner (amid the uncertainty inherent in online transactions) due to the perception that these retailers would have much to lose from untrustworthy online transactions.

## 4. Model and hypotheses

Based on social capital theory, we constructed our research model and developed hypotheses that are depicted in Fig. 1. The dependent variable of interest to a brick and click retailer is a customer’s online purchase intention, which was defined as the likelihood that a customer will purchase from the online operations of the brick and click retailer [21].

In the model, we assumed that online trust was a full mediator between social capital constructs and online intention to purchase. Coleman and Lin both suggested that other people’s trust in the online operations of the brick and click retailer is a form of social capital to be mobilized to influence economic action and the outcome of mobilizing this capital ultimately leads to the formation of the trust of a particular actor. Also, social learning theory [2] says that an individual gathers evidence of retailers’ trustworthiness from people they respect about a retailer’s online operations and integrate them in their belief structure. However, if the potential customer does not integrate such evidence into his or her own belief structure, he or she is unlikely to engage in economic transactions with the vendor. Thus, only when the consumer trusts the retailer, he or she will feel comfortable in purchasing from the retailer [61,64].

Customers’ information channels exist in the form of indirect information from social contacts and offline interactions with the physical stores of the retailer, yielding two sources: word-of-mouth and offline trust. As potential customers do not have any prior experience with a retailer, they are unable to assess available sanctions accurately, but, their expectations of sanctioning power may play a role in influencing online trust.

## 4.1. Online trust and purchase intention

Online trust positively affects online purchase intention because the customer believes that the retailer’s online operations is able (because of competence) and willing (due to benevolence and integrity) to deliver the products purchased. As such, if customers place their trust on the retailer online, they rule out possible but unfavorable online actions of the retailer [45], leading to higher online purchase intention. The relationship between online trust and online purchase intention has been empirically investigated during the initial online visit phase [47] and post-initial purchase phase [27] but not during the before-online-visit phase. Hence, we hypothesized:

H1. Online trust is positively related to online purchase intention.

## 4.2. Information channels

Customers who have not interacted with a brick and click retailer through its website primarily rely on indirect information in their social networks. We defined word-of-mouth as the favorability of indirect information about online purchasing from the retailer obtained through a customer’s offline and online social relations and network (see [53,66]). It can be in the form of recommendations from friends, relatives, colleagues, and acquaintances but the influence of newspapers and printed advertisements were not examined in our study.

The word-of-mouth encapsulates the influence of both positive and negative referrals. In most empirical research, only the effect of either positive or negative aspect of word-of-mouth communication has been examined (e.g. [39]) although negative referrals have a larger influence than positive ones on brand and retailer perceptions [48]. However, both favorable and unfavorable information of online purchasing experiences needed to be examined because positive and negative referrals are not direct opposites. Customers can have some social contacts providing positive referrals while others provide negative ones concurrently. Therefore, we conceptualized word-of-mouth as a formative construct which consisted of both referrals to present a more holistic investigation of the impact of word-ofmouth on online trust. Hence, we hypothesized:

## H2. Word-of-mouth within a customer’s social network is positively related to online trust.

Furthermore, an individual’s offline interactions with retailers’ physical stores provide another information channel during the before-online-visit phase. In making offline interactions and purchases from a retailer’s physical stores, a direct relationship with the retailer is likely to have been developed, resulting in the formation of offline trust. Since customers in the before-onlinevisit phase have no direct information on a retailer’s online presence, they are likely to draw upon their offline trust to infer the attributes of the online operations of the retailer.

Other studies have implicitly suggested that the offline presence can enhance the online trust of a brick and click retailer without sufficient empirical evidence. Offline trust pertains to customers’ own interactions with the offline presence of the retailer and was defined in our study as the belief of the competence, benevolence and integrity of the brick and click retailer’s physical stores. According to Yamagishi and Yamagishi’s explanation of interpersonal trust, customers’ offline trust affects their online trust as they are dealing with the same retailer through another channel. Thus, if a customer has high offline trust in the physical stores of the retailer, he or she is likely to have high online trust in the retailer’s online operations. Thus we postulated,

## H3. Offline trust is positively related to online trust.

To extend this, we proposed one moderating factor, customers’ online purchasing experience, that may influence the relationship between offline trust and online trust because customers are less likely to trust a specific retailer’s online operation when they do not trust the Internet shopping medium in general [40]. Internet purchasing experience affects a customer’s trust in the Internet shopping medium and with increased online purchasing experience, a customer transfers his or her trust in the retailer’s physical stores to the retailer’s online operations. Therefore, increased Internet purchasing experience is likely to strengthen the relationship between offline trust and online trust and we hypothesized:

H4. Internet purchasing experience is a positive moderator of the relationship between offline trust and online trust.

## 4.3. Sanctions: expected sanctioning power

Based on the social capital of sanctions, we defined the formative construct of expected sanctioning power as an individual expectation of the effectiveness of complaint measures available to a customer to rectify his or her online purchasing problems and punish the retailer [55]. This definition is also drawn from French and Raven’s [23] concept of coercive power, where it is defined as the perception of an individual that he or she can impose punishment on a party. Despite the knowledge that customers use complaint measures to express their dissatisfaction and punish the retailer [10], the effect of customers’ expectations of complaints effectiveness on online trust has been neglected in ecommerce research.

Since customers are unable to evaluate the effectiveness of available sanctions for themselves before any online visit to the online brick and click retailer, their expectations of sanctioning power can be a form of calculus-based trust towards the retailer online [41]. When customers expect that they are able to rectify their online purchasing problems and sanction the retailer, they believe that they are san impose negative consequences should the online operations be untrustworthy.

Day and Landon [17] split complaining measures into two types: public action (direct) to seek redress from the retailer and private action (within the social network), such as boycotting and discouraging others from purchasing. Singh also used two categories in his framework: voice (directly complaining to the retailer) and private. However, the measures of these frameworks were mainly relevant to retailers with only an offline presence. Adapting these classifications, we considered three modes for customers of brick and click retailers: indirect, online direct and crosschannel.

Indirect sanctions are private measures (when customers complain to social contacts about bad experiences or decide not to purchase online from the retailer). Online direct sanctions are measures that customers may use to directly to seek redress for disappointing purchases (e.g., emailing the retailer’s website and attempt thereby to obtain some compensation for the problem). Cross-channel sanctions, which are unique to brick and click retailers, allow customers to go to the retailer’s physical stores to complain and thus seek resolution to problems resulting from their online purchases.

Using Shapiro et al.’s reasoning, if the level of expected sanctioning power is high, customers have greater online trust in the retailer. We posited:

H5. Expected sanctioning power is positively related to online trust.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Procedure and data collection

We collected our data in May 2005, using a survey administered to real customers within the offline physical stores of a brick and click retailer; customers were selected had never visited the retailer’s website. The brick and click retailer in this study was an established supermarket in Asia. Partnering with a nationwide broadband network, this retailer had been the first supermarket (in 1997) in the region to offer its merchandise in cyberspace. The number of regular online customers has grown from 6000 in 1998 to over 15,000 in 2006. The survey was administered to 246 customers and the demographic profile of the customers is given in Table 2. Customers shopping for their groceries were asked if they would assist in completing a questionnaire and a \$10 shopping voucher was promised as an incentive for completing it.

## 5.2. Measurement of variables

We drew on existing literature to measure the variables of our study in order to ensure the selection of reliable and valid scales. Constructs with no existing instruments were also developed for this study. Following Moore and Benbasat’s [49] study, judges were asked to sort the various items into construct categories to check the conceptual validity of constructs with multiple indicators. Labeled sorting was carried out by four IS PhD students to check for convergence and divergence of items within categories. We detected no major problems, indicating that the constructs possessed adequate conceptual validity. Pre-tests were carried out on a small sample of 20 IS graduate students before the instrument was launched to the sample of the target population. For all variables given in Table 3, ratings were made on a seven-point Likert scale. The questionnaire items are listed in Appendix A.

As there were no prior studies examining the relationship between offline and online trust, we adapted previous trust items to measure it. However, we asked customers responding to the questionnaire to answer questions dealing with offline trust based on of the retailer’s operations in the physical stores and to complete items of online trust based on their beliefs about the operations on the retailer’s website. Furthermore, as we administered the questionnaires, we encouraged customers to ask questions if they could not understand these instructions; thus, we reduced the confusion of customers in distinguishing between offline and online trust items in the questionnaire.

## 7. Discussion and implications

The study has examined the factors that affect the formation of online trust for customers of a brick and click retailer who have never visited its website. First and foremost, word-of-mouth of the retailer’s online operations exerts the dominant effect on online trust and the effect was found to be much stronger than offline trust. Secondly, expected sanctioning power occurring through social relations plays a significant but peripheral role in influencing the online trust of customers. Together with the significant relationship between online trust and online purchase intention during the before-online-visit phase, these findings underscore the importance of social relations and networks to build customers’ online trust and stimulate online purchases from brick and click retailers.

## 7.1. Online trust and online purchase intention

This study focused on online purchase intention as a dependent variable in the before-online-visit phase. The path coefficient of online trust (0.606) showed that if customers believed that the online operations of the brick and click retailer had characteristics of competence, benevolence and integrity, they would feel comfortable when purchasing online from the retailer. This extended previous online trust literature as this relationship had not been empirically tested during the before-online-visit phase. This relationship should motivate brick and click retailers to build online trust among customers who have not visited their websites.

## 7.2. Information channels: word-of-mouth within social network and offline trust

The strongly significant effect of word-of-mouth on online purchasing from brick and click retailers (H2), supported social capital theory by showing that customers relied on their social relations as an information channel in forming online trust. Brick and click retailers may thus decide to implement referral programs more aggressively, both offline and online. For example, existing online customers can be asked to share their positive purchasing experiences through system-generated emails. Online coupons with special privileges and discounts can also be given to customers to suggest that their social contacts purchase from the retailer online.

The significant influence of customers’ trust in the offline presence of brick and click retailers (H3) showed that customers relied on their offline experiences with the retailers’ physical stores as an information channel to build trust. Existing brick and click retailers need to leverage their customers’ offline trust to achieve greater success. When retailers promote their websites, they should capitalize on and emphasize the competence, benevolence, and integrity of their offline presence. To stimulate online purchases, a dual-channel membership program could be launched to allow customers to enjoy discounts and earn loyalty points from both their online and offline purchases. With such programs online, existing brick and click retailers can lock in customers, understanding their purchasing behavior and can further improving their customer relationships

Internet purchasing experience was an insignificant moderator of the relationship between offline trust and online trust (H4). Because 50% of the sample had at least 6 months of online purchasing experience, a large part of the sample had prior online purchasing experience with other retailers and thus they might already have developed trust in the online shopping environment.

## 7.3. Sanctions: expected sanctioning power

The effect of expected sanctioning power was only of peripheral importance in affecting online trust (H5).

Customers who had been purchasing offline from the retailer and had not made a single purchase online may have felt that there were few remedies or sanctions that they could impose on the retailer.

## 7.4. Academic and practical implications

Our results revealed that customers relied more heavily on their social relations and networks in forming trust than on their own experiences with the physical store (the path coefficients of word-of-mouth was 0.769 and offline trust was 0.166). As the nature of online shopping entails risk, customers would seek out recommendations from social contacts before making purchases online even though they have offline trust of the retailer. As such, Barnes & Nobles (which has had a long history of serving customers offline) has been experiencing difficulties in competing with Amazon due to Amazon’s favorable reputation). This implies that brick and click retailers should be more proactive in leveraging on customers’ word-of-mouth of their online operations instead of merely depending on the offline trust of customers to develop online trust. Jeff Bezos, CEO of Amazon.com, noted: ‘‘Word-of-mouth remains the most powerful acquisition tool we have, and we are grateful for the trust our customers have placed in us [36].’’

We included both positive and negative referrals to constitute word-of-as a holistic examination of the effect of word-of-mouth on online trust. Influence of positive referrals was stronger than negative referrals on online trust in our study (from the item weights); this is contrary to previous marketing research (i.e. Mizerski’s study), and may be due to the loyalty of customers to the brick and click retailer.

## 7.5. Limitations

Despite several significant findings, we acknowledge a number of limitations in our study. First, it focused only on the context of a supermarket offering groceries and daily necessities. It may be difficult to generalize the results to other retailers offering different products and services. Secondly, as the customers in our sample were surveyed within physical stores, sampling bias could exist because its respondents were revisiting and thus probably loyal to a retailer’s physical store. Furthermore, our study only focused on two categories of social capital theory: information channels and sanctions.

## 7.6. Concluding remarks

Prior research has mainly examined trust in the context of pure online retailers, and hardly, if ever, examined the influence of the customers’ social relations and networks and offline trust at the before-online-visit phase in the context of brick and click retailers. In our attempt to bridge this gap, we found that word-of-mouth from social networks, offline trust, and expected sanctioning power were significant factors in the building of online trust. This suggests that brick and click retailers should do more to encourage the referral activities of their online operations instead of merely relying on customers’ trust in their offline physical stores.
