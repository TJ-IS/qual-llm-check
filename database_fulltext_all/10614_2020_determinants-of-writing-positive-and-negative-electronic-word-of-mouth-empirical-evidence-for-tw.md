## Abstract

While many studies focus on how electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) affects the readers of eWOM, this study examines what influences the writers of eWOM. We analyze survey results from 438 TripAdvisor hotel reviewers to reveal that two distinct types of expectation confirmation influence the writing of eWOM, as do satisfaction and consumers’ personal characteristics. Thus, this study makes two primary contributions, first by identifying and describing consumers’ two types of expectation confirmation, and second by noting that one set of antecedents leads consumers to write negative eWOM while a different set of antecedents leads consumers to write positive eWOM.

Keywords: expectation confirmation model (ECM), electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), online review, online community, dissatisfaction, disconfirmation, smart tourism, TripAdvisor

## 1. Introduction

Researchers have long understood the importance of word-of-mouth (WOM) in shaping consumers

opinions and behaviors [1-6]. With the advent of the Internet, the study of WOM communication has also

come to include the study of electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM). eWOM is “any positive or negative

statement made by potential, actual, or former customers about a product or company, which is made

available to a multitude of people and institutions via the Internet” [7 p. 39]. eWOM possesses several

unique characteristics that distinguish it from WOM, including greater scalability, speed of diffusion,

persistency, accessibility, measurability, and quantifiability [8-11]. With the growing use of social media

platforms, eWOM is becoming a popular source of obtaining reliable information as well as influencing

consumers [12]. For instance, the influence of eWOM is undeniable [13-16], with 91% of individuals

consulting blogs, product reviews, or other online user-generated comments before buying a product or

service, 84% stating that they trust online reviews as much as a personal recommendation, and 74% of

consumers stating that positive reviews make them trust a business more [17]. The specific influence of

negative reviews has also been noted [11], with researchers observing that negative reviews are

particularly influential for consumers [18], and thus have significant influence on firms’ financial returns,

customer churn, business continuity, and growth. Because practitioners are keenly focused on these

outcomes, researchers are also continuing to study eWOM.

Given the unique characteristics of eWOM, its influence on consumers, its relevance for businesses,

and the differing effects of positive and negative eWOM, several important questions arise. What

determines whether people write eWOM or choose not to? Are the determinants for writing positive

eWOM the same as from those for writing negative eWOM – and if they differ, how so? While

researchers have studied online reviews and eWOM for many years, much of this foregoing work has

examined how eWOM influences readers of eWOM. We take an alternate perspective, examining what

influences writers of eWOM. Put differently, we intend in this paper to shift focus from factors on the

consumption side of eWOM to those on the supply side of eWOM.

Furthermore, while a small number of studies examine reviewers’ motivation for writing eWOM [19, 20], to the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to study reviewers’ potentially differing motivations for writing positive or negative eWOM. The specific purpose of our study is thus to develop a model that explains determinants of writing positive eWOM and negative eWOM, and to find out how determinants of writing positive eWOM are different from those of writing negative eWOM.

Toward this purpose, we build a research model based on the expectation confirmation model (ECM) as we consider how expectation confirmation, satisfaction, and personal characteristics influence whether consumers choose to author eWOM that is positive or negative. One of the major contributions of this study is that we develop and integrate two distinct types of expectation confirmation into our research model: the first type is the offline expectation confirmation that forms after individuals experience a degree of confirmation of their expectations about a real-world product or service (such as a hotel stay); and the second type is the online expectation confirmation that forms as individuals assess the accuracy of prior eWOM such as the online reviews that led them to purchase a product or service.

The paper proceeds as follows. We first review prior literature on WOM, then narrow our focus to studies of eWOM. We then describe the ECM, which is the theoretical foundation that undergirds a major contribution of this study, namely our explanation of reviewers’ eWOM writing behavior. In the Method section, we explain our survey methodology, operationalization of constructs, instrument validation, and data collection from TripAdvisor reviewers (n = 438). We analyze our results using PLS and logistic regression, finding that there are different factors that influence consumers to write positive eWOM and negative eWOM. We also find evidence for two distinct types of expectation confirmation in eWOM writing behavior. Specifically, we find that individuals first experience confirmation of their expectations about a real-world, offline product or service such as a hotel stay, and then second, confirmation of their expectations about the accuracy of prior eWOM such as online reviews. Our results indicate that these two types of expectation confirmation differ in the way that they influence the likelihood that consumers write eWOM. Distinct sets of antecedents are identified for writers of positive eWOM and for writers of negative eWOM. We discuss the theoretical implications of these findings, and explore the practical implications both for websites that publish eWOM as well as for businesses that are affected by it. We note directions for future research before concluding.

## 2. Prior Research

## 2.1. WOM and eWOM

Researchers have studied WOM for decades, consistently noting its importance in shaping individuals’ opinions and behavior. Initial studies focused on how and why WOM was generated, identifying that greater levels of involvement – including involvement with the product/service being described, with other consumers, with messages about the product/service, and even involvement with and awareness of one’s own needs as a consumer – lead to increased WOM [3]. Others noted the importance of integration observed that positive WOM regarding a product/service increases the likelihood of its purchase while negative WOM decreases the likelihood of its purchase [1]. From this early base of work, researchers have gone on to explain that WOM communication is an extremely important process through which individuals share opinions and information about goods and services [21]. WOM often has a greater influence on individuals’ perceptions than traditional marketer-generated sources of information and even third-party “expert” reviews [2]. Individuals clearly seem willing to receive WOM messages and make decisions based on information from those whom they perceive to be unbiased peers [4, 8].

Research on WOM has been extended to include electronic eWOM, which is defined as “any positive or negative statement made by potential, actual, or former customers about a product or company, which is made available to a multitude of people and institutions via the Internet” [7 p. 39]. Six major characteristics distinguish eWOM from WOM and contribute to its popularity and increasing use [4]: (1) the opinions and information shared can immediately reach a wide audience (2) eWOM recipients can seek information from multiple review sites, (3) eWOM is immediately available and has a persistent (perhaps permanent) digital footprint, (4) the anonymous nature of eWOM, where writers often cannot be identified or held accountable for their comments encourages people to publish it, (5) individuals can build up social networks and status within those networks by writing eWOM, and (6) consumers increasingly rely on other consumers’ eWOM-based opinions because eWOM generally has a clear negative or positive valence. Product/service information from friends, relatives, or an online community is viewed as more honest, credible, and trustworthy than that provided through merchants’ websites and advertisements.

Empirical work identifies several motivational reasons why people read eWOM [22, 23]. Individuals often read eWOM for brand or product comparisons. They also use eWOM to seek negative information. eWOM is also sometimes used to support purchase decisions that have been previously made. Additionally, people read eWOM to search for consumers with similar interests and concerns and finally, eWOM is used for fun or for other intrinsic enjoyment.

IS researchers have studied why people read and respond to eWOM, investigating the influence of eWOM from computer-mediated communication platforms such as online discussion forums [24], online consumer recommendation sites [25] and social media [26]. Nevertheless, very little of this foregoing research identifies motivations for people to write eW now therefore turn to the limited set of papers that examine eWOM from the writers’ perspective.

## 2.2. eWOM from the Writers’ Point of View

We reviewed literature that studies how eWOM affects creators of WOM and writers of eWOM. From this review, we were able to identify four key motivational factors for people to share eWOM: selfinvolvement, other-involvement, product-involvement, and message-involvement [3]. Extensions of this initial research were able to identify additional factors that encouraged people to share WOM: an inclination to help other people, a desire to provide feedback to the company, a sense of public responsibility, a sense of authority, satisfaction/dissatisfaction with a purchase, a sense of self-esteem, monetary rewards, an awareness of a convenient channel for addressing a product or service issue, and the expression of positive or negative emotions [7, 27, 28]. These results are echoed by others who find that self-orientation (expressed in ways such as self-expression, writing skill improvement, topic understanding enhancement, utilitarian motives, and personal enjoyment), as well as other-orientation (including altruism, social affiliation, and reciprocity) lead people to contribute eWOM [19, 29]. Elsewhere, social identity, motivations (purposive value, social enhancement, and intrinsic enjoyment), novelty seeking, and opinion leadership are antecedents of an individual-level desire to engage in eWOM [30]. First, even though these studies extend our understanding of eWOM communication, the findings are limited to an explanation of the general motivations for why people engage in eWOM communication. What remains underinvestigated is what negative eWOM. In WOM research, it is commonly accepted that people share negative and positive WOM as an outcome of their experiences; dissatisfied customers often seek to have their issues with a product or service addressed, to share their unpleasant experience with others, or state that they will stop using the products or services altogether [31-35]. Previous studies explicitly show that people tend to share negative WOM out of anger, regret, or disappointment [36]. Extending these findings of offline WOM to online eWOM, it is reasonable to assume that all diverse motivations should be comprehensively included in eWOM research. However, as shown in Table 1, previous studies of writing eWOM failed to include important motivation factors affecting negative WOM. At the same time, it is unlikely that people write negative eWOM solely for monetary rewards or personal enjoyment. People who generally tend to write positive eWOM are more likely to do so for monetary rewards or enjoyment. Since previous studies have considered different motives as homogeneous factors, we will go beyond prior work by examining the differing motivations for creating positive eWOM or negative eWOM.

Second, the six motives listed in Table 1 represent an inclusive list, one that contains inconsistent results. The first three motives – enjoyment, altruism, and attachment – are found to be consistently significant in affecting writers’ behavior. Each are included in the present study. In the case of the fourth motive, utilitarian motivation, results are mixed. This variable is included in the present study because it is frequently reported that people intentionally write eWOM for monetary purposes [39, 40]. Indeed, of the motivations that have been identified, the potential to enhance one’s own self-worth, concern for others, the desire for social interaction, and economic incentives are the most influential [7]. And the last two motives – product involvement and message involvement – are the least investigated and results across previous studies are not consistent. Given the inconsistent results and only sporadic interest in the t – we have excluded the last two motives without further consideration.

Third, extant eWOM research has not fully considered the influence of prior eWOM. The Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) has been often employed as a key theoretical support to explain continued usage of information system [43, 56]. Assuming that writing eWOM resembles continued information system usage, the motives of writing eWOM would be well explained with more theoretical robustness by employing the concept of expectation and its confirmation. If expectations are created by prior eWOM, then confirmation of those expectations and continued IS usage in the form of subsequent eWOM can be explained by the ECM. It may be the case that motivation for writing eWOM goes beyond the motivations listed in Table 1.

## 3. Theoretical Development

## 3.1. Theoretical Background

Given our interest in why consumers write eWOM, we focus on the post-consumption evaluation phase of consumers’ decision-making [27]. Most research explains consumers’ post-consumption evaluation in terms of satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) [27], where consumers’ satisfaction depends on how their prepurchase expectations compare to their post-consumption outcomes [41, 42]. Consumers initially form an ex-ante expectation about a good or service prior to purchasing it, during the information search and product evaluation phases of their decision making process. Expectations are formed by prior experience, existing knowledge, mass media messages, package information, salesperson interactions, or comments from other users [27, 43]. Expectation forms an anchor, a basis from which comparisons can be made [44]. After the product/service is purchased, used or consumed, consumers form a perception about the product or service. If the performance of product or service meets or exceeds the initial expectation, the consumer experiences positive expectation confirmation. If, however, product/service performance fails to exceed the consumer’s initial expectation, he or she experiences disconfirmation (or negative expectation confirmation<sup>1</sup>) [45]. The consumer’s level of expectation confirmation then influences the based on their level of satisfaction [41, 42, 46]. The expectation confirmation model (ECM) is shown in Figure 1.

This research on expectation confirmation, satisfaction, and repurchase intention has been adapted by IS scholars, who have developed the ECM as a model of continued information system usage [47-50]. In this model, confirmation of users’ expectations is positively related to their post-adoption expectations. Users continually update their post-adoption expectatio as continue to interact with an information system in light of their level of confirmed expectations. Users’ updated expectations determine their level of satisfaction and likelihood to continue usage of the information system [51-53]. Post-adoption expectations are positively related to satisfaction, with satisfaction then positively related to continued usage of the IS. In this model, users’ post-adoption expectations are distinct from expectations that were formed before adoption and usage [47, 54].

We choose to build our research model on the ECM for several reasons. First, we are interested in how a consumer’s eWOM-formed level of expectation confirmation regarding a product or service influences his or her level of satisfaction with that product or service. The relationship from confirmation to satisfaction is quite clearly addressed in the ECM and related research. Second, we are interested in whether consumers contribute eWOM after experiencing a product or service. Expectations about products and services are formed in part by the eWOM that appears on review websites. Online review forums are a type of IS. When consumers read eWOM, then contribute eWOM after experiencing a product/service, their eWOM contribution behavior is a type of continued IS usage. Given that the ECM explains factors that influence continued usage of an IS, we argue that it can be utilized in the specific research context of online review websites. Confirmation with respect to expectation could be one of the important motivations for writing eWOM, a motivation that previous studies have disregarded.

Based on the ECM, we specify two types of expectation confirmation to explain eWOM writing behavior. The first type is offline expectation confirmation, a type that is formed based on consumers’ levels of expectation confirmation with regard to a product or service experience. When consumers write eWOM on a review site, they do so on the basis of their real-world experience and expectation. They also experience another type of confirmation: online expectation confirmation. As we explained previously, consumers make purchase decisions based, in part, on review-based eWOM. After their purchase, it is rational for them to consider whether their time reading reviews was well-spent and valuable (or ill-spent and useless). Was the eWOM accurate and helpful? That is, previous online eWOM content is another source of expectation confirmation, which leads consumers to write positive or negative eWOM. Utilizing the ECM with two types of expectation confirmation, it is now possible to examine eWOM writing behavior more closely, where we can examine consumers’ levels of expectation confirmation about eWOM and how those levels provide a basis for them to evaluate their expectations about the previously-written eWOM.

## 3.2.1. Offline Expectation Confirmation and Satisfaction

Initially, a consumer forms an expectation about a product or service, a baseline against which he or she will make a post-consumption evaluation [41, 42, 45, 55]. After experiencing the product/service, the consumer makes an evaluation, forming a level of satisfaction [41, 45, 47]. When a consumer realizes the expected benefits and product/service performance meets or exceeds expectations, this is known as positive confirmation and the consumer’s satisfaction increases. Conversely, when performance lags expectations, the consumer experiences negative confirmation and satisfaction decreases [47, 48, 50, 54- 57]. Thus, confirmation and satisfaction are positively associated, while disconfirmation and dissatisfaction are similarly positively associated. To confirm this relationship, which is well-established in prior studies, we hypothesize

HYPOTHESIS 1a: Offline expectation confirmation will be positively associated with satisfaction regarding a product/service that has been experienced by the consumer.

HYPOTHESIS 1b: Offline expectation disconfirmation will be positively associated with dissatisfaction regarding a product/service that has been experienced by the consumer.

## 3.2.2. Online Expectation Confirmation and Satisfaction

While the initial confirmation construct, offline expectation confirmation, captures the consumer’s degree of expectation confirmation regarding the real-world product or service, the additional confirmation construct of online expectation confirmation captures the expectation confirmation regarding the accuracy of prior eWOM. When using online resources such as eWOM for search and evaluation, a consumer would rationally evaluate the usefulness of their online resources. After purchase, he or she would ask, “Was my time spent reading reviews well-spent?” and “Were the reviews accurate?” and “Did my experience with the product or service align with what previous consumers said in their reviews?”

Expectations arise from a variety of sources, including personal messages and comments from other review website users [58]. eWOM is one form of such communication. Online reviews form one of the evaluation rationally includes evaluation of the product/service bundle as well as the information on which the purchase was based. A satisfied consumer may attribute his or her satisfaction both to the quality of the good or service as well as to the accuracy of information available before purchase. Similarly, a dissatisfied consumer may attribute dissatisfaction to the product/service as well as to information about the product or service. More specifically, consumers will experience a high level of satisfaction when they rationally evaluate eWOM as valuable after an eWOM-driven purchase decision and consumption experience. Conversely, a high level of dissatisfaction after an eWOM-based decision would lead the consumer to conclude that eWOM is not as valuable nor reliable as he or she had initially thought. Formally,

HYPOTHESIS 2a: Online expectation confirmation will be positively associated with satisfaction regarding an eWOM-influenced product/service experience.

HYPOTHESIS 2b: Online expectation disconfirmation will be positively associated with dissatisfaction regarding an eWOM-influenced product/service experience.

## 3.2.3. Offline and Online Expectation Confirmation, Satisfaction, and Writing eWOMs

We now examine the link between consumers’ level of expectation confirmation and the likelihood of writing eWOM. The writing of eWOM is the dependent variable in this study. The phenomenon we seek to explain is why consumers write eWOM, and more specifically, why consumers choose to write either positive or negative eWOM.

After a consumer experiences a real-world, offline product or service and moves to the post-evaluation stage of the decision process, two possible situations can exist: first, the consumer may experience confirmation because expectations were met or exceeded, or second, the consumer may experience disconfirmation because expectations were not met [47, 48, 50, 54-57]. Positive eWOM is the potential outcome of confirmation while negative eWOM is the potential outcome of disconfirmation. Indeed, marketing literature has consistently indicated that when ms o meet customer expectations, this leads to complaints [33], anger, regret, switching intention, and negative WOM [36, 38].

We emphasize that we are not arguing that expectation confirmation should generally lead to an increase in the amount of eWOM generated, but that the level of expectation confirmation should lead to a specific type of eWOM generated. Resta , expectation confirmation should lead only to an increase in positive eWOM while expectation disconfirmation should lead only to an increase in negative eWOM. We are unaware of a rationale for positive expectation confirmation increasing negative eWOM, nor of one where negative expectation confirmation leads to positive eWOM. In the case of online expectation confirmation, the rationale for contributing positive or negative eWOM is not different from that of offline expectation confirmation. For offline expectation confirmation – that is, confirmation regarding a real-world product or service – consumers who were satisfied contribute positive eWOM out of altruism towards other consumers [29], or to reward the provider of the product or service (as stated in H4a). And dissatisfied consumers contribute negative eWOM out of a desire to vent, to take revenge, or to warn others [36-38].

With online expectation confirmation, consumers who are satisfied with the eWOM contribute positive eWOM because they expect other consumers to find the eWOM as helpful as they did. And dissatisfied consumers, that is, those who found the eWOM inaccurate, incorrect, unhelpful or not useful, will likely contribute negative eWOM out of a desire to “correct” the inaccurate eWOM that they read during their search and evaluation.

In the ECM, satisfaction increases the likelihood of continued IS usage [47-50]. Applied to the context of eWOM, it is possible to interpret eWOM contribution behavior as a type of continued information system usage. Satisfaction with a good or service would then increase the likelihood of continued usage of an IS that was initially used in consumer search and evaluation. Indeed, higher levels of satisfaction increase the likelihood of consumers submitting positive eWOM [29]. Conversely, low levels of satisfaction increase the likelihood of negative WOM [33, 36, 38]. We therefore hypothesize:

HYPOTHESIS 5a: Satisfaction will be positively associated with writing positive eWOM.

HYPOTHESIS 5b: Dissatisfaction will be positively associated with writing negative eWOM.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data Collection

This study chose the TripAdvisor website for the collection of data and solicited its users to participate in our study for several reasons. First, the increasing popularity of online communities and social networking sites means that it is becoming easier to find respondents who meet the criteria for our research. The possibility of finding qualified respondents makes alternate approaches such as student sampling unnecessary. Second, and closely-related to our first point, collecting data from real-world online users increases the reliability and validity of our research results. Third, after a thorough review of several sites, we chose TripAdvisor since it is known as one of the most highly visited tourism websites with more than 530 million reviews and close to 400 million unique visitors per month, providing a large pool from which to draw qualified research subjects.

After the development of a preliminary questionnaire, we gathered comments from seven individuals: two Management Information Systems and two marketing scholars, and three prospective participants. After revisions were made, we conducted a pilot test with 70 undergraduate and MBA students to make sure that the questions were unambiguous and that there were no measurement problems. Based on the result of the pilot test data analysis, several questions were refined to further improve readability and clarity.

We selected Qualtrics to assist us with data collection because they are able to contact TripAdvisor members based on their large database of potential subjects. Before formal data collection was done, we also conducted a trial collection of 30 responses to assess the data collection methods of the agency. In order to collect data while minimizing the risk of sample bias, a website was built and the survey was tested for several trials with randomly selected respondents.

Given our research objectives, we chose to survey individuals with the following three characteristics: (1) the respondent should be a member of the TripAdvisor community, (2) within the past six months the respondent had to stay in one of hotels listed on TripAdvisor and (3) the respondent had to read TripAdvisor reviews about the hotel in advance of their stay. If prospective subjects satisfied all three criteria, they were administered one of two questionnaire forms. Survey form 1 was designed to collect data from TripAdvisor members who did write a review after staying at a hotel; survey form 2 was designed to collect data from TripAdvisor members who did not write a review after their hotel stay. The two forms were developed to address our research objective of identifying the reasons why people do or do not contribute eWOM to online review websites. For both survey form 1 and form 2, respondents were requested to fill out the name of the city and of the specific hotel where they stayed in order to refresh their memories as well as to increase the credibility of their responses. The participant screening process and subsamples are illustrated in the flow chart shown in Figure 3. Ultimately, the survey administration process yielded a subsample of respondents who contributed a positive review to TripAdvisor, a subsample who contributed a negative review to TripAdvisor, and a subsample that contributed no reviews. ## 5. Results and Analysis

The Partial Least Square (PLS) method was used to perform a simultaneous evaluation of the measurement model as well as the structural model. The PLS structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) method is more suitable for theory building as well as theory testing. Given that PLS-SEM is more prediction-oriented, PLS is considered to be appropriate because this is one of the first studies to investigate the determinants that affect the writing of positive and negative eWOM.

One limitation of PLS, however, is when an endogenous variable is binary or nominal [65, 66]. In such a situation, the PLS algorithm is inappropriate because ordinary least squares (OLS) assumptions are violated. For this reason, we use the PLS method as well as logistic regression together in the analysis of the structural model.

When scores of both tests exceed 0.7, it is accepted that constructs satisfy the internal consistency requirements [63, 64]. ## 5.2. Structural Model Assessment

We assessed the structural model based on the test of the hypothesized effects in our research model. We subdivided the respondents who wrote reviews in order to test two structural equation models separately and to compare the different factors that influence authors of positive reviews and those that influence authors of negative reviews. In the first model, we have those who wrote positive eWOM (184 responses) and those who did not author eWOM (211 responses). Again, we have a binary dependent variable to compare two different groups of people, where the variable takes on a value of 1 when eWOM is written and a value of 0 if no eWOM was written. In the second model, we compared authors who wrote negative reviews (43 responses) with those who did not author eWOM (211 responses). Even though our sample in the second model satisfies the required assumptions of the logistic model regarding independent observations and sample sizes [67], the small number of subjects who wrote negative eWOM relative to those who wrote positive eWOM may create a concern as to statistical power for accurate estimation during the analysis. To ensure that our statistical tests have adequate power, we performed a power analysis to calculate power values for our PLS model using Cohen’s (1988) power table for multiple regression analysis [68]. As PLS estimates a structural model block by block, we calculated power values block by block [69]. As suggested by Cohen [70], we took a hierarchical approach for three blocks with the writing of eWOM involving control variables to analyze power for incremental explanation of variance. Consequently, we conducted five power analyses. The power values for all these analyses ranged from 0.89 to 0.97 indicating that our sample provides sufficient power at the 0.95 level to detect effects when they are present [70]. With the statistical power of the model validated, we perform the hypothesis testing in the next section.

## 6. Discussion

Our main objectives in this study have been to examine why consumers contribute eWOM to online review websites, and also whether motivations for contribution differ for those who contribute positive eWOM and those who contribute negative eWOM. Our results enable us to now comment on these issues as we discuss the theoretical, methodological, and practical implications of this study.

## 6.1. Theoretical Implications

Our study offers multiple contributions to theory. First, we shed light on the steps in the postconsumption evaluation portion of the consumer decision making process. Our results identify the distinct roles of expectation confirmation regarding the offline product or service, expectation confirmation regarding the online eWOM, and satisfaction. While the roles of expectation confirmation and satisfaction have long been known [41, 45, 55], multiple types of expectation confirmation have not been heretofore described.

Significant results for H1a, H1b, H2a, H2b, as well as H4b indicate that when eWOM is among the stimuli that consumers attend to during the pre-purchase information search and alternative evaluation stages of the decision process, two types of expectation confirmation will form during the postconsumption evaluation stage. The first is with regard to the offline product or service and the second is with regard to the online eWOM. Restated and contextualized for our study, after consumption, consumers will ask both, “Were my expectations for the hotel met?” and “In light of my experience at the hotel, were the reviews that I read beforehand accurate and helpful?” These two distinct types of expectation confirmation represent an extension of the Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) and should influence both IS researchers studying the influence of eWOM on purchase, continuing usage, and repurchase, as well as Marketing researchers studying consumers’ information search, pre-purchase alternative evaluation, and post-consumption evaluation in the Internet era.

Second, by providing empirical evidence that the motivations for writing positive and negative eWOM differ, we further contribute to explanations of post-consumption evaluation. While prior studies have provided evidence for the influence of positive and negative eWOM on consumers during the information search and pre-purchase evaluation of alter natives [74, 75] – that is, the influence of eWOM on readers of eWOM – prior eWOM research has not thoroughly investigated the influence of eWOM on writers of eWOM. This study thus improves researchers’ understanding of eWOM by showing that the determinants for writing positive eWOM are different from those for writing negative eWOM.

Specifically, we observe in this study that consumers write positive eWOM when they have a genuine desire to help others with their decisions (altruism), when they have a strong sense of belonging to a particular online community (attachment), and when they find writing reviews to be fun and interesting (enjoyment). Expectation confirmation and satisfaction are less influential. In our findings, members of the online community write about their positive experience after their trip regardless of their experience with the product/service, and regardless of the accuracy and usefulness of previous eWOM. Additionally, younger consumers are more likely to write positive eWOM. This behavior may be related to the fact that younger people are known to share their happy moments with family and friends on social networking sites [76, 77]. Thus, certain personal characteristics, intrinsic motivations, and hedonic drives motivate consumers to write positive eWOM; the product/service experience is much less influential.

In contrast, writers of negative eWOM write reviews primarily because they are not satisfied with the product or service (H5b) and because the perceived inaccurate content of previous eWOM (H4b). This is distinct from the observed factors that influence consumers to write positive eWOM. While altruism, enjoyment, and age factors show up as factors that stimulate the writing of negative eWOM just as they do for positive eWOM, attachment to the community is not a factor. Thus, even consumers without a strong relationship to the online community will get online to make their negative feelings known. These findings regarding the different influences on the writing of positive versus negative eWOM should shape the work of IS researchers who study eWOM writing behavior.

Finally, our significant results linking satisfaction and online expectation confirmation to the writing of negative eWOM (H4b and H5b, respectively) provide evidence for the influence of prior eWOM on subsequent eWOM. The link from the reading of eWOM to the subsequent writing of eWOM is heretofore unexplored in literature, and thus a portion of our contribution. We recommend additional future research to more thoroughly investigate the ways in which online reviews may stimulate or contribute to future, additional reviews.

## 6.2. Methodological Implications

One of the strengths of our study is that our results are obtained based on the analysis of post-behavior data, not on intention-oriented variables such as intention to write or continued-usage intention. The growing availability of online data, the swelling number of eWOM contributors, and the increasing number of online communities makes real-world data more widely available than in the past. Results obtained from such data offer the advantages of increased reliability and validity when compared to results obtained with intention-oriented responses in a hypothetical situation or in a controlled experimental environment. We encourage the use of real-world, post-behavior data in future studies.

## 6.3. Practical Implications

Online review websites that publish eWOM are keenly interested in attracting and retaining users and in stimulating users’ eWOM contributions. This study indicates that consumers contribute eWOM (both positive and negative eWOM) because of altruism and personal enjoyment. Review websites that can develop marketing and strategies to appeal to altruistic motivations will stimulate the contribution of another approach that could stimulate contributions.

Attachment to the website and its online community is identified here as a factor that stimulates positive eWOM contributions. Thus, review websites that can cultivate and reward a dedicated pool of users, perhaps through loyalty programs, will also stimulate contributions. Finally, younger users have been users of social networking sites, including online review websites [76, 77]. Thus, review sites can take advantage of this insight by targeting this demographic that contributes more actively than do older users.

Additional implications exist for online review websites and hotels that use decision support systems, customer relationship management systems, or other new artificial intelligence-based customer-oriented applications. Some online review websites allow anyone to post a review but some sites only allow a user that actually booked a hotel through the website in the past six months to post a review. Therefore, it is possible for the hotels to identify guests by their reviewer IDs, or details of their review - they might encourage these customers to write favorable review for them. A similar step would be for hotels to solicit reviews from guests (reviews that mention factors such as cleanliness, sleep quality, location, or other characteristics) in a computer-generated, ghost-written review for the guest to post to online review websites such as TripAdvisor. Since reviews are often based on ratings and deliberate use of sentiment, it is difficult to detect dishonest posts [78]. In these ways, hotels could begin to curate the reviews that are posted on review websites for their benefits.

## 6.4. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

As with all studies, our research has limitations. First, this study was conducted using a single website, TripAdvisor, which focuses largely on hotel reviews. The hotel industry provides a highly experiencebased product/service bundle. Future research could replicate this study with other types of experiencebased product/service bundles such as those concerned with restaurants, events, attractions, and activities. Replications could also be attempted with less-experience-based product/service bundles such as reviews for movies, TV programs, or music; for electronic devices such as gaming systems or smartphones; or for other products and services as well. Testing the conceptual framework of this research in other types of online communities can enable the development of a more robust and generalizable model of eWOM contribution behaviors.

Second, the generalizability of this study may be limited as a result of the nature of its sample. not strictly random. The sample size for positive eWOM is larger than that for negative e spite of this limitation, no evidence exists that our results were impacted negatively. Furthe more, as we noted when presenting our results, the relative sizes of our positive eWOM subsample and negative eWOM subsample reflect the reality that positive reviews outnumber negative reviews in many rating websites. The average review score on TripAdvisor is 3.9 out of 5 when hotels have 101 reviews or more [79], while approximately 95% of listings on Airbnb have ratings of 4.5 or 5 (with 5 being the highest) [80]. Nevertheless, future research might want to consider expanding the sample size for both positive and negative eWOM writers by approaching them immediately after they write eWOM.

Third, related to the sample characteristic of booking and reviewing hotel, our questionnaires were collected from customers who experienced (dis)confirmations with respect to a certain level of positive offline and online expectations about the hotel. We assume that this is a typical situation when consumers write reviews. If they had expected poor service in advance, they would have not stayed at the hotel. However, extreme cases are possible. For example, there might be a situation where both a prior offline and online expectations are extremely low but the consumer books anyway, and the actual experience is extremely positive. This case rarely happens in reality and requires a different research design.

Fourth and finally, future research will provide an opportunity to examine interaction effects. For instance, expectation confirmation and altruism could interact to influence the writing of eWOM. Perhaps satisfied individuals who simultaneously have a strong desire to help others, by enabling them to find the satisfying experience they themselves have had, would be more likely to author positive eWOM. While such a relationship was not borne out in our data, studies in other contexts may be able to identify such a relationship.

## 7. Conclusion

Prior research studies have investigated the determinants of writing eWOM as a single type of behavior. However, consumers’ motivations for writing positive eWOM differ from consumers’ motivations for writing negative eWOM. This paper therefore developed a model to explains why people choose (or choose not) to write eWOM, and for those who do choose to write, why they choose to write positive or negative eWOM. The use of control variables from previous studies further signifies the distinctions between the writing of positive or negative eWOM.

Moreover, prior studies of eWOM neglected to examine the influence of online factors such as previous reviews even though previous online reviews clearly influence whether eWOM writers author positive or negative eWOM. In order to overcome these shortcomings, we modified the ECM and included two distinct types of expectation confirmation: offline expectation confirmation and online expectation confirmation.

Our dataset from real-world eWOM readers and writers largely confirms our research model and identifies key differences in the motivations for writing positive and negative eWOM. Implications exist for the study of eWOM, the consumer decision-making process, and for the management of online review websites. We hope that these findings and their implications stimulate others to further investigate the ways in which consumers evaluate their experiences and, in turn, influence other consumers.
