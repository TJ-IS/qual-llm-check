# A Rejoinder to Rigdon et al. (2014)

Miguel I. Aguirre-Urreta Texas Tech University, Lubbock, Texas 79409, miguel.aguirre-urreta@ttu.edu

George M. Marakas Florida International University, Miami, Florida 33174, gmarakas@fiu.edu

Diamantopoulos A, Karahanna E, Straub DW, Dijkstra TK (2014) Conflating antecedents and formative indicators: A comment on Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas. Inform. Systems Res. 25(4):780–784.] in our recent work and for the time and effort spent in carefully considering it and offering their comments and concerns. In what follows, and within the limitations of a short rejoinder, we offer our response to their comments, highlighting points of agreement and noting where more research is necessary.

Keywords: formative specification; partial least squares; structural equation model; data generation; endogenous constructs

History: Ritu Agarwal, Senior Editor. This paper was received on August 12, 2014.

## Concern #1: Population Model Inconsistent with Formative

## Measurement

The first concern enunciated by Rigdon et al. (2014) and, in their own view, their most fundamental one, is that our population model is not consistent with formative measurement as is commonly understood in the literature. We find two issues with this comment. First and foremost, it is not evident to us that the implications of formative measurement for the case of an endogenous, formatively specified construct are as well established in the methodological literature as Rigdon et al. (2014) would like to believe. The authors cite several papers in support of their position. One of those, however, by Petter et al. (2007), models these constructs in the same way we did in our research (see Figures A1 and A2 in that work, whose population models are consistent with the one employed in our work). Several of the other seminal works in this area, but which were omitted from the list of supporting studies (see, e.g., Jarvis et al. 2003, Figure 3; MacKenzie et al. 2005, Figure 4), also modeled formatively specified, endogenous constructs in the same way in which we did in our research. The other studies cited by Rigdon et al. (2014) do not delve directly into the case of formatively specified endogenous constructs (though it should be noted that Diamantopoulos et al. (2008) does mention the two studies by Jarvis and colleagues just cited, without noting any fault with their work). Given this, we believe we have modeled the endogenous, formatively specified construct in our population model in exactly the same way as was done in all prior research in this area, none of which—to the best of our knowledge, and Rigdon et al. (2014) do not cite any evidence either—has been publicly challenged.

Second, it is quite hard to disagree with a concern that we ourselves raised in our own research in the first place, though we find it unusual that Rigdon et al. (2014) do not note this in their commentary. Indeed, in our research we devote 3 pages (a substantial portion of a research note that is, in totality, 18 pages long) to discussing the different ways in which latent predictors and formative indicators may be related and how those may be modeled (see Aguirre-Urreta MI, Marakas GM (2014) Partial least squares and models with formatively specified endogenous constructs: A cautionary note. Inform. Systems Res. 25(4):761–778, pp. 771–774, including Figures 4, 5, and 6). Speaking more specifically to this particular concern, we devote an entire page (pp. 773–774) to the issue of whether our current approaches to modeling formatively specified endogenous constructs are consistent with the conceptualization of formative measurement as has been discussed in the literature.

For example, we note that, “Finally, there is another take on this issue that may lead researchers to rethink the nature of the relationships between latent variables and formative indicators in current research models” (Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas 2014, p. 773), and that “the issue raised in this last discussion [about our current modeling of formatively specified endogenous constructs] has to do with whether this is the correct approach to conceptualizing formatively specified endogenous latent variables, or alternative relationships are needed. We believe this is an area where further research and thinking are needed, particularly about the implications that the latter conceptualization would have for our interpretation of both existing and future research. In that regard, we hope this brief discussion will bring the issue to the fore and help inspire renewed efforts to examine these issues” (Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas 2014, p. 774).

We believe, as such, we have taken the discussion further than Rigdon et al. (2014) by speculating how an effect from a latent predictor that is channeled through a formative indicator should be interpreted (e.g., is that formative indicator now a reflective indicator of the predictor?) (see Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas 2014, Figure 6). As discussed previously, their concern is a restatement of something we already noted in our own research, and thus we respectfully submit that this point is neither novel nor a material indictment against our study or approach.

## Concern #2: Modeling in Partial Least Squares

The second concern expressed by Rigdon et al. (2014) has to do with the way in which we employed partial least squares (PLS) to analyze the data. In their commentary, Rigdon et al. (2014) present an alternative specification that they argue is a more accurate match to our population model. There is unfortunately a dearth of guidance on the specification of formative models when employing PLS to estimate them—even though it is commonly believed that PLS can handle them. In fact, we are not aware of any other research that has shown the PLS specification that it is actually estimating, as we did in our work. As Rigdon et al. (2014) correctly note, the alternative specification they propose cannot be estimated with PLS,<sup>1</sup> thus making it unlikely that this is the specification researchers actually employ when using PLS to estimate a model such as the one of interest in our research. In addition to the graphical depiction of our specification, we also provided the equations underlying its estimation when using PLS, and note in our research that the differences between those equations and the equations underlying our population model are responsible for the effects chronicled in our study, and compare those to the case of a reflective model to further clarify our findings. As such, the argument we present in our research is that, given a true population model as presented—which, as noted above, is consistent with the current modeling of endogenous, formatively specified constructs in the literature—the approach currently used to specify those models in PLS will result in the estimation of something other than what the authors are intending.

Therefore, that there is a mismatch between population models of interest and current modeling specifications when employing PLS to estimate those is, in fact, at the center of our research. To the extent that researchers are interested in estimating a population model as we provided, and are using PLS in the manner we discussed, the results will be problematic— on this we wholeheartedly concur with Rigdon et al. (2014). The question is now whether this is the manner in which PLS is actually employed within the discipline and its body of research for this kind of model. Given our interest in assessing current practice in the specification of these models with PLS, we went back to the studies identified in Table 1 of our research that have modeled an endogenous, formatively specified construct using PLS, to examine whether the reporting of the results provides some indication as to how the authors were including the formatively specified constructs in PLS. Indeed, even though the reporting is quite varied in terms of details included, the authors report weights for the formatively specified constructs instead of regression paths, as they would had they estimated a model similar to the one presented by Rigdon et al. (2014) (if the authors could find a way to make the alternative model estimable with PLS). As such, our research accomplishes what we set out to do: show that if researchers are interested in a model such as the one we included, the current specification of PLS cannot accommodate that, and the estimates obtained represent relationships different than those of interest.

## Concern #3: Data Generation Procedures

The last concern postulated by Rigdon et al. (2014) relates to the data generation procedures used in our research, though it is not entirely clear if they object to the fact that our population model is a “factor model” itself, or to the approach we used to generate the data, as the two seem to be inextricably linked. We believe the only test of a data generation process is whether it leads to samples that are drawn from a population that has the same structure and distributional assumptions as presented in the research, and our approach does this much. In fact, we include sample data generation code in the online appendix that is available with our research (Appendix C), which provides any interested reader with the opportunity to validate our data generation process. There are surely many different ways to generate the data for this research: by specifying the model parameters directly (as we did here), through a piecemeal approach that first generates construct scores and then generates indicator scores from those (as done, for example, by Goodhue et al. 2007; see their Appendix A), or by deriving the covariance matrix implied by the population model and then sampling directly from this matrix (we included sample code to do this much in Appendix E, which also facilitates the validation of our data generation process). When done correctly, all three approaches will lead to the same results. To be clear, the data generation approach and code we employed generate data as if they had been sampled from a population model with the prescribed structure and distributional assumptions, as outlined in our research, which is what they are supposed to do. As such, we see no issues with our data generation process, nor with our use of one particular piece of software versus another to generate the data, and we provide the means to verify this if someone is so inclined. We are not clear whether Rigdon et al. (2014) chose to perform this formal validation because no empirical results were provided in their commentary.

The objection of Rigdon et al. (2014) to our data generation approach, however, seems to go beyond this and into whether we should be employing a “factor model” in the first place if the estimation of the parameters will be done with a composite approach, of which PLS is but one of many. By doing so, they seem to confuse model and estimator. A model is a system of equations that specifies the structure underlying a set of observed variables. Conversely, an estimator is a procedure by which estimates for the parameters in the model are derived from a proposed structure (which may or may not match the true structure of the model) and a sample of data. “SEMs [structural equation models] are sets of equations that encapsulate the relationships among the latent variables, observed variables, and error variables. Parameters such as coefficients, variances, and covariances from the same model are estimable in different ways 4e.g., ML, PLS5 with the different estimators having different properties. So the model and estimation method are distinct and should not be confused” (Bollen 2011, p. 369).

Rigdon et al. (2014) correctly note that when a “factor model” is estimated with a composite approach, the resulting estimates will be biased when compared to their population counterparts. It is indeed well known that PLS will result in biased estimates if the underlying model is a “factor model,” and this has been the case ever since Wold (1982) outlined the technique as an alternative estimator of what were then called “LISREL” models (which are “factor models”

in the terms of Rigdon et al. 2014). It is with knowledge of this bias, however, that the technique has nonetheless become quite popular in the information systems (IS) discipline (as well as others like marketing and some areas of management), as researchers postulate “factor models,” but then estimate those using PLS. It is under this approach that the seminal works of Hui and Wold (1982), Chin (1998), Chin and Newsted (1999), and Goodhue et al. (2007, 2012), to name a few, were conducted, as have been—as best as we can tell—all empirical applications of PLS in the major IS journals (certainly all those included in the review by Ringle et al. 2012). This is how PLS is used in practice in the discipline: as an estimator, albeit a biased one, of “factor models.” To the extent that Rigdon et al. (2014) object to the use of PLS in this fashion, it appears they are objecting to the way in which the entire IS discipline employs PLS. Rigdon et al. (2014) are certainly within their rights to do so, but it cannot be presented as a criticism of our work that we investigate the performance of PLS under the same conditions in which the technique is used by our colleagues in the discipline—as an estimator of “factor models”—because it is that community whom we seek to inform with our research. To the extent that Rigdon et al. (2014) believe that the choice of estimator should match the theoretical model postulated by the researcher, they are more than welcome to convince IS researchers to stop employing PLS when they postulate “factor models,” as has been the case for the past 20 years, and only do so when they believe their population model conforms to a composite structure. (We would not support the alternative that researchers should rethink their research models of interest so that the use of PLS can be justified.) They would not be alone in suggesting this (McIntosh et al. 2014), and we can only speculate as to the reaction of the IS community to this idea. However, while this is still the way in which PLS is used in the field, results obtained from an underlying common factor model, we believe, are informative to researchers who choose to employ PLS to estimate a similar underlying model.

We must also express our confusion with the lack of empiricism in this comment by Rigdon et al. (2014). To the extent that they believe that employing an alternative data generation approach—but which still conforms to the models we are interested in examining—would lead to different results, we encourage them to pursue this empirically and submit such a study for peer review. We do find it somewhat problematic to address this issue, however, because they point out that perhaps our results would have been different had a different data generation approach been used, yet do not present any evidence that this would indeed be the case.

## Summary and Conclusion

We appreciate the interest of Rigdon et al. (2014) in our work. In their commentary, Rigdon et al. (2014) raise three concerns with our research. The first two of these concerns we believe point to a dissatisfaction with the current state of both the methodological and empirical literature in this area, of which our research is the most recent exemplar. In their first concern, Rigdon et al. (2014) disagree with the current modeling of endogenous, formatively specified constructs, which we followed, as developed in the work of Jarvis et al. (2003), MacKenzie et al. (2005), and Petter et al. (2007), and note that it may not be compatible with other formulations of the formative specification—something we ourselves noted in our research as well. In their second concern, Rigdon et al. (2014) argue that the PLS specification we employed is not a good match to the population model presented in our research, and that is the source of the findings. This is essentially the main point we are making in our research as well—that the current use of PLS is problematic in light of the postulated research model that researchers are interested in estimating. In our research, we examine current practice in this regard and find it problematic. In their commentary, Rigdon et al. (2014) disagree that this should be current practice in the first place, and they are certainly within their rights to do so. As we have noted repeatedly in both our research and this commentary, there is a dearth of guidance in this area, though researchers are moving forward without this guidance. We do not expect to have the final say in this matter—indeed, that is how research is supposed to work—and as such look forward to future work, possibly by Rigdon et al. themselves, that better clarifies how researchers should be approaching these issues.

The last concern presented by Rigdon et al. (2014) lies in our approach to data generation, which is consistent with past research examining various aspects of PLS modeling (e.g., Chin 1998, Goodhue et al. 2007). Rather, Rigdon et al. (2014) argue that a different data generation approach should be employed. To the extent that their proposed alternative can be shown to be sampling from the same population model we proposed, the results will not differ based on how the data are generated. If, on the other hand, their proposed data generation approach does not lead to samples as if they were obtained from our population model, then it would not be an appropriate procedure for data generation for our model of interest. Given the lack of empirical evidence in the commentary by Rigdon et al. (2014), we can only speculate as to which would be the case. If, on the other hand, Rigdon et al. (2014) wish to argue that PLS should be employed only with population models that present certain characteristics, they are also welcome to do so, but we note this is in contrast with the entire use of PLS in our discipline as an estimator of SEM (“factor like”) models.
