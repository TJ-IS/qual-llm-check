# A study of the impact of information technology on firm performance: a flexible production function approach

Sumit Sircar\* & Jung Choi<sup>†</sup>

\*Richard T. Farmer School of Business, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, email: sircars@muohio.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Ferris State University, Big Rapids, MI 49307, email: choij@ferris.edu

Abstract. Most previous studies of the impact of information technology (IT) on firm performance have relied on the Cobb–Douglas production function, which makes unrealistic assumptions. These include assuming that (1) the scale elasticity, i.e. returns to scale, is constant throughout the entire output range; (2) the elasticity of substitution between inputs is one, whereas it can be anywhere from zero to infinity in practice; and (3) there are no complex interrelationships between inputs, i.e. no squared or product terms. The translog production function (used in two previous studies) does not make these assumptions and is therefore more accurate. However, the two previous studies found no significant difference between the two. This research has extended previous work on the impact of IT investments by providing more accurate results in some areas and new findings in others. IT’s importance is shown to be much greater than previously thought. The translog function is used to demonstrate that the productivity paradox does not hold (i.e. both IT labor and capital indeed have a significant impact on firm revenues), but with considerably different results, i.e. the impact of IT is markedly more significant than that obtained from the Cobb–Douglas. Another interesting finding is that of scale elasticity. Our results show that this is less than 1, implying decreasing returns to scale of output. Finally, the substitutability between inputs was determined, which is not possible with the Cobb–Douglas. It was found that the partial elasticities of substitution for IT labor with IT capital and non-IT labor were quite substantial.

Keywords: information technology impact, information technology investments, Cobb–Douglas production function, translog production function, information tech nology capital, productivity paradox

## 1. INTRODUCTION

With annual median spending in information technology (IT) reaching 2% of revenues (Computer Economics, 2006), there is continued interest in measuring the impact of these investments. The present study concentrates on individual organizations, i.e. firms. A large proportion of a firm’s investments in IT comprises spending on IT infrastructure – the hardware, networks and software upon which each individual application must be built. The focus o concern relating to IT spending of most senior executives is therefore at the firm level. This partly explains the plethora of articles written on the topic (an extensive bibliography is provided in Sircar et al., 2000). Fuel was added to the fire when the ‘productivity paradox’ was widely publicized in the mid-1980s, which essentially stated that increased investments in IT did not result in corresponding increases in productivity. Robert Solow, winner of the Nobe Prize in economics remarked that ‘We see computers everywhere except in the productivity statistics’ (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003). This caused a furor among corporate managements because they needed justification for their enormous investments in IT.

The research aimed at resolving the productivity paradox has tended to show that IT investments do indeed increase various aspects of a firm’s performance, i.e. the paradox is refuted. However, it became clearly evident that isolating the effects of these investments and showing that they accounted for observed improvements in various measures of output have been extraordinarily difficult and not entirely clear-cut. The problem of relevant and accurate data collection alone (IT investments and specific firm outputs over several years) has been formidable. Further compounding the problem is that some of the effect of such investments on such items as customer satisfaction or product quality may take years to pay off and may be obscured by stronger forces such as an economic downturn or marketing failure.

A favoured approach for assessing the impact of IT has been the use of the well-known Cobb–Douglas production function, in which IT is treated as one of the inputs to the firm’s production mix and its impact on the firm’s output is measured. Brynjolfsson (1993) used this approach in a widely quoted paper to demonstrate that IT investments increased a firm’s revenues and, later, a firm’s value added (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996). A similar study was performed by Lichtenberg in 1995, and yet another using the Cobb–Douglas function as recently as March 2002 by Kudyba and Diwan.

The Cobb–Douglas function is the most commonly used specification of the generalized production function, owing to its simplicity. The generalized production function explicitly shows the functional relationship between a set of inputs and a set of outputs. For a 1-output and n-input process, a production function at a particular point in time can be written as follows:

$$
y = f \left(x _ {1}, x _ {2}, \ldots , x _ {n}\right)\tag{1}
$$

The firm’s production function for a particular good or service $\dot { \boldsymbol { y } }$ during a particular period shows the maximum amount of the good or service that can be produced using alternative combinations of the $" n '$ number of inputs $\displaystyle x _ { 1 } , \ldots , x _ { n } .$ This abstract model of production allows economic analyses involving relationships between inputs and output avoiding discussion o the engineering intricacies involved.

A general form of the production function as shown above is not suitable for computing the various economic quantities using the data on a firm’s inputs and output. In other words, in order to be able to compute the various economic quantities, one will need a given specification of the production function that will show the exact relationship between inputs and output. It is possible to have innumerable specifications of a production function. The choice of a particular specification of a production function to be used for economic analysis with an empirical data set, for practical matters, should obviously be influenced by the very nature of the production process. A specification for a 2-input case can be as simple as a linear function of the following form:

$$
y = f (K, L) = a K + b L
$$

At the other extreme, it can be a very complicated non-linear function. Some of the specifications of production functions that have been traditionally used for economic studies include Cobb–Douglas, constant elasticity of substitution (CES), generalized Leontief and transcendental logarithmic (translog) specifications. Of these four types of production functions, the Cobb–Douglas has enjoyed the most popularity and has been used in many economic studies, including the studies involving the economic impact of IT on firm performance. A general version of the Cobb–Douglas specification for an n-input and 1-output case can be expressed as

$$
y = C x _ {1} ^ {a _ {1}} x _ {2} ^ {a _ {2}} \dots\dotsX _ {n} ^ {a _ {n}} + \varepsilon\tag{2}
$$

where $C , a _ { 1 } , \ldots , a _ { n }$ are all positive constants and e is the stochastic disturbance that accounts for factors (industry-specific, company-specific, management-specific, time-specific and so on) other than the inputs. Some important properties of this version of the Cobb–Douglas produc tion function are:

1 It is linear in logarithmic format and hence allows an easy avenue for econometric investi gation.

2 The indices $a _ { 1 } , \ldots , a _ { n }$ are the output elasticities with respect to various inputs. As these power indices are constants, the elasticities as such are not dependent on the input or outpu levels.

3 It can exhibit any degree of returns to scale depending on the values of the power indices The three cases are shown below:

(a) $a _ { 1 } + a _ { 2 } + \ldots { } + a _ { n } = 1$ Constant returns to scale (CRS);

(b) $a _ { 1 } + a _ { 2 } + \ldots + a _ { n } > 1$ Increasing returns to scale (IRS);

(c) $a _ { 1 } + a _ { 2 } + \ldots + a _ { n } < 1$ Decreasing returns to scale (DRS).

Again, the scale elasticities do not depend on the level of output and remain the same throughout the entire range of production.

4 In the 2-input case, the elasticity of substitution is exactly 1, allowing the same substitut ability among the two inputs throughout the entire production range.

5 Elasticities do not depend on the product or power terms of various inputs. In other words, the inputs do not have any joint impacts on the important economic quantities of interest to us

From the above, it is clear that although the Cobb–Douglas specification is quite simple to use and the interpretation of the results is straightforward, it puts unnecessary restrictions on the economic quantities.

Unfortunately, the various restrictions associated with the Cobb–Douglas specification impose some limitations on the explanatory power of the computed economic quantities. For example: (1) according to the Cobb–Douglas production function, the output elasticity with respect to an input is the same throughout the entire production range, which is not a reasonable assumption for most production technologies; (2) the Cobb–Douglas specification assumes a value of unity for the elasticity of substitution between inputs, which is unrealistic in most cases; and (3) the Cobb–Douglas specification, in its log-linear form, does not include any product or squared terms, which inhibits some of the important relationships between the output and various inputs. In view of such limitations and the lack of explanatory power of the Cobb–Douglas production function, two previous studies used different versions of the more general and flexible translog production function (described in more detail later), which provides greater insight into the relationship between the output and the inputs, including the IT inputs. This is an example of building a ‘cumulative tradition’ (Keen, 1980). The current study also uses the translog function somewhat differently and arrives at markedly different conclusions in some areas, particularly in the impact of IT on output and its substitutability with non-IT capital and non-IT labor. This is the major contribution of this paper. The data set used for this analysis is also more comprehensive than that used by the previous studies (in terms of time span) and therefore amenable to more confident statistical interpretation.

The ‘debate’ between the proponents of the Cobb–Douglas and translog approaches should be of significant interest to the IT community for several reasons. First, the translog methodology is more comprehensive and should be able to corroborate the results of Cobb–Douglass with regard to the productivity paradox. Second, the marginal product of IT with this more accurate formulation, i.e. the effect on output of each unit of IT investment, is of particular interest. Finally, the substitutability between IT capital and IT labor and non-IT labor and non-IT capital is of importance, and is not determinable with the Cobb–Douglas.

The following sections are arranged as follows. First, we provide a brief review of the pertinent literature. Next, we outline the rationale for using production functions in general and the translog function in particular. We then list our hypotheses and describe the data set and research methodology, followed by an analysis of the results.

## 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

There is an extensive IT investment literature, which has been thoroughly reviewed in a number of previous papers, among them Sircar & Turnbow (1998; 2000), Shang & Seddon (2002) and Dedrick & Gurbaxani (2003). Here we shall only review some recent works that have sought to measure the impact of IT investments at the firm level using the production function.

Brynjolfsson & Hitt (1995) applied both the Cobb–Douglas and the translog production functions to firm-level data for several components of Information Systems spending over the time period 1987–1991. Their data set included 367 large firms, which generated approximately \$1.8 trillion in output in 1991. They reported no significant differences in their results for the two functions. Dewan & Min (1997) also arrived at essentially the same conclusion using a variation of the translog function. Interestingly, Brynjolfsson and Hitt combined computer capital and IS labor (a stock and a flow variable) into a single variable and treated it as a stock. In 1996, they used the same data (supplemented by other data) to assess several econometric models of the contribution of IS to firm-level productivity. Once again applying the Cobb–Douglas function, their results indicated that IT spending makes a substantial and statistically significant contribution to firm output, i.e. the gross marginal product for computer capital averaged 81% for the firms in their sample. They further found that the marginal product for computer capital is at least as large as the marginal product of other types of capital investment and that, dollar for dollar, IS labor spending generates at least as much output as spending on non-IS labor and expenses. They concluded that the productivity paradox disappeared by 1991, at least in their sample of firms. However, they used the same treatment for IT capital and IT labor.

Sircar & Turnbow (2000) used panel data for 624 big US companies belonging to different industrial sectors and covering a period from 1988 to 1993 to study the correlations between IT investments and several performance indices, such as sales, income, market share and share price. They used canonical correlation analysis and found that overall, both IT and non-IT investments (labor and non-computer capital) are directly related to these indices.

Lin & Shao (2000) used three different production functions (the generalized Cobb–Douglas, the more general Box–Cox transformation and the most general Box–Tidwell transformation) to examine the relationship between the relative size of IT investments by firms and thei efficiency in the production process. They found consistent empirical evidence that the relative level of IT investments has a positive effect on the firm’s productive efficiency, implying that firms investing comparatively more in IT are likely to be more efficient in their production process than those investing less.

The most recent study by Kudyba & Diwan (2002) extends the work by Brynjolfsson & Hit (1996) and Lichtenberg (1995) by applying only the Cobb–Douglas function to data obtained from Information Week for the years 1995–1997. They found that IT investment contributes positively to firm output and has resulted in increased productivity over time. Interestingly, they used the concept of IT capital flow in addition to the previously used IT capital stock. Usage of stock in the production function is conceptually incorrect because the output variable (revenues) is a flow variable, as are input and investment. These are all related to a period of time. In contrast, assets and liabilities are stock variables. The current study treats capital as a flow, i.e. investment, in the production function, which may explain the differences with the earlier studies that used the translog function. The treatment of IT investments as flows (not stocks) is also supported by their very short lifespan and the move towards treating them as expenses.

## 3. SIGNIFICANCE OF THE TRANSLOG PRODUCTION FUNCTION

In view of the production processes of the firms under consideration, it is not logical to assume that throughout the entire output range, the economic quantities will behave exactly in th same way or will remain constant. A more general specification that contains square and multiplicative terms enables us to explore the relationships between the inputs and the output. This kind of less restrictive model allows us to examine to what degree the various economic quantities may vary with the output and input levels. The translog specification is a good candidate in this respect. Greene (1997)<sup>1</sup> reports that the function was developed by Kmenta (1967) as a means of approximating the CES production function and was introduced formally in a series of papers by Berndt (1976) and Christensen & Jorgenson (1971), including Berndt & Christensen (1973). The translog function has remained the most popular flexible functional form, however, and by one account, Guilkey & Lovell (1983), is the most reliable of severa alternatives.

For an n-input and 1-output case, the translog production function has the following specification:

$$
\ln y = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \beta_ {k} \ln x _ {k} + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {K} \gamma_ {k l} \ln x _ {k} \ln x _ {l} + \varepsilon\tag{3}
$$

Here, the $\beta \mathsf { s }$ and $\gamma \mathsf { s }$ are the constants. The following are the important facts about the translog production function:

1 This function is derived from the most general functional form $y = f ( x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \ldots , x _ { n } )$ by using logarithmic transformation and Taylor series expansion around value 1 for each x.

2 The Cobb–Douglas specification is a special case of the translog function.

3 It contains multiplicative and square terms, allowing us to see the possible joint impacts on output as well as the impacts of inputs on output through higher powers.

4 Like the Cobb–Douglas, this function may assume any degree of returns to scale. For example, if $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \beta _ { i } = 1 { \sf a n d } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \gamma _ { i j } = 0$ , for all $i ,$ this function exhibits constant returns to scale. But the general expression for scale elasticity contains the logarithms of the input variables with the implication that the returns to scale depend on the output level.

5 Other economic quantities including elasticity of substitution depend on the output level.

Thus, the translog production function, being less restrictive in nature, provides us with ample scope to investigate the properties of the economic quantities in more general terms and thereby shed more light on the relationships between inputs and output. This is very significant for this paper because there may be interactive effects (which we test) between the effects of all the inputs to a firm’s output considered: IT capital, IT labor, non-IT capital and non-IT labor. Moreover, the elasticity of the effects of these inputs may vary with the level of output (which we also test). Finally, the exponential and multicative terms allow us to predict the effect of the inputs on output more precisely.

## 4. THE DATA SET

Sircar & Turnbow (2000) have correctly asserted that ‘large-scale empirical studies are a rarity in the information systems field, because of the paucity of data’. Brynjolfsson & Hitt (1996) also bemoaned a similar data crisis: ‘industry-level output statistics have historically been the only data that are available for a broad cross section of the economy’. Sircar & Turnbow (2000) explain why the data set used by them (the same data used in this research) is the most appropriate of all the currently available data sets for the empirical study of IT productivity. Data sets used by other studies are smaller and often contain observations not suitable for firm-level productivity analysis. The data set was initially created by combining data collected from three different sources. The first source is the series of six annual primary surveys of 624 companies (IS managers were the respondents) by the International Data Corporation (IDC) over 6 years, from 1988 to 1993, and then discontinued. These are publicly traded, US-based companies belonging to the Fortune Industrial 500 and Fortune Service 500 groups of companies. The survey was completed at the end of the year with data for the following year. Previous studies from this source used only 5 years of data (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1995; Dewan & Min, 1997).

The second source of the data for this data set was Standard & Poor’s Compustat tapes. Finally, Moody’s Compact Disc reports were used to fill in the gaps found in the Compustat data. Naturally, because of mergers and acquisitions, the same firms could not be surveyed in each of these 6 years. Sircar & Turnbow (2000) opine that ‘this is a unique data set, a rarity in the field of information systems, because of the difficulty and expense of collecting such data’.

Because of mergers and acquisitions, reorganizations and other reasons, instead of 3744 observations (6 ¥ 624), the data set ended up with only 2009 observations, still considerably more than other studies mentioned earlier. In order to obtain the highest quality database possible, the data were carefully culled, which involved the following steps:

1 bringing about uniformity in the company names to fix the problem of slightly different names in different years for some of the companies;

2 merger of common SIC and CUSIP codes with the IDC data to form the basis of a merge with Compustat data;

3 verification of the IDC survey data against Compustat and Moody’s data wherever possible, for example, for sales, net income, employees, etc. Surprisingly, the data from various sources were quite close to one another. However, for consistency and greater accuracy, published data were used for survey data;

4 companies that had undergone reorganization were treated as separate companies. There were only 39 of these.

5 financial data were converted to 1987 dollars using the same procedure as reported. The only difference was that the deflator used for computer capital was obtained directly from the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

## 5. RESEARCH HYPOTHESES

It is traditional in economic research to formulate 2-input (capital and labor) production functions. However, when using production functions for analysing IT investments, the capita input is customarily split into IT capital and non-IT capital and, similarly, labor input is divided into IT labor and non-IT labor. Accordingly, the classic production function is specified as follows:

$$
Y = f (C, K, S, L)\tag{4}
$$

where Y stands for firm-level output, C stands for IT capital, K stands for non-IT capital, S stands for IT labor and L stands for non-IT labor.

The economic impacts of IT on organizational performance using both the translog and Cobb–Douglas functions are measured with the following microeconomic tools: (1) the various input elasticities of output; (2) marginal productivity of the four different inputs; (3) scale elasticity of output to get an estimate of the returns to scale; and (4) elasticity of substitution between selected inputs to assess the substitutability, e.g. IT labor and IT capital. Whether the results vary over time and by industry is also explored. Output in this context is measured in terms of revenues.

The two regression models used for data analysis, which contain the input variables defined earlier, are given below. The first one is the Cobb–Douglas specification and the second is the translog specification:

$$
\ln Y = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {C} \ln C + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {S} \ln S + \beta_ {L} \ln L + \varepsilon\tag{5}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln Y = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {C} \ln C + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {S} \ln S + \beta_ {L} \ln L + \frac {1}{2} \gamma_ {C C} \ln^ {2} C \\ \qquad + \frac {1}{2} \gamma_ {K K} \ln^ {2} K + \frac {1}{2} \gamma_ {S S} \ln^ {2} S + \frac {1}{2} \gamma_ {L L} \ln^ {2} L + \gamma_ {C K} \ln C \ln K \\ \qquad + \gamma_ {C S} \ln C \ln S + \gamma_ {C L} \ln C \ln L + \gamma_ {K S} \ln K \ln S \\ \qquad + \gamma_ {K L} \ln K \ln L + \gamma_ {S L} \ln S \ln L + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

The basic hypotheses tested in the current study are given below. The null hypotheses are stated as if IT is expected to have no effect on output. Note that Hypotheses 1, 2, 5 and 7 state assumptions that we wish to challenge. Therefore, we would want to reject these hypotheses. On the other hand, Hypotheses 3, 4 and 6 state assumptions that we wish to demonstrate, hence we would want to fail to reject them.

H1: Throughout the entire output range, there is a negative or no relationship between output and the level of inputs used in production.

Rejection of the hypothesis would mean that the regression coefficients of the inputs lnC, lnK, lnS and lnL as well as the other coefficients (i.e. of square and product terms in the translog model) should be collectively significantly positive, which will establish the legitimacy of the two production function specifications. This will allow us to proceed with the remaining hypotheses.

H2: Marginal products of each of the inputs are negative or equal to zero.

Rejection of this hypothesis would imply that an increase in one dollar of investment in each input will result in an increase in the dollar value of firm output. The expressions for margina products for both the Cobb–Douglas and translog models incorporate both output and inpu variables, as discussed earlier, and the mean output levels will be used to compute the marginal products. Therefore, the rejection of this hypothesis will refute the productivity paradox because it implies that increased spending on IT labor and IT capital will increase output.

H3: Computed regression parameters vary by (1) year and (2) industry.

This addresses the question of parametric stability with respect to time and industry. The effect of both labor and capital (both IT and non-IT) can be expected to vary considerably by year and by industry because of economic conditions and advances in technology and variations in their implementation in business. This provides us with a way of examining the credibility of both production functions.

H4: Computed economic quantities (marginal product, scale elasticity, output elasticity and elasticity of substitution) depend on (1) year, (2) industry and (3) level of output.

Again, this allows an analysis of the credibility of the production functions. This hypothesis follows from the preceding hypothesis, as the economic quantities are computed using the estimated parameters.

H5: The scale elasticity is equal to 1.

This is the CRS assumption of the Cobb–Douglas model. Rejection of this hypothesis in favour of a scale elasticity smaller than 1 would mean that the model will show DRS, i.e. doubling all the inputs will result in less than double the output in contrast to CRS. It would also demonstrate that the production functions have a convex shape. Note that the scale elasticity for the translog, as discussed earlier, depends on the output level. The mean output level will be used to compute the translog scale elasticities as is customary (Greene, 1997).

H6: The partial elasticity of substitution in the translog model between any two of the inputs is a finite number.

This addresses the degree of substitutability between inputs. Substitutability of zero implies a pair of complementary inputs, while a very large value implies unlimited substitutability between inputs, which is not practical. These quantities can be computed only for the translog model because they are simply constant 1 in the Cobb–Douglas. As discussed earlier, the partial elasticities of substitution depend on the input and output levels. The mean output level will be used for computational purposes.

H7: The Cobb–Douglas and translog models provide similar results.

The Cobb–Douglas specification fits well to some production situations. Moreover, the Cobb–Douglas can be considered to be an approximation of the more general and flexible translog model (Greene, 1997). In view of this, the results obtained from the translog should be consistent with those obtained from the Cobb–Douglas. However, because of the more general and flexible nature of the function, the translog specification should provide more accurate results.

## 6. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND RESULTS

Multiple linear regressions were run using both Cobb–Douglas and translog specifications for the entire data set as well as for each year and each industry. Regressions were run without any restrictions on returns to scale. Regression parameters were used to compute various economic quantities. As discussed earlier, the subscripts C, K, S and L denote IT capital, non-IT capital, IT labor and non-IT labor, respectively.

Table 3 presents the estimated values of the various parameters of the Cobb–Douglas production function. Both the estimates before and after taking into account ‘firm effects’ are listed. The computations are based on the full data set consisting of 1154 (1141 after the transformation for evaluating ‘firm effects’) observations covering eight industrial sectors and 6 years from 1988 to 1993. All the estimated parameter values are significantly different from zero, both before and after adjusting for ‘firm effects’ (t-values well above 1.96, i.e. the 95% confidence level). $\beta _ { C } , \beta _ { K } , \beta _ { S }$ and $\beta _ { L }$ are the output elasticities of the various inputs. For example, a 1% increase in IT capital (C) will lead to about 0.06% increase in output; a 1% increase in IT labor (S) will give rise to a 0.19% increase in output, and so on. The more general version of the Cobb–Douglas production function was used, which means that the restriction $\beta _ { C } + \beta _ { K } + \beta _ { S } + \beta _ { L } = 1$ was not imposed on the function. In other words, CRS was not imposed. $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values obtained from the regression runs were then used to ascertain the value of the returns to scale. In every case – for the full data set as well as for the industrial and yearly subsets of the full data set – it was found that the elasticity of scale was less than 1, i.e. the production function displayed DRS and not CRS. This implies that it is a convex function.

Thus, decreasing returns to scale are exhibited by the entire economy and the industrial sectors separately as well. Year-wise computations also point to similar results, i.e. doubling all the inputs yields less than double the output.

The marginal product (MP) of an input is the increase in output owing to the increase in one unit of input, keeping quantities of all other inputs fixed. According to the law of diminishing marginal product, at the optimum level of output this quantity should be zero, and beyond this, the optimum level of output MP should be negative. Positive values of MP indicate that more of the input under consideration can be used profitably. The MPs as recorded in Tables 8 and 9 show high values of MP for IT capital and IT labor irrespective of years and industries, except in one case (a negative value of $M P _ { C }$ for 1990). Comparison with the MPs for the non-IT inputs clearly shows the high potential for investments in IT throughout the years 1988–1993. All the expressions used for the computation of the various economic quantities using the Cobb– Douglas model have been provided in Appendix I.

Further evidence of the superiority of the translog model (Hypothesis 7) can be demonstrated by an F-test comparing the Cobb–Douglas and the translog model. In this case, the Cobb–Douglas model is considered as a reduced model of the translog model. The full mode for our analysis is a translog model that includes the four linear terms and six more interactive terms mentioned in the main text, i.e.:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln Y & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {C} \ln C + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {S} \ln S + \beta_ {L} \ln L + \gamma_ {C K} \ln C \ln K \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {S L} \ln S \ln L + \gamma_ {C L} \ln C \ln L + \gamma_ {S K} \ln S \ln K + \gamma_ {C S} \ln C \ln S \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {K L} \ln K \ln L + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

The reduced model is the simple linear Cobb–Douglas model:

$$
\ln Y = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {C} \ln C + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {S} \ln S + \beta_ {L} \ln L + \varepsilon\tag{8}

$$

## 8. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Many previous studies of IT impact on firm performance have relied on the Cobb–Douglas production function, which is practical to use but makes unrealistic assumptions. These include assuming that (1) the scale elasticity, i.e. returns to scale, is constant throughout the entire output range; (2) the elasticity of substitution between inputs is one, whereas it can be anywhere from zero to infinity in practice; and (3) there are no complex interrelationships between inputs, i.e. no squared or product terms. The translog production function does not make these assumptions and is therefore more accurate. Two previous studies have used the translog, but with a smaller time span and database and with different treatments, most notably the use of stocks as input variables rather than flows. The translog was also used in this study to demonstrate that the productivity paradox does not hold, i.e. both IT labor and capital have a significant impact on firm revenues. Furthermore, this impact is markedly more significan than that obtained from the Cobb–Douglas (a finding different from the two previous ones).

Another interesting finding is that of scale elasticity. Our results show that this is less than 1, implying DRS of output, which is in line with previous work.

Finally, the substitutability between inputs was determined, which is not possible with the Cobb–Douglas, and this is one of the major contributions of this study. It was found that the partial elasticity of substitution for IT labor with IT capital and non-IT labor were quite substantial. However, it was almost zero with non-IT capital, which can be attributed to the dominance of manufacturing firms in the sample.

This research has therefore extended previous work on the impact of IT investments by providing more accurate results in some areas and new findings in others. The importance of IT has been shown to be greater than previously thought.

## Appendix I: Expressions for Various Economic Quantities Derived from the Cobb–Douglas Production Function

The general form of the Cobb–Douglas production function with the four inputs $C , K ,$ S and L and output denoted by Y is as follows:

$$
Y = A C ^ {\beta_ {C}} K ^ {\beta_ {K}} S ^ {\beta_ {S}} L ^ {\beta_ {L}}
$$

Taking the natural log of both sides and using $1 n A = \beta _ { 0 }$ , we have the following form of the function:

$$
\ln Y = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {C} \ln C + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {S} \ln S + \beta_ {L} \ln L
$$

Output elasticities with respect to the inputs are

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\varepsilon_ {C} = \beta_ {C}} \\ & {\varepsilon_ {K} = \beta_ {K}} \\ & {\varepsilon_ {S} = \beta_ {S}} \\ & {\varepsilon_ {L} = \beta_ {L}} \end{array}
$$

The scale elasticity or the returns to scale for the Cobb–Douglas production function is

$$
\varepsilon = \beta_ {C} + \beta_ {K} + \beta_ {S} + \beta_ {L}
$$

Marginal products depend on the output level.

The marginal products have the following expressions:

$$
M P _ {c} = \frac {Y}{C} \varepsilon_ {c}
$$

$$
M P _ {K} = \frac {Y}{K} \varepsilon_ {K}
$$

$$
M P _ {s} = \frac {Y}{S} \varepsilon_ {s}
$$

$$
M P _ {L} = \frac {Y}{L} \varepsilon_ {L}
$$

Appendix II: Expressions for Various Economic Quantities Derived from the Translog Production Function

Output elasticity with respect to the input C is

$$
\varepsilon_ {C} = \frac {\partial \ln Y}{\partial \ln C} = \beta_ {C} + \gamma_ {C C} \ln C + \gamma_ {C K} \ln K + \gamma_ {C S} \ln S + \gamma_ {C L} \ln L
$$

with respect to K

$$
\varepsilon_ {K} = \frac {\partial \ln Y}{\partial \ln K} = \beta_ {K} + \gamma_ {C K} \ln C + \gamma_ {K K} \ln K + \gamma_ {K S} \ln S + \gamma_ {K L} \ln L
$$

with respect to S

$$
\varepsilon_ {s} = \frac {\partial \ln Y}{\partial \ln S} = \beta_ {s} + \gamma_ {c s} \ln C + \gamma_ {k s} \ln K + \gamma_ {s s} \ln S + \gamma_ {s L} \ln L
$$

with respect to L

$$
\varepsilon_ {L} = \frac {\partial \ln Y}{\partial \ln L} = \beta_ {L} + \gamma_ {C L} \ln C + \gamma_ {K L} \ln K + \gamma_ {S L} \ln S + \gamma_ {L L} \ln L
$$

Thus, the elasticity depends on the levels of the inputs used in production.

The marginal products are

$$
M P _ {C} = Y _ {C} = \frac {\partial Y}{\partial C} = \frac {\partial Y}{\partial \ln Y} \cdot \frac {\partial \ln C}{\partial C} \cdot \frac {\partial \ln Y}{\partial \ln C} = \frac {Y}{C} \varepsilon_ {C}
$$

$$
M P _ {K} = Y _ {K} = \frac {Y}{K} \varepsilon_ {K}
$$

$$
M P _ {S} = Y _ {S} = \frac {Y}{S} \varepsilon_ {S}
$$

$$
M P _ {L} = Y _ {L} = \frac {Y}{L} \varepsilon_ {L}
$$

The elasticity of scale can be found by adding up the above four elasticities:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\varepsilon = (\beta_ {C} + \beta_ {K} + \beta_ {S} + \beta_ {L}) + (\gamma_ {C C} + \gamma_ {C K} + \gamma_ {C S} + \gamma_ {C L}) ^ {\ln C}} \\ & {\qquad + (\gamma_ {C K} + \gamma_ {K K} + \gamma_ {K S} + \gamma_ {K L}) ^ {\ln K} + (\gamma_ {C S} + \gamma_ {K S} + \gamma_ {S S} + \gamma_ {S L}) ^ {\ln S}} \\ & {\qquad + (\gamma_ {C L} + \gamma_ {K L} + \gamma_ {S L} + \gamma_ {L L}) ^ {\ln L}} \end{array}
$$

The translog function exhibits constant returns to scale if the following conditions are satisfied:

$$
\beta_ {C} + \beta_ {K} + \beta_ {S} + \beta_ {L} = 1
$$

$$
\gamma_ {C C} + \gamma_ {C K} + \gamma_ {C S} + \gamma_ {C L} = 0
$$

$$
\gamma_ {C K} + \gamma_ {K K} + \gamma_ {K S} + \gamma_ {K L} = 0
$$

$$
\gamma_ {C S} + \gamma_ {K S} + \gamma_ {S S} + \gamma_ {S L} = 0
$$

$$
\gamma_ {C L} + \gamma_ {K L} + \gamma_ {S L} + \gamma_ {L L} = 0
$$

For the general n-input case, Allen (1938) has developed partial elasticities of substitution. The partial elasticity of substitution between two factor inputs r and s is defined as

$$
\sigma_ {r s} = (v _ {1} f _ {1} + v _ {2} f _ {2} + \dots + v _ {n} f _ {n}) / (v _ {r} v _ {s}) \cdot (F _ {r s} / F)
$$

where $f _ { j }$ denotes the first-order and $f _ { i j }$ denotes the second-order partial derivatives of $f . F _ { r s }$ is the co-factor of $f _ { r s }$ in $F$ defined as the following determinant:

$$
F = \left| \begin{array}{c c c c c} 0 & f _ {1} & f _ {2} & \ldots & f _ {n} \\ f _ {1} & f _ {1 1} & f _ {1 2} & \ldots & f _ {1 n} \\ f _ {2} & f _ {2 1} & f _ {2 2} & \ldots & f _ {2 n} \\ \cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \cdot \\ f _ {n} & f _ {n 1} & f _ {n 2} & \ldots & f _ {n n} \end{array} \right|
$$

Using the above definition, the expressions for the elasticity of substitution between factor C and factor K has been computed as follows:

$$
\sigma_ {C K} = \sigma_ {K C} = (\varepsilon_ {C} + \varepsilon_ {K} + \varepsilon_ {S} + \varepsilon_ {L}) Y / (C K) \cdot \left(F _ {C K} / F\right)
$$

where,

$$
F _ {r s} = \left| \begin{array}{c c c c c} 0 & Y _ {C} & Y _ {K} & Y _ {S} & Y _ {L} \\ Y _ {C} & Y _ {C C} & Y _ {C K} & Y _ {C S} & Y _ {C L} \\ \dot {Y} _ {K} & Y _ {C K} & Y _ {K K} & Y _ {K S} & Y _ {K L} \\ Y _ {S} & Y _ {C S} & Y _ {K S} & Y _ {S S} & Y _ {S L} \\ Y _ {L} & Y _ {C L} & Y _ {K L} & Y _ {S L} & Y _ {L L} \end{array} \right|
$$

Following some tedious calculations, it can be shown that the elasticity of substitution term depends on the output level, the output elasticities with respect to the factor inputs, and the input levels. For the computation of the elasticities of substitution, we will also need the following relations:

$$
Y _ {c c} = \frac {Y}{C ^ {2}} \big (\gamma_ {c c} + \varepsilon_ {c} ^ {2} \big)
$$

$$
Y _ {C K} = \frac {Y}{C K} (\gamma_ {C K} + \varepsilon_ {C} \varepsilon_ {K})
$$

$$
Y _ {c s} = \frac {Y}{C S} (\gamma_ {c s} + \varepsilon_ {c} \varepsilon_ {s})
$$

$$
Y _ {C L} = \frac {Y}{C L} (\gamma_ {C L} + \varepsilon_ {C} \varepsilon_ {L})
$$

$$
Y _ {K K} = \frac {Y}{K ^ {2}} \big (\gamma_ {K K} + \varepsilon_ {K} ^ {2} \big)
$$

$$
Y _ {K S} = \frac {Y}{K S} (\gamma_ {K S} + \varepsilon_ {K} \varepsilon_ {S})
$$

$$
Y _ {K L} = \frac {Y}{K L} (\gamma_ {K L} + \varepsilon_ {K} \varepsilon_ {L})
$$

$$
Y _ {s s} = \frac {Y}{S ^ {2}} \big (\gamma_ {s s} + \varepsilon_ {s} ^ {2} \big)
$$

$$
Y _ {L L} = \frac {Y}{L ^ {2}} \big (\gamma_ {L L} + \varepsilon_ {L} ^ {2} \big)
$$
