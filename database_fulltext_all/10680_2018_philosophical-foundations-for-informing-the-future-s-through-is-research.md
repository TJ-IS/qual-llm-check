# Philosophical foundations for informing the future(S) through IS research

Mike Chiasson<sup>a</sup>, Elizabeth Davidson<sup>b</sup> and Jenifer Winter<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Faculty of Management, University of British Columbia, Kelowna, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Technology Management, Shidler College of Business, University of Hawaii at Manoa, Honolulu, HI, USA; <sup>c</sup>School of Communications, University of Hawaii at Manoa, Honolulu HI, USA

## ABSTRACT

Information systems (IS) scholars have suggested IS researchers have a responsibility to consider how information and communication technologies could, in the future, influence sociotechnical practices and outcomes. However, research focused specifically on “the future” has yet to gain a strong foothold within the scholarly IS field. In this essay, we suggest a philosophical foundation and epistemological basis for futures-oriented research to advance such scholarship in the IS field. We first highlight epistemic assumptions about futures-oriented research drawn from the discourse of futures studies. We then draw on Feenberg’s philosophy of “potentiality and actuality” of technology as a foundation to consider how knowledge generated through IS research about the sociotechnical past and present might inform futures-oriented inquiry. We illustrate these arguments with examples from the emerging arena of “big data” research.

Philosophy of technology can join together the two extremes – potentiality and actuality – norms and facts – in a way no other discipline can rival. It must challenge the disciplinary prejudices that confine research and study in narrow channels and open perspectives on the future. Andrew Feenberg (2010a, p. 81)

## 1. Introduction and motivation

As we approach the end of the second decade of the twenty-first century, ongoing transformations in business forms, economic institutions, and society are greatly influenced by developments with information and communications technologies (ICTs). Some transformations dazzle us with possibilities for improved social and economic welfare, while others suggest ICT innovations will largely preserve the status quo or even worsen some societal issues. Whether viewed optimistically or pessimistically, there is little debate about the extent to which ICT innovations will influence individuals, organisations, economies, and nations in the future.

Whether the scholarly Information Systems (IS) field could, or even if it should, engage in research focused on sociotechnical futures is less clear. In this essay, we use the term sociotechnical futures very broadly to include ICTs, related practices for their development, deployment and use, institutional arrangements supporting these practices, and related organisational and societal outcomes, which might develop or be realised in the future. Ethical issues have not yet become central concerns in IS research (Stahl et al., 2014), where public debates related to research and innovation are notably less visible than in other fields (Stahl, 2012). Yet, as ICT innovations contribute to ever more disruptive social and economic transformations, ethics-based research will likely emerge (Stahl, 2012) – for example, the recent debates about ethical artificial intelligence (AI) (Dietterich & Horvitz, 2015; Markof, 2016). To inform these discourses will require that IS researchers look beyond conventional research boundaries to consider broader societal needs (Stahl et al., 2014).

In this essay, we posit that ethical concerns about ICTs are entangled with questions about sociotechnical futures – what futures are possible, whose interests and values will shape ICTs and the futures they engender, and which futures are desirable and for whom. Along with ethics-based research (Stahl et al., 2016), we suggest that scholarly research focused on sociotechnical futures will be increasingly important to the IS field. We refer to such research with a broadly inclusive term, futures-oriented IS research, to include inquiry that explicitly seeks to generate insights on, and to inform, possible sociotechnical futures, whether through futures studies methodologies, social science approaches, action research, design science methods, or other approaches.

As with ethics-based research, there is as yet a paucity of scholarly IS research focused on sociotechnical futures per se (Lee, 2016). Some IS researchers have advocated adopting approaches from the interdisciplinary field of futures studies (FS), by highlighting FS methodologies (Gray & Hovav, 1999, 2007; Hovav, 2014) and by illustrating the linkages between FS and ethical considerations (Markus & Mentzer, 2014). ICTs and futures have also been explored in IS conferences (Chiasson, Henfridsson, Karsten, & Degross, 2011). Nonetheless, the IS research field has not fully embraced sociotechnical futures as a central focus of study. IS research generally relies on empirical studies of existing or emerging ICTs and related practices. Such studies provide a basis for projecting tech nology developments generally or generatively (Tilson, Lyytinen, & Sørensen, 2010; Yoo, Boland, Lyytinen, & Majchrzak, 2012; Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010), their implications for business practices in the present and near future (Aral, Dellarocas, & Godes, 2013; Eaton, Elaluf-Calderwood, Sørensen, & Yoo, 2015), forecasting fashionable research topics (Baskerville & Myers, 2009; Hirschheim, Murungi, & Peña, 2012; Wang, 2010), or guiding future research in various areas (Agarwal, Gao, DesRoches, & Jha, 2010; Barrett, Davidson, Prabhu, & Vargo, 2015; Belanger & Xu, 2015; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Vodanovich, Sundaram, & Myers, 2010). However, rendering extrapolations of current technologies and practices is insuficient for forecasting sociotechnical futures if we accept that futures are complex and emergent (Gray & Hovav, 2011) and acknowledge the entanglement of ethics with futures.

To foster scholarly futures-oriented IS research, we suggest a philosophy of technology could inspire such inquiry and also heighten awareness of how research practices tend to restrict how IS researchers approach the future as a phenomenon of study. Our primary goal in this essay is to explore a philosophical foundation and to highlight epistemological issues for the study of sociotechnical futures. To do so, we first consider epistemic assumptions about future(s) in the broad discourse of futures studies (FS). We then draw on Andrew Feenberg’s (1991, 1995, 1999, 2002, 2010a) philosophy of technology for intellectual structures (Chiasson & Henfridsson, 2011) for futures-oriented IS research. Based on Feenberg’s philosophy, we consider how epistemic assumptions and research practices influence how knowledge generated about past and present ICTs might inform knowledge about possible sociotechnical futures. We then draw examples from the emerging arena of “big data” research to illustrate these ideas. We conclude by discussing how a philosophy of technology based on “potentiality and actuality” could help apply the IS field’s expertise with research of past and present ICTs to better inform sociotechnical futures.

## 2. Epistemic assumptions about sociotechnical futures

We draw on futures studies, or FS, an interdisciplinary field involving systematic thinking about alternative potential futures, to consider what researching the future might mean. FS involves a wide range of social scientific methods (e.g., the Delphi technique, cross-impact analysis, and scenario building) and theories to examine social, economic, and technical change. While the field is broad, it is founded on key assumptions about knowledge of possible futures, the implications of values and normative judgements in futures research, and the role of human agency in shaping possible futures. Our purpose in this section is to highlight key epistemic assumptions. A full review of the field of Futures Studies, which broadly includes research and policy activities, is beyond the scope of this essay. We refer readers to Dator (1996), Bell (1997a), and Slaughter (1998) for readings on FS generally. We direct the reader to Helmer (1983), Gray and Hovav (1999, 2008), Glenn and Gordon (2009), and Lindgren and Bandhold (2009) for guidance and recommendations on specific methods for futures studies in the IS field.

FS represents a “paradigmatic turning point in the production and use of knowledge” (Slaughter, 1998, p. 373), maintaining that the future is not merely an abstraction but rather it exists as “a profoundly vital component of the present (however defined) or, more fundamentally, a principle of present action” (Slaughter, 1998, p. 372). FS rejects the idea of prediction based on an empiricist frame that assumes the future does not yet exist (Slaughter, 1998). Importantly, the future is not merely an extension or extrapolation of the past and present. Instead, the future is seen as dynamic, complex, and unknowable, but it is not beyond the ability of humans to direct in a meaningful way. Thus, FS rejects the idea of a single future that must be uncovered (Bell, 1997a).

Broadly speaking, FS presumes that possible futures are open and can be influenced by human action (Slaughter, 1998). Due to this lack of a single, predestined future, we can have no knowledge of the actual future, but we can envision desired (normative) futures and seek to bring them about (de Jouvenel, 2000). A central tenet of FS is that “images of the future” actively influence individual and group action (Bell, 1997a; Dator, 1996; Polak, 1961). This is also evident in Giddens (1990) assertion that “[a]nticipations of the future become part of the present thereby rebounding upon how the future actually develops” (p. 178).

The goals of FS research are to distinguish parameters of the future environment, explore potential, alternative futures, and identify areas where policy interventions (broadly defined) may act to bring about a more desired outcome (Bell, 1997b). Glenn (2009) observes that futures inquiry seeks to “systematically explore, create, and test both possible and desirable futures to improve policy decisions. It includes analysis of how those conditions might change as a result of the implementation of policies and actions, and the consequences of these policies and actions (pp. 1–2)”. Aanestad (2011) also notes for IS research, the “durability of the present” – through the accumulation and interactions across past and present sociotechnical artefacts and practices – impacts future ICT change and innovation and must be accounted for in futures-oriented inquiry.

FS researchers and practitioners are themselves action scientists who make empirically grounded value judgements (Bell, 1997b) relevant to possible futures and identify preferred consequences. To inform policy-making, goals for future conditions are set in the present based on data available now. FS seeks to reduce the gap between desired (normative) and likely (exploratory) futures by supporting the development of policies designed to increase the likelihood of desirable outcomes, or decrease the likelihood of undesirable ones. This is an iterative process that includes identifying a number of probable paths from an infinite number of possible futures; evaluating possible futures and determining which are most desirable; and, taking action by devising, implementing, and revising policies and plans along with societal stakeholders (Dator, 1996; Glenn, 2009). As a result, FS is reflexive and self-altering, focusing on the reduction of uncertainty and identification of key branching points where early policy interventions may be most successful (Helmer, 1983). The success of a forecast is determined not by whether it is validated in the future but on its ability to help inform policy in the present (Glenn, 2009).

In these and other ways, FS seeks to provide insight into both “the future consequences of alternative actions (a prediction problem) and whether such future consequences will be desirable or undesirable possibilities (a value-judgmental problem)” (Bell, 1997b, p. 109). Consistent with the discourse of RRI, FS upholds that value judgements do exist in scientific inquiry, argues for the testing (i.e., valid grounding) of normative judgements (Bell, 1997b), and acknowledges that making ethical judgements is futures-oriented. Advocates for integrating FS and ethics in IS research include Markus and Mentzer (2014), who call for a general framework for futures-oriented explorations of ICT-related impacts to understand and mitigate potential negative outcomes related to global financial networks. Wakunuma and Stahl (2014) argue that a “broader use of futures and foresight methodologies (embedded within a broader framework of responsible innovation) may contribute to a more responsible environment in research, innovation and practice” (p. 383). Stahl et al. (2016) highlight emerging ICT developments that call for research attention to their possible future social and economic consequences.

Such normative stances require a departure from conventional research boundaries in the IS field (Stahl, 2012; Stahl et al., 2014). Not all IS researchers would agree that normative judgements about technologies are appropriate in scholarly research. Some would argue that categorical terms such as “better” or “desirable” are not decidable without abandoning a neutral scholarly position to align with some stakeholders’ values and interests and thus would eschew FS approaches. Thus, despite these and other calls for futures-oriented approaches in information systems development, management, and governance research (cf. Chiasson & Henfridsson, 2011; Gray, 2008; Gray & Hovav, 1999, 2007, 2008, 2011; Hovav, 2014; Hovav & Gray, 2002; Stahl, 2011), such research within the scholarly IS field remains quite rare (Lee, 2016).

## 3. A philosophical foundation for futuresoriented IS research

In many streams of FS the researcher’s (and practitioner’s) goal is the creation of transformative spaces for the production of alternative futures through organising and examining diferent views of, and concerns about, the future (Inayatullah, 2002). This goal may appear illegitimate compared to a primary contribution of scholarly IS research, that is, producing values-neutral theoretical generalisations through empirical study of past and present ICTs. Acknowledging this gap between the premises of futures-oriented IS research and more conventional scholarly IS research, Chiasson and Henfridsson (2011) call for “an initial and developing set of intellectual structures from which IS research can respond to the needs of our future society … we must then ask whether and how our approaches to inquiry can afect our ability to do so” (p. 1). The epistemic assumptions of FS, highlighted above, provide a partial foundation to do so. However, a philosophical foundation is also needed to critically analyse the possibility and desirability of alternative ICTs and the sociotechnical futures they might engender. Philosophy also grounds a critique of how research on past and present ICTs could inform futures-oriented IS research.

To address Chiasson and Henfridsson’s (2011) call, we therefore suggest that a philosophy of technology that encompasses the “two extremes – potentiality and actuality – norms and facts” (Feenberg, 2010a, p. 81) would usefully guide futures-oriented IS research. Philosopher Andrew Feenberg has long written about technology (e.g., 1991, 1995, 1999, 2002, 2010a), contending that technology is the most critical influence in modern societies and economies and is fundamental to what it does (and will in the future) mean to be human:

Diferent worlds, flowing from diferent technical arrangements, privilege some aspects of the human being and marginalise others. What it means to be human is thus decided in large part in the space of our tools. (Feenberg, 2002, p. 19)

We draw on Feenberg’s philosophy for several reasons. Feenberg himself draws from critical social theory, as well science and technology studies (STS), so that his positions have similarities with a wide range of philosophers and social theorists (including Bijker, Foucault, Habermas, Heidegger, Latour, Marcuse, Marx, and Plato), whose works have inspired IS researchers. Adopting a critical theory stance, Feenberg explicates how values and interests, particularly the exercise of power (cf. Foucault, Marcuse), are necessarily implicated in technology developments, but he maintains that technologies, and through technology, society, are open to (re)appropriation towards progressive societal ends (cf. Marcuse). In doing so, Feenberg navigates an intellectual path between: (1) instrumental views of technology per se as values-free and (2) substantive views of technology as embodying deterministic utopian or dystopian outcomes (cf. Heidegger). He critiques the indeterminacy of social constructivist and processual views of technology (cf. Latour), which he characterises as “exaggerated and largely rhetorical empiricism” (Feenberg, 2006, p. 187), though he values their historical methods that open up the processes through which past and present technical systems developed.

In these ways, we find that Feenberg provides a comprehensive, technology-centred philosophy, and a hopeful perspective on sociotechnical futures that might be realised through ICT developments. To argue these points, we focus on three central themes in Feenberg’s works: technical codes that guide the realisation of sociotechnical outcomes, technology’s unrealised potentialities, and democratic rationalisation of technologies.

## 3.1. Technical codes and potentialities

Feenberg’s philosophy is concerned with how and why technologies develop as they do and the implications technologies then hold for human societies. He articulates the involvement of technical codes in technology development in this way:

A technical code is the realisation of an interest or ideology in a technically coherent solution to a problem … Goals are “coded” in the sense of ranking items as ethically permitted or forbidden, or aesthetically better or worse, or more or less socially desirable. (Feenberg, 2010b, p. 68)

A technical code represents values, interests, and priorities that are widely applied in a society (Feenberg, 2001). Feenberg critiques technical rationality as a pervasive and dominant technical code in modern capi talist economies, which he claims prioritise eficiency, power, profits, and autonomous managerial control for the benefit of selected elites (e.g., corporate owners and their representatives). He argues that alternative rationalities are possible that encompass more varied values and interests, such as aesthetics, environmental preservation, and quality of human life.

Technical codes are implicated in two analytically distinct but interrelated processes of technology development, termed instrumentalisation. In one process, primary qualities of a technology are abstracted and decontextualised from particular settings or contexts, allowing these qualities to be applied elsewhere (Feenberg, 2002, p. 178). For technology to become useful, further instrumentalisation is needed to repackage decontextualised primary qualities with secondary qualities relevant to a particular setting. Technologies are actualised through a technical code that directs the design and implementation of primary and secondary qualities. Feenberg allows that technology’s primary and secondary qualities are inherently intertwined so that both influence how technology and related sociotechnical outcomes develop:

No matter how abstract the afordances identified at the primary level, they carry social content from the secondary level in the elementary contingencies of a particular approach to the materials. Similarly, secondary instrumentalizations such as design specifications presuppose the identification of the afordances to be assembled and concretized. (Feenberg, 2006, p. 186)

In accord with constructivist understanding of interpretive flexibility (cf. Bijker, 1995), Feenberg also assumes that instrumentalisation processes are underdetermined with any one technical code, so that users retain a degree of initiative and ability to (re)shape how a technology design or system is realised in social practices.

To illustrate, consider this simple example. A tree may be cut down and turned into paper. The tree’s fibrous substance (primary qualities) are decontextualised from the natural environment as wood pulp and then adapted to the sociotechnical context of paper production (secondary qualities). Decontextualising the fibrous properties of the tree presumes a society with written language and practices for using paper, which in turn presumes the availability and utility of fibrous materials extractable from the natural environment. Decontextualised wood pulp may be repackaged with various secondary qualities as paper, boxes, posters, building material, and so on, and users may further re-appropriate these qualities, for instance, turning newspapers into blankets.

For Feenberg, technology cannot be values-free, because it is only through the imposition of a technical code and its underlying values, interests, and priorities that technologies are actualised. For instance, paper production relies on a technical code that prioritises eficient appropriation and monetisation of natural resources for human consumption. At the same time, Feenberg contends that technology is not deterministic. The existence of wood pulping and paper producing techniques does not mean all trees are destined to become paper; forests may be preserved through a technical code that encompasses environmental preservation for social good.

Taking a critical stance, Feenberg argues that sociotechnical outcomes are not equally likely, given the power dynamics that lead to a narrowing of interests in instrumentalisation:

Where, further, society is organised around technology, technological power is the principle form of power in the society. It is realised through designs which narrow the range of interests and concerns that can be represented by the normal functioning of the technology and the institutions which depend on it. (Feenberg, 2010b, p. 82)

The result is a soft form of hegemony, which guides and restricts the realisation of alternative technology designs and sociotechnical practices through a dominant technical code in modern economies – technical rationality. Thus, trees are more likely to be transformed into paper than preserved in forests if that technical code is unchallenged.

Because technical codes actualise some designs and practices, and thus engender some sociotechnical futures, other possible designs, practices, and futures are necessarily suppressed. That is, a tree cannot serve both as wood pulp and as a natural carbon absorption mechanism in the forest. Drawing extensively from Marcuse’s (1964) concept of potentialities, Feenberg emphasises not only the historical paths of technological systems that have been realised, but importantly he points to those that might have, or might in the future, be realised through alternative technical codes. That is, potentialities are not merely preferences or choices about technology designs, nor are they simply technically feasible alternatives. For Feenberg, potentialities have an ontological reality in terms of the technology designs and sociotechnical outcomes that are unrealised yet realisable through alternative technical codes. Potentialities do not imply merely diferent means or paths to the same end, but instead imply diferent ends, diferent goals, and thus diferent sociotechnical futures. Feenberg argues that potentialities can only be realised if the dominant technical codes that direct technological developments are challenged and overturned. Returning to the forest, we note that some trees could be used to make paper, while others are left to absorb carbon or serve as animal habitats, as a “technically coherent solution”, a potentiality Feenberg would argue requires challenging the dominant technical code of technical rationality.

## 3.2. Democratic rationalisation of technology

Feenberg (2002, 2009), and Jin and Feenberg (2015) argues strongly that technology, particularly the Internet and other ICTs, embodies democratic potentialities. These potentialities are largely suppressed today, but they could be realised in the future through transformation of social institutions, culture, and technology. In what he terms democratic rationalisation, new technologies (and particularly ICTs) can be re-appropriated to undermine existing social hierarchies or force them to address a broader range of needs and values (Feenberg, 1999, p. 76). Doing so requires broad participation in societal discussions and debates:

I argue that the technical enterprise itself is immanently disposed to address the demands we formulate as potentialities, but that it is artificially truncated in modern industrial societies. Opening technical development to the influence of a wider range of values is a technical project requiring broad democratic participation. Radical democratisation can thus be rooted in the very nature of technology, with profound substantive consequences for the organisation of industrial society. (Feenberg, 2002, p. 34)

Democratisation of technology is not solely (or primarily) a matter of political structures such as citizen rights (like voting), but of opening technology debates and choices to a broader range of interests and values. Feenberg comments:

Most fundamentally, democratisation of technology is about finding new ways of privileging these excluded values and the public which articulate them. (Feenberg, 2010a, p. 192)

Feenberg acknowledges that social groups and publics are often disengaged with debates about technology developments and that suppressed potentialities remain unrealised. However, he maintains that social groups could challenge and overturn current technological arrangements and dominant technical codes. For instance, technology users may re-appropriate technology designs to serve diferent goals and ends, even after a technological system has been codified and implemented. This is possible because technologies retain some interpretive flexibility and users some agency in how they utilise technology and for what purposes and goals. This localised, bottom-up form of resistance may difuse widely, so that technology designers redesign a technology to more fully accommodate the potentialities actualised by their users. ICTs are particularly susceptible to re-appropriation, for instance, users “jailbreaking” the iPhone operating system (iOS) to instal their own programme applications and Apple Corporation’s subsequent development of the iTunes App Store to facilitate user-generated mobile applications (“apps”).

Collective actions to challenge technological systems may also develop when negative consequences of technology accumulate until individuals and communities that are afected recognise their common interests. Feenberg points to the rise of environmental movements, when various social groups became aware of, and concerned with, environmental degradation as instances of democratic rationalisation of some technical systems. ICTs per se can become the sites of collective actions and protests, for instance, the “net neutrality” movement in the United States to challenge telecommunication network owners’ plans for Internet services (Miranda, Young, & Yetgin, 2016). Feenberg speaks optimistically of the potential of such actions to open technology to a wider range of interests and to guide radical reform of the technical sphere (Feenberg, 2006, p. 180). However, spreading knowledge of technological consequences alone is not suficient to engender the type of radical reform Feenberg envisions. Instead, he argues, technology’s negative consequences cannot be ofloaded to disempowered groups, but rather those who design and control technologies must also be exposed to its consequences. In this way, a broader range of interests and values are more likely to be accounted for in a technical code (Feenberg, 2006, p. 182). Consider, for instance, whether the designers of ICTs that automate other workers’ jobs might become more open to the values of professional influence and control in work redesign, when their own expertise becomes subject to automation (and their jobs eliminated) with artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques.

The difuse nature and soft hegemony of technical rationality leads Feenberg (and Marcuse) to argue for a more nuanced, yet also more radical, form of democratic participation involving resistance and confrontation of the power and logics of this dominant technical code of modern societies (Feenberg, 2002; Veak, 2006). The result is a call for democratic rationality that extends the priorities, values, and interests that direct technology developments within society, and thus the direction of society, beyond the pursuit of power and profit for elites (owners, managers, their technicians) and towards experiments with potentialities actualised through alternative technical codes. In his philosophical goal to expose “the obstacles to the release of technology’s integrative potential” (Feenberg, 2002, p. 177), Feenberg does not shy away from the normative implications and ethical complexities of democratically altered sociotechnical futures:

A fundamentally diferent form of civilization will emphasise other attributes of technology compatible with a wider distribution of cultural qualifications and powers. They include the vocational investment of technical subjects in their work, collegial forms of self-organisation, and the technical integration of a wide range of life enhancing values, beyond the mere pursuit of profit or power. (Feenberg, 2002, p. 35)

## 4. Informing futures-oriented IS research

Although we would not characterise him as a futurist, we can see that Feenberg’s philosophy of technology provides a comprehensive, technology-centred, critical foundation for the epistemic assumptions evident in FS: a focus on multiple possible sociotechnical futures (potentialities) realised through the actions and policies of human actors (instrumentalisation of primary and secondary qualities of technology) in relation to the interests and values brought to bear in technology design and implementation (technical codes, technical rationality, democratic rationality). With this background, we turn next to questions about the approaches to inquiry into sociotechnical futures (Chiasson & Henfridsson, 2011). Feenberg (2010b) articulates a series of what he terms paradoxes in how we can know or understand technology that are implicit in his philosophy. These paradoxes challenge how inquiry into past and present ICTs might usefully inform futures-oriented IS research and have implications for common IS research approaches.

## 4.1. Epistemic challenges for futures-oriented IS research

Feenberg’s philosophy of technology poses substantial challenges to the pursuit of generalisable theories that could be applicable across contexts or into future socio-economic settings. Feenberg (2010b) cautions that technical systems are inherently complex, so that the whole cannot be understood without examining its parts (e.g., hardware, software, use practices, institutional arrangements), and the parts cannot be explained without reference to the whole (e.g., purposes and outcomes of technical systems). Primary and secondary qualities of technology are intertwined and recursive. Cause–efect analyses of technical systems and their outcomes thus become circular arguments, so that choosing the entry point and boundaries for inquiry into trajectories of technical systems and their efects is problematic.

Moreover the contexts in which instrumentation processes occur become taken-for-granted or invisible to the analyst or designer, making it dificult to foresee the complications that will arise when technical systems are removed from their initial context and implemented in diferent cultures, economies, or in future settings. Constructivists have demonstrated that technical systems develop through a specific history of choices made during instrumentalisation. Feenberg (2010b) argues further, “there is no general rule under which paths of development can be explained”, and in particular, “eficiency does not explain success, success explains eficiency” (p. 8). That is, we cannot assume that historical trajectories of technology development will necessarily be repeated in the future. Nor can we assume general principles such as eficiency (e.g., marketplace eficiency) are what inevitably will guide future developments.

While these paradoxes challenge the pursuit of generalisable theory, Feenberg’s critical perspective does focus our attention on dominant technical codes and their implicit values, interests, and priorities, which influence how technology’s potentialities are actualised. Thus, we would expect that patterns in how potentialities are actualised should be discoverable, for instance, a preference for centralised, autonomous control consistent with technical rationality versus more distributed, interactive designs possible through a technical code of democratic rationalisation. To consider how technical codes might trigger collective resistance and thus change in the future, Feenberg (2010b) posits that the consequences of technology choices, “invisible in the immediate zone of action become[s] visible when a wider or longer range view is available”. To discern these possible future trajectories requires a wide scope of inquiry into future timeframes as well as possible consequences for “workers, users of technology, victims of its consequences” (p. 9). That is, to inform futures-oriented IS research, inquiry into past and present ICTs would require longer timeframes and a wider scope of stakeholders (e.g., beyond managers, firms, and specific technology users) than we believe is typical of most IS research endeavours.

Consistent with a FS understanding of futures, Feenberg (2010b) views the future as a moving target, shifting and transforming in response to technical actions undertaken in the present, so that “our actions not only come back to us through causal feedback, they also change the meaning of our world” (p. 9). In line with recent theorising about performativity (Orlikowski & Scott, 2013), Feenberg concludes, “Neither society nor technology can be understood in isolation from each other because neither has a stable identity or form” (pp. 13–14). This perspective presents substantial challenges to relying on empirical studies of past or present ICTs to project into sociotechnical futures. However, Feenberg’s concepts of technical codes, potentialities, and democratic rationality does provide intellectual structures for exploring possible futures and informing actions to devise, implement, and revise policies and plans with societal stakeholders (Dator, 1996; Glenn, 2009).

In this essay we have assumed that the values and interests that ethical arguments entail are fundamental to futures-oriented IS research. In this regard, Feenberg dissolves the diference between values and facts, contending that today’s values are the “facts of the future”:

Values are not the opposite of facts, subjective desires with no basis in reality. Values express aspects of reality that have not yet been incorporated into the taken for granted technical environment. That environment was shaped by the values that presided over its creation. Technologies are the crystallised expression of those values. New values open up established designs for revision. (Feenberg, 2010b, p. 12)

Drawing on knowledge of past or present ICTs to inform futures-oriented IS research thus entails critical questioning to uncover the values and interests implicit in past “facts” as well as those vying to become future “facts”. Sociotechnical futures evolve not only in relation to which values and interests have been translated into the technical solutions of the past or present (Aanestad, 2011), but also depend on whose values and interests are vying for attention and consideration and may thus become taken-for-granted facts in the future. Questions of how values, including unmet public demands, have been in the past or might be in the future formulated as viable technical specifications (Feenberg, 2010b), cannot be avoided. What could have been (suppressed potentialities), what might still become (alternative potentialities), and what should be (ethical direction to potentialities) all are relevant to informing sociotechnical futures.

## 4.2. Implications for IS research practices

The types of epistemic challenges we highlight in Feenberg’s philosophy of “potentialities and actualities” are not unknown in the scholarly IS field, and indeed most have been debated in terms of the nature of causality, generalisability, theory, research relevance, and so on (cf. Gregor, 2006; Lee & Baskerville, 2003; Markus & Robey, 1988; Mingers, 2004). Moreover, scholarly IS research presents a broad tapestry of philosophical traditions and research methods. Our purpose in this section is simply to highlight aspects of commonly used research practices that we believe limit the potential to draw on the IS field’s considerable knowledge of past and present ICTs to better inform futures-oriented IS research (see Hovav & Carmel, 2014, for discussion of applications of FS methods in IS research).

Consider, for illustrative purposes, a prototypical positivist, quantitative research study, such as an experimental design or cross-sectional survey that develops and tests a research model using standardised measurement instruments (e.g., a survey of users’ attitudes towards the complexity and usability of an enterprise system, or a survey across organisations about the factors afecting its successful implementation). In these studies, the sociotechnical ICT system is typically abstract, referred to only by its generic name (e.g., ERP, BI, CRM), so that neither the contextual and historical whole nor the parts are examined with any specificity. Contextual details around the individuals and organisations may be limited to specified moderating, mediating, or control variables, and only those achieving statistical significance are considered relevant. Such studies are ahistorical, ignoring the trajectory of design and adoption choices that leads to sociotechnical outcomes (or of outcomes that contribute to choices), and yet particularly with organisational-level studies, the historical trajectories are often highly variable. Importantly, such studies seek to uncover general law-like relationships among various secondary qualities of the ICTs associated with outcomes such as user acceptance and use of system features or eficiencies like organisational agility, competitiveness, or profitability.

These approaches ofer much in terms of rigorous methods that could be re-applied in diferent settings to study past or present ICTs. However, for futures-oriented IS research, methodological rigour comes at the cost of distancing inquiry from the complexity, context-specificity and history of the sociotechnical system and from the priorities, values, and interests through which some features and practices were realised, while others were suppressed. To draw on such empirical knowledge to inform futures-oriented IS research would, we suggest, require contextualising the inquiry to consider these issues, in order to consider whether alternative technical codes (societal values and interests) might shape technical developments and thus lead to sociotechnical changes in the future. In this regard, drawing from generalised associations, laws, or theories to inform futures-oriented studies rests on the assumption that observed measures, factors, and relationships will hold the same social meanings and will persist in much the same form in the future as in the past, assumptions that Feenberg’s philosophy questions.

Also for illustrative purposes, consider a prototypical interpretive, qualitative organisational field study. An interpretive researcher generally seeks to reveal how human actors make sense of, adopt and adapt ICT systems (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). In doing so, the researcher examines with some detail and specificity the ICT system and sociotechnical practices in the context studied, although inquiry into the ICT’s history and implications are generally limited to the organisational field study setting. The utility of such knowledge for futures-oriented IS research lies primarily in the explication of how and what to observe about ICT implementation or assimilation, that is, analytical details that philosophers such as Feenberg tend to ignore. Theoretical knowledge may be abstracted (decontextualised) from the context (Klein & Myers, 1999), for instance as a general process model like structuration theory (Orlikowski, 1992) or improvisation (Orlikowski, 1996), which then might guide research in other organisational sociotechnical settings. As with positivist studies, interpretive IS inquiry may take for granted the technical codes of current and emerging ICT systems and thus lose sensitivity to the suppressed potentialities that might be realised in other settings or with alternative technical codes. Focusing only on “what is” limits the utility of these rich studies to explore the multiplicity of possible sociotechnical futures and contributes to what Feenberg (2002) criticises as “the inability to develop criteria of progress out of the analysis of local situations and struggles” (p. 31).

Pragmatic concerns about how broadly to frame a research setting or how long a research study can be conducted tend to limit inquiry into the consequences of recent ICTs to short-term and narrow boundaries. For instance, a cross-sectional survey or an in-depth organisational field study of cloud computing adoption might assess near-term organisational consequences of adopting cloud computing (e.g., improved ICT eficiency, displacement of organisational IS workers, processes of transitioning from on-premises to cloud infrastructure), but neither study is likely to consider longer term consequences for the natural environment of massive data centre construction, of IT industry restructuring around a few dominant infrastructure providers, or of increased ease of surveillance from aggregated data stores “in the cloud”, all of which might accrue in possible futures. Informing futures-oriented IS research on these possible consequences through empirical research on current practices would require a broader scope of inquiry beyond organisational managers or technology professionals and their current values and interests.

IS research approaches aimed at contributing more directly to the construction of sociotechnical futures are not immune from tendencies to embrace the taken-forgranted code of technical rationality and to ignore alternative codes and potentialities. In design science (e.g., Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004; March & Storey, 2008; Pries-Heje & Baskerville, 2008), design artefacts or design process as theory are used to help practitioners inform their specific situations. Without consideration of a broad range of values and interests, the resulting ICTs will leave technically viable potentialities unnoticed or dormant and might reinforce hegemonic control enacted through the technology. Even when design studies intend to be broadly inclusive, the delayed feedback and the gradual accumulation of potentially negative consequences of technology designs may not be discernable to the researcher. For instance, it is doubtful that the designers of social media platforms anticipated the proliferation of “fake news” and the consequences for political processes, which developed over a decade of increasing sophistication in user-targeting advertising tools.

Similarly, action research (McKay & Marshall, 2001) often addresses the social construction of problem definitions and solutions in organisations, using process theories such as soft systems methodology (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). Problem structuring methods typically involve diverse organisational members working together to define root causes, specify participant actions, and guide a best course of action for realising specific sociotechnical practices. In this regard, Habermas’s (1984) critical theory of communicative action posits an “ideal speech situation” that could liberate participants to question and possibly overturn technical codes and thus direct instrumentalisation towards more humanistic ICT designs (Hansen, Berente, & Lyytinen, 2009; Ross & Chiasson, 2011), at least within the specific setting. Yet, if researchers and participants assume problem and solution formulations are free from latent values and interests, for instance, with tacit acceptance of centralised managerial control and putative eficiency goals, the surfacing of alternative values and interests from diverse constituents will be thwarted, a restricted range of potentialities considered, and possible sociotechnical futures unduly limited.

Feenberg’s concern with the self-referential and performative nature of technical systems is evident in recent theorising on sociomateriality, defined as the mutual entanglement of the social and material and the intra-relational across human and non-human actants (Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers, Henfridsson, Newell, & Vidgen, 2014; Leonardi, 2011; Orlikowski, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). Performative theorists examine the history and intra-action across semi-independent or completely dependent actants that have produced the sociotechnical present. Such work helps open a sociotechnical system to observation of the co-construction of its technical and social aspects (primary and secondary instrumentalisation). This work also constantly opens up the numerous other sociotechnical potentialities that could have emerged in this particular time and space. However, although this “could have been otherwise” view is critical in intent, performative researchers tend to assume an agnostic position towards any particular sociotechnical ensemble and to the technical codes that shaped the past and present (Feenberg, 2002). Without engaging with the ethical dilemmas and choices that shape past, present, and sociotechnical futures, we suggest that the relevance of this approach to inquiry will be limited for futures-oriented IS research.

## 5. An example of engaging with futuresoriented IS Research

We now turn to one of the “hottest” research topics currently in the IS field to highlight key arguments about and examples of futures-oriented IS research. Big data analytics represents an array of technical functions and emerging practices for generating, aggregating, analysing, and visualising large quantities of data about human activity. This array of technologies involves the statistical modelling of very large, dynamic data-sets, including large amounts of unstructured content arising from social media and other digital innovations enabled by the Internet. Business firms see big data analytics as a “fruitful foundation for innovation, competition and productivity” (Lycett, 2013, p. 381), with widespread impact on economies and society (Davenport, Barth, & Bean, 2012; Mcafee & Brynjolfsson, 2012).

Big data analytics is receiving substantial attention from IS researchers, “with all the attendant uncertainty that new and potentially revolutionary technologies bring” (Chen, Chiang, & Storey, 2012, p. 1185). Calls for big data research opportunities in leading IS research journals (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Chen et al., 2012; Muller, Junglas, Vom Brocke, & Debortoli, 2016) acknowledge issues such as researcher ethics and informed consent, privacy of personally-identifiable information, and system security. For instance, Belanger and Xu (2015) discuss the future of privacy, noting that “Information Systems (IS) researchers can contribute substantially towards the goal of developing a “responsible use framework” in the Big Data era by encouraging privacy from the point of data acquisition through the point of data use, dissemination, maintenance and disposition” (para. 3). However, generally these calls do not highlight the values-laden, ethical dilemmas posed by the many potentialities of big data analytics, particularly compared to their enthusiastic embrace of business opportunities to exploit big data for entrepreneurship, market domination, advertising, profitability, and so on. Instead, early indications suggest to us that much IS research will be conventional, focusing on how to design applications or implement big data analytics to support corporate interests, with less consideration given to future consequences arising from the underlying technical code of eficiency and autonomous control by business owners over consumers (and citizens generally).

For instance, big data analytics are seen as a means to improve certainty by shaping user behaviour through “choice architectures” in the design of information systems, influencing their behaviour indirectly by “nudging” them towards certain actions (Thaler, Sunstein, & Balz, 2014). While the combination of nudging and big data analytics (“big nudging”) might be used to motivate citizens to use less energy and protect the natural environment, a more likely application is to promote consumerism, or possibly even to influence voters in a way that undermines the democratic process (Helbing et al., 2017). Barocas and Selbst (2016) notes that big data analytics practices are shaped by existing interests, and that analysis based on proxies (i.e., correlating variables) often “discovers” patterns that merely reinforce existing social inequalities. There are also emerging concerns about unjust discrimination (Boyd, Levy, & Marwick, 2014; Custers, 2013) in the form of diferent pricing and employment opportunities, denial of health insurance or increased premiums, and loss of autonomy and privacy through big data analytics.

Clearly, developments with big data analytics technologies could lead to undesirable sociotechnical futures, such as a world without personal privacy and autonomy (Zubof, 2016) and heightened discriminatory business profiling to maximise corporate profitability (Turow, 2012). These outcomes are not, as yet, determined, and there are many other potentialities evident, including improved energy conservation, government transparency, illumination of social injustices in order to remedy them, and advances in medical research and care. Feenberg would argue, and we echo, that these potentialities could be realised through alternative technical codes beyond technical rationality. To engender the pubic debate and participation to do so will require that undesirable consequences be recognised and shared by all parties, and the interests and values of diverse constituencies be brought to the foreground through democratic rationalisation of big data technologies and practices.

We suggest that the scholarly IS field can help inform, and thus help realise, desirable sociotechnical futures. For instance, empirical studies could reach beyond organisational boundaries (or more deeply within organisations beyond management informants) to include a broader range of perspectives on the design and implementation of big data analytics systems in order to surface diverse values, interests, and conflicts. Inquiry could expand beyond corporate interests in “extracting value” from big data to consider where undesirable consequences may be developing and what types of social actors are afected. Doing so would entail reaching beyond IT firms or corporate users as constituents for IS research into diverse communities of users, constituents, and actors potentially afected by big data applications. Analysis of current data and privacy practices could reveal how taken-for-granted technical “facts” (such as organisational ownership and control over personally identifiable information) developed historically and highlight where current struggles and excluded voices might represent values and interests to be incorporated into future designs (e.g., the individual’s “right to be forgotten” by search engines).

Inquiry could build on empirical studies of “what is” (i.e., research on past and present big data practices) to consider alternative potentialities for data ownership, use, and control. For instance, what if big data were treated as a resource like air, rather than a privately owned resource like oil? How might data governance and stewardship be enacted? Would data stewards grant individuals full control over their personal data, even if sharing their data might also serve a greater social good? Opening inquiry to alternative potentialities for big data ownership might then inspire design science research to a radical rethinking of data governance that problematises corporate control of personally identifiable information, such as an Internet of Trusted Data that includes robust digital identities and citizen-controlled personal data stores for all individuals (Pentland et al., 2016) and techniques for informing the consent of subjects’ data and for monitoring and for compliance of big data users.

We have highlighted only a few possible approaches for engaging with futures-oriented IS research on big data analytics. While some studies might adopt FS methods, others researchers might rely on research practices that are more common to the IS field. In either instance we suggest drawing on Feenberg’s critical philosophy of technology to address technology potentialities as well as actualities would help researchers expand their research approaches to more efectively inform, and thus to help shape, sociotechnical futures.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Senior scholars in the IS field have called for futures research (Hovav, 2014; Markus & Mentzer, 2014; Wakunuma & Stahl, 2014), but as yet there has been limited response. Building on these earlier calls through this essay, we hope to challenge the IS research community to move beyond its goal of being relevant to current practice (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Robey & Markus, 1998; Rosemann & Vessey, 2008) and to strive to be relevant to future practice. To do so, we highlight epistemic assumptions about sociotechnical futures that are well accepted in FS research but are largely foreign in traditional IS scholarship. As an “initial set of intellectual structures” (Chiasson & Henfridsson, 2011), we suggest Feenberg’s philosophy provides a foundation that could guide IS researchers’ engagement with futures-oriented IS research. Grounded in critical and constructivist theories, Feenberg presents a hopeful perspective on the democratising potentialities of technology that support ethical inquiry into sociotechnical futures. We also consider the substantial challenges to develop knowledge and conduct research studies of past and present ICTs in ways that could inform inquiry on sociotechnical futures. We illustrate these possibilities and issues with examples from an emerging research area in the IS field, big data analytics.

There are, of course, substantial obstacles to gaining legitimacy for future-oriented IS research. Researchers would have to engage in activities that are still rare in the IS field: value-based critiques of present conditions and practices, consideration of multiple, mutable sociotechnical futures, and critical engagement with the demands, values, and interests of policymakers, practitioners, and constituents of ICTs and of IS research. Given that futures-oriented IS research will be concerned at some level with questions of ethics, researchers may eschew engaging with research into sociotechnical futures to avoid taking particular ethical stances. Current publication norms could be restrictive, if journal editors and reviewers are unable to evaluate the evidence and logic of such work. In our experience as authors and editors, we have found that discussions of future(s) in IS research publications tend to be limited to implications for practice, the generalisability of theory to other settings, and future research topics. Understandably, for many IS scholars, futures-oriented IS research will be too risky and uncertain.

Despite these dificulties we believe futures-oriented IS research would enable inclusion of a broader range of perspectives and discussion about values and ethics that guide the development of ICTs and the futures they will engender, which are not yet a key part of the scholarly discourse in the IS field (Stahl, 2012). Yet, theoretical, methodological, and topical choices made individually and collectively by researchers in a field of study are performative choices, which by their nature afect the direction and attention of practitioners (cf. Constantinides, Chiasson, & Introna, 2012) and thus have an influence on and in sociotechnical futures (Callon, 2007; Law & Urry, 2004). Choosing to avoid explicit engagement with values and norms in inquiry into sociotechnical futures does not mean we are not implicitly doing. What we are proposing here are taking some first steps to gain comfort and experience with these new forms and goals for scholarly IS research. We conclude with science fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke’s second law from his book, Technology and the Future: “The only way to discover the limits of the possible is to go beyond them into the impossible.”
