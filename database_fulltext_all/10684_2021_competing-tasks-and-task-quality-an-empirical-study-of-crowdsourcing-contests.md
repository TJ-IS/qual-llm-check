# COMPETING TASKS AND TASK QUALITY: AN EMPIRICAL STUDY OF CROWDSOURCING CONTESTS<sup>1</sup>

Jiahui Mo Wilbur O. and Ann Powers College of Business, Clemson University, Clemson, SC, U.S.A. {jiahuim@clemson.edu}

Sumit Sarkar and Syam Menon

Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX, U.S.A {sumit@utdallas.edu} {syam@utdallas.edu}

An outcome of the rising popularity of crowdsourcing contest platforms has been an increase in the number of tasks available on a platform at the same time. These concurrent tasks compete for the attention of the same pool of solvers. There is some evidence that an increase in the number of competing tasks can reduce the number of solvers participating in a focal task. However, it is not clear exactly how competing tasks influence the quality of tasks, as represented by the best solutions they receive. Using data collected from a popular crowdsourcing platform, we investigate the effects of competing tasks and uncover important underlying mechanisms governing the relationship between competing tasks and task quality. Through analyses at the task, solver, and submission levels, we establish that competing tasks can influence task quality in two ways. First, the number of solvers in each task decreases when there are more competing tasks, and this reduction negatively impacts task quality. Second, the availability of a greater number of competing tasks leads to solvers participating in more tasks simultaneously. This multitasking in turn allows solvers to learn from other tasks, generate better solutions, and improve task quality. Interestingly, we find that these opposing forces offset each other, and that the overall solution quality of the focal task is not affected, despite the fact that an increase in the number of competing tasks reduces the number of solvers participating it.

Keywords: Multitasking, learning effects, parallel effects, incentive effects, switching, task variety

## Introduction

Advances in information technology have changed the nature of work by allowing companies to go beyond the internal workforce to leverage the “wisdom of the crowd” via online crowdsourcing contest platforms. Crowdsourcing contest platforms are intermediaries that provide a two-sided marketplace with one set of members (seekers, usually firms) posting tasks, and another set of members (solvers, usually individuals) submitting solutions for these tasks.

To conduct a crowdsourcing contest, seekers first need to describe the task they want done along with associated task parameters. Some typical parameters include the number of awards they intend to provide, the prize amount associated with each award, and the duration that the task would be open for solutions. After a seeker has provided the necessary information, the task is released, at which point it gets displayed in the open task list for the specified category. Solvers then select tasks to participate in and begin to submit solutions. Before the task closes, some platforms provide seekers the option to evaluate solutions and assign scores. Upon reaching the task deadline, the seeker makes a final decision and chooses a winner (or winners). The platform collects a commission from the seeker for each completed task.

The potential benefits of crowdsourcing platforms (such as reduced costs and greater solution variety) have enticed many firms to resort to them for solutions to their business problems, and the number of tasks posted on several crowdsourcing platforms has been growing rapidly (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010; Mo et al. 2018). For example, there are usually about 70 new logo design tasks posted daily on Freelancer, while there are over 100 tasks posted in a single task category on 99designs every day. However, as the tasks that are open at the same time compete for solvers, this increase in the number of task postings may not be to the seeker’s advantage.

Tasks are categorized by skill requirements (logo design, programming, etc.), and tasks within a category require similar sets of skills. For example, tasks within the logo design category usually require solvers to have design skills. Therefore, all tasks within a category essentially target the same pool of solvers. Solvers tend to have limited resources— particularly time—thus, they can participate only in a few concurrent contests. As noted by Ge et al. (2010) and Chen et al. (2014), tasks displayed in the same category that are open at the same time (the competing tasks) compete for participants. The fact that most platforms list tasks according to posting time makes the competition more acute—these competing tasks will be presented together to all solvers who log onto the platform and are consequently likely to be considered by the same set of solvers.

The increased number of competing tasks affects all stakeholders. Schenk and Guittard (2011, p. 97) point out that “crowdsourcing contests rely on voluntary participation and the reaching of a critical mass of solvers cannot be guaranteed.” This is validated by Mo et al. (2018) who note that about 10% of the tasks on the platform 99designs did not succeed because they could not attract an adequate number of solvers. As the number of competing tasks increases, so could the number of tasks that fail to attract enough solvers and, consequently, solutions of acceptable quality. This is not new—researchers and practitioners have both realized that having more participants is critical to ensuring that tasks receive solutions of acceptable quality. For example, Terwiesch and Xu (2008) find that a greater number of solvers results in a more diverse set of solutions, which increases the chances of the seeker finding solutions of higher quality. Platforms also recognize the importance of the number of solvers on task quality—many platforms refund the prize money when a task does not receive the minimum number of solvers (e.g., 99designs, crowdspring). All of this suggests that a reduction in the number of solvers is not a desirable outcome, as it can negatively impact task quality. However, a greater number of competing tasks could lead to a decrease in the number of solvers in any given task and if the number of tasks continues to increase at the expense of task quality there are likely to be long-run consequences. Seekers who do not receive enough submissions of acceptable quality could leave the platform. This not only hurts the platform directly, but also makes it less attractive for solvers, who may switch to other more lucrative options. Consequently, the reduction in the number of seekers could potentially hurt the entire crowdsourcing contest ecosystem.

Given the significant impact that competing tasks could have on the nature of crowdsourcing contests in general, it is important to understand the impact of competing tasks on each other. However, research that explores this dimension has been scarce. In the context of expertise-based crowdsourcing contests, Chen et al. (2014) found (in a working paper) that an increase in the number of competing tasks results in a decrease in the number of solvers for the focal task. While the number of solvers plays an important role in the eventual success of a task, it is important to recognize that a seeker’s ultimate concern is the quality of solutions her task receives (in particular, the quality of the best solution received). However, not much is known on the impact of competing tasks on task quality, and this important relationship remains unclear to both practitioners and researchers.

The presence of competing tasks is likely to lead to a decrease in the number of solvers in a task, and this could impact the quality of the best solutions received by the task. Boudreau et al. (2011) found that solvers increase their effort levels to improve their chances of winning when there are fewer solvers in a task, and this incentive effect could improve task quality. However, some studies also suggest that a decrease in the number of solvers can hurt task quality, as fewer solvers can lead to a reduction in solution variety, which can in turn decrease the chances of finding a satisfactory solution (Terwiesch and Xu 2008). The reduction in task quality caused by a decrease in the variety of solutions is referred to as the parallel effect (Boudreau et al. 2011).

The presence of competing tasks has the potential to affect solvers’ solution qualities by influencing their participation behavior in other ways. When there is only a single task, solvers participating in it can focus their entire attention on that task. However, the existence of multiple tasks makes it possible for solvers to participate in other tasks while continuing to work on the focal task (i.e., solvers have the opportunity to multitask). Multitasking can degrade the quality of the tasks being performed, e.g., by increasing the number of errors made (Speier et al. 1999; Monsell 2003). However, studies have shown that multitasking can also help improve performance (Aral et al. 2012). Schilling et al. (2003) find that new insights gained by alternating across different tasks can help solvers improve the quality of their solutions to the tasks—i.e., solvers can learn by “doing something else.” Such a learning effect could improve a solver’s solution quality in the multitasking context as well, and consequently has the potential to improve the quality of the best solutions in a task.

The potential existence of a positive multitasking force leaves unclear the impact of competing tasks on task quality. Our primary objective in this paper is to shed light on this relationship. In order to do so, we examine the overall effect of competing tasks on task quality. We also explore the underlying mechanisms that govern this effect through task participation— specifically, through the number of participating solvers and their multitasking levels. To understand the underlying mechanism, we first checked whether the number of solvers decreases with a greater number of competing tasks and whether this reduces task quality. We then investigated whether task quality is affected by multitasking behavior, which may be facilitated by an increase in the competing tasks. There is evidence that the learning benefits from job rotation by working on a variety of tasks are greater when the tasks are similar to each other than otherwise (Schilling et al. 2003; Staats and Gino 2012). Therefore, we examine how the relationship between multitasking and task quality is affected by task similarity.

We collected data from crowdspring, a large crowdsourcing design platform, and focus on tasks in the logo design category (called “Logo & Identity” on crowdspring). The richness of our data allowed us to conduct analyses at the task, solver, and submission levels. We conducted analyses at the submission level first to see whether solvers do learn from multitasking and consequently improve performance. We find that, relative to focusing on the same task, multitasking does result in better performance. We also found a differential effect of task similarity, with solvers benefiting more when multitasking on similar tasks. We then conducted task-level analyses and find that competing tasks affect task quality by influencing solver behavior. As predicted, competing tasks do hurt solution quality by reducing the number of solvers in each task; however, multitasking while participating in tasks allows solvers to learn and improve solution quality. Interestingly, we found that the negative effects on solution quality resulting from the decrease in the number of solvers are offset by the positive effects of an increased incidence of multitasking. As a result, the overall task quality is unaffected by the number of competing tasks.

This paper makes several contributions to the literature on crowdsourcing contests. Most existing research investigates participation behavior within a single task and ignores the impact of the other tasks on the platform. Our work adds to the understanding of competing tasks and task quality on these platforms and to the scant research that exists on that front. It identifies, through large-scale data analysis, interesting effects in the competing task environment and provides empirical evidence on how these effects can influence task quality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to take a detailed look at the impact of competing tasks in a crowdsourcing contest platform. It also fills a crucial gap in the understanding of multitasking behavior in crowdsourcing contest environments.

This work also contributes to the literature on multitasking. Not only do we present evidence on the existence of the positive effects of multitasking on performance in the context of crowdsourcing platforms, our findings reveal the existence of a positive learning effect among the multitasking solvers and the effect is greater when solvers multitask among similar tasks. By providing empirical evidence of learning through multitasking in crowdsourcing contest environments, this study also extends theories of learning to both the multitasking and the crowdsourcing literatures.

The paper is organized as follows. The following section reviews related literature. Then, we present relevant theories and develop hypotheses. Next, we introduce the dataset used in our analysis. Various analyses and findings on the relationship between competing tasks, task participation, and task quality are presented in subsequent section. Then, we describe models and findings on the direct effect of competing tasks on task quality, followed by supplementary evidence on the findings. Finally, we conclude the paper.

## Literature Review

## Crowdsourcing Contests

Research on crowdsourcing contests has its roots in the literature on all-pay auctions (e.g., Taylor 1995). One stream of work in this domain looks into how task characteristics like prize amounts influence tasks in terms of the number of solvers and the quality of solutions received. For example, Chen et al. (2014) observed that a higher reward leads to an increase in the number of solvers in the focal task, while Liu et al. (2014) found that increasing rewards can result in more submissions of higher quality.

Another stream of research investigates the effects of solver participation (i.e., the number of participants) on task quality. Through an analytical model, Terwiesch and Xu (2008) show that tasks can benefit through the increased diversity of solutions that results from a greater number of solvers. Using data from the contest platform Topcoder, Boudreau et al. (2011) found that a greater number of solvers in a task can reduce the efforts of all solvers. At the same time, more solvers joining a task increases the likelihood that at least one solver will provide an acceptable solution.

While these research streams conduct analysis at the task level, a third stream analyzes contests at the level of the individual solver. This stream explores solver participation strategies within a task (like the number of submissions to make and when to make them), and how these submission strategies influence their performance (i.e., their chances of winning that task). For example, Yang et al. (2011) and Chen and Liu (2012) found, using data from different crowdsourcing contest platforms, that solvers who make submissions soon after a task has been posted or make submissions towards the later stages are more likely to win the task. Bockstedt et al. (2016) investigated participation behavior in terms of (1) when to make the first submission, (2) the number of submissions made by the solver, and (3) the length of participation by the solver, and determined that these behaviors influence winning chances of the focal task differently.

When arriving at these results, these papers focused on factors associated with a focal task, such as its prize and duration— that is, they did not consider factors outside the focal task, such as the effect of competing tasks. Along the same lines, existing research has also limited itself primarily to looking at solver behavior within the focal task (e.g., the number of submissions to the focal task). In the presence of competing tasks, this view is too narrow to understand solver behavior, as participation is no longer restricted to a single task. Concurrent tasks provide solvers the option to multitask, and results based on the analysis of a single focal task may not extend to an environment where tasks compete for solvers. Analysis that explicitly incorporates the effects of competing tasks can shed light on the impact of competing tasks and multitasking behavior, particularly on task quality.

There are a few exceptions. As mentioned earlier, Chen et al. (2014) found that an increase in the number of competing tasks reduces the number of solvers in the focal task. Ge et al. (2010) demonstrated that while a single prize is optimal in the single-task environment, this is no longer the optimal prize structure in the presence of competing tasks. The impact of competing tasks on task quality, however, remains unaddressed. We consider competing tasks explicitly and examine how they influence the quality of solutions submitted to tasks.

## Competing Auctions

While the impact of competition has not been explored in much depth in either the crowdsourcing contest or the all-pay auction literatures, there is some work that explores competition in the context of other types of auctions, particularly, traditional online auctions (Anwar et al. 2006; Bapna et al. 2009).

Competing auctions are auctions involving identical or substitutable products in the same time period. Existing research on competing auctions falls into two primary streams. The first stream investigates how competing auctions change bidder strategies. For example, Peters and Severinov (2006) showed analytically that when there are no bidding costs, and when auctions end at the same time, submitting a bid to an auction with the lowest standing bid is the equilibrium strategy for bidders in simultaneous identical ascending auctions. Anwar et al. (2006) empirically confirmed that the presence of competing auctions on eBay entices bidders to cross-bid, and bidders tend to bid on auctions with the lowest price. These findings are echoed by Kayhan et al. (2010) who found that, in the absence of bidding costs, more bidders cross-bid as the number of simultaneous auctions increases, with the effect being more pronounced when there are a greater number of experienced bidders in the market. However, Bapna et al. (2010) determined that if participation in multiple auctions comes at a cost (e.g., auction entry fees), placing multiple bids may no longer be optimal.

The second stream investigates how competing auctions influence a focal auction’s price. In general, competing auctions have been observed to exert negative externalities on the focal auction. For example, Bapna et al. (2009) found that the winning price of the focal auction is negatively related to the extent of overlap with competing auctions. Along the same lines, Muthitachareon et al. (2014) determined that the number of competing auctions available during the time frame of the focal auction is negatively associated with the auction’s price.

We note that there are significant differences between all-pay and traditional online auctions. For example, while only the winner pays the winning bid price in a traditional auction, all bidders pay their bids in all-pay auctions, regardless of whether they win or not (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). That is, participants in all-pay auctions incur significant costs just to participate. In the context of crowdsourcing contests, this nontrivial cost is reflected in the time and effort needed for each submission. On the other hand, the costs of participation in traditional online auctions like those on eBay are quite low (Anwar et al. 2006). Participant objectives tend to be quite different in the two settings as well. While solvers in a crowdsourcing contest would be happy winning every task participated in, this is not true of most other types of auctions. Cross-bidders on auction sites are typically not interested in winning more than one auction, as these auctions are usually for identical or substitutable products.

The objectives of the seller are also quite different from those of a seeker in a crowdsourcing contest. In a traditional auction, sellers usually pick the buyer who pays the most. In a crowdsourcing contest, the seeker is more interested in the solution that best fits the seeker’s preferences and not necessarily in the solution from the solver who put in the most effort. So, while seeker subjectivity is an integral feature of crowdsourcing markets, it is nonexistent in traditional auction markets.

Another difference arises in the context of independence among bids. In crowdsourcing contests, there is a trade-off between the time and effort spent across tasks. This affects the quality of submissions, and therefore a solver’s chances of winning. Consequently, submissions across different tasks impact each other. In traditional competing auctions, where bidders usually want to win only one auction, they need not distribute their resources across different auctions during the bidding process—they only pay for the auction they finally win. As a result, the degree of dependence across submissions in competing crowdsourcing contests is much higher. Therefore, despite some similarities between competing traditional auctions and crowdsourcing contests, the findings from traditional auctions do not necessarily hold for crowdsourcing contests.

## Multitasking and Learning

Multitasking has been studied in a variety of areas, including psychology, cognitive science, experimental economics, information technology, and operations (e.g., Lindbeck and Snower 2000; Delbridge 2001; Salvucci et al. 2009; Aral et al. 2012; Buser and Peter 2012; Diwas Singh 2014). Salvucci et al. (2009) have characterized multitasking to occur across a continuum of time ranging from seconds (e.g., driving and talking) to hours or longer (e.g., cooking and reading a book). We are interested in multitasking that requires switching between different tasks conducted in parallel over a substantive time frame, e.g., hours or days. We adopt the definition provided by Delbridge (2001, p. 1)—“accomplishing multiple task goals in the same time period by engaging in frequent switches between individual tasks”—as that nicely captures the nature of multitasking we observe in crowdsourcing contests. While the crowdsourcing literature has recognized the possibility of such behavior among solvers (Bockstedt et al. 2016), its implications have not yet been studied either directly or in the context of learning.

Learning has been studied on organizational, team, and individual levels and has been shown to promote performance in a variety of ways (Schilling et al. 2003; Narayanan et al. 2009). For example, learning by doing (Arrow 1962), or “intratask” learning (Lindbeck and Snower 2000)—whereby a worker focuses on a specific task and improves productivity by getting better at it—has been studied for decades. Subseqently, researchers have shown that learning can happen even when doing something else (Schilling et al. 2003)—i.e., insights and skills gained from one task can transfer to others and improve their outcomes as a result. The extent of such “intertask” learning (Lindbeck and Snower 2000) and the associated impact on performance can depend on the extent of similarity among the tasks handled by the individual (Narayanan et al. 2009). To date, learning mechanisms have been closely examined primarily in the context of nonconcurrent tasks (e.g., Schilling et al. 2003).

The works by Lindbeck and Snower (2000) and Aral et al. (2012) have considered multitask learning. Lindbeck and Snower (2000) have considered this phenomenon as intertask learning in general and not multitasking specifically, and they contrast it to specialization within a functional area. They focused on the increased role of teamwork and job rotation in organizations, and how advances in production and information technologies, coupled with worker preferences, can help the reorganization of work in business environments. Aral et al. (2012) considered multitask learning in the context of an executive recruiting firm in order to investigate how ITenabled knowledge networks can impact recruiter performance. They found that (1) recruiters are more productive in the presence of such networks when multitasking levels are not high, and (2) recruiters whose network contacts have heterogeneous knowledge are better able to cope with recruitment project portfolios that are more heterogeneous.

Therefore, while prior work has not examined the impact of multitasking or associated learning in crowdsourcing contest platforms, they provide useful insights on multitasking and learning. These insights form the bases for our theories regarding whether multitasking and its associated learning effects play a role in the eventual quality of tasks in our context.

## Theoretical Foundations and Hypotheses Development

## Competing Tasks and Multitasking

Tasks that open concurrently on crowdsourcing contest platforms target the same set of solvers (Chen et al. 2014) and can be viewed as competing tasks. This is particularly true when the tasks belong to the same category. For example, Tasks A and B in Figure 1 (from the same category, say logo design) compete with each other, as both are open concurrently from $t _ { 2 }$ to $t _ { 3 } \cdot$ —i.e., there is a good chance that solvers logging onto the platform between $t _ { 2 }$ and $t _ { 3 }$ will see both tasks.

When there are competing tasks, solvers may choose to participate in more than one task at a time. Following the definition provided by Delbridge (2001), multitasking in our context would involve a solver making submissions to other tasks while participating in a focal task. We restrict the scope of our definition to submission activities on the platform, and this operationalization is consistent with prior research (Diwas Singh 2014).

We examine the underlying mechanisms governing the impact of competing tasks on task quality by exploring the two paths. We first build the theoretical basis through which competing tasks can influence the number of solvers and, through that, the quality of a focal task. We then develop the theory behind how competing tasks influence multitasking levels and associated learning and thereby the quality of the focal task.

## Path I: Competing Tasks, Number of Solvers, and Task Quality

Most research on crowdsourcing contests considers a single task as the unit of analysis, ignoring the existence and therefore the influence of the multiple concurrent tasks that usually exist on the platform. As already established, tasks compete with each other for resources in any multitasking environment (Payne 1995). Bockstedt et al. (2016) recognize that competing tasks can impact crowdsourcing contests as well, noting that the existence of more competing tasks will likely reduce the attention received by the focal task. In the context of crowdsourcing contest platforms, the most obvious scarce resource is the solver’s time, with solvers usually selecting a few tasks (from the many available) to handle in any particular time period. Therefore, one would expect the number of solvers in each task to decrease as more competing tasks become available.

In their working paper, Chen et al. (2014) observed this in the context of expertise-based contests on the crowdsourcing contest platform TaskCN. While there are some differences between ideation-based contests and expertise-based ones (Terwiesch and Xu 2008), the effects of competing tasks on the number of solvers observed in the context of expertisebased contests will likely hold in our ideation-based context as well. This leads us to propose the following:

## H1: If there are more competing tasks, there will be fewer solvers in the focal task.

The relationship between the number of solvers and task quality has been explored in both offline and online contexts. As discussed earlier, when there are many participants, the increased competition intensity reduces the expected chances of winning for every participant.

This in turn reduces each participant’s incentive to exert effort, i.e., the incentive effect, with the net effect of lowering overall task quality (Taylor 1995; Boudreau et al. 2011). On the other hand, more solvers can increase solution variety, which in turn can have a positive effect on task quality, i.e., the parallel effect (Terwiesch and Xu 2008; Boudreau et al. 2011).

The ultimate effect on task quality is the net result of these two opposing forces. In the context of expertise-based contests, Boudreau et al. (2011) found that the positive impact on task quality from the parallel effect can dominate the negative impact of effort reduction from the incentive effect for more uncertain projects. Ideation-based contests are inherently more uncertain, and this is likely to result in greater variation between the seeker’s preferences and submitted solutions. Therefore, it is likely that the parallel effect will be more important in ideation contests than in expertise-based ones, with a greater number of solvers in a task increasing the chances of the seeker finding at least one acceptable solution. Because we focus on a type of ideation contest (involving logo design tasks), the parallel effect is likely to dominate the incentive effect in our environment, and the overall quality of a task should increase when there are more solvers in it. Therefore, we propose:

H2: If there are more solvers in a task, task quality will increase.

## Path II: Competing Tasks, Multitasking, Learning, and Solution Quality

## Competing Tasks and Multitasking Levels

There are arguments in favor of increased multitasking with an increase in the number of competing tasks in several contexts. Task outcomes are inherently uncertain in many environments. Tasks that look promising initially may eventually fail, while tasks that seem uncertain at first may eventually succeed (Klingebiel and Rammer 2014). Multitasking can provide an effective hedge in the face of uncertainties relating to task success (Scherer and Palazzo 2011). Along the same lines, Girotra et al. (2010) note that people and organizations typically invest in multiple innovation ideas (rather than in just one) to hedge against uncertainties in the quality of ideas.

Similarly, in our context, solvers cannot be certain about their chances of success when participating in crowdsourcing contests. In general, these chances tend to be quite low, given that a large number of solvers compete in a contest, often for a single prize. The difficulty of establishing a solver’s chances of winning is exacerbated by the fact that seeker preferences in ideation tasks like logo design are subjective and difficult to observe in a crowdsourcing contest environment (Bockstedt et al. 2016). Lakhani et al. (2007) and Brabham (2010) note that the primary motivation for solvers participating in crowdsourcing contests is to win. Given the difficulties inherent in estimating the chances of winning and given that losing leaves solvers without any compensation for their invested efforts, solvers could distribute their participation efforts across tasks to reduce uncertainty and to increase their chances of winning. In addition, switching across tasks can enable the application of new ideas generated from one task to another task (Kapadia 2017). In summary, the more tasks that are open simultaneously, the greater is the opportunity to multitask. Therefore, we hypothesize that overall multitasking levels are likely to increase in our context.

H3: If there are more competing tasks, the multitasking levels of solvers in the focal task will increase.

## Learning by Multitasking and Task Quality

The availability of competing tasks provides solvers the opportunity to choose between working on a variety of tasks (i.e., multitasking) or focusing on a single task. Theories on learning recognize focusing on one task as a key driver of learning and productivity in various environments (e.g., manufacturing and software development). Workers who focus on the same task benefit from the advantage of repetition, as they accumulate experience on that specific task; as a result, the effort required to complete a subsequent unit of the task can decline (Narayanan et al. 2009). The lack of distraction also allows workers to gain a deeper understanding of the problem domain (Schilling et al. 2003). Switching across tasks may also impose additional cognitive costs in terms of reacquainting oneself with the requirements of other tasks which could result in decreased productivity (Staats and Gino 2012). A dedicated focus therefore can lead to greater proficiency and, as a result, better performance. On crowdsourcing contest platforms also, solvers can focus exclusively on a single task and hope to improve their performance by making multiple repeated submissions to that task. There is some evidence of this in prior literature on crowdsourcing contests—Bockstedt et al. (2016) find that submitting more solutions to a logo design task can indeed result in better performance.

On the other hand, there is also evidence of benefit from variety—i.e., learning can result from doing something else (Schilling et al. 2003). Variety enables individuals to broaden their knowledge base (Schilling et al. 2003), giving them a better understanding of the broader schema and rules relevant to each of the diverse tasks (Paas and Van Merriboer 1994; Graydon and Griffin 1996; Narayanan et al. 2009). Through such learning (which has been termed explicit learning), the individual can better extract and apply knowledge relevant to the task, and “individuals exposed to a variety of tasks can tackle problems within a single domain more effectively” (Narayanan et al. 2009). There is also evidence that task variety can lead to implicit learning (Reber 1989; Simon 1991;

Wulf and Schmidt 1997)—for example, workers can “store correlations between task requirements in different domains” without realizing it (Narayanan et al. 2009). Along the same lines, variety is known to result in “stimulating insightful synthesis between different problem domains,” and both explicit and implicit learning can lead to benefits across different problem domains (Schilling et al. 2003). In our context, multitaskers switch among different concurrent tasks, and could potentially realize the benefits of task variety. Multitasking allows workers to better identify knowledge relevant to a task and allows them to master and apply such relevant new knowledge (Narayanan et al. 2009). Moreover, switches among concurrent tasks may not be mere changes in activities; rather, they may require changes in cognition (Delbridge 2001; Salvucci et al. 2009). These cognition rotations can stimulate broader associative thinking and enable the application of new ideas generated from other tasks to the focal tasks (Nijstad and Stroebe 2006; Kapadia 2017). Therefore, multitasking solvers could learn and improve their solutions from participating in a variety of concurrent tasks. Ideation tasks like logo design require creation and novelty. As a result, exposure to variety—by doing other tasks—could be particularly useful in this context. Solvers who multitask across logo design tasks—by doing something else and switching back to the focal task—should therefore perform better than those who only do one task. We thus propose that:

H4: Multitasking (i.e., switching between tasks) is more likely to improve a solver’s solution quality in the focal task relative to continuously working on a single task.

If H4 is validated, it would imply that multitasking solvers could find inspiration from other tasks, allowing them to apply new ideas to the focal task. Therefore, solvers are likely to generate better solutions when multitasking than otherwise. Therefore, we expect that if higher levels of multitasking result in better solutions for solvers in general, then the quality of tasks on average is likely to be higher. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H5: The greater the level of multitasking manifest in a focal task, the better the quality of that task will be.

## Task Heterogeneity, Learning, and Task Quality

Prior literature on learning suggests that different levels of variety can result in different levels of learning. For example, Schilling et al. (2003) found that workers can learn more when they do different, but similar tasks than when they focus on the same task, or when they work on dissimilar tasks. Staats and Gino (2012) also note that focusing on similar tasks leads to greater effectiveness, relative to being involved in a broad variety of tasks.

These findings should also apply to multitasking on crowdsourcing contest platforms. Tasks on a platform could be dissimilar in terms of skills required. For example, while software development tasks require programming skills, logo design tasks require artistic skills and knowledge of design tools and usually attract solvers with completely different skill sets. As mentioned earlier, tasks needing similar skill sets usually fall into the same task category. Tasks within a category (e.g., logo design) can also be dissimilar in varying degrees in terms of their requirements—logos intended for business cards, for example, are likely to be simpler than images for t-shirts.

Within a category (e.g., logo design), we expect solvers to gain more from multitasking across tasks with relatively similar requirements (like logos for the subcategory business cards), as working on such tasks concurrently increases familiarity with these types of tasks, and such familiarity with task requirements has a direct impact on their ability to understand, enhance, and modify designs and transfer knowledge from one task to another. This is in line with Chen et al.’s (2014) suggestion that because ideation contests require inspiration, solvers may easily transfer the insights gained from other tasks to a focal task if the tasks have similar requirements.

In contrast, while working on relatively dissimilar tasks can improve a worker’s breadth of knowledge, the associated effect is likely to be lower, as the experiences from dissimilar tasks may not directly apply to the current task. When tasks have different requirements, insights from one task may not apply easily to the focal task. Even if they do, it may be more cumbersome to adapt them. Given that tasks on crowdsourcing contest platforms usually last a few days, it is not going to be easy to adapt designs from dissimilar tasks to the focal task quickly. Therefore, from a knowledge transfer standpoint, solvers are likely to learn less while working on dissimilar tasks than on similar tasks. As a result, they are likely to perform better when multitasking across similar tasks than across dissimilar tasks. Therefore, we propose that:

H6: Multitasking (switching) between similar tasks is more likely to lead to improvements in a solver’s solution quality than multitasking (or switching) between dissimilar tasks.

We expect each solver to improve more by multitasking on similar tasks than on dissimilar tasks. Therefore, solvers who handle a greater proportion of similar tasks are likely to perform better and thus the overall task quality is likely to increase when a greater fraction of solvers works on similar tasks. We, therefore, propose:

H7: The greater the similarity between the focal task and the other tasks handled concurrently by solvers, the better the quality of that task will be.

## Competing Tasks and Overall Task Solution Quality

Ultimately, the impact on seekers depends on the quality of the best solutions received in their tasks. As discussed earlier, the number of competing tasks may affect both the number of solvers and their multitasking levels, thereby impacting task quality.

We theorized earlier (Hypothesis 1) that a greater number of competing tasks may lead to fewer solvers in a focal task. When fewer solvers participate, the parallel effect becomes weaker and solution quality decreases. On the other hand, the incentive effect could lead to increased effort from the solvers and, consequently, higher quality solutions. We also theorized that if there are more competing tasks, the solvers in the focal task are more likely to multitask, which could lead to higher quality solutions due to learning effects.

Given that different effects can impact task quality differently, it is difficult to predict the net impact of competing tasks on task quality. While we hope that our empirical analysis can help improve the understanding of this relationship, there is no existing theory that helps to determine the direction of this joint effect. As our research environment shares some similarities with traditional online auctions, and a negative externality has been identified in that environment, we cautiously predict that the negative externalities from the decreased number of solvers are likely to dominate in our environment as well. Consequently, we propose:

H8: An increase in the number of competing tasks will reduce the overall solution quality of the focal task.

## Data and Measures

## Data

The data for our analysis comes from crowdspring, a large crowdsourcing contest platform. The tasks on the platform are organized into categories (Logo and Identity, Programming, etc.), with tasks needing similar skills belonging to the task category. We collected information on tasks posted between 2008 and 2015 in the logo design category, called “Logo & Identity” on crowdspring. We focused on this category for two main reasons. Not only does it account for more than 90% of all tasks posted on the platform during that period, logo design tasks are more similar to each other in terms of task requirements and workloads relative to other popular crowdsourced tasks, such as programming or writing. Programming tasks may require knowledge of different programming languages and can be quite varied in terms of requirements—while some projects may be very simple (like the design of a webpage), others may be fairly complicated (such as the development of a whole website). Tasks that require different skills and effort are unlikely to be competing tasks, as they are likely to be targeting different sets of solvers. This makes it harder to identify competing tasks within such task categories. Logo design tasks, on the other hand, tend to have comparable requirements.

As discussed earlier, tasks within a category can also be dissimilar to some degree in terms of their requirements—e.g., requirements for logos intended for business cards are likely to be simpler than images for t-shirts. The platform had 11 subcategories (Book-Cover, Clothing, Illustration, Business Card, Letterhead and Stationery, Logo and Stationery, Logo Design, Package, Presentation, Print Design, Stationery) within the “Logo & Identity” category at the time the data was collected, and our dataset includes every subcategory.

There were 18,083 tasks and 43,673 solvers in total over the eight-year period. The dataset includes information along three main dimensions—tasks, submissions, and solvers. For each task, we obtained data on task characteristics (e.g., prize, duration, and number of awards). Most tasks were open for a duration between 1 and 15 days, with tasks lasting 10.71 days on average. Tasks attracted an average of 30.95 solvers and 98.51 solutions. crowdspring allows seekers to choose multiple winners. On average, the number of awards per task was 1.04, while the average total prize amount was \$301.55.

We also obtained detailed data on all submissions to every task, including the solvers associated with each solution, the exact time a solver submitted a solution to a task, and the quality rating scores given to the solutions by the seekers. Our data allowed us to identify the submission sequences for each solver across tasks, which we used to gain insights into multitasking behavior. We were able to observe whether a solver continuously submitted solutions to one task without making submissions to others or not. We also collected information on solvers, including the time each solver registered with the platform as well as past participation and winning histories. On average, solvers participated in 14.34 tasks.

## Measures

## Main Variables

Task quality: Seekers evaluate solution quality by providing scores. TaskQuality is the highest score across all submissions the task has received. Task quality reflects the subjective preferences of seekers, as the seekers rate the solvers, and the solvers revise their solutions based on the seeker’s rating (and preferences). Therefore, it may also be viewed as a measure of seeker satisfaction.

Number of solvers: All solvers who have made at least one submission to the focal task is counted as a participant (NumberSolvers).

Competing tasks: Conceptually, a task that is open at any point of time the focal task is open may be regarded as a competing task. In reality, however, it is very difficult for solvers to examine all open tasks, as they are usually displayed across several pages. Chilton et al. (2010) observed that solvers usually browse only the first few of these pages. Therefore, tasks displayed on the first few pages are more likely to be considered by a solver at a point in time. We consider this abbreviated search behavior when constructing competing tasks. Rather than considering every open task that shares some time window with the focal task, we consider tasks posted within five days of the focal task (i.e., ±5 days of the posting time of the focal task) as a proxy for competing tasks. Some of these tasks only have a small overlap with the focal task (i.e., less than a day); these are either tasks that are about to end when the focal task is posted or tasks that get posted close to when the focal task is about to end. These tasks are unlikely to compete directly with the focal task and we therefore excluded them from the set of competing tasks. The variable associated with the number of competing tasks is ????????????????. We also considered two other ways to operationalize this variable. First, we considered an extreme case where only tasks posted on the same day as the focal task were considered competing tasks. Next, we considered all tasks that overlapped at least one day with the focal task as competing tasks. We used the number of tasks posted within five days as the primary measure for competing tasks in all the analyses, with the other two measures being used for robustness checks.

Multitasking level: The multitasking level of each solver in the focal task was obtained by counting the number of competing tasks (in addition to the focal task) the solver handles while participating in the focal task (????????????????????????????????????). For example, a solver who participates in three competing tasks, including the focal task, would have a multitasking level of two. We represent the overall multitasking level (MTLevel) in a focal task as the average number of multitasking levels across all the participants in the task.

Multitasking similarity: This was determined by first computing for each participating solver, the proportion of multitasked tasks that are from the same subcategory as the focal task. We then averaged this proportion over all the solvers participating in the focal task (RatioMT\_SameSubCategory).

## Overall Effect of Competing Tasks on Task Quality

So far, our analyses have shown how competing tasks influence task quality in crowdsourcing contests through either the number of participating solvers or their multitasking behaviors. However, they have not shown the overall effect of competing tasks on task quality—that would depend on which of the two opposing forces dominates the other. In particular, it would be important to see whether the existence of competing tasks adversely affects task quality. To answer these questions, we examined the relationships between competing tasks and overall task quality through the model specified in Equation (4) below (Model 4).

$$

$$

As mentioned earlier, the number of competing tasks available in a time period may not be exogenous, as they may be influenced by other variables such as the number of highquality tasks in the previous period $( N u \ r { m } b e r  u a l i f i e d T a s k s _ { \mathrm { { j , t - 1 } } } )$ , and the number of highability solvers $( N u m b e r H i g h A b i l i t y S o l v e r s _ { \mathrm { j , t - 1 } } )$ active on the platform in the previous period. We also considered several alternative measures and model specifications to assess the robustness of the main results of this section. First, we considered the alternative characterizations of competing tasks. The results are shown in Columns C2 and C3 of Table 6. This indicates that the number of competing tasks does not have a significant relationship with the overall quality of solutions in a task. That is, H8 is not supported. This is somewhat surprising, as prior studies suggest that a negative externality exists in the context of competing traditional auctions, i.e., when there are more competing auctions, bidders cross-bid and reduce prices. In contrast, our findings indicate that competing tasks do not impose a negative externality in an ideation contest environment. This is because the two opposing forces—the negative parallel effects due to fewer solvers, and the positive learning effects due to increased multitasking—offset each other and do not lead to significant changes in task quality.

## Additional Evidence on Underlying Mechanisms

The analyses thus far attest to the robustness of identifying the mechanism through which competing tasks affect task quality. In this section, we conduct additional analyses (1) to provide more evidence on the existence of multitasking effects and (2) further support our findings by ruling out other possible explanations.

## Multitasking and Winning Probability

## Ruling Out Effects of Off-Platform Activities

Tasks usually last several days, and solvers could be involved in off-platform activities (such as doing routine jobs or participating in tasks on other platforms) while participating in these tasks. As clarified earlier, we stayed consistent with the definition of multitasking in previous literature and did not consider activities outside of the focal platform. However, involvement in these activities may influence a solver’s solution quality in the focal task. One potential concern is that such activities are not fully captured in our main models, which may dilute the validity of our findings. To mitigate this concern, we investigate how offplatform activities may influence our findings. If the effects for solvers without off-platform activities are consistent with the effects identified earlier, it will alleviate concerns caused by potential out-of-platform activities.

In order to do this, we first identified solvers who were unlikely to engage in off-platform activities during the course of designing solutions for a focal task on this platform. Such solvers were identified based on the interval between their submissions. Our dataset contains the submission times associated with each submission and we used this information to identify solvers who were unlikely to be involved in out-of-platform activities while working on the focal task. If a solver kept making submissions on a platform without long gaps, we determined that the solver was unlikely to be involved in off-platform activities. Therefore, we considered two characteristics—the interval between any two consecutive submissions (Time Interval), and the total time spent working on the focal task (Time Span).

Time interval: We identified all submissions made on the platform (including submissions to the focal and other tasks on the platform) within the chosen time span. We checked the gap in time between every pair of consecutive submissions. If the gaps between all consecutive submissions on the platform were small, we considered the solver unlikely to be involved in other time-consuming things while participating in the focal task. Based on our dataset, 10% of tasks received their first submissions within 50 minutes, and 50% of tasks received their first submissions within 267 minutes. Therefore, we conservatively estimated that designing a solution requires at least 40 minutes.

Time span: For each task and each participant in the task, we calculated the difference in time between the first and last submissions to the focal task. It is possible that solvers with long time spans (e.g., 48 hours) between submissions were involved in off-platform activities. Therefore, we restricted the time span to a narrow range in order to ensure a low likelihood that the solvers would have been involved in other activities. On average, solvers submitted four solutions per task. Given that it may take 40 minutes to design one solution, a solver needs to have about 320 minutes (\~ 2×4×40) for multitasking across two tasks. We conservatively rounded this up to six hours. Based on this, we specified two conditions: (1) the interval between any two consecutive two submissions is, at most, 40 minutes, and (2) the total time span is, at most, six hours. We then identified all solvers who e met these conditions for each focal task.

## Conclusion

The proliferation of crowdsourcing contests on a platform can be overwhelming, making it important to understand how tasks that compete for the same resources influence each other. However, research on competing tasks in crowdsourcing contests remains limited. Existing research mostly focuses on single tasks without consideration of other tasks that might exist concurrently. This is a limiting assumption, as many tasks are available at the same time on crowdsourcing contest platforms. In this paper, we explore the effects of competing tasks on task quality in crowdsourcing contests, focusing on ideation tasks, and reveal mechanisms governing the effects of competing tasks on task quality. We leveraged a unique dataset that contains detailed information on solver submission behavior to understand multitasking behavior, and conducted analyses at the task, solver, and submission levels. The ability to conduct analyses at different levels enabled us to examine and understand the impact of competing tasks more comprehensively. Through these analyses, we identified how competing tasks influence solution quality.

By deepening the understanding of the effects of competing tasks on task quality, our research makes several contributions to the theory and literature on crowdsourcing contests. We find that an increase in the number of competing tasks does not affect the overall quality of solutions received by a task. At first glance, it would appear surprising that competing tasks do not influence task quality adversely. However, our findings indicate that there are two opposing effects that offset each other, resulting in the net effect being insignificant. As expected, we find that more competing tasks reduces the number of solvers; we also find that this reduces solution quality through the parallel effect. On the other hand, our results indicate that the presence of more competing tasks leads solvers to multitask more. It turns out that solvers generate solutions of higher quality in the process, as they learn from the various concurrent tasks they handle. Our findings show that the improvement in solution quality is greater when solvers multitask across tasks that are relatively similar, suggesting that solvers learn more from multitasking across similar tasks than from multitasking across dissimilar ones. Overall, we conclude that the positive effects of multitasking are high enough to offset the negative parallel effects, leading to no discernible deterioration in task quality.

These findings add to the limited literature on the effects of competing tasks in crowdsourcing contests. In addition, they extend our understanding of solver participation behavior when multiple concurrent tasks exist. Our study also integrates learning theory with multitasking and provides insights on learning transfer through multitasking, especially among tasks with varying similarity levels.

From a practical standpoint, this study provides several insights for practitioners managing contests. From a seeker’s perspective, while the intensity of competing tasks does reduce the number of solvers participating in a task, it does not hurt the quality of the best solutions. Therefore, seekers need not avoid posting tasks when there are several competing tasks on the platform. From the platform’s perspective, platforms should encourage the availability of multiple tasks at the same time and could further encourage solvers to move freely across tasks. It may be also in the best interest of platforms to assist solvers in their search for suitable tasks. On most platforms, tasks are usually displayed by either prize amount or task posting time. There are limited options for solvers to sort or filter tasks and the process of comparing and selecting tasks is not easy. Platforms could provide more display options to ease the task selection process and thereby facilitate multitasking. From the solvers’ point of view, our results imply that solvers who multitask should continue to do so. Multitasking is an effective way for them to improve the quality of their solutions, with this being particularly true for solvers multitasking across similar tasks.

While our data is quite rich and we have conducted various robustness checks and finer level analyses to rule out alternative explanations, we were unable to completely capture all factors that could potentially be influential. For instance, although crowdspring is designed for participation by individual solvers, it cannot prevent private collaboration among solvers who share the same account. Future research seeking to identify such behavior would be interesting.

We examined the problem from the perspective of the seeker in order to understand how competing tasks influence task quality. However, our findings regarding multitasking behaviors open the door for future research from the solvers’ perspective—for instance, identifying the number of tasks and the specific type of tasks a solver should handle simultaneously. It would be also interesting to examine how multitasking affects the solver’s performance across the entire portfolio of tasks that a solver worked on in parallel. Another interesting research direction would be to examine how solvers strategically switch among tasks based on solver feedback.
