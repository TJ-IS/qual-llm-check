# Collaborative e-Government: impediments and benefits of information-sharing projects in the public sector

J. Ramon Gil-Garcia<sup>1</sup>, InduShobha Chengalur-Smith<sup>2</sup> and Peter Duchessi<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Information-sharing projects are becoming increasingly important in both public and private organizations. Generally, organizations base their decision to move forward with an information-sharing project on the project’s expected benefits such as better services, operational savings, and increased program effectiveness. Clearly, potential impediments particular to the government environment could limit the attainment of benefits, jeopardizing the project’s business case and even the project itself. Yet, we know very little about how users’ perceived impediments affect a project’s expected benefits in collaborative digital government initiatives. Using data from six public sector information-sharing projects, this paper reveals how some managerial and cultural impediments (e.g., control-oriented management) limit perceptions of expected benefits. Surprisingly, political impediments were not found to significantly affect the expectations of benefits. Additionally, the paper provides managers with specific advice on how to better understand users’ perceptions and expectations. The paper should be of interest to both academicians and practitioners who are interested in government information technology implementation, in general, and collaborative e-Government, in particular. ## Introduction

Information technology (IT) is potentially capable of changing government organizational structures and business processes and, if implemented correctly, of producing substantial organizational, technical, and business benefits (Heeks, 1999; Kraemer & King, 2003). Information sharing is a relatively new type of IT initiative; it involves building systems, instituting formal standards, and changing business processes to allow organizations to share data and information with many other organizations (Caffrey, 1998; Dawes & Prefontaine, 2003; Gil-Garcia et al., 2005). Government information sharing offers a real opportunity to share databases and make decisions based on more complete information (Dawes, 1996).

Information-sharing projects benefit both public and private concerns. For example, in the criminal justice system, the courts, law enforcement agencies, and corrections offices can share evidence and criminal histories to better coordinate their efforts and improve their performance and accountability (Cresswell & Connelly, 1999). In modern supply chains, companies can share forecasts, inventory, and item movement data to attain several benefits, including reduced inventory levels, reduced order variability, and improved capacity utilization (Clark & Hammond, 1997).

Organizations that embrace an information-sharing project consider several factors. For example, organizations working on electronic data interchange, which involves the exchange of typical business data (e.g., purchase orders), contemplate organizational readiness, system complexity, and expected application benefits before committing to an implementation (Chwelos et al., 2001). Generally, organizations base their decision to move forward with an information-sharing project on a business case that incorporates expected benefits and all relevant costs, including development, installation, and recurring costs (Dawes et al., 2004; Irani et al., 2005; Cresswell et al., 2006). Additionally, they consider potential impediments, including technological, organizational, political, and legal barriers, which could limit the attainment of benefits or completely derail an information-sharing project (Dawes, 1996; Caffrey, 1998; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005).

The objective of this paper is to analyze how perceived impediments to government information-sharing projects affect expected benefits, while controlling for user experience. By relating perceived impediments to expected benefits, this research directly extends previous work on factors that affect net benefits (DeLone & McLean, 1992, 2002; Seddon, 1997). Moreover, by controlling for user experience, this research contributes to a better understanding of the direct effect of perceived impediments on expected benefits.

The following section presents the literature on expected benefits of and perceived impediments to IT projects in general and information-sharing projects in particular. We do this to explicate some of the pertinent impediments and expected benefits that researchers have considered. Next, the paper describes the pertinent research method, focusing on the research model, sample, questionnaire, and measurement issues. Then, the paper describes the analysis approach and presents the results of the empirical analyses. Finally, the paper discusses the implications of our findings, points out several shortcomings of the analysis, and provides some directions for future research.

## Literature review

IT projects have both negative and positive consequences; the term ‘expectations of net benefits’ (e.g., Seddon, 1997) subsumes both of those results. This research separates net benefits into ‘expected benefits,’ which represent positive consequences, and ‘perceived impediments,’ which represent negative consequences (or existing conditions) and focuses on users’ perceptions of impediments and expectations of benefits. Authors from different disciplines argue that decision makers, when evaluating the feasibility of IT and other decisions, consider impediments that may limit the attainment of expected benefits (Giddens, 1979; Simon, 1986; North,

1990; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Boisot & Child, 1999; Fountain, 2001). Additionally, Chan et al. (1997) discuss the difficulty organizations have in identifying and agreeing on the contributions that information systems make because of differences in users’ perceptions about those contributions. Robey & Boudreau (1999) also acknowledge the importance of understanding different interests and perceptions when studying the role of IT in organizational transformation.

One influential model for understanding IT projects incorporates ‘intention to use’ and ‘user satisfaction’ concepts (DeLone & McLean, 1992, 2003). To augment this model, some authors include a behavioral component, ‘expectations of net benefits,’ hypothesizing that expected benefits affect the consideration, use, and ultimate success of an IT application (Seddon, 1997). These behavioral models are similar to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which also uses expectations, or perceptions, to predict future IT use (Davis, 1989).

Seddon (1997) suggests that the DeLone & McLean (1992) model of IS success has several limitations; for example, the ambiguous meaning of IS use. To clarify the confusion, Seddon (1997) proposes the inclusion of a partial behavioral model. Seddon’s (1997) partial behavioral model of IS use is consistent with previous work about expectations-based frameworks to predict future IS use (Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Adams et al., 1992; Segars & Grover, 1993; Thompson et al., 1994; Taylor & Todd, 1995). The partial behavioral model states that expectations about the net benefits of future IS use affect IS use and that net benefits incorporate both expected costs and benefits. In practice, organizations incur significant costs to overcome impediments (e.g., organizational resistance to changes). Consequently, we examine the relationship between perceived impediments and expectations of benefits, expanding Seddon’s (1997) partial behavioral model. In addition, expectations about the benefits of future IS use are significantly affected by the impediments perceived in the organizational and institutional environment in which a project is embedded and these perceptions vary among different stakeholders (Chan et al., 1997; Seddon, 1997; Robey & Boudreau, 1999). Below, we describe some of these expected benefits and perceived impediments in the context of information-sharing initiatives.

## Expected benefits

According to the extant literature, successful information-sharing projects produce several benefits, such as increased productivity, improved decision making, reduced costs, increased revenues, and integrated services (Benjamin et al., 1984; Jenster, 1987; Caffrey, 1998; Dawes & Pardo, 2002; Rolda´n & Leal, 2003; Gil-Garcia & Helbig, 2006). For improved clarity, we classify benefits of information-sharing projects into three categories, namely technical, organizational, and political (Dawes, 1996).

Technical benefits pertain to data processing and information management. Information sharing reduces duplicate data collection, processing, and storage activities, which mitigate the administrative and data processing costs that attend every public program (Caffrey, 1998). Additionally, information-sharing projects promote the development of formal standards and formation of shared technical resources (Dawes, 1996).

Organizational benefits relate to the solution of organization-wide problems or the enhancement of organizational capabilities (Lee, 2001). Improved decision-making processes, enhanced professional networks, better control and coordination of the organization, high quality services, and reduced costs are typical benefits that organizations derive from information-sharing projects (Walton, 1989; Andersen & Dawes, 1991; Caffrey, 1998; Kuan & Chau, 2001; Dawes & Pardo, 2002; Rolda´n & Leal, 2003; Carter & Belanger, 2005; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005).

Political benefits may deal with the enhanced public image or value created for public concerns by information-sharing projects (Dawes, 1996). Enhanced dissemination of government-wide policy goals, increased public accountability, more comprehensive public information, integrated planning, and improved service delivery are other examples of political benefits for public sector organizations (Andersen & Dawes, 1991; Carter & Belanger, 2005). Interestingly, enhanced political image and other individual goals can accrue to public officials as a result of the above benefits (Kraemer et al., 1989; Fountain, 2001).

## Perceived impediments

IT impediments can be grouped into technological, organizational, political, and legal categories (Gil-Garcia & Pardo, 2005). Technical impediments cover problems associated with hardware, software, and/or communications networks. Perceived hardware and software complexity and incompatibility of system components are often cited impediments to IT implementation (Dawes, 1996; Caffrey, 1998; Chengalur-Smith & Duchessi, 1999; Chang et al., 2001; Dawes & Pardo, 2002). Additionally, mismatched data structures, incompatible database designs, and incongruous data and information distribution channels present problems in information-sharing initiatives (Barret & Greene, 2001; Ambite et al., 2002; Umble et al., 2003). Even when the involved organizations use the same kind of data, conflicting data definitions and different terminology will limit the extent of data and information sharing (Dawes, 1996; Ambite et al., 2002). These perceived impediments may reduce the expectation of benefits. Conversely, technical deficiencies may provide the impetus for organizations to move forward with information-sharing projects, primarily to ameliorate or eliminate those deficiencies and achieve expected benefits. Consequently, perceived technical impediments may increase the expectation of benefits.

Organizational impediments relate to individuals, the organization as a whole, and characteristics of the implementation, including length of the project, understanding of strategic goals, extent of change in business processes, project management approach, and lack of implementation guidelines (Chang et al., 2001; Tan & Pan, 2003; Umble et al., 2003; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005). At the level of individual contributors, the impediments often encountered include resistance to change, different individual agendas and goals, misinterpretation of shared information, and misuse of shared information (Best, 1997; Bradshaw-Lynn, 2000; Du Plooy, 2003; Kolekofski & Heminger, 2003). Obviously, as perceived resistance to change and the perceived inability of users to interpret and use the data (in a meaningful way) increases, organizations will expect to receive fewer benefits. Organization-wide impediments are due to explicit and implicit differences among the organizations participating in an information-sharing project. They include misaligned organizational missions; overly ambitious and conflicting organizational goals; lack of understanding and even respect among the organizations; significant diversity in cultures, structures, and philosophies; and conflicting organizational priorities (Chang et al., 2001; Faerman et al., 2001; Dawes & Pardo, 2002; Zhang et al., 2002). As perceptions of these impediments increase, the participating organizations discount the chance of attaining the expected benefits. Impediments that are specific to the implementation relate to perceptions that individuals and organizations have about an IT project. They include lack of funding for the project; mismanagement or misallocation of funds to project activities; limited (or no) access to implementation models and/or guidelines; aggressive time frames for the implementation; and lengthy time frame for manifestation of organizational benefits (Best, 1997; Caffrey, 1998; Umble et al., 2003; Gil-Garcia & Pardo, 2005; Moon & Norris, 2005). Organizations that perceive a project to be under funded and mismanaged from the beginning will expect few benefits after the project. This perception is heightened, especially when the timeframe is believed to be unrealistically short and there are few guidelines or models during the implementation.

Political and legal factors play an important role, especially in public organizations (Fountain, 2001; Gil-Garcia & Pardo, 2005; Irani et al., 2005; Madon, 2005). McCaffrey et al. (1995) contend that political principles become barriers to participative systems because they are considered to have higher value than the potential benefits from such systems. Some examples of political and legal barriers in an information-sharing project are: restrictive laws and regulations (e.g., civil service regulations) that constrain activities that may be important to the project; lack of executive and legislative support for an information-sharing project; and the requirement to assure confidentiality of important data and information (Walton, 1989; Hammitt, 2000; Harris, 2000; Chang et al., 2001). For example, the power of agency discretion refers to the capacity of bureaucrats to influence programs and policies (Crozier, 1964; Crozier & Friedberg, 1980; Derthick, 1990). This power can significantly affect an information-sharing project, especially if executive and legislative support are partial at the beginning of a project or wane as the project is underway. Obviously, the perception of significant political and legal barriers will limit the attainment of expected benefits.

## Individual experience

Individuals’ perceptions about expected benefits may be shaped by prior experiences. For example, Venkatesh (1999) finds that users who have a positive training experience perceive an IT system to be ‘easy to use.’ Conversely, another study finds that neither training nor an individual’s tenure in the workforce affects beliefs about ‘ease of use’ (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999). However, they find that an individual’s level of education and any prior, similar experiences have a positive effect on perceived ‘ease of use’ and indirectly on perceived ‘usefulness.’ Another study finds that users’ characteristics (e.g., innovativeness) and perceptions about an IT system affect their decision to adopt an IT innovation (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998). Nelson (1990) also suggests that users’ characteristics affect IT acceptance. Seddon et al. (1999) find that stakeholders’ interests and perceptions may change depending on their previous experience. Thus, prior work experience and experience with information-sharing projects may alter expectations of benefits. In addition, Bobrowski & Bretschneider (1994) establish that internal and external relationships have an impact on the adoption of new technologies. Their findings can be extended to individual perceptions. Therefore, expected benefits are affected by working relationships among project participants. Thus, users’ backgrounds and pre-existing relationships can impact their expectations about IT and its associated benefits.

## Research design and method

## Research model

The current research model is based on the above-cited research about perceived impediments, expected benefits, and user experience. This model relates specific individual perceived impediments and user experience items to particular expected benefits (see Figure 1). As explained earlier, the model extends the work of DeLone & McLean (1992) and Seddon (1997) and incorporates the literature on decision-making (Giddens, 1979; Simon, 1986; North, 1990; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Boisot & Child, 1999; Fountain, 2001) and IT benefits and barriers in both public and private organizations. This item-level approach increases the granularity of our analyses and allows us to state how specific perceived impediments affect distinct expected benefits. In our model perceived impediments could reduce expected benefits, or conversely, expected benefits could provide the motivation to overcome perceived impediments. Thus, a priori, we cannot state the nature of the effect. Our alternative hypothesis is that perceived impediments just affect expected benefits; via the analysis, we discover the nature of the effect, if it exists. Additionally, an informationsharing project involves multiple users who could have different backgrounds, work experiences, and roles, which may affect their perceptions of benefits. Thus, any effort at relating perceived impediments to expected benefits needs to incorporate the users’ experience base, as a control. Based on the prior literature review, the existing model incorporates several experience items, namely general administrative experience, prior information-sharing project experience, and pre-existing relationships, and relates them directly to expected benefits. In summary, the existing research model relates specific perceived impediments, the independent variables, to distinct expected benefits, the dependent variables. Thus, the model aims to better understand the relationship between perceived impediments and expected benefits, adjusting for the effects of user experience.

Previous studies use either structural equation modeling (SEM) or multiple regression analysis to understand the relationships between a benefits construct and one, or more, other constructs. These studies are useful in the analysis of overarching relationships between abstract constructs, but produce limited insights into the relevance of and relationships between distinct items – or variables – limiting the applicability of results in real-life situations. Because our main intention is to examine and comment on specific items rather than more abstract constructs, we do not employ SEM. (Thus, we do not create and relate expected benefits and perceived impediments constructs here.) We choose canonical correlation analysis because it relates multiple dependent items to multiple independent items. Multiple regression analysis would allow a similar level of granularity as canonical correlation analysis, but multiple regression analysis prefers to have the explanatory variables be independent from each other. In addition, multiple regression analysis is appropriate for modeling one-tomany relationships (i.e., one dependent variable to many independent variables), whereas canonical correlation is appropriate for modeling many-to-many relationships (many dependent variables to many independent variables), which we have here.

## Sample

The present study arises from a four-year research project launched in 1999 by the Center for Technology and Government (CTG) at the University at Albany, SUNY. CTG selected six information-sharing projects at several different State government agencies with the following characteristics. Besides being projects whose purpose was to share information and knowledge, they were all initiated by a single state agency and with some degree of participation from other state agencies, local governments, nonprofits, and private sector organizations. The membership in the projects was voluntary and the structures were not formal (see Zhang et al., 2002, 2005). The information-sharing projects were diverse, ranging from the Central Accounting System, a centralized accounting system, which involves all state agencies, local governments, and many nonprofit service agencies, to the Council on Children and Families’ repository, which stores statistical indicator data about the health and well being of children.

Because CTG has a unique project-based relationship with State government agencies, CTG staff has exceptional access to those agencies, staff, and associated partners. CTG distributed the questionnaire to 478 managers and users that participated in the six information-sharing projects. Thus, CTG used a purposively selected sample with the unit of analysis being individual participants in information-sharing projects. To ensure a high rate of return, CTG distributed the questionnaires at CTG project events. The response rate was 100%.

## Questionnaire development and measurement

CTG staff generated the items from available literature on information-sharing and inter-organizational collaboration (Hosmer, 1995; McCaffrey et al., 1995; Dawes, 1996; Landsbergen & Wolken, 1998; Faerman et al., 2001; Landsbergen & Wolken, 2001; Dawes & Pardo, 2002).

CTG also appealed to its own internal expertise, which is considerable given the large number of IT projects in which CTG has participated. In addition, key members of the research team were already working in partnership with public managers on similar topics. All of these activities ensured content validity for a questionnaire designed to assess professional characteristics, prior experiences, other user attributes, expected benefits, and perceived impediments as they relate to information-sharing initiatives.

The questionnaire measures all expected benefits and perceived impediments with a 7-point Likert scale. Concerning expected benefits, 1 equals ‘not at all likely to be achieved’ and 7 equals ‘very likely to be achieved.’ With regard to perceived impediments, 1 equals ‘not a barrier’ and 7 equals ‘a severe barrier.’ Checklists capture the nature of the organization (e.g., nonprofit organization), nature of work experience, degree of administrative experience, degree of information-sharing project experience, and nature of working relationships. Because the latter three variables represent various contextual experiences (e.g., administrative experience), they appear as dichotomous measures in subsequent analyses. For example, to determine the impact of information-sharing project experience on expected benefits, ‘my first experience’ receives a code of 0, while the other categories receive a code of 1.

## Analysis and results

## Analysis approach

Canonical correlation is especially appropriate when there are multiple variables and the focus is on relating a set of dependent variables (i.e., expected benefits items) to a set of independent variables (i.e., perceived impediments and experience items) (Hair et al., 1998). The analysis includes a set of dummy variables, namely general administrative experience, prior informationsharing project experience, and existence of working relationships, for the various contexts considered. Concerning the dummy variables, we used a full factorial design to account for any possible interactions among them. (We do not permit interactions between the impediment and dummy variables due to the overwhelming number of interaction terms that would result. Thus, the experience variables are not moderating variables.)

Canonical correlation analysis creates linear composites from the data for both sets of dependent and independent variables. Each linear composite is a canonical variate and a pair of variates constitutes a canonical function. The canonical correlation coefficient measures the strength of the relationship between the dependent and independent variates in a function. The canonical R<sup>2</sup>, or squared canonical correlation, is an alternative measure of explained variance. Successive canonical functions are based on residual variances and so the canonical correlation coefficients become smaller. The redundancy index is used to measure how much variance in one set of variables is shared by the variability in the other set. The redundancy index of the dependent variate represents the amount of variance in the dependent variables (expected benefits) that can be explained by the independent canonical variate (Hair et al., 1998).

Canonical correlation analysis also shows the loadings and cross loadings for each variable. A variable’s canonical loading measures the correlation of the variable with its canonical variate (much like a variable loading in factor analysis), while a variable’s cross loading measures the correlation between the variable and paired variate. For example, the canonical cross loading of an independent variable measures the correlation between the variable and the corresponding dependent variate.

## Sample profile

The average age of the respondents is 47 years, suggesting considerable amount of work experience (see Table 1). The respondents include slightly more males than females. Just over 40% of the respondents are currently working for a State agency, 48% for a local government, and 11% for nonprofit or private organizations. A little less than 45% of the respondents are general administrators, while the rest are program, technical, and service delivery employees. Interestingly, many of the respondents have previous experience in information-sharing projects: 63% of the respondents had been in at least one information-sharing project prior to the current project. A large majority (82%) of the respondents have positive relationships with their counterparts in the organizations involved with the information-sharing project.

## Perceived impediments, experience, and expected benefits

The magnitudes of the canonical correlation coefficient, canonical $R ^ { 2 } ,$ , and redundancy index for each pair of linear composites, or canonical function, reveal the strength of association between expected benefits and perceived impediments. The maximum number of canonical functions is 13 because there are 13 dependent variables, or expected benefits. Initially, the first two canonical functions appear to be significant, with canonical correlation coefficients of 0.652 and 0.633, respectively $( P { \leqslant } 0 . 0 5 )$ (see Table 3). The magnitudes of the canonical $R ^ { 2 } s$ and redundancy indices for these functions reveal that only the first canonical function has practical value. The function’s perceived impediments explain 18.4% of the variation in its expected benefits, with the remaining functions contributing almost nothing (p3%) to the residual amount of explained variation (see Table 4). For this canonical function, the canonical loadings, which indicate the relative importance of each variable, and the cross loadings, which indicate the relevant relationships, suggest that all expected benefit variables and several of the perceived impediment variables merit further discussion.

As demonstrated by the high canonical loadings, which range from 0.431 to 0.760, for the expected benefit variables, users expect to receive numerous diverse benefits from information-sharing projects (see Table 5). The most important expected benefits are technical in nature, including reduced duplicate data handling efforts, reduced duplicate data collection efforts, and shared information infrastructure. Users also expect improved program decisions and accountability. Overall, users expect a range of benefits from technical benefits through administrative, service, and program benefits to cost efficiency, service effectiveness, and improved professional networks benefits.

## Discussion and implications

## Expectation of benefits

The results reveal that the perception of a controlledoriented management style limits the expectation of benefits. A control-oriented management can inadvertently create an environment that leaves project personnel and involved users with the feeling that their decisions are second-guessed, actions are delayed, and individual initiative is not valued. This type of environment will severely limit the expectation of benefits and even their actual attainment.

Additionally, lack of agreement on goals can limit the expectation of benefits. When there is little or no agreement on project goals, there are several negative consequences: management fails to articulate goals; goals constantly change; and/or goals are in conflict across the organizations involved with the information-sharing project. Projects without clear and consistent goals lack direction and coherence, and are difficult to control; they are essentially rudderless. It becomes impossible for managers, users, and technical personnel to attain expected benefits when the targets keep changing. Not surprisingly, this situation can severally limit the expectation of benefits.

Finally, the results suggest that the perception of management impatience can limit the expectation of benefits. Projects that fail to show benefits early on in the life cycle lose senior management’s attention and support, either because senior management moves on to a new, higher priority and/or it considers the project of limited success. Information-sharing projects are complex, involve several organizations, and result in new roles and responsibilities that take time to learn and execute. Project personnel and involved users may have to manage senior management’s expectations, keeping expectations in line with reality.

Clearly, in the public sector, senior management has a huge impact on the ultimate success of an informationsharing project. When users perceive that senior management cannot articulate clear and consistent goals, is impatient, and control-oriented, their expectations of benefits are lowered. This situation is detrimental to the ultimate success of any IT project, especially when these perceptions occur both before and during the course of the project.

The MANOVA include interactions among the various experience dimensions via a full factorial design. Concerning perceived impediments, the results reveal that there are no differences in perception of impediments across various types and levels of experience. Controlling the level of significance (Pp0.004) for the number of tests performed, subsequent t-tests reveal that the only significant difference between users with administrative experience and without it is in the area of accountability. Apparently, users with more administrative experience have a higher expectation of achieving more accountability with an information-sharing project versus users with no administrative experience. This is not surprising, considering that administrative personnel and managers place a high value on accountability, particularly in government settings.

The above findings on expectations and perceptions provide evidence to support the relationship between perceived impediments and expected benefits. Specifically, our findings indicate that perceived impediments could significantly detract from expected benefits and, therefore, have an impact on net benefits. This suggests that the ‘expectations of net benefits’ component, which is a single variable in Seddons’ model (1997), may be better understood by differentiating between ‘expected benefits’ and ‘perceived impediments’ and by explicitly including these components and their interrelationships in IT success models, such as those proposed by DeLone & McLean (1992, 2003). Additionally, in Seddon’s (1997) model, only ‘user satisfaction’ affects the ‘expectations of net benefits’ component. Our findings demonstrate that previous experience has some impact on ‘expected benefits,’ suggesting that experiential components should be included in future IT success models. In summary, our findings support the elaboration and extension of Seddon’s (1997) model, by incorporating perceived impediments and user experience as antecedents of expected benefits.

## Management directives

Based on the above findings, the implications for executive, functional, and IT managers that are involved with public sector information-sharing projects are straightforward and appear as a set of directives below:

 avoid a control-oriented management style,

 set clear project goals,

 keep expectations real.

For expensive and highly visible information-sharing projects, public sector managers have to resist controloriented micro management at the lower organizational levels, or risk creating an environment that depresses expectations of benefits and even their attainment. As information is power, information-sharing projects have the potential to level the playing field for the users (Best, 1997; Bellamy, 2000; Fountain, 2001; Gil-Garcia et al., 2005). However, control-oriented management could send the wrong signal and derail the project by deterring future participation. In collaborative digital government projects, hierarchy and authority are not always clear (Fountain, 2001; Dawes & Pardo, 2002). Therefore, managers should understand these differences and apply a more participatory and flexible management approach.

Public sector managers have to create clear and consistent project goals (Best, 1997; Brown, 2000; Dawes & Pardo, 2002; Garson, 2003; Gil-Garcia & Pardo, 2005). Clear, so that personnel understand the purpose of the project and its designated targets. Consistent, so that competing and possibly conflicting operations are not perceived early on or do not develop across organizations at some later point. Without this approach, managers should not be surprised that users have reduced expectations of benefits and even fail to attain those benefits. This is particularly important for collaborative digital government due to the number and heterogeneity of participating organizations (Caffrey, 1998; Dawes & Pardo, 2002).

Finally, there is a need for public sector and IT managers to control users’ expectations, especially senior management’s expectations. As demonstrated, the perception of management impatience can limit the expectation of benefits and even their attainment. Similar to other IT initiatives, without perceived management leadership and support, the benefits of informationsharing projects are suspect (Jiang et al., 1996; Brown, 2000; Landsbergen & Wolken, 2001; Edmiston, 2003; Garson, 2003; Mahler & Regan, 2003; Gil-Garcia & Pardo, 2005). As soon as project managers and users feel that management is becoming impatient and support is waning, they adjust their expectations downward and strive for fewer benefits as well. In addition, collaborative digital government projects frequently involved a great number of organizations and jurisdictions and, therefore, expand over several years. An incremental approach able to produce some results relatively soon in the life of the project could be a useful strategy (Gil-Garcia et al., 2005).

## Limitations and future research

The generalizability of the results to other IT projects is an empirical question, given that the current study covers only government information-sharing projects. From a methodological perspective, respondents could not be assigned evenly to the various dimensions of experience considered here, although the dimensions are fairly well balanced in our sample. The analysis did not incorporate a feedback loop between perceived impediments and expected benefits. The canonical correlation analysis establishes a relationship between perceived impediments and expected benefits, but it is not able to make inferences on the relationship in the reverse direction. As the current study is exploratory in nature, the number of experience variables is limited. Certainly, others should investigate other dimensions of experience beyond those considered here. Additionally, the relationship between perceived impediments and benefits may be affected by the nature of the system studied. The existing study does not address this issue. Each of these uninvestigated areas provides opportunities for additional research.

Finally, although there is no direct relationship between the three experience items and expected benefits, administrative experience did appear to have an impact on expected benefits. To better understand the relationship between perceived impediments and expected benefits, we incorporate the experience variables as control variables. Perhaps the relationship between experience and expected benefits is a moderating one

## Conclusion

Information sharing is a challenge for public and private organizations. For example, collaborative e-Government initiatives require multiple organizations, including government agencies, private companies, and non-profit organizations, to share important information and, in some cases, to integrate some of their business processes. Expected benefits are an important reason for starting collaborative e-Government initiatives. However, the perception of benefits is clearly affected by perceived impediments and prior experiences. From an applied perspective, managers from public and private concerns need to understand that not everyone will have the same expectations of a project’s benefits. Managers should be aware of the relationship between impediments and benefits and attempt to improve the conditions responsible for impediments. From a research perspective, future models of IT success should incorporate both impediments and experience-based components as determinants of expected benefits. This approach will result in more granular models that will ultimately increase our understanding of the complexities of managing IT projects.
