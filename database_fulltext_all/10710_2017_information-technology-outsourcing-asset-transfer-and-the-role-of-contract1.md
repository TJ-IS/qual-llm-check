# INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY OUTSOURCING: ASSET TRANSFER AND THE ROLE OF CONTRACT<sup>1</sup>

Young Bong Chang

Business School, Sungkyunkwan University, 25-2, Sungkyunkwan-ro, Jongno-Gu, Seoul, REPUBLIC OF KOREA {ybchang01@skku.edu}

Vijay Gurbaxani Center for Digital Transformation, The Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine, CA 92697-3125 U.S.A. {vgurbaxa@uci.edu}

Kiron Ravindran

IE Business School, Calle Maria de Molina, 11-13-15,

28006 Madrid, SPAIN {kiron.ravindran@ie.edu}

Information technology outsourcing (ITO) is the predominant mode of acquiring information systems services, providing clear evidence that the economics of service delivery favor external service providers over in-house information systems departments. An interesting feature of many large ITO arrangements is that the production assets necessary for service delivery are transferred to the vendor. The argument in favor of such asset transfers, based in property rights theory, is that they are necessary to incentivize vendors to continue to invest in transaction-specific assets to improve service. On the other hand, transaction cost economics predicts that transferring such assets increases bilateral dependence and will elevate the risk of post-contractual opportunistic behavior. The contracting challenge in this context is to specify the terms of exchange to achieve the client’s objectives for outsourcing while managing the risks of asset transfer. We develop a theoretical framework to derive propositions on contract design in the presence of asset transfer. We identify the impor tance of contractual clauses that mitigate the associated risks and the complementary role of compensation mechanisms, specifically the pricing scheme and IT-related performance incentives. We have compiled a unique dataset that allows us to test our propositions by comparing ITO contracts that include asset transfer to those that do not. We find that asset transfer does significantly affect contract design, manifested in the inclusion of clauses that protect both clients and vendors. Outsourcing objectives are more likely to be met when contracts include compensation mechanisms that complement asset transfer

Keywords: IT outsourcing, asset transfer, contract structure, transaction cost economics, property rights, ITrelated performance incentives

## Introduction

Information technology outsourcing (ITO) is the predominant mode of acquiring information systems services, with a worldwide market estimated at \$274.2 billion in 2015 that is growing at 6% annually (Gartner 2016). Over 60% of the respondents in a global survey indicated that ITO was a standard practice within their company and reported high levels of satisfaction (Deloitte 2012). While these data indicate the prevalence of ITO as the preferred delivery option, examples of contractual disputes and premature terminations are not uncommon (Deloitte 2012; DiamondCluster 2006). These observations suggest that companies see economic benefit in outsourcing IT services, but that these relationships are also fraught with risk.

The academic literature has recognized this tradeoff. Prior research has noted the advantages that specialized providers possess, generated by economies of scale and specialization. It has also recognized the significant transaction costs inherent in ITO arrangements, stemming from the deployment of relationship-specific assets and the considerable technological and business uncertainty in multiyear deals. While some analysts have argued against external provision because of the difficulty in writing efficient contracts (Wholey et al. 2001), the widespread adoption of ITO indicates that its economic advantages outweigh contractual concerns. Economic theory suggests that outsourcing contracts will be designed to maximize the gains from the arrangement and economize on transaction costs. This paper studies how contracts can be used to manage the tradeoffs between the risks and the available gains.

We define ITO as a multiyear, annuity-based arrangement, in which a service provider manages part or all of a client’s infrastructure and operations.<sup>2</sup> We exclude project-based arrangements or those defined in terms of specific deliverables such as bespoke software development. Our scope sets a specific context for the relationship, crucial to identifying the nature of the underlying risk and the potential gains.

Consider a typical situation where a firm has historically sourced IT services internally and “owns” the assets underlying service delivery—data center and networking equipment, personal computers, mobile devices, proprietary software, facilities, human capital, and so on. The firm then decides to source some or all of these services from an external provider with the goal of improving performance on dimensions such as cost and quality. In many, but not all, cases, the vendor acquires the aforementioned assets from the client and uses them to deliver the contracted services. While there can be other motivations for asset transfer (AT), one particularly important reason is that service improvement requires both up-front and ongoing investments in the production assets.

This requirement for investment in the underlying production assets sets up the essential tension between the interests of the client and the vendor. The argument in favor of asset ownership by the vendor derives from property rights theory, which recognizes that firms will likely not make investments in assets they do not own. In this view, conferring the associated property rights to the vendor provides it with incentives to invest in relationship-specific assets (RSA) over the life of the contract (Grossman and Hart 1986). The argument against transferring RSA to the vendor, derived from transaction cost economics, is that it intensifies bilateral dependence in the relationship and leads to increased transaction risk. Williamson (1985) observed that the existence of a durable, transaction-specific asset is often the source of large market transaction costs. In situations where market exchange occurs in spite of high transaction costs, contracts are the primary mechanism by which these risks can be mitigated (Joskow 1985; Kern and Willcocks 2001).

In our setting, the client’s switching costs increase when the vendor owns the assets as it becomes more difficult to engage another vendor or bring services back in-house. This increases the risk of post-contractual opportunistic behavior by the vendor. Simultaneously, the vendor’s risk also increases as a result of its significant up-front and largely sunk investment in the acquisition of these RSA. Given their transactionspecific nature, the market value of these depreciating assets is lower than their value in their current use, thus improving the clients’ bargaining position.

While all ITO arrangements involve some level of transaction-specific investment and switching costs, the transfer of assets underlying service delivery has the potential to substantially increase the risk to both parties. In the annuity-based outsourcing arrangements prevalent when our dataset was compiled, the delivery assets purchased by the vendor were used to provide service to the client for whom they were purchased; vendors did not typically commingle their clients’ technology assets. Therefore, we expect that arrangements with AT will include additional contractual clauses that safeguard the interests of both parties.

Of course, the goal of an ITO arrangement is to achieve the client’s objectives, which most often are related to decreasing cost, improving quality, and increasing the business impact of technology (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998). Asset ownership, performance incentives, and job design<sup>3</sup> are complementary dimensions of a management system (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991, 1994). A well-designed system that accounts for these complementarities should lead to better performance outcomes. In our case, when assets are transferred, the set of outsourced tasks and performance incentives incorporated in the contract should complement vendor ownership of the assets and lead to better outcomes. The simultaneous goals of achieving the client’s strategic objectives for outsourcing and mitigating the risks of AT creates the central challenge that motivates our research question: How are contracts used to improve IT outsourcing outcomes both in terms of achieving the goals for outsourcing and mitigating the risk of ex post opportunism associated with AT?

Our study contributes to the understanding of ITO contracts, showing them not just as a mechanism to mitigate transaction risk, but also a means to achieve better outcomes, which is the primary purpose of outsourcing. We establish that contract structure differs considerably with asset ownership. Specifically, these contracts are more extensive, incorporating specific clauses that encourage cooperative behavior from client and vendor. We also show complementarity between AT and specific compensation mechanisms—flexible pricing and IT performance incentives—that jointly lead to improved outcomes.

Most prior studies have relied on secondary data, which has required diverse ITO arrangements with very different individual risk profiles to be examined as a group. These data, in the main, include smaller deals with lower potential economic consequences. Due to data limitations, these prior studies infer asset specificity from the nature of the outsourced tasks. We take a fundamentally different approach, studying production assets essential to service delivery, where the level of risk is high given the typical scale and scope of the arrangement. In our context, AT is both observable and contractible. Moreover, our primary dataset allows us to account for factors that can critically influence contract design but are unobservable in secondary data such as strategic intent, relative magnitude of the deal, and prior relationship.

This paper is structured as follows. First, we present our theoretical approach. Our literature review follows. The relevant hypotheses on the role of contract are then derived. We present the data and a preliminary analysis, highlighting observable differences between contracts with and without

AT and validate the need for further analysis. The formal analysis and our results are presented in the following two sections, respectively. In the penultimate section, we discuss the results and conclude with managerial implications. In the final section, we present the limitations of our study.

## Theory

Our research adopts the theoretical perspective of incomplete contracts. Its central premise is that contracting for all future states of the world is either impossible due to bounded rationality (Williamson 1975) or inefficient due to the ex ante cost of contract design (Segal 1999). Within this framework we examine two related streams of literature: property rights theory (PRT) which deals with ex ante incentive alignment (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore 1990), and transaction cost economics (TCE) (Williamson 1985), which deals with contracting and ex post economizing of transaction costs. PRT proposes the transfer of residual rights<sup>4</sup> to the relation-specific assets as an incentive for vendor investment. However, TCE cautions against the transfer of these rights to a vendor due to the risks associated with potential opportunism. The two theories are complementary in that one focuses on the benefits of AT ex ante while the other highlights its costs ex post.

## Property Rights Theory

PRT applies in situations where an agent requires an asset to deliver a service to the principal. It is assumed that the effort invested by agents is not verifiable even if it is observable. It then shows that ownership of the RSA either by the principal or jointly by both parties can lead to underinvestment by the agent in these assets. Agents might choose to under-invest anticipating that the principal might force renegotiation of terms after the investment has been made. On the other hand, owning the residual rights gives the agent an incentive to invest. Thus, Grossman and Hart (1986) argue that allocation of ownership rights is not a function of coordination cost or contracting “ink cost” but rather an ex ante incentive mechanism to enhance efficient investment. The underlying assumption here is that aligning incentives ex ante will prevent opportunistic behavior ex post.

Optimal arrangements for production must balance asset ownership, job design, and explicit incentives. In situations where effort is not verifiable, as is the case in our context, and if the principal owns the assets required for service delivery, optimal contracts tend to have restricted incentives for production, especially in multitask settings. However, when asset ownership shifts to the vendor, and the vendor must allocate effort to multiple tasks, the optimal incentive contract will provide stronger incentives to engage in production to prevent the vendor from being too cautious (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991). Hence, no discussion of services outsourcing is complete without simultaneous discussion of the appropriate asset ownership structure and the incentive mechanisms.

PRT offers an appropriate approach to examine asset transfer in IT outsourcing and suggests that

the relevant comparison is not between the nonintegrated outcome and the complete contract outcome but instead between a contract that allocates residual rights to one party and a contract that allocates them to another (Grossman and Hart 1986, p. 716).

## Transaction Cost Economics

TCE, developed largely by Williamson (1975, 1985), has focused on understanding the relationship between governance structure and the characteristics of exchange. It holds that firms will choose to adopt market-based governance over hierarchical governance if production cost advantages outweigh transaction costs. Transaction costs result from the asset specificity of the underlying production assets. The presence of durable transaction-specific assets in production gives rise to the possibility of ex post bargaining to appropriate the surpluses accrued from the investment in these assets. Such ex post bargaining is the central source of transaction costs.

TCE devotes significant attention to durable transactionspecific assets because they elevate ex post transaction risk and thus pose a challenge to securing the production cost advantage of markets. Specifically, investment in relationship-specific assets by either party creates mutual dependence. Combined with uncertainty in supply or demand, it becomes costly to account for future contingencies leading to a heightened risk of ex post opportunism. It is to minimize this cost of ex post opportunism that Williamson says “support institutions of contract do matter” (1985 p. 29). TCE provides us with a contractual lens with which to examine ex post mechanisms of governance.

## Literature Review

In the Information Systems (IS) literature, only a few studies have empirically analyzed ITO contract structure. These have generally focused on mitigating transaction risks. None have examined the role of contract in helping achieve the client’s performance objectives. In this section, we review the key papers related to our work. Before we begin, we mention that our research is motivated by the seminal papers of Joskow (1985, 1987), who notes that market exchange is often the observed solution even when transaction costs are seemingly high. He posits that contracts are the mechanism by which transaction costs are minimized. He demonstrates how contractual clauses mitigate specific transaction risks in the context of coal procurement by electric power plants.

Gurbaxani (2007) is the first study to demonstrate the role of contract clauses in mitigating transaction risk for both clients and vendors in ITO arrangements with asset transfer. In this qualitative study, he observes that specific clauses, which we discuss later, are indeed matched to the particular transaction risks. Barthélemy and Quélin (2006) study IT and business process outsourcing and observe that contracts are more complex when outsourced activities have high switching costs or are central to the firm.

Two noteworthy studies, Chen and Bharadwaj (2009) and Susarla et al. (2010), analyze datasets of “material contracts” for IT services publicly disclosed to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. Their data includes details of over 100 contracts with average total contract value of \$10M and \$13M respectively. In contrast, the mean contract value in our dataset is \$613M. International Data Corporation (IDC 2006), using a definition of ITO consistent with ours, reports that the average size of the top 100 ITO deals in 2005 was \$700M. This suggests that the data in the prior studies are drawn from a sample that reflects a very different class of arrangements, such as custom software development projects, and may be less central to the client’s operations.

Chen and Bharadwaj link elements of risk in ITO to contract design. They posit that task characteristics—asset specificity, process interdependence, complexity, and uncertainty— influence contract extensiveness, measured as the total number of relevant clauses. They find that asset specificity, defined as “use of customized technologies specific to the client firm” (p. 495) has no significant association with the overall measure of extensiveness but it is associated with property rights safeguards and dispute resolution mechanisms (in fixed price contracts).

The central premise of Susarla et al. is that complexity and scope can raise the need for relationship-specific investments and, in turn, the risk of holdup. They find that complex tasks tend to be governed by extensive contracts but not necessarily long-term contracts, which may be a reflection of the smaller scale and scope of the arrangements in their data. They also show that when vendors own the results of service delivery, the existence of a provision for contract extension is less likely. In our context, in which vendors operate IS functions previously run in-house, IT deliverables are rarely, if ever, owned by a vendor, again highlighting key differences in our focus and in the nature of specific transaction risk.

In summary, theory suggests that vendors can be incentivized to make ongoing investments in relation-specific assets (RSA) essential to service delivery if they possess the associated residual rights. However, ex ante incentives provided by transferring these residual rights will not eliminate vendors’ ex post opportunism and likely increases it. Contracts manage ex post opportunism through the use of specific features that mitigate anticipated forms of transaction risk.

While we have made progress in understanding how contracts mitigate risk associated with asset specificity, there is much that is not well understood. For instance, due to differences in the assets studied and in the interpretations of specificity, there is no consensus on the relationship of asset specificity to contract extensiveness. In many cases, the asset of interest is a deliverable rather than a production asset. Given the difficulty in measuring asset specificity, these studies have inferred its degree by examining some of its drivers. There are no statistical studies of the role of contract when the assets of interest are primary production assets, nor studies of how contract design helps achieve the goals for outsourcing. This paper is a step toward further advancing this understanding.

## Hypothesis Development

Fundamentally, IT outsourcing arrangements are subject to a range of business and technological uncertainties making it impossible to fully specify contracts. As a result, contracts focus on a limited set of parameters, usually the most relevant or verifiable ones (Salanié 1997). By design, ITO contracts specify decision rights and payment mechanisms for the vendor, which determine the quantity and quality of effort expended on service delivery. While the vendor is expected to meet specified performance requirements, a failure to do so willfully or inadvertently can impose considerable costs on the client. Such standard problems of agency are compounded when asset ownership rights are transferred along with decision rights. Now, the client no longer owns the production assets, making it more difficult to bring service delivery back in-house and further raising the risk of holdup by the vendor. Moreover, the vendor is now charged with ongoing investment in the assets, which may result in underinvestment or decaying assets. From the vendor perspective, its exposure to holdup risk also increases once it has made a large transaction-specific investment. Therefore, even though contracts that govern complex ITO arrangements are incomplete, they should include measures to mitigate the risks to both parties.

## Contract Extensiveness

Contracts have been argued to be “the only certain way to ensure that expectations are realized” (Kern and Willcocks 2001, p. 5). Better-specified contracts are generally considered to lead to reduced opportunistic behavior (Lacity and Hirschheim 1993; Lacity and Willcocks 1998) and have been shown to predict IT outsourcing success (Saunders et al. 1997). Kern and Willcocks (2001) distinguish between the management control and legal dimensions of a contract. They identify key elements of a post-contract management agenda: service description and exchanges, service enforcement and monitoring, financial exchanges, financial control and monitoring, key vendor personnel, dispute resolution, and change control and management. In this paper, we build on this conceptual framing to focus on 18 clauses that capture the management control aspects (see Table 3 for the set of clauses).<sup>5</sup>

Of these 18 clauses, we expect that some clauses will be present in all ITO contracts, regardless of AT. Given uncertainty in future business and technology trends, all contracts should specify the allocation of decision rights, termed the formal specification of rights and responsibilities, and should also allow for scheduled renegotiation, which specifies the dates at which the parties may revise the terms of exchange. Service level agreements and the associated performance measurement clauses are likely to be present in most, if not all, contracts. Dispute escalation and dispute resolution clauses should also be specified in most contracts. Similarly, the case for a clause that specifies the terms and conditions that govern termination for cause is self-evident. A termination for convenience clause is also indicated since it may be difficult to prove nonperformance given non-verifiability of vendor effort. A force majeure clause frees both parties from liability in case of an extraordinary event such as war or an act of God.

Now consider our context wherein production assets central to service delivery are transferred. Once a vendor invests in these durable transaction-specific assets,<sup>6</sup> it will want a commitment that the client will buy at least a predetermined volume of services over the life of the contract, termed a specified purchase obligation. Similarly, the client will want a specified supply obligation to ensure that the vendor will not withhold services. However, given business uncertainty, say about demand, both parties will prefer a flexibility clause that allows supply and demand to adjust to changes in economic conditions. The client, recognizing uncertainty in future technology costs, will want assurance of the best price via a most favored customer provision. The vendor may require a preferred supplier clause, which requires the client to negotiate exclusively with the vendor in good faith for any new business, which further incentivizes additional investments and avoids opportunistic behavior. The client may also require a key people provision, which specifies that some named employees now working for the vendor continue to be assigned to the client. In the event of contract termination, the client may incorporate an employee return clause and retain the right to rehire certain employees. To the extent that arrangements with AT are wider in scope and perhaps more strategic in nature, the client firm may also restrict its vendor’s relationships with its competitors using an exclusivity clause. To summarize, five of the eight clauses protect the client, two protect the vendor, and one protects both parties.

Based on the above discussion, we test three related hypotheses on the enforcement and monitoring dimensions of a contract’s management control function. Specifically, we hypothesize that contracts with asset transfer (CA) will include more clauses than contracts with no asset transfer (CNA) in total and that safeguard the interests of each party separately.

Hypothesis 1A: AT is positively associated with more extensive contracts.

Hypothesis 1B: AT is positively associated with the number of clauses that limit the risk of clients holding up the vendor.

Hypothesis 1C: AT is positively associated with the number of clauses that limit the risk of vendors holding up the client.

## Performance Outcomes: Payment Mechanisms

We now turn our attention to payment mechanisms—pricing structure and performance incentives—to examine how they influence performance outcomes when assets are transferred. As already discussed, the allocation of decision rights to the residual claimant of the assets does itself provide an implicit control mechanism. Yet, this instrument may not be sufficient especially when a typically risk-averse vendor is expected to make further investments (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1994). In cases of AT, the choice of ongoing investment decisions made by the vendor can result in improved performance. A client should incentivize these investments by rewarding decisions to invest in a manner consistent with its goals and by allowing the vendor to capture a share of the higher net value (Jensen and Meckling 1992). Therefore, beyond asset ownership, compensation mechanisms, including both the pricing structure and rewards and penalties for exceeding or missing performance targets, are also incentive instruments.

## Pricing Structure

The pricing structure in large ITO arrangements is either variable (such as cost-plus) or predetermined for the duration of the contract. Variable pricing allows adaptation to the actual cost and effort levels in each period. Predetermined prices are either specified for a service as a whole (e.g., manage the data center for \$X per period) or for service units (e.g., per utilized MIP) where the actual compensation depends on service volume.

The choice of variable or predetermined pricing depends largely on task complexity (Bajari and Tadelis, 2001) and the nature of uncertainty in the transaction. Variable pricing is preferred in the presence of ex ante uncertainty in cost (Kalnins and Mayer, 2004), requirements, project size, or resources (Gopal et al. 2003), and vendor ability (Banerjee and Duflo 2000); and ex post uncertainty in quality (Kalnins and Mayer 2004) and outcomes (Eisenhardt 1989). Moreover, the risk associated with unforeseen contingencies, as discussed, is amplified when assets are transferred; variable pricing provides both parties with flexibility in dealing with future contingencies.

Besides flexibility, clients are likely to prefer variable pricing when vendors are expected to invest in RSA, since fixed-price contracts can induce underinvestment (Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008). Vendors accrue higher profits from variableprice contracts relative to fixed-price contracts (Ethiraj et al. 2005), while generating fewer overruns (Banerjee and Duflo 2000). For example, when a vendor that has invested in an asset, increasing its fixed costs, is confronted with an unanticipated decrease in demand, it will be affected more negatively under a predetermined pricing structure relative to a variable pricing structure that can account for these costs. This reduces its incentives to invest in production assets. For a client, an unanticipated increase in demand will not result in lower fees even though the vendor may capture economies of scale. Therefore, when assets are transferred, it is likely that both clients and vendors would prefer a flexible pricing structure. Taking these factors together, we hypothesize that clients achieve greater gains with flexible pricing in cases of AT.

Hypothesis 2: Performance from the client perspective in ITO arrangements with AT is higher when the pricing structure reflected in the contract is flexible.

## Performance Incentives

Vendors can be compensated for investment risk by incentive payments for the achievement of objectives (Fama and Jensen, 1983). Moreover, Holmstrom and Milgrom (1991, 1994) have shown that asset ownership is likely to be complemented by strong performance-based incentives. For example, a vendor, in the absence of such incentives, could choose to postpone investment or only invest in less risky activities even when riskier investments may be desirable. In multitask settings, as is the case with ITO arrangements, performance-based incentives have the additional benefit of prioritizing effort where value to the client is greatest (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012). Therefore, beyond compensation for ongoing investment in the assets through the pricing scheme, which encourages investment but does not direct it, the client must also provide incentives to the vendor to select the investments and engage in effort that will result in better performance outcomes for the client. This leads us to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Performance from the client perspective in ITO arrangements with AT is higher when the contract provides explicit IT-related performance incentives.

## Data and Preliminary Analysis

We conducted a comprehensive survey of outsourcing client firms in 2005 with the assistance of an experienced market research firm. Our dataset is based on responses to closedended questions about contract features, including objectives, duration, whether assets were transferred, the presence of specific clauses, performance measures, incentives, price structure, and so on. We targeted 291 North American firms that had outsourced for at least 1 year between 1993 and 2004. Given the sensitivity of the information, the response rate of 15% (44 firms) is excellent. Respondents were senior (above the level of IT director) and were knowledgeable about the specifics of the ITO contract. Our sample is representative of the top 100 worldwide outsourcing contracts (IDC 2006) in the same time frame in terms of contract size, revenues, employees, industries, and services outsourced (see Table 1).

We begin by comparing the key features of contracts with and without AT. First, we find that CA are bigger than CNA on all dimensions: total contract value, annualized value, the number of services, and the number of clauses (see Table 2). They are also longer on average. Turning to the set of 18 potential clauses, 11 are present in more than two-thirds of the contracts (see Table 3). These cover baseline specifications of service description, enforcement, dispute resolution, and change control. The other seven, including such clauses as purchase and supply obligations, price guarantee, employee return, etc., reflect an additional level of control beyond the baseline, and occur far more frequently in CA. In addition, we examine the use of specific compensation mechanisms. We find the frequency of IT incentives to be far greater in CA and the use of flexible pricing to be similar. The structure of contracts in the two groups is statistically distinguishable, indicating the desirability of further analysis.

Seven clauses represent ways (beyond the 11 baseline clauses) in which opportunistic behavior by clients and vendors are restricted. Vendor cooperation clauses require it to guarantee the supply of services, offer the best price, retain key people, return named employees at contract termination, and limit relationships with the client’s competitors. ## Econometric Models

We first examine the impact of AT on contract extensiveness while controlling for potential endogeneity. To do so, we set up a model based on the control function approach (Terza et al. 2008; Wooldridge 2007). Using the same empirical strategy, we then examine the complementarity between AT and compensation scheme, estimated as the joint effect of pricing structure and incentives on performance.

## Contract Extensiveness

Our specification uses the number of clauses as the dependent variable, $C o n t _ { E x t }$ and AT as the primary independent variable. We include the variables for transformation-based objectives and cost-based objectives as controls. The ratio of spending on outsourced services to total IT expenditures (%Out) is included as a control to capture the potential association between the scale of services and extensiveness. We also control for the existence of a prior relationship between the client and vendor (Prior). We incorporate duration as an explanatory variable. Since firms in a long-term relationship are more likely to see it as a partnership, they may rely less on contract specificity, resulting in lower extensiveness. Finally, the observed number of contract clauses is likely to be higher when the ITO arrangement has broader scope. Industry dummies are included. This leads to the following specification:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Cont} _ {\text {Ext}} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A T + \alpha_ {2} \text {Duration} + \alpha_ {3} \% \text {Out} + \alpha_ {4} T O b j \\ & + \alpha_ {5} C O b j + \alpha_ {6} S c o p e + \alpha_ {7} \text {Prior + controls} + \varepsilon_ {1} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

## Endogeneity Issues

Equation (1) is likely to exhibit endogeneity in the relationship between AT and $C o n t _ { E x t }$ Further, both dependent and independent variables are categorical. In such a case, the control function approach (Blundell and Powell 2003, 2004; Terza et al. 2008) is more appropriate than classical 2SLS. While similar to 2SLS, the control function approach uses (instead of the predicted values) the residual terms for endogenous independent variables obtained from the firststage as surrogates for unobserved attributes in the secondstage (Wooldridge 2007). This approach eliminates the problem of the fitted values of the categorical endogenous variable falling outside the acceptable range, which is likely in standard 2SLS.<sup>9</sup>

We obtain the residuals for AT by regressing it on the instrument variables. Specifically, we use the extent to which revenue is generated through ITO (RevIT), measured on a fivepoint scale, as an instrument, because a firm is more likely to retain its assets when its strategic intent is to raise revenue from IT outsourcing (Choudhary et al. 2011; DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998). However, revenue intent is unlikely to be directly associated with the presence of additional clauses in the contract. The set of independent variables such as prior experience and scope are also employed as additional instruments (Greene 2000; Wooldridge 2009).

Since AT is a binary variable, we obtain its predicted value with a Probit model and compute the residuals. Then, we respecify equation (1) with the residual terms as follows where residual is the difference between the observed and fitted values for AT in the first stage.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Cont } _ {\text { Ext }} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A T + \alpha_ {2} \text { Duration } + \alpha_ {3} \% O u t + \alpha_ {4} T O b j \\ & + \alpha_ {5} C O b j + \alpha_ {6} S c o p e + \alpha_ {7} P r i o r \\ & + \alpha_ {8} \text { residual } + \text { controls } + \varepsilon_ {1 A} \end{array}\tag{1A}
$$

## Vendor and Client Cooperation Clauses

We also examine contract extensiveness from the perspectives of clients and vendors separately. In equations (1B) and (1C) below, we replace the number of contract clauses in the original model (eq. 1) with the number of clauses that induce client cooperation (Ccoop) and vendor cooperation (Vcoop). Then, similar to equation (1A), we specify the second stage equations for equations (1B) and (1C). This yields:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C \text {coop} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A T + \alpha_ {2} \text { Duration } + \alpha_ {3} \% O u t + \alpha_ {4} T O b j \\ & + \alpha_ {5} C O b j + \alpha_ {6} S c o p e + \alpha_ {7} P r i o r \\ & + \alpha_ {8} \text { residual } + \text { controls } + \varepsilon_ {1 B} \end{array}\tag{1B}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} V \text {coop} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A T + \alpha_ {2} \text { Duration } + \alpha_ {3} \% O u t + \alpha_ {4} T O b j \\ & \quad + \alpha_ {5} C O b j + \alpha_ {6} S c o p e + \alpha_ {7} P r i o r \\ & \quad + \alpha_ {8} \text { residual } + \text { controls } + \varepsilon_ {1 C} \end{array}\tag{1C}
$$

## Performance

To test the association of ITO performance with AT, we use the ordered probit model since our dependent variables are measured on a five-point Likert scale.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Perf } & = \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} A T + \theta_ {2} I T I n c + \theta_ {3} F l e x P r i c e + \theta_ {4} T O b j \\ & + \theta_ {5} C O b j + \theta_ {6} A T \times I T I n c + \theta_ {7} A T \times F l e x P r i c e \\ & + \theta_ {8} S c o p e + \theta_ {9} P r i o r + \theta_ {1 0} D u r a t i o n \\ & + \theta_ {1 1} C o n t _ {E x t} + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{2A}
$$

where Perf denotes either IT cost reduction or overall satisfaction, ITInc is IT incentives and FlexPrice is flexible pricing and all the other variables are the same as those in equations (1A/B/C).

Similar to the equations of (1A/B/C), we use the control function approach to account for potential endogeneity. We plug residuals for asset transfer into equation (2A). Then, we estimate the coefficients by incorporating all cut-points using the maximum likelihood method.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{P} (p e r f = j | X) & = P (k _ {j - 1} <   \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} A T + \theta_ {2} I T I n c + \theta_ {3} F l e x P r i c e \\ & + \theta_ {4} T O b j + \theta_ {5} C O b j + \theta_ {6} A T \times I T I n c + \theta_ {7} A T \\ & \times F l e x P r i c e + \theta_ {8} S c o p e + \theta_ {9} P r i o r \\ & + \theta_ {1 0} D u r a t i o n + \theta_ {1 1} C o n t _ {E x t} + \varepsilon <   k _ {j}) \end{array} \tag {2B}
$$

where j = 1, 2,…, 5 which reflects a five-point Likert scale and X includes the independent variables specified in equations (2A) and the residual computed as the difference between observed value and fitted value for AT in the first stage.

## Results

We begin by presenting the results of our estimation of equations (1A), (1B), and (1C), which examine the impact of AT on contract extensiveness and bilateral safeguards (see Table 5).

## Contract Extensiveness

We find that, on average, CA include 8.48 more clauses than CNA (see column (i) of Table 5), supporting the hypothesis that these contracts account for more contingencies. The negative coefficient of duration (-0.490, p < 0.05) implies that longer contracts are less likely to be more extensive. ITO arrangements undertaken primarily for cost reduction are likely to have less extensive contracts (-1.907, p < 0.05). The residual obtained from the first stage is significant at the 5% level, confirming that our model adequately controls for potential endogeneity. However, the share of ITO spend, while having the expected sign, is not associated with the number of contract clauses due to large standard error.

We find positive and significant estimates for AT on both vendor and client cooperation clauses (columns (ii) and (iii) of Table 5), supporting hypothesis 1B and 1C. This finding shows that the contract manages the risk of ex post opportunism by both parties. Prior literature(e.g., Barthélemy and Quélin 2006; Wang 2002), on the other hand, has focused on vendor opportunism. Our paper highlights the clauses that distinguish CA from CNA and points toward the specific manner in which contingencies are anticipated and planned for to mitigate the risk of bilateral holdup. Further, we observe an association between the client cooperation clauses and strategic intent. Specifically, vendors require additional commitments from the client in cases of asset transfer when the strategic intent is cost reduction.

## Performance

We now turn our attention to the role of contractual features on performance in the presence of AT. As discussed in the “Literature Review” section, performance is likely to be associated with IT incentives and flexible pricing in contracts where assets have been transferred. The results of the ordered probit estimation to test hypotheses 2 and 3 are presented in Table 6.

The coefficient of AT (column 1) is negative but not statistically significant and contract extensiveness is positively associated with achieving the cost objective. However, when we include the interaction terms with AT, the results (column 2) provide us with interesting insights. While the main effect of AT continues to be negative,<sup>10</sup> its interaction terms with the use of IT-related performance incentives and flexible pricing are positive and statistically significant. AT, IT-related performance incentives, and flexible pricing are complementary instruments when the objective is IT cost reduction. That is, performance incentives and flexible pricing in the presence of AT lead to better cost outcomes (2.855, p < 0.01 and 4.265, p < 0.01). The results with overall satisfaction are not qualitatively different from ones with reduced IT cost. An exception is that the interaction term between AT and IT incentives is not statistically distinguishable from zero. We conjecture that it is more difficult to improve overall satisfaction when the emphasis is on cost reduction.

## Discussion and Concluding Remarks

This paper contributes to the empirical literature on the role of contract. In particular, asset specificity is recognized as a central influencer of the make versus buy decision. Yet, empirical observation provides many instances of outsourcing relationships even when asset specificity is high. Research has identified contract as the crucial mechanism to manage transaction risk. We examine the role and performance impacts of contractual mechanisms in large IT outsourcing arrangements where assets essential to service delivery are transferred to the vendor. Unlike most prior research in ITO, we focus exclusively on assets central to service delivery. Our research offers valuable insights into the design of outsourcing contracts. Our findings suggest that the contract is a comprehensive set of measures to mitigate ex post opportunism of both parties and to motivate better performance outcomes.

We hypothesize that both parties must provide assurances via contractual features that limit opportunistic behavior. Since asset ownership, performance incentives, and job design are complementary mechanisms in motivating vendor performance, contracts should reflect simultaneous use of these mechanisms, which should lead to better performance outcomes. Vendors must be provided with incentives through pricing and incentive schemes to invest in production assets and toward meeting clients’ performance objectives, especially in light of the multitask nature of ITO arrangements.

We find that CA provide additional measures to reduce ex post opportunism than CNA, with both clients and vendors accepting contractual restrictions in their actions. Qualitatively, we achieve a better understanding of the risks with which each party is primarily concerned. From the client perspective, 92% of CA include a guaranteed supply obligation relative to 19% of CNA, indicating a substantial concern with vulnerability to a vendor’s ability to restrict service provision. Clients are also concerned with the loss of firmspecific human capital, indicated in the use of the key people provision, which requires specific people to work on their account for a specified period. From the vendor perspective, the primary concern is with the minimum level of purchased services, which is unsurprising given their investment in production assets. We also find that well-designed contracts recognize the complementarity between payment mechanisms and AT. The use of IT-related performance incentives and flexible pricing when assets are transferred lead to superior outcomes.

While our sample size is small, it is unusually comprehensive. Given the sensitivity of and confidentiality associated with outsourcing arrangements, it is especially difficult to collect data on contract specifics. Our survey-based dataset is unique in that it not only includes contract features but it also captures non-contractual parameters of the decision such as objectives for outsourcing, relationship structure, and outcomes. Prior research has typically used publicly available secondary data, which pools different kinds of ITO, each of which features different risks, and does not include data on the outsourcing intent, which is an important control. Our study is unique in that we examine the underlying mechanism of contractual governance in large ITO arrangements while controlling for the strategic intent for outsourcing. Avoiding this omitted variable bias provides added robustness to our results.

## Limitations and Future Research

While our dataset is unique in its depth of contract details, and the sample represents mature outsourcers of various industries and sizes, the number of observations is nevertheless small. This is not uncommon in the ITO literature.<sup>11</sup> Therefore, we chose aggregate measures for objectives, cooperation clauses, and performance measures to preserve degrees of freedom, limiting the extent to which these measures can be examined. Confidentiality agreements prevented us from knowing the identity of client firms. As a result, we could not augment the data with publicly available data.

Extensions to this research require larger datasets and more detail, which would allow a better examination of heterogeneity in contract structure. Another direction is to explore contract at a finer level of granularity to allow for the vast number of clauses, performance metrics, incentives, and service bundles. Finally, with the emergence of cloud-based service providers with economics that are different from traditional vendors, future supply arrangements will require different contract designs, which in turn demands further research.

## Appendix

## Service Descriptions

The commonly outsourced set of IT processes includes help desk management, data center management, distributed system maintenance, networking and telecommunications, existing software maintenance, and development of new software. We use service descriptions that are consistent with those used in industry (Gartner 2007). Help desk management refers to ongoing product support over telephone or e-mail. This service is offered as a stand-alone service or can be bundled with other services. Data center management refers to the day-to-day maintenanc of operating server/host platforms. The client or the vendor may own the IT assets involved. Distributed systems and desktop outsourcing covers the day-to-day maintenance of the desktop/client platforms. Often product support, professional services, and help desk services form part of the service. The networking and telecommunication environment covers the hardware, software, and peripherals that enable the networking of physical assets in the client firm. Data center management, distributed and desktop management, and network related outsourcing are generally referred to as infrastructure services while applications services cover new software development and existing software maintenance.
