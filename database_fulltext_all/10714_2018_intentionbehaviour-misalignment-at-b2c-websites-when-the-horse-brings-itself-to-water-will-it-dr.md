# Intention–behaviour misalignment at B2C websites: when the horse brings itself to water, will it drink?

Greta L. Polites<sup>a</sup>, Elena Karahanna<sup>b</sup> and Larry Seligman<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management & Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA; <sup>b</sup>Management Information Systems Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, USA; <sup>c</sup>Advanced Consumer Analytics, InterContinental Hotels Group, Atlanta, GA, USA

## ABSTRACT

We examine factors leading to intention-behaviour misalignment during the current website visit for two groups of website visitors: (1) “directed buyers” who visit with an a priori intention to purchase on the current visit but end up not doing so, and (2) “search/deliberators” who visit with an a priori intention to only gather information for a specific future purchase but end up making a purchase during the current visit. A qualitative study analysed responses by 2660 visitors to the websites of a large hotel management company, 749 of whom acted against their a priori purchase intentions. Based on the results, we propose a theoretical model of factors influencing the intention-behaviour relationship, emphasising those factors that have received little attention in extant research and that have noticeably diferent impacts for each group, in either facilitating or inhibiting purchasing behaviour. We further explore the interrelationships between various types of uncertainty, anticipated action and inaction regret, and disconfirmed expectations in distorting the intention-behaviour relationship. We close with suggested topics for future research.

## 1. Introduction

The intention-behaviour link has long been posited in such popular theoretical models as the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, Bagozzi, & Warshaw, 1989). The literature provides compelling evidence of a strong intention-behaviour relationship with a meta-analysis by Sheppard, Hartwick, and Warshaw (1988) showing a .53 correlation between the two constructs. In TAM-related research, Davis et al. (1989) found correlations between .35 and .63. Nevertheless, this relationship sometimes fails, leading researchers over the years to investigate factors that may lead to intention-behaviour misalignment. For example, the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991) introduced the construct of actual behavioural control to represent a variety of internal and external constraints on one’s ability to follow through with their stated intentions. More recent studies (e.g., Polites & Karahanna, 2013; Verplanken & Faes, 1999) have identified habit (as a form of actual behavioural control in the TPB) as a key factor preventing intentions from being realised through actual behavioural performance. Yet, there has not been much research attention devoted to the factors vthat influence intention-behaviour misalignment. The objective of the study is to examine intention-behaviour misalignment in the context of online shopping.

Intention-behaviour misalignment has great practical significance in online consumer shopping. Conversion rates, defined as the percentage of website visitors that actually make a purchase during a specific visit to a website (thus representing the website visitor’s purchase decision in response to the question, “Should I buy now?”; see Berthon, Layland, & Watson, 1996; Moe & Fader, 2001), have remained relatively low. Even consumers who place items in an online shopping cart fail to convert that cart into an actual purchase at a rate ranging from 61 to 68% across common retail industries (IBM, 2012). In fact, conversion rates of 5% or less have been reported for most industries, and these are particularly low for consumers shopping via smartphones rather than more traditional computing devices (Monetate, 2015). As such, much recent research has sought to develop a better understanding of how website visitors make purchasing decisions (see Table A1 in Appendix A). For example, researchers have investigated search behaviour (e.g., De, Hu, & Rahman, 2010; Johnson, Moe, Fader, Bellman, & Lohse, 2004), visit patterns (e.g., Moe & Fader, 2004), the role of user perceptions of the website (e.g., Deng & Poole, 2010; Gefen & Straub, 2000; Pavlou, Liang, & Xue, 2007; Wells, Valacich, & Hess, 2011), and the role of trust in the e-vendor (e.g., Gefen, Karahanna, & Straub, 2003a; Kim, Ferrin, & Rao, 2009; McKnight, Choudhury, & Kacmar, 2002). Most such research has focused on predicting intentions to purchase (implicitly assuming intention-behaviour alignment), frequency or total dollar amount of purchases (across visits), or intentions to return to the website (e.g., Bhattacherjee, 2001; De et al., 2010; Pavlou, 2003; Venkatesh & Agarwal, 2006; Wells et al., 2011).

To examine intention-behaviour misalignment, it is necessary to focus on purchasing behaviour during a specific website visit (a topic which has received far less attention in the literature), so that we capture both a priori purchase intentions and as well as the consumers’ actual purchase decision at the end of the visit. Clearly, not all consumers come to a website with the intention of purchasing during that visit. While some do visit with the a priori intention to purchase, others visit simply to gather information for future purchases, others for hedonic purposes, and still others to build their knowledge on various products and vendors with no explicit future purchase in mind (Liu, Wang, Zhang, & Zuo, 2012; Moe, 2003). What leads an individual who came to a website with no intention of making an immediate purchase to do so? What leads an individual who came to the website with an explicit purchasing intention to leave without completing that purchase?

Popular theoretical models such as TAM (Davis, 1989) and the IS Success model (DeLone & McLean, 1992) that are commonly used to explain other types of e-shopper behaviour do not address the issue of unintended purchases, implying a need to develop new models (Aljarallah & Ramaprasad, 2014). Research on why website visitors don’t follow through with an intended purchase has been largely limited to studies of shopping cart usage patterns and shopping cart abandonment (e.g., Cho, 2004; Kukar-Kinney & Close, 2010), leaving a gap in our understanding of other situations where online customers fail to carry through with an intended purchase. If a prospective customer leaves a site, they might not return but rather decide to buy elsewhere or not buy at all. Thus, understanding the factors impacting intention-behaviour misalignment provides a more nuanced understanding of purchasing behaviours and their distinct determinants across these diferent groups of website visitors. Such an understanding is important in order to efectively manage each individual website visit. More generally, results of our study contribute to a cumulative body of IS research on attitude-behaviour models by surfacing a set of new factors and pointing to new theoretical directions.

Respondents for the study are visitors to a large hotel management company’s hotel reservation websites who visited the sites on their own volition. At the end of the visit, these visitors were asked as to why they did or did not purchase (defined as completing a hotel room reservation during the specific website visit). The visitors also reported their a priori intention of visiting the site. To identify factors that impact the intention-behaviour relationship, the open-ended responses were first analysed separately for (a) visitors who did not intend to purchase (called search/deliberators) but ended up purchasing and (b) visitors who intended to purchase (called directed buyers) and decided not to purchase. Then a theoretical model was developed that leverages our findings as well as existing literature to propose both factors that moderate the intention-behaviour relationship and, thus, result in misalignment and mediating mechanisms via which these factors operate.

The theoretical model derived from our study identifies promising directions for future research both in terms of understanding the factors associated with intention-behaviour misalignment and in terms of understanding actual purchasing behaviour. Specifically, the derived model distinguishes between purchase facilitators (i.e., factors for which high values encourage purchase even for consumers with a priori intentions not to do so, but low values do not necessarily result in failure to purchase, meaning that these factors are causally asymmetric) and purchase inhibitors (i.e., factors for which high values result in failure to purchase even for consumers with a priori intentions to do so, but low values do not necessarily lead to purchasing, meaning that these factors are also causally asymmetric), as well as factors having symmetric efects (i.e., they act as facilitators when values are high, and as inhibitors when values are low). It further identifies actual behavioural control (a construct in the TPB (Ajzen, 1991)) and various types of uncertainty as important inhibitors of purchase behaviour, and suggests uncertainty reduction mechanisms and anticipated regret reduction as promising web design interventions for increasing purchasing rates.

The paper proceeds as follows. First, we describe the theoretical background of the study. Next, we describe the study’s methodology, and present its results. We then propose and discuss a model of factors impacting the intention-behaviour relationship. We close with a discussion of limitations, contributions to research and practice, and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

To better understand purchasing behaviour during a specific website visit, we must recognise that diferent types of website visitors have diferent motivations for their visits, as explained by Moe’s (2003) taxonomy of shopping strategies (Table 1). This taxonomy classifies individuals based on purchase intentions and purchase time horizon, and suggests four distinct classes of website visitors: directed buyers who have a specific purchase need and visit the website intending to purchase on the current visit; search/deliberation visitors who have a specific purchase need and visit to gather information for a future specific purchase; knowledge-building visitors who visit to gain a general understanding of the vendor or products but have no specific purchase intention; and hedonic browsers who visit out of curiosity about the site.

Given our interest in intention-behaviour misalignment specifically as it relates to completing a purchase on the current website visit, we focus on the two groups of website visitors who have a specific planned purchase need and who are therefore most likely to complete a purchase. These groups are the directed buyers (who intend to purchase on the current visit), and the search/ deliberators (who intend to eventually make a specific purchase though not necessarily from this site, but at the moment are only gathering information with no intentions of purchasing during the current visit). Both groups of visitors are considered goal-oriented as opposed to experiential consumers (Lin & Chan, 2009), yet difer in the time frame for their intended purchase. In viewing these two types of visitors in terms of Simon’s (1960) decision-making model, directed buyers have completed alternative generation (design phase) and are ready to purchase (choice phase), whereas search/deliberators are still at the design phase. We further categorise these two types of visitors based on intention to purchase vs. actual purchase behaviour as depicted in Table 2.

Intention and behaviour may not be aligned if: (a) the behaviour is not entirely under a user’s volitional control because of impediments to performing the behaviour that are beyond an individual’s control (called actual behavioural control factors; see Ajzen, 1991); (b) the intention has been assessed before an individual has all the necessary information to form a completely confident intention; or (c) disruptive events intervene between measurement of intention and measurement of behaviour, and these events change salient perceptions (Ajzen, 1988; Sheppard et al., 1988). Therefore, as new information becomes available after an initial intention is formed, this information may change salient perceptions, and thus the initial intention is abandoned and a new intention is formed based on these revised perceptions.

Consequently, in our context, unless the website visit changes user perceptions in an unexpected manner, or users are precluded from carrying out their a priori intentions for reasons beyond their control (Ajzen, 1988), we would expect users to act according to their a priori intentions. Thus, we would typically expect directed buyers to make a purchase, and search/deliberators not to make a purchase, on the current website visit. Nevertheless, while a website visitor comes to a site with a specific a priori intention, this intention may be based in part on a priori perceptions of the site. The visit itself may create or alter perceptions of the website, the vendor, and the product oferings, and as a result of these perceptual changes, the visitor may or may not behave in alignment with her a priori intention. This suggests that both a priori intentions and current perceptions will influence actual purchasing behaviour, as well as any impediments to completing the purchase (actual behavioural control).

Against this theoretical backdrop that (a) distinguishes between directed buyers and search deliberators in terms of a priori intention, and (b) acknowledges the efect of a priori intention, current perceptions, and actual behavioural control impediments in determining actual purchasing behaviour, we examine intention-behaviour misalignment by exploring the factors that lead search/deliberators to make a purchase when they intended only to browse, as well as the factors that lead directed buyers not to make a purchase when they intended to do so. The ultimate objective is a model of intention-behaviour misalignment that can be used as the basis for future research.

## 3. Methodology

Data were gathered via a web-based survey of visitors to the websites of a large hotel management company, with more than 5200 hotels in almost 100 countries. Thecompany has several diferent hotel brands and a leading customer loyalty programme. Individuals can make online reservations and gather information via the parent company’s website, the loyalty programme website, or the websites of any of the individual brands. The data were gathered from a random sample of visitors to these websites as part of a study conducted by the parent company that included both Likert-style and open-ended questions. The visitors, who came to the websites on their own volition (i.e., unprompted), received the questionnaire at the beginning or at the end of their website visit. Since we were interested in perceptions that led to their decision to purchase or not during this specific visit to the website, only responses that were completed at the end of the visit were analysed. To alleviate concerns that making a reservation may have modified one’s reporting of their a priori intention (i.e., if an individual made a reservation they would be more likely to report their intention to be “intention to purchase”), we compared the percentage of respondents who indicated intentions to purchase in the sample which received the questionnaire before their visit with that which received the questionnaire after their visit, and saw no significant diferences (χ<sup>2</sup> statistic = .3044; p-value = .5811; not significant at p < .05).

Respondents were asked to indicate the purpose of their visit to the website by selecting the appropriate response from a list of nine options (the final being “Other”). Those who indicated that they came intending to make a reservation were classified as directed buyers, and those who came intending to research rates/availability or to gather hotel information for a future reservation were classified as search/deliberators (Moe & Fader, 2001). Thus, the respondents’ a priori intention for their current website visit is reflected in this categorisation. 4061 search/deliberators and directed buyers were identified from a total of 6930 survey participants (the rest visited the website without a specific purchase need).

Open-ended questions were used to explore influences on actual purchasing behaviour, which also helped us understand the reasons for intention-behaviour misalignment. Our approach was to ask each site visitor why they did or did not complete a purchase. Specifically, those site visitors who reported that they did not book on this particular visit were asked: “Why did you decide not to make a reservation on this particular site?” (“Why Not” question). Those site visitors who reported that they had booked on this particular visit were asked: “Why did you decide to make a reservation on this particular site?” (“Why” question). Table 3 shows response rates (number and per cent of survey respondents who responded to the corresponding qualitative questions) by visitor group. In total, 2660 out of the 4061 directed buyers/search deliberators provided usable qualitative responses, meaning that they provided a usable answer to the open-ended “Why” or “Why Not” question that was posed to them (66% response rate). To test for non-response (selection) bias for those who did not respond to the qualitative questions on the survey, we compared responses to the quantitative questions of the survey between those who provided qualitative responses and those who did not for both directed buyers and search deliberators. No significant diferences were detected on any of the quantitative responses (all t-tests for equality of means were not significant at p < .05) alleviating the concern that there are systematic diferences between those who responded to the qualitative questions and those who did not. A description of the quantitative questions and summary of the t-test results appear in Tables B1 and B2 of Appendix B, respectively.

## 3.1. Coding method

We draw upon grounded theory coding techniques (specifically, open and axial coding and constant comparison) to analyse our data. That is, we followed what is described by Matavire and Brown (2013) as an “analytical” grounded theory approach where grounded theory techniques are used for analysis purposes only. We started out with a list of categories (i.e., constructs) informed by prior research. Recall that one cause of intention-behaviour misalignment is changes in perceptions of factors (called beliefs in TPB) that are important in determining the behaviour. As a result, prior theoretical perspectives used to examine purchasing behaviour in an e-commerce setting provide a suitable starting point for identifying these factors. Our starting list of coding categories (see Table 4) was based primarily on the core constructs from the DeLone and McLean (1992, 2003) IS Success model as adapted by Seddon (1997) and Rai, Lang, and Welker (2002), and the TAM (Davis, 1989) as adapted by Gefen et al. (2003a) to an e-commerce setting. This is because both models have been extensively used in a wide variety of contexts including e-commerce, to predict a range of system behaviours from usage to purchase intentions to purchasing behaviour (see Table A1 in Appendix A for representative studies). The adapted IS Success model suggests that the system characteristics of information quality, system quality, and perceived usefulness are important determinants of user satisfaction, usage (which, per DeLone & McLean, 2004; includes the execution of a purchase transaction), and ultimate IS success. TAM suggests that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are important antecedents of system usage behaviours. Though perceived ease of use has been included as part of system quality in some prior IS success studies (see Rai et al., 2002), given its prominence in the TAM model we treated it as a separate construct for the purpose of initial coding. It is important to point out here that navigability (ease of navigation) and the overall information architecture (ease of locating information) are considered to be distinct aspects of system quality, which in turn influence more general perceptions of the system’s overall ease of use (Nelson, Todd, & Wixom, 2005; Vance, Elie-Dit-Cosaque, & Straub, 2008; Wixom & Todd, 2005).

Simply viewing a website as an information technology, and focusing solely on consumer perceptions of that technology, is not adequate for understanding purchasing behaviour. A B2C website is both an information system and an interface with a vendor (Gefen et al., 2003a). Thus, in addition to system-related factors, vendor-related factors, and even product-related factors may become salient. Cheung, Chan, and Limayem’s (2005) review of online consumer behaviour studies supports this broader approach, proposing that characteristics of the technology, merchant, and product all influence online consumer behaviour. In this vein, Gefen et al. (2003a) extended TAM to include trust which is the most commonly studied vendor-related factor to date (e.g., Gefen et al., 2003a; Jarvenpaa & Tractinsky, 1999; McKnight et al., 2002; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006), while the most commonly studied product-related factors have been price and the closely related construct of value (e.g., Devaraj, Fan, & Kohli, 2002; Liao & Cheung, 2001; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006). Thus, we also included the constructs of trust and perceived value in our initial list of coding categories.

In addition to belief changes, the literature suggests that people may behave in a manner inconsistent with their intentions if impediments prevent them from enacting their intentions (i.e., actual behavioural control factors). Given that the literature is silent on actual behavioural control factors in the context of online purchasing, we let these factors emerge from our data.

It is important to emphasise that during coding we did not restrict responses to our starting list of codes, but rather we allowed the list to expand and be modified in order to encompass new factors discovered in our data. Specifically, additional categories in the final lists (e.g., anticipated regret) were developed from responses that did not relate to one of the existing coding categories. This category development reflects the application of dialogical reasoning (Klein & Myers, 1999) in which coding categories are informed by, but not constrained by, extant theory. This is consistent with what Urquhart (2013) describes as a “top-down” open coding approach where “codes from the literature are applied to the data” (p. 38) but researchers “add some bottom-up categories too – those suggested by the data” (p. 39). This diverges from the classical grounded theory approach to open coding where codes solely emerge from the data (what Urquhart, 2013 labels as the bottom-up approach).

The initial “open” coding was performed in the following manner. Beginning with the starting list of categories, two of the authors coded a subsample of responses. Both coders were well versed in the theories underlying the study, empirical studies in the e-commerce context, as well as the empirical context of the study and were both intimately familiar with the initial code categories and their definitions. When new concepts were identified, new codes were added to the coding scheme along with the definitions of these new codes. While coding, the coders engaged in the process of constant comparison (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) where each response assigned a code was compared with other responses assigned the same or a similar code. In doing so, definitions for all codes were reviewed as the coding process evolved and new codes were added, and where necessary, codes were revised to provide improved clarity or discriminate from another similar code. Furthermore, the two coders developed research memos to capture instances where additional work might be required to refine a code, a new code may be required, where there was evidence of potential relationships between codes, or where the coder identified something of potential significance that required further investigation. After the coders independently coded the same 100 responses from each of the two categories (“Why” and “Why Not”), they would meet in order to compare codes, discuss, resolve any disagreements, and finalise new code names and definitions. This process was reiterated until convergence had been reached, that is, when no further category refinement or expansion seemed necessary for categorisation of the responses.

The revised coding scheme resulting from open coding included 100 codes at the code and sub-code levels for Search/Deliberators (“Why” question) and 135 for Directed Buyers (“Why Not” question). For example, Information Quality had 22 sub-codes to capture the various types of information quality issues mentioned in the responses such as lack/presence of map/directions, of pictures of rooms, of descriptions of rooms, of descriptions about hotel amenities, of availability information, of information about local attractions and points of interests, complete/detailed/incomplete information, etc. As another example, Perceived Value had 18 subcodes to indicate why respondents thought this was/was not good value such as better rate from another hotel, better rate by calling hotel directly, better rate through another website such as Travelocity or Hotels.com, better rate through travel agent, rate too high, rate too high for the type of room, better rate at this site, great special ofer, price guarantee, etc.

A single coder then coded a random subsample of half of the responses in the final data-set, and a second coder independently coded the other half of the responses. Each response was assigned one or more codes, since some responses mentioned multiple reasons for purchasing or not purchasing. In order to determine intercoder reliability as recommended by Miles and Huberman (1994, p. 64), the two coders also independently coded a randomly selected 10% of the responses in each other’s subsamples. Intercoder reliability for the coding of the “Why” question was 83%, and for the “Why Not” question was 87%. In cases of disagreement in the overlapping 10%, we retained the codes of the coder who was the primary coder for that subsample.

We used an adapted version of axial coding to identify higher level concepts (i.e., categories). Axial coding is the “process of relating categories to their subcategories” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 123) to identify how a category relates to its subcategories. In our study, axial coding was used to analyse the open codes (sub-categories) and group them into categories (i.e., higher level concepts). For example, 22 sub-codes from open coding were grouped together under the category (higher level concept) of “Information Quality”. The axial coding focused primarily on the new codes created during open coding given that the initial coding scheme derived from the literature already provided us the higher level codes for those initial concepts (e.g., Information Quality). When a concept emerged, we consulted the literature to identify whether a similar concept existed and to leverage the literature in labelling the concept (e.g., Anticipated Action Regret/Anticipated Inaction Regret, which although technically behavioural beliefs, have distinct enough efects in TPB-based models to be classified separately in Table 4; see Ajzen & Sheikh, 2013). The axial coding resulted in 6 additional categories (i.e., higher-level concepts) for the “Why” question and 10 additional categories for the “Why Not” question. Table 4 shows both the initial list of codes (6 categories for the “Why” question and 6 categories for the “Why Not” question) that emerged from the literature and the final list of codes (that also includes categories which emerged from our data) for each question (12 categories for the “Why” question and 16 categories for the “Why Not” question). ## 3.2. Results

While all responses were coded, we are only interested in responses from the two groups representing intention-behaviour misalignment. Only 68 (6.4%) of 1058 search/deliberators went against their a priori intentions and booked a room on this visit. However, 681 (42.5%) of 1602 directed buyers went against their a priori intentions and failed to complete a booking. The coding frequencies for these two groups of respondents are shown in Table 6. By examining the factors that lead to purchase for search/deliberators, and that lead to non-purchase for directed buyers, we can create an integrated model of factors that distort the intention-behaviour relationship and should be included in future research. We can also compare these factors against those appearing in extant models of other types of online purchasing behaviour, and identify the factors that have received limited attention in prior research.

It is further important to note that most e-commerce studies have assumed that behavioural beliefs always have simple (generally positive) linear efects on purchase-related behaviours. However, Cenfetelli (2004) suggests that factors facilitating and factors inhibiting usage behaviours may not be the same, but rather the two may be qualitatively diferent. Our study provides further evidence for this contention. The concept of causal asymmetry – that is, the idea that the causes leading to the presence of an outcome may be quite diferent from those leading to the absence of the outcome (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008) – has been receiving attention in various disciplines. This is akin to the concept of motivators and hygiene factors in Herzberg’s (1959) two-factor motivation theory of job satisfaction that suggests that presence of motivators (e.g., challenging work, recognition) leads to job satisfaction and absence of hygiene factors (e.g., job security, work conditions) to job dissatisfaction but that presence of hygiene factors will not lead to satisfaction.

Figure 1 presents all of the factors from Table 6 in graphical form. It is important to point out that in this model, purchase intentions and purchase behaviour are both conceptualised as binary constructs (i.e., intentions to buy or not buy; completing or not completing a purchase). The figure indicates that there are six distinct sets of factors that influence the intention-behaviour relationship: (1) behavioural beliefs that act primarily as facilitators to purchasing (meaning that high values encourage purchase, but low values do not necessarily result in failure to purchase, i.e., they are causally asymmetric); (2) behavioural beliefs that have symmetric efects (acting as facilitators when values are high, and as inhibitors when values are low); (3) various types of uncertainty that act as inhibitors to purchasing (meaning that high values result in failure to purchase, but low values do not necessarily lead to purchasing; i.e., they are causally asymmetric); (4) channel preference that is causally symmetric acting as a facilitator if the preference is for online reservations through the site and as an inhibitor if the preference is for reservations via other sites, over the phone, or through travel agents; (5) two types of anticipated regret (Nasiry & Popescu, 2012) that are causally asymmetric; and (6) components of actual behavioural control (i.e., obstacles preventing website visitors from purchasing despite their wanting to do so) that act primarily as inhibitors but in some cases may have facilitating or symmetric efects.

## 4.1. Behavioural beliefs acting as facilitators

TAM-based research (e.g., Gefen et al., 2003a; Lin & Chan, 2009; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006) has consistently posited the efect of perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) on online purchasing as being fully mediated by purchasing intentions. For that reason, purchasing intention is often used as a proxy for the actual downstream behaviour. However, our study found that search/deliberators with a priori intentions not to purchase mentioned PU (7%) and PEOU (16% when including “nonspecific easy/quick”) as factors influencing their decision to instead complete a purchase by making a hotel reservation, suggesting that the website visit caused these consumers to revise their perceptions of PU and PEOU. Typical responses by purchasing search/deliberators referencing PU and PEOU included “easy to use and find locations close to business events”, “quick, convenient”, “all the info is right in front of me, and also site is very user friendly”. Further, lack of PU and PEOU were not considered important reasons for directed buyers failing to purchase (being mentioned by less than 2% of respondents). Thus, we regard these two constructs as having causally asymmetric efects and operating solely as purchase facilitators.

P1: PU will positively moderate the intention-behavior relationship for search/deliberators but not for directed buyers.

P2: PEOU will positively moderate the intention-behavior relationship for search/deliberators but not for directed buyers.

## 4.2. Behavioural beliefs demonstrating symmetric efects

Some behavioural beliefs show evidence of symmetric efects on purchasing behaviour. However, we posit that these efects will be mediated through uncertainty. In other words, these behavioural beliefs will influence purchasing behaviour to the extent to which they reduce or increase the various types of uncertainty in our model. Three types of uncertainty were specifically coded in our study: product uncertainty (or the inability to accurately assess important product attributes and whether the product will meet specific needs) (Hong & Pavlou, 2010, 2014; Pavlou et al., 2007); value uncertainty (or the inability to determine whether the price presented on the website was a good value, whether lower prices could be obtained elsewhere, or what was included in a spe cific rate; possibly analogous to Hong, Wang, & Pavlou, 2016 construct of “valuation uncertainty” in the context of bidding on auction goods); and plan uncertainty (or user uncertainty regarding their specific travel plans and needs with respect to the product). Since plan uncertainty was only mentioned by search/deliberators who followed their a priori intentions in failing to make a purchase; it is not considered further in this study. Our focus on a well-known reputable vendor likely prevented vendor uncertainty (i.e., uncertainty as to whether the vendor is credible and will fulfil its post-purchase obligations, delivering the product or service as promised without acting opportunistically or exploiting the transaction; see Gefen et al., 2003a) from emerging as an important factor in our study. However, prior research (e.g., Pavlou et al., 2007) indicates that we should nevertheless include it in our model for completeness, as it helps to explain the mechanisms by which other factors such as information quality and trust influence key outcomes. We now present our propositions on how behavioural beliefs with symmetric efects impact uncertainty.

Information quality is a key enabler of product diagnosticity, or the extent to which the consumer believes the website is helpful in evaluating a product (Jiang & Benbasat, 2004; Kempf & Smith, 1998; Pavlou et al., 2007). Thus, low information quality is posited here as increasing product uncertainty, since a lack of the right type of information, with the right amount of detail, prevents consumers from assessing whether product characteristics will meet their needs when physical inspection is impossible (Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006). Typical responses relating information quality to product uncertainty in our study included “Not sure on what rooms look like or if they have spas in them”, “I like to speak to a representative to make sure I get exactly what I am looking for. Like bed type non smoking views and rate confirmation”, “Not sure of hotels’ locations meeting a specific need”. On the other hand, detailed rich information leads to a confident assessment as to whether a product meets or does not meet the consumer’s needs. Thus, we posit:

## P3: Information quality will negatively influence product uncertainty.

Information quality, in conjunction with perceived value, is also posited as influencing value uncertainty (see Hong et al., 2016). For example, hotel rate descriptions that detail what the rate encompasses, along with the relevant terms and conditions should reduce uncertainty over the value of the hotel room. A low price (and high perceived value) would result in low value uncertainty since it decreases the probability that a better rate can be found elsewhere. A high price (and low perceived value) would also result in low value uncertainty since it is likely that a better rate can be found elsewhere. A moderate price and moderate perceived value would lead to higher value uncertainty. Thus, we posit that perceived value is nonlinearly related to value uncertainty. Indicative quotes include: “In a short period of search time it was the best rate”,“I want to check prices at other hotels before making any reservation”, “not sure if website always gives you the best price”,“Not sure if it is the cheapest one available” “ Not certain of rate change during stay at hotel; it was not stated what the rate would change to”.

P4: Information quality will negatively influence value uncertainty.

P5: Perceived value will have a nonlinear relationship with value uncertainty, such that both high and low perceived value will reduce value uncertainty, while moderate levels of perceived value will increase value uncertainty.

When a consumer has had no prior interactions with a particular vendor, high-quality information will aid them in making an accurate assessment of that vendor, thus reducing vendor uncertainty. Trust is also posited as influencing vendor uncertainty. Limitations of the web interface that make it dificult to assess trustworthiness, the absence of proven website guarantees that the e-vendor will not engage in opportunistic behaviour such as unauthorised use of credit card and other private information, and consumers’ knowledge of how easy it is for e-vendors to take advantage of consumers (Reichheld & Schefter, 2000) have all made trust in the e-vendor a significant antecedent of online purchasing behaviour (Gefen et al., 2003a; Kim et al., 2009). Prior research indicates that trust acts as an uncertainty mitigator, through mitigating perceptions of information asymmetry, fears of seller opportunism, and concerns over information security and privacy (Pavlou et al., 2007). Typical responses relating trust to vendor uncertainty include: “I trust the site’s reliability more [than Orbitz]”, “I have never had a problem with secure information on the site”, “confirmation arrives right away”, “most discreet”, “I feel comfortable that my reservation is made correctly”. Thus we posit:

P6: Information quality will negatively influence vendor uncertainty.

P7: Trust will negatively influence vendor uncertainty.

## 4.3. Uncertainty (via anticipated action regret) as an inhibitor

Uncertainty emerged as an inhibitor to purchasing behaviour for directed buyers. This is not surprising since the literature has long acknowledged the role of uncertainty in consumer behaviour (Hansen, 1972). In fact, consumers will not make decisions until uncertainty is reduced to a tolerable level (Wilkie & Dickson, 1975). Uncertainty is viewed solely as an inhibitor because high uncertainty will prevent purchasing, but low uncertainty will not necessarily encourage purchasing.

Collectively, product and value uncertainty were mentioned in 5% of the responses for why directed buyers did not carry through with making a purchase. For these individuals, uncertainty resulting from the current website visit appears to have changed their intended behaviour. While none of the search/deliberators completing a purchase specifically mentioned uncertainty reduction, their lack of comments about uncertainty compared to search/deliberators who did not make a purchase (35%; not reported in Table 6) would seem to reflect the fact that they had decided to exit the information gathering phase due to the website visit reducing product or value uncertainty suficiently to proceed with making a purchase. This is in line with recent research (e.g., Hu, Kettinger, & Poston, 2015) that emphasises a value perspective in understanding online consumer behaviours.

We propose anticipated regret (Rivis, Sheeran, & Armitage, 2009; Zeelenberg, 1999) as a key cognitive mediating mechanism via which uncertainty influences purchasing behaviour. Regret is a negative, cognitively determined emotion that a person experiences when she realises or imagines that her present situation may have been better had she acted diferently (Bell, 1982; Loomes & Sugden, 1982; Zeelenberg, Beattie, van der Pligt, & de Vries, 1996). Decision theorists suggest that anticipated regret is only relevant in decision-making when decision-makers have a choice of alternatives and the outcomes of choosing one alternative over another are not known with certainty (Loomes & Sugden, 1982). Website visitors have a range of alternatives available to them, and search costs of evaluating alternatives on websites are lower than those of traditional commerce. Thus, if a website visitor is uncertain as to whether a product meets her needs, whether a better price can be found elsewhere, and whether the vendor will deliver as promised, she may experience anticipated regret of buying (that is, she will consider whether buying during this visit is likely be regretted later on because a better product, price, or vendor is found), which in turn will reduce her chances of making a purchase. Nasiry and Popescu (2012) refer to the regret of buying as “action regret”. There is some evidence of the link between uncertainty and anticipated action regret in responses from non-purchasing directed buyers: “[I did not book] because of the confusion on price for extra people on the room. I didn’t want a surprise charge when I checked in”. Thus, we posit:

P8: Product uncertainty will positively influence anticipated action regret.

P9: Value uncertainty will positively influence anticipated action regret.

P10: Vendor uncertainty will positively influence anticipated action regret.

P11: Anticipated action regret will negatively moderate the intention-behavior relationship

As previously noted, unreadiness was also a frequently mentioned inhibitor of purchasing behaviour for directed buyers. However, most respondents gave no further indication of the cause or type of unreadiness (e.g., they only stated that they were “not ready” or “not quite ready …” In some cases, they did state that they planned to return and complete the reservation “later”

or even “later today”, giving further confidence that they did report themselves accurately as directed buyers. It is not clear whether and how unreadiness relates to uncertainty, although some responses imply a possible relationship between unreadiness and anticipated regret: “NOT quite ready and concerned about making a NON refundable reservation in order to get the lowest rate”. Unreadiness might represent one or more conceptually diferent inhibitors than the others identified in the current study, or it might simply be an undetailed explication of a desire not to purchase right away that, if examined, would yield the same previously identified inhibitors. As such, we do not propose a specific proposition for the efect of unreadiness. Future research could use interviews to examine why non-purchasing visitors (particularly those with a priori intentions to do so) decide that they are unready to purchase.

## 4.4. Low uncertainty and anticipated regret – facilitating vs. inhibiting efects

While high levels of information quality, trust, and perceived value will all reduce uncertainty, low uncertainty does not, in and of itself, mean that a consumer will complete a purchase. Uncertainty may be low because the consumer is convinced that the product is inferior, that the vendor is unacceptable, or that the price is a bad bargain. To better illuminate the relationship between low uncertainty and purchasing behaviour, we focus on the example of perceived value. Many of the e-commerce studies listed in Table A1 of Appendix A have excluded perceived value as a predictor of online purchasing behaviours. Yet, perceptions of value emerged as a critical explanation of purchasing behaviour for search/ deliberators in our study. In fact, value was among the top two most frequently cited reasons for both purchasing and not purchasing. Consistent with expectation confirmation theory (Oliver, 1980), directed buyers did not purchase because the price they found on the website was higher than they expected, while search/deliberators made a purchase because the price they found was too good to pass up, ending the need to continue searching. Typical responses by non-purchasing directed buyers were “Price was too high”, “I cannot aford a rate of \$199 per night plus taxes”, “I found a cheaper rate at another website”, “I did not find the special deal for Northwest Airlines Elite member”. Therefore, it appears that directed buyers had some expectation of a price/special rate/deal in mind that was negatively disconfirmed in the current visit and led to non-purchase despite initial intentions otherwise.

Typical responses by purchasing search/deliberators included “an incredibly good price for the dates I was considering” and “… the price was so low that as Mr. T would say “I”d be a fool not to take it’”, indicating an unexpected good value that ended their search process. Such comments point to the concept of anticipated inaction regret (i.e., anticipated regret of not buying; see Nasiry & Popescu, 2012) as an important motivator for purchasing search/deliberators (Bell, 1982; Loomes & Sugden, 1982; Zeelenberg et al., 1996). In other words, the perceived value was considered unambiguously too good to pass up without regretting it.

A similar situation exists in regard to expectations of product and vendor characteristics, although respondents commented on these factors much less frequently than they did value. For example, one purchasing search/ deliberator stated that they “know exactly what I’m getting … and know when the price is good compared to other options”. Another stated “Why not make a reservation if the price is right and it is a hotel you trust?” Directed buyers who failed to complete a purchase made statements such as “concerned about making a NON refundable reservation in order to get the lowest rate”. We thus posit that low uncertainty will interact with disconfirmation of expectations in predicting anticipated regret (and therefore purchasing behaviour). Specifically, low product, vendor, or value uncertainty will only result in a purchase when one’s expectations have been positively disconfirmed, leading one to experience anticipated inaction regret if they do not buy. Similarly, low product, vendor, or value uncertainty will fail to result in a purchase when one’s expectations have been negatively disconfirmed, leading one to experience anticipated action regret if they do buy. Possible support for the view that value uncertainty in particular will lead to anticipated regret can be found in Hong et al.’s (2016) study of bid auctions in online labour markets, where “valuation uncertainty” leads to the “winner’s curse, or fear of winning at a suboptimal price.

P12: Low (product, vendor, value) uncertainty combined with negatively disconfirmed (product, vendor, value) expectations will positively influence anticipated action regret.

P13: Low (product, vendor, value) uncertainty combined with positively disconfirmed (product, vendor, value) expectations will positively influence anticipated inaction regret.

P14: Anticipated inaction regret will positively moderate the intention-behavior relationship

## 4.5. Channel preference as a symmetric factor

The efect of alternative online channels for completing a transaction (such as making a reservation), and the efect of ofline channels, has received relatively little attention in models predicting online purchase behaviours in the IS literature. Our dependent variable in the model is whether the website visitors made an online reservation. For some directed buyers, channel preference led to a decision to make a reservation via an ofline channel such as the call centre or the hotel directly (“because I like to speak with the call centre’s people,” “Calling the hotels direct makes me feel better”) or via an alternative online channel such as Expedia. This might be particularly true if the online reservation turns out to be more dificult to complete on one’s own than expected (e.g., due to unusual reservation needs). Therefore, a strong preference for an alternate channel may sway a website visitor away from their a priori intention to book through the site (“Made a direct phone call instead”, “I decided to call the hotel instead”). Similarly, a strong preference for booking through the site may sway a search deliberator to make the reservation.

P15: Channel preference will moderate the relationship between intention and purchasing behavior.

## 4.6. Actual behavioural control – symmetric and asymmetric efects

Ajzen (1991) makes a distinction between perceived behavioural control (which influences intentions) and actual behavioural control (ABC) (which prevents behavioural performance despite otherwise intentions). Consistent with the TPB ( Ajzen, 1991), ABC factors in our derived model prevent purchasing despite positive a priori intentions and perceptions. Both product-related factors (e.g., presence/absence of product requirements, lack of product availability) and system-related factors (e.g., system problems, and lack of requisite system functionality) materialised as key ABC factors. However, they each have diferent efects on the intention-behaviour relationship. For example, 31% of search/deliberators who completed a purchase mentioned product requirements as influencing their decision to purchase, but only 2% of directed buyers mentioned it as a factor in their failure to make a purchase. Thus, we regard product requirements as having causally asymmetric efects and operating solely as a purchase facilitator.

Obviously, a website visitor who otherwise wants to complete a transaction will not be able to do so if the product is out of stock or otherwise not available (prod uct availability), or if the website is not working properly (system problems). Further, visitors with special needs may find that they cannot complete a specialised transaction given limitations of the website design (system functionality). This is exemplified in responses to the “Why Not” question such as: “No rooms with two queen beds available”, “There was a problem on the site & it came up ‘page cannot be displayed’”, “Couldn’t get the priority points redemption link to work”, and “Unable to easily book 2 nights stay 1 of which was a reward night”. Such factors were mentioned by 37% of non-purchasing directed buyers. However, while system problems can logically be viewed only as an inhibitor, the other two factors (product availability and system functionality) emerged as having symmetric efects. This might imply that when search/deliberators found an ideal room available, or realised that they could perform tasks on the site that helped speed their decision-making process, they jumped at the chance to go ahead and complete the purchase. Example responses include: “disabled room available”, “compare hotels in the region of my visit”, “all my reward frequent flyer info is already loaded into site”. Overall, product availability accounted for 19% of the reasons given for search/deliberators making an unintended purchase, and system functionality accounted for another 4%. While system problems accounted for only 2% of the reasons given for failure to make a purchase by directed buyers, it is possible that its salience in inhibiting purchasing would be greater for sites that are not as well-designed as this company’s websites. ## 5. Limitations

A limitation of this study is the use of a single company’s websites and the products that can be purchased through these websites, which suggests that the results may not be generalizable to other companies’ websites or products. The focal company’s websites are well-de signed, which may have reduced the impact of system quality factors on the purchasing decision. The fact that the company is well-known, well-reputed, and has a lengthy history might have caused trust to appear insignificant or be mentioned infrequently. Results may dif fer for less well-known vendors, or where reputations have not been established, as trust might play a more important role in these contexts. For this reason, we have incorporated trust as a symmetric factor, as well as vendor uncertainty, into our integrated model of intention-behaviour misalignment (Figure 2). It is also possible that consumer-specified product requirements did not emerge as having substantial inhibiting efects on purchasing due to the size and industry position of the company studied (which might be viewed as ofering convenient locations with adequate amenities for most website visitors). Similarly, product requirements might be less significant for commodity or “search” products such as books than for non-commodity or “experience” products like hotel rooms (Nelson, 1970). Diferences may also exist for products that, unlike hotel rooms, are digital in nature (Hui & Chau, 2002). Thus, future studies that incorporate multiple vendors and multiple types of products should lead to a more nuanced understanding of factors that influence intention-behaviour misalignment in this context.

Despite the limitation of studying a single company, there are many ways in which the results of our study could nevertheless be generalizable to other research contexts. For example, the intention-behaviour relationship is a common component in models for predicting many diferent types of IS behaviours, e.g., technology adoption and continuance. In all of these situations, modifications to initial behavioural beliefs could act upon the intention-behaviour relationship in ways that overrule one’s a priori intentions. We also expect that our findings and propositions regarding perceived value and value uncertainty could generalise to many other purchasing contexts, regardless of whether the product under consideration was an “experience” or “search” product. Specifically, any time that the consumer sees a particularly good deal, they will be likely to experience anticipated inaction regret if they don’t act in contradiction to initial intentions otherwise. Various forms of uncertainty would also be expected to act to strengthen anticipated action regret in any number of behavioural contexts.

The study included only individuals who visited the company’s sites, and not those who might have chosen not to visit the sites for one or more reasons. However, since the purpose of the study was to examine factors that lead website visitors to make a purchase, the sample was appropriate. We were unable to assess non-response bias; thus, our results reflect only respondents to the questionnaire.

There is also a potential for self-selection bias, in that we only used the questionnaires that were completed at the end of the visit. Though statistical tests indicated that the per cent of search/deliberators and the per cent of directed buyers in the respondents who received the questionnaire before the visit and after the visit were not significantly diferent (search deliberators: $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic  =  .1677, p-value  =  .6822, not  significant at $p < . 0 5 ;$ directed buyers: $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic = .3044; p-value = .5811; not significant at $p < . 0 5 )$ , it is still possible that these individuals would have responded diferently to the “Why” or “Why not” question. It is also possible that there is self-selection in who chose to respond to the questionnaire in both the before and after samples. In addition, it is also possible that the classification of respondents could be imperfect, in that some of them might have modified their intentions during their visit. Since we used a self-report survey, it is also possible that self-perception could have led to an overestimation of the efect of some factors.

There may be further relationships between model constructs that have not been explored here. For example, behavioural beliefs such as perceived ease of use, and ABC factors such as product availability, could potentially impact one’s channel preference. In the case of a product or service appearing unavailable via the website, a consumer may choose to call the vendor directly to verify inventory and possibly complete the transaction ofline. If such discrepancies occur frequently enough, the consumer may come to prefer an ofline channel to using the website.

Finally, there are many other variables, not included in the present study, that could be considered as potential controls or even as additional model constructs. For example, an individual who has previous experience with a particular website or e-vendor, and knowledge about online reservations, might be more likely to make a purchase in the face of a “really good deal”, even when their a priori intention was not to do so. This implies that experience might be an additional facilitator to consider in the model. Future research could thus test for diferences between experienced and inexperienced website visitors.

## 6. Contributions and future research directions

We address a theoretical gap in understanding intention-behaviour misalignment in the context of online purchasing. Only 4% of search deliberators made a purchase and primarily only after encountering a very strong motivator – e.g., a bargain that they could not pass up. This motivator possibly reduced anticipated action regret. For directed buyers, however, inhibitors resulted in only about a 50% purchase rate. These inhibitors were either impediments to performance beyond the control of the individual (e.g., no product availability; system problems), failure of the product to meet the consumer’s requirements, or uncertainty. The current study suggests that diferent mechanisms are necessary to reinforce (for directed buyers) and to overcome (for search/deliberators) pre-existing intentions revealing asymmetric efects of factors on the intention-behaviour relationship.

Though the context of the study was online purchasing, the categories of factors that influence the intention-behaviour relationship – behavioural beliefs, uncertainty, anticipated regret, and actual behavioural control – as well as the fact that some act as facilitators and others as inhibitors, some have asymmetric and some have symmetric efects, and the mechanisms via which these influence the intention-behaviour relationship likely generalise to other IS contexts and behaviours. For example, we found that behavioural beliefs can have both asymmetric and symmetric efects, actual behavioural control symmetric efects, and uncertainty an asymmetric inhibiting efect. Further, given that behaviour implies a choice between action and inaction, we theorise anticipated regret as a salient mediating mechanism. Though the specific factors in each category (i.e., the specific types of behavioural beliefs and the specific types of uncertainties) may vary across contexts, these groups of factors are general in nature and relevant for predicting other types of behaviours (such as adoption) and for distorting the intention-behaviour relationship. Future studies can examine these groups of factors and their relative efects on the intention-behaviour relationship in other contexts such as diferent types of behaviours and products to identify boundary conditions and moderators. For example, what types of behaviours (e.g., non-reversible) and products (search vs. experience, costly) would make uncertainty and anticipated regret more salient?

We also contribute to a better understanding of the efect of commonly found factors in IS models on the intention-behaviour relationship. For example, we found that while perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use have asymmetric efects (in that revised perceptions of usefulness and ease of use are reasons for overturning a priori intentions and purchasing), information quality had symmetric efects. Collectively, our study contributes to a better understanding of the intention-behaviour relationship in the context of IS behaviours.

The study also enhances our understanding of factors influencing the choice phase of online purchasing (Simon, 1960), and the underlying rationale for purchasing decisions for goal-oriented consumers (Lin & Chan, 2009). It thus provides theoretical insights for future research examining online purchasing behaviours and actionable guidelines for practice. This is important from a practical perspective as well, since once a customer leaves a website, they may never return. Thus, efectively managing the current visit becomes critical.

Our study has identified several other influences on purchasing behaviour that have received limited attention in prior literature. We now summarise each of these key areas of influence, and suggest directions for future research.

## 6.1. Causal asymmetry and other complex construct relationships

For the most part, models examining behaviour in general, and models examining online purchasing in particular, have implicitly assumed causal symmetry, that is, that the same factors lead both to the presence (e.g., purchase, satisfaction, adoption) and the absence of an outcome (e.g., non-purchase, dissatisfaction, non-adoption). Work by Cenfetelli (2004) and Cenfetelli and Schwarz (2011), as well as some work in management (e.g., Herzberg’s (1959) motivator and hygiene factors), point to the existence of facilitators and inhibitors that exhibit causal asymmetry, that is, the causal antecedents leading to the presence of the outcome may difer from those leading to the absence of the outcome (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008). Indeed, our study highlights the fact that many constructs included in popular IS behavioural models may have more complex relationships than what has been proposed to date. For example, some behavioural beliefs included in prior studies have asymmetric efects while some have symmetric efects. Uncertainty only has asymmetric efects in that it serves as an inhibitor of behaviour (high uncertainty will preclude one from purchasing while low uncertainty does not lead one to purchase). Future research should give more attention to identifying factors that may serve as facilitators or inhibitors of system usage behaviours, or that may have asymmetric or other non-linear efects (Cenfetelli, 2004; Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011; Cheung & Lee, 2009).

## 6.2. The role of uncertainty and uncertainty reduction mechanisms

Another important contribution of this study is uncovering the mediating mechanisms via which behavioural beliefs impact the intention-behaviour relationship. Results indicate that uncertainty plays a particularly important mediating role for purchasing behaviours involving non-commodity or “experience” products (Nelson, 1970), thus, this cognitive influence needs to be explicitly incorporated into models of online consumer behaviour. Recent studies have started doing this to some extent. For example, Pavlou et al. (2007) investigated the impact of perceived uncertainty on purchase intentions, focusing on uncertainty from a principal-agent perspective. Specifically, they viewed uncertainty as a consequence of perceived information asymmetry, fears of seller opportunism, and concerns over information privacy and security. Their study addresses vendor uncertainty and to some extent product uncertainty arising from concerns over seller opportunism.

Dimoka et al. (2012) and Hong and Pavlou (2010) went further in examining the efect of product uncertainty on price premiums in car auctions, identifying two dimensions of product uncertainty: description uncertainty (representing dificulty in obtaining reliable information about a product’s characteristics) and performance uncertainty (representing dificulty in predicting how the product will perform in the future). Hong and Pavlou (2014) added two more dimensions of product uncertainty (product fit uncertainty, representing dificulty in determining whether a product matches the consumer’s needs and preferences, and product quality uncertainty, representing dificulty in determining a product’s true quality), in a study predicting product returns of experience goods. Though these studies enhance our understanding of uncertainty, they do not address the impact of specific types of uncertainty on consumer purchasing decisions and how they impact the intention-behaviour relationship. Nonetheless, these studies, combined with the insights gained from our study, provide a foundation for future research to explore the types and role of uncertainty in purchasing behaviour and in other types of online behaviours. In our study of a specific type of experience product (hotel reservations), description uncertainty and quality uncertainty were occasionally mentioned as inhibitors by respondents (keeping in mind that even within a single hotel, individual rooms can difer greatly in regard to cleanliness and other features, even from one visit to the next); however, product fit uncertainty seemed to be of particular concern (e.g., “Not sure of hotels’ locations meeting a specific need”, “I could not find out if there was enough room for our family to stay ‘comfortably’ in a large room w out being on top of each other”), and often led to directed buyers not completing an intended purchase. It is likely that for commodity or “search” prod ucts (Nelson, 1970), all types of product uncertainty, but particularly description and quality uncertainty, would be far less likely to become a problem resulting in intention-behaviour misalignment.

The literature has suggested a number of trust build ing mechanisms for websites to reduce uncertainty and enable consumers to engage in e-commerce (e.g., Gefen et al., 2003a; McKnight et al., 2002; Stewart, 2003). Similarly, we are proposing that future research identify uncertainty reduction mechanisms to alleviate product and value uncertainty. For example, improving customers’ product diagnosticity (Dimoka et al., 2012) through such means as virtual and functional controls (Jiang & Benbasat, 2004) would reduce product uncertainty. Further, third party assurances (e.g., inspections, history reports, warranties Dimoka et al., 2012) or online product reviews by other consumers can provide “objective” information that can reduce product uncertainty. In addition to such mechanisms, the nature (depth, breadth, content, presentation) of information presented on a website should be re-examined. The role of information quality in determining user satisfaction has been studied in prior research (e.g., DeLone & McLean, 1992, 2003; Seddon, 1997); however, the role of information quality as a determinant of perceptions of whether a product meets consumer requirements has not. Specifically, for B2C e-commerce, the website is the medium by which the product is assessed and the consumer is persuaded to engage in a transaction. Thus, system quality, as well the quality of information provided by that system, play a critical role. Future research should investigate additional conceptualisations of system quality and information quality that take into account the unique role of the information system in B2C e-commerce.

## 6.3. The dual nature of anticipated regret

We explicitly theorise the role that anticipated regret, in regard to both buying (“action regret”) and not buying (“inaction regret”), plays in online purchasing decisions. Though anticipated regret did not emerge strongly in our study (only 4% of non-purchasing search/deliberators and 3% of non-purchasing directed buyers directly mentioned it), we believe that it is nevertheless an important factor in explaining the mechanisms by which uncertainty impacts purchasing behaviour, and is therefore worthy of further exploration. Specifically, managerial interventions should be designed to both mitigate negative efects, and leverage positive efects, of anticipated regret. One example of such an intervention to reduce anticipated action regret is the institution and prominent display of price protections and guarantees (McConnell et al., 2000; Nasiry & Popescu, 2012). Recent research on anticipated afect in relation to the TPB (e.g., Rivis et al., 2009) could provide further direction in this area of study.

## 6.4. Actual vs. perceived behavioural control

Our results illustrate the importance of including actual behavioural control, rather than simply perceived behavioural control, in models of consumer behaviour. Though assessment of perceived behavioural control factors influences the formation of one’s intentions, it is actual behavioural control that can disrupt intention-behaviour alignment and lead individuals to act at odds with their a priori intentions. Only in cases where perceptions of behavioural control are accurate, can it serve as a proxy of actual control and be used for the prediction of future behaviour (Ajzen, 1988).

Though actual behavioural control is a key component of intention-behaviour-based models such as TPB and would intuitively be expected to have a substantial impact on online purchasing behaviour, with few exceptions, it has been largely ignored by the majority of extant B2C studies. Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) empirically derived a set of perceived behavioural control factors by asking 56 faculty, staf, and students what would prevent or facilitate their gathering information or purchasing from a website. Factors elicited included download delay, time resources, website navigability, information gathering skills, monetary resources, product diagnosticity (related to information quality), information protection, and purchasing skills. We emphasise that these are factors the respondents anticipated may prevent or facilitate their behaviour and not what actually prevented them from purchasing as in our study. Therefore, it is not clear whether these perceived behavioural control factors accurately capture actual behavioural control factors. In fact, Pavlou and Fygenson’s factors capture system-related concerns and personal skills, but fail to capture product-related concerns that emerged by far as the most frequently cited reasons for the failure of directed buyers to complete a purchase in our study (33% for product-related vs 4% for system-related). If the aim is to predict actual behaviour, identifying salient actual behavioural control factors is critical to understanding what prevents people from acting in accordance to their intentions. For this reason, an important contribution of our research is to provide an initial set of actual behavioural control factors that have emerged here as relevant to online purchasing. These, together with the perceived behavioural control factors identified by Pavlou and Fygenson, provide a starting point for future research endeavours.

## 7. Conclusion

This study has examined factors leading to intention-behaviour misalignment for two groups of website visitors: (1) “directed buyers” who visit with an a priori intention to purchase on the current visit but end up not doing so, and (2) “search/deliberators” who visit with an a priori intention to only gather information for a specific future purchase but end up making a purchase during the current visit. Qualitative survey data collected from a large pool of actual visitors to the websites of a large hotel management company, all of whom acted against their a priori purchase intentions, suggest several factors that influence the intention-behaviour relationship and yet have received little attention in extant research. These factors include both purchase facilitators and inhibitors, as well as factors having symmetric efects. Our findings point out the importance of further investigating the role of various types of uncertainty, anticipated action and inaction regret, and disconfirmed expectations in distorting the intention-behaviour relationship. The study thus suggests many avenues for future research to improve understanding of both online purchasing behaviour and other forms of intention-behaviour misalignment.
