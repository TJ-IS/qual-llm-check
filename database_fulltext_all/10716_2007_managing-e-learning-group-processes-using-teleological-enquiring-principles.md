# Managing e-learning group processes using teleological enquiring principles

## Abstract

This paper is concerned with managing e-learning communities. It reports on the process and methods adopted in an EU project called MEDFORIST. The challenges of managing this project were considerable because there was significant social diversity amongst the e-leaning community members. The paper outlines some key principles derived from teleology (or, the ‘study of purpose’) that were used to structure some key aspects of the management of the e-learning group processes. These principles were developed and used, in order to structure enquiring activities to inform the design, monitoring and evaluation activities in the provision of e-learning group processes. The paper outlines the use of such enquiry as a foundation for the continued development of the application of e-learning in contexts in which divergent human groups come together in unstructured, non-prescriptive human learning activities. As such, the paper outlines some key methodological requirements for managing e-learning group processes.

## Introduction

he research discussed in this paper concerns the problems and vagaries of developing unstructured problem-based learning processes, when they are mediated by information and communications technologies (ICT). It is based on the experiences of and involvement in a 3-year action-based project that was focused on ‘e-learning and knowledge management’ (termed KM). The project commenced in 2002 and was known as the MEDFORIST project (see http://www.medforist.net). The explicit objective was to help community members to share experience, resources, techniques, ideas, etc., in a way that would help them to undertake their roles, and improve their practice as trainers in e-business. For the purpose of the MEDFORIST project, a view of KM emerged, which defined it as being characterized as a social process of learning through collaboration, based on human interactions, mediated by computer-based technology and human language. This was because, at the outset of the project, it was perceived that the requirements for learning would vary considerably, and would depend on members’ changing perceptions of need over the duration of the project. Therefore, the KM component of the project was considered to require a ‘design’ of sorts, but one that could respond to changing human perceptions of need, centrally focused on social interactions for (broadly) unstructured human problem solving. Thus, the starting point was not with a perceived ‘body of knowledge’ relating to a set of learning needs, but in the possibilities of a process of social collaboration for the purpose of generating learning. It was for this reason that, in the MEDFORIST project, KM was considered to be primarily concerned with the pedagogic and process issues.

It was in undertaking the preparatory work for this project that the literature on KM and related areas were evaluated to help guide both (i) the practicalities of operationalizing the project tasks in hand and (ii) to give guidance on the research process. In undertaking this evaluation, it was concluded that the KM literature was considered to provide a wide variety of different perspectives (see Carter and Scarborough, 2001; Neville et al., 2005) with a plethora of conceptual frameworks and ideas, but largely failed to provide a robust philosophical underpinning or methodology (see Kawalek, 2004). Frameworks that describe characteristics of KM, or discuss cases of successful initiatives, gave some useful insights and ideas to help guide the needs of the MEDFORIST community. However, it was also felt that they did not generally provide sufficient guidance on the actions required to achieve particular purposeful goals or how to evaluate the effectiveness of action towards achieving them. For instance, frameworks that suggest activities such as ‘identify’, ‘create’, ‘acquire’, ‘develop’, ‘share’, ‘use’, ‘retain’ and ‘store’ knowledge (e.g. Probst et al., 2000; CEN, 2004) imply that there is clarity about the function and purpose of such actions. In other words, the ‘function and purpose’ was considered to be an ‘as-given’ in most of the KM literature.

Having now completed the MEDFORIST project, this is considered to be a significant omission in the KM literature, and this in turn helps to explain why there appeared to be weak methodological guidance for the field of practice. If enquiry into ‘function and purpose’ is missing, then the methodological guidance for practitioners will have significant limitations, because it is not guiding them into looking for (or enquiry into) the ‘function and purpose’ in:

\- the behaviours of people undertaking the learning;

\- the design assumptions of those who are involved in setting up e-learning processes and projects; and

\- the management and monitoring of e-learning processes.

If these are missing, it is impossible to accommodate the different and disparate purposeful goals of the members of a given e-learning community. It is a view shared to a certain extent by other writers, who are critical of the KM and e-learning literature, because it fails to take an holistic perspective, which must consider the entire knowledge management process: purpose of the organization, knowledge, technology, learning and people/culture (Rubenstein-Montano et al., 2001: 7/8). Without clarity about ‘function and purpose’, it was considered impossible to (i) manage the practicalities of the MEDFORIST project and (ii) organize effective learning activities and processes. At one level, enquiry into ‘function and purpose’ might appear simple, but the researchers found this to be a much more thorny and problematic area than it first appeared. Yet it process was also considered essential in designing and managing the ‘problem-based’ e-learning approach, which characterized the MEDFORIST project (see also, McLure Wasko and Faraj, 2000; Ardichvili et al., 2003).

The paper is structured as follows: the next section will briefly outline the context of the MEDFORIST project and the relevance (or otherwise) of the related literature; the subsequent section focuses on the historical roots of teleological thinking and discusses why it is important in e-learning contexts. Then, some high-level models that have helped to structure the thinking into e-learning processes are outlined, and how the enquiry into e-learning group process was undertaken. Further sections discuss some of the practicalities of the enquiry and how this informed interventions, in a form of ‘monitoring’ of e-learning group processes, and some of the wider implications.

## Context

MEDFORIST was a project that was set up by the European Union, with the specific objective of supporting and developing e-business education and training in various organizations and institutions based around the Mediterranean region (south-east Europe, Middle East and North Africa). The participant communities were all invited to participate in the project because they were in some way concerned with ‘e-business training and development’. Thus the MEDFORIST community were trainers or educators and were building a range of training and educational programmes that nurture an ‘appropriately skilled local workforce’ in their own countries. Ultimately, the assumption was that this workforce was required to help local businesses and organizations to appropriately exploit the increasingly accessible new ‘e-technologies’. The raison d’etre for the MEDFORIST project was based on certain assumptions. For example:

\- e-business technology exploitation was important for the development of regional economies and communities;

\- on-going learning and knowledge development was important in supporting the exploitation of the ‘e-business’ technologies because both the technologies and the organization development opportunities were rapidly changing;

\- those who are involved in training and development schemes in the region were a potentially powerful source of innovation and support, in the process of on-going and continued growth in the use of the (increasingly accessible) technologies.

A range of explicit project documents were available to inform an initial analysis of the participants in the community (e.g. educational backgrounds, professional experience, current roles, motivations and experience). This analysis revealed the extent of the social, cultural, political and economic diversity of the MEDFORIST community members; it also revealed diversity in terms of academic discipline/perspective, prior knowledge and experience, the perceived needs of practice in their own country, the culture and languages, etc. This diversity was considered to present particular challenges for the KM initiative because it could not be assumed that there was consensus about what might constitute ‘valid knowledge’. It was perceived that there would be significant diversity in terms of constructs used, ideas on methods, assumptions of purpose and language, etc. As a result, the research team perceived that there would be some significant implications: for example, that certain ‘canons’ of KM, such as conversions of ‘tacit’ to ‘explicit’ knowledge (e.g. Blackler, 1995; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2000) or the exchange of explicit knowledge ‘artefacts’, would be much more difficult, or would simply be impossible to achieve in practice.

The challenge of nurturing communities of practice, and, in particular, supporting their learning, has been discussed in detail in other work (see Brown and Duguid, 1991; Lave and Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998; Wenger et al., 2002), and there has been interest in different types of ‘virtual communities’ (e.g. Rheingold, 1993, 2000; Wellman and Gulia, 1996, 1999). The challenges of the MEDFORIST project gave the opportunity to build on such research, by considering how to manage socially diverse groups. Discussions in the academic press reveal questions about the extent to which ICT can realistically support the learning process (Walsham, 2001; Hislop, 2002) and how the motivational challenges presented by communities (particularly with diverse membership) can be overcome (e.g. Kollock, 1999; McLure Wasko and Faraj, 2000). In the consulting and practitioner-oriented discussions, there have been analogous discussions with a range of calls from, for example, ICT that provides a database repositorybased type approach, complimenting such an approach with ‘communities of practice’ of sorts (e.g. KPMG, 2003). Both academic and practitioner literature have tended to agree that ICT-mediated learning communities of practice have great possibilities, but they are, as yet, not delivering those possibilities, and there is much to learn about the processes that are involved. To the MEDFORIST research team, it also appeared that while there was much literature in the field of KM that discusses guiding principles and frameworks, analytical devices or taxonomies (see, e.g., Carter and Scarborough, 2001), the literature is relatively weak in providing methodological guidance for those responsible for such communities (e.g. ‘community managers’). It is this methodological focus that has been of primary concern for the research team involved in the MEDFORIST project, with the broad aim of developing a set of principles that can help guide the practice of managing communities of practice, and to apply and refine them.

An initial evaluation of the literature and practice on KM concluded that (i) the theoretical literature was too ambiguous to be useful in providing methods, and is particularly weak in methodology (i.e. the ‘principles of method’), and (ii) in practice KM was often used to mean a wide variety of different things (see Kawalek, 2004). Furthermore, it was considered that the literature was limited in its contribution and indeed ‘has caused unnecessary and avoidable lack of clarity’ (Farmer, 2002: 5). It was perceived that this resulted in inconsistent practice in the implementation of KM initiatives and, as a consequence, many have failed (see also Ambrosio, 2000). The MEDFORIST researchers’ conclusions about the ambivalence, incongruity and the lack of methodological guidance in KM has since been shared (see, e.g., CEN, 2004), but it seems that the field is still lacking in coherence, and, in particular, research that focuses on guiding and helping in practice. From the literature, it was concluded that perspectives on KM tended to have one of two dominant ontological positions:

\- The ‘knowledge transfer’ perspective , which tends to consider KM as something distinct from e-learning, and the human learning process. In this perspective, knowledge is predominantly considered a ‘product’ or ‘artefact’, and as such assumes that it can be considered as a resource that can be ‘managed’. This resource is then perceived to exist either tacitly embedded in people and manifested by their actions, or explicitly manifested in the documents, products, and processes of human organization (see, e.g., Prahalad and Hamel, 1990; Boisot, 1998). Where knowledge is considered to be tacit and ‘held’ by the individual, in order for this to be useful to others who may wish to apply it, it must be ‘transferred’ from one or more individuals to one or more individuals. Thus, such a perspective often tends to concentrate on enabling individuals to undertake the necessary conversions between tacit and explicit knowledge (Blackler, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2000; Probst et al., 2000). Further, it is sometimes assumed that there was a direct positive correlation between the quantity of ‘knowledge artefacts’ accumulated and shared, and the performance of a human organization (see Newell et al., 2002: 143).

\- The ‘practice’ perspective assumes that what humans know of the world is not discovered, but constructed through our interactions with each other and our environment, and is reflected in models that consider that knowledge cannot be separated from the dynamic process that leads to knowing (see, e.g., Brown and Duguid’s 2001, ‘social-practice’ perspective). Knowledge is always contextual and constantly being updated by new interactions and experiences and so can never be absolute. These interactions occur in cultures, organizations and communities etc., and KM is therefore more concerned with initiatives that focus on the management of human activities and social processes and conditions that encourage the generation and verification of knowledge (see, e.g., Brown and Duguid, 1991; Lave and Wenger, 1991; Wenger et al., 2002; ). This approach has also been called the ‘process perspective’ (Newell et al., 2002).

While it is perhaps over-simplistic to carve up the field of KM based on two simple perspectives with different ontological assumptions, the MEDFORIST research team considered it cognitively helpful when evaluating both the conceptual literature and the details of other case studies (in the construction industry, the military and the humanitarian sectors). As a general principle, and, as a result of this, it was concluded that the ‘knowledge transfer perspective could only be operationalized effectively if there were very high levels of consensus about how to use an ‘artefact of knowledge’ (e.g. a recipe in cooking), and this was generally in situations where there was consensus and conformity of the culture, context, language and meaning. For example, in a closely knit team of workers in an office, it is much more reasonable that the team members can develop a common understanding and meaning of a ‘knowledge artefact’. The consequence of such a conclusion was that (i) it was the social context that justified such a position and (ii) it was limited (or even invalid in some contexts). In the context of the MEDFORIST, it was hard to see how such a perspective could add significant value because of the diversity of the participants in terms of the location, culture, language and prior knowledge. As such, in 2002, the research team embarked on outlining an approach that was more akin to the ‘practice’ perspective.

In a way that is familiar to action researchers, the role of the researchers in MEDFORIST was not only to observe practice, but was to be involved in practice, by undertaking aspects of the operationalization and evaluation of the project. It was perceived that this provided the opportunity to make a further contribution to the theoretical body of work on KM by developing the discourse on the methodological principles for developing KM initiatives in communities of practice. Implicit in developing ‘methodology’ is the need to make clear to potential future users of the methodology its underpinning philosophy and rationale (see also Jayaratna, 1994). In essence, the action research approach enabled the opportunity to construct some foundational principles, to test and further develop these constructs, and to assess their relevance to the practical challenges of KM in the context of the MEDFORIST project. It involved the challenges of design and operationalization of the human processes through which the members undertake actions in attempting to achieve certain common learning goals. This observation resulted in viewing KM as a ‘design discipline’ (see Kawalek, 2004). For instance, it involved designing the organization of resources, activities, etc., that form components of the whole human process, in which technology played a role. The term ‘organization’ is used loosely to refer to a social grouping where humans and their activities form ‘a social arrangement for achieving controlled performance in pursuit of collective goals’ (Huczynski and Buchanan, 2001: 5). Hence, the design process is often based on the assumption that it is possible to identify common objectives from the milieu of purposeful action and behaviour of community members, and that this action can be ‘controlled’ in some way, that is, that certain action will lead to predictable outcomes. ‘Controlling’ sometimes carries with it the connotation that human behaviour is rational and predictable, but the research team took a stance akin to and informed by Dilthey (1961), that action is not based on a logical system of judgements, but perhaps could be considered to be a type of configuration, that integrates facts, values, situational context, etc., which acts to underpin an individual’s action in everyday situations. This type of perspective is shared by other commentators that a ‘ycommon purpose does not imply a shared ‘‘world-view’’’ (Walsham, 2001: 602), and thus ‘any social dynamic will always remain beyond control’ (Flood, 2001: 143). That is to say, a form of mechanistic control is not possible or desirable, but that control of some sort is nonetheless essential if communities of practice are to coordinate action and activities in the performance of achieving purposeful goals.

It was therefore considered that the design and operationalization of KM activities presented significant challenges because of the diversity of purposeful behaviours of the participants, in part due to the diversity of the practice contexts to which their learning was to be applied. It was a natural conclusion that the relevance of teleology was explored. It was also felt that ‘purposeful action’ in the context of those undertaking the action and the search for meanings and reasons for that action, as it relates to members’ own social circumstances and context, was appropriate in a community as diverse as that of MEDFORIST. Further, the research team took that, in looking at learning communities of practice from a teleological perspective, they should neither deny the social and power issues of the context of the members of the community nor deny the social and power context of those who are designing or operationalizing the community (see also Fox, 2000; Contu and Willmott, 2003). Rather, it was a view that was highly sensitive to the particular social context of members, as will be discussed.

From this, it can be seen that a ‘community of practice’ can be conceptualized as an implicit or explicit ‘whole’, united temporarily by a common purpose. In operation, it has various levels of functionality and common purpose (or levels of ‘purposeful dysfunctionality’), of which learning might be considered to be only one, and they also operated in a social/political context and each are required to respond to perceived changes in members’ contexts. The approach assumes that knowledge is constructed and emergent through social interactions. From this perspective, knowledge is always contextual and dynamic due to being constantly updated by new interactions and experiences. The MEDFORIST KM initiative would therefore be concerned with the management of the social processes and conditions that encourage members to critique each others’ perceptions and actions (e.g. by structuring actions, methods, activities, attitudes, pedagogy). This approach to KM has been of some interest, as the traditional ‘knowledge transfer’ and ‘knowledge repository’ approaches have been found to have had limited success (see Ambrosio, 2000; Adamson and Handford, 2002; Britain and Liber, 2004; JISC, 2004). It was an approach in which learning is considered to be rather unstructured and characterized as being one of ‘problem based’ (see also, KPMG, 2003).

## Teleology and processes

The starting assumption about the nature of ‘managing’ such a process in this context was that it would involve clarifying the purposeful objectives of any given e-learning group. The management of a group could then undertake the organizing activities to achieve those specific purposeful objectives. It also involved the ‘monitoring’ of e-learning activities in some way, in order to make interventions to ‘improve’ the conditions for achieving the purposeful objectives. Hence, the method for the management of the KM process was essentially based on (i) enquiry into members’ objectives and needs; (ii) enquiry into members’ perceptions and actions; and (iii) interventions that might help achieve particular objectives. The research in MED-FORIST became primarily concerned with the improvement of the method for the management of the e-learning process.

In the early stages, face-to-face workshops were organized to explore with participants their expectations of the process and the barriers they perceived to their participation. Other project managers and managers of countryspecific sub-communities within the project were consulted at each stage of the planning process. It was during this process that it was concluded that the diversity of the MEDFORIST community implied that the e-learning and KM needed to accommodate small group learning activities (termed ‘learning sets’). Each group process was considered to have specific ‘function and purpose’, and thus the e-learning process had a specific element structure consisting of different activities and actions of members. Thus each process could be explored via some form of teleological process model. These needed to be considered in order to inform the plans and operational choices that were most likely to meet the purposeful objectives. Consultation was concerned not only with proposed models of given learning set activities and their evaluation criteria and methods, but also any fundamental adjustments following initiation and operationalization of them (i.e. this involved some sort of ‘monitoring’ and ‘facilitation’ of them). The models were to assist in an enquiry into the ‘social process’ involved in e-learning, where people were interacting with (i) each other and (ii) other materials (i.e. they are not merely interacting with a computer, as in the traditional ‘computer aided learning’ concept). Groups of people ‘come together’ to achieve specific purposeful goals: they are simultaneously consumers of existing knowledge and, at the same time, are generating new knowledge. Each group has a process, reflected in the models, consisting of various actions, activities, roles, etc. The degree to which a process achieves its purposeful goals is determined by the quality of the process. In the MEDFORIST project, specific technologies were adapted for this purpose, which allowed for different types of e-learning groups, linked to databases of materials that were ‘owned’ by specific groups, sub-groups or all project members. During the process of planning and design, the management team were challenged to put together a conceptual compilation of components, activities and tasks, which were considered likely to provide specific outcome for different groups. The research component was, at the same time, grappling with the most appropriate models, methods and conceptual guiding structures that had the potential to help in this process. It was this search that brought the researchers to consider teleological processes.

It was Singer (1959) who has commonly been attributed with outlining the ‘science’ of functional ‘processes’: his work outlines the shift in analytical methods and imagery, from the physicist’s focus on cause–effect relationships to the biologist, whose focus has been on the function of the components within a functional whole. He uses the term a ‘product-producer relationship’ to depict how ‘transformation’ occurs in a given situation and under a given set of conditions. For example, how does an acorn transform into an oak tree? What are the component elements and conditions that enable such a ‘transformation’? Singer’s work grapples with the scientific basis of observations about the functional components of a transformation, which can, on one level, exhibit spontaneity freedom of action, but can be considered to have a function, in producing something ‘yAre we not then, invited to look deeper into that historically most interesting, but most obscure topic of a teleological interpretation of natural phenomena’ (341). It was Singer’s exploration of the movement in science from observations of phenomena as cause–effect, often expressed as variables and their relationships to each other, to a search for the link between the observations and the functional transformational processes. This has prompted and promoted a long line of literature in the field of management and organization (see, e.g., Churchman, 1968; Mitroff, 1983; Mitroff and Lindstone, 1993). In particular, Singer’s work was taken by Churchman (1971) to examine human organization as transformational processes. Thus, enquiry would be concerned with, on the one hand, the observations of components that had some role in the function of a given process in achieving purposeful objectives and, on the other, the validity of the models themselves as they depict some aspects of transformational processes. Thus, the ‘function and purpose’ can be explored using implicit or explicit models, constructed by an observer. That is, to say,

\- the ‘real-world process’ has purposefulness and its purpose will be fulfilled, given a set of conditions and elements. Thus, the elements themselves will have a purpose that will affect the whole ‘process’. As such, the challenge for enquiry is in the nature of the function of the process and in the nature of the function of the elements and the framing of observation to play a role in refining or refuting the understanding of the process or of its constituting elements;

\- the model used to depict the process is also imbued by purposefulness. The observer will construct a model of the process and will have purpose in doing so. The purpose is often hidden, but can be explored.

It is on these discourses that the MEDFORIST research team focused on the application into a ‘functional process approach to KM and e-learning. Thus, a given e-learning process (e.g. a learning set) can be considered to be modelled as ‘purposeful process’. While the process is not natural, mechanical or biological (see Boulding, 1956; Checkland, 1981; Vickers, 1983; Checkland and Scholes, 1990), they can be usefully considered to be transformational processes, which simultaneously helps to structure enquiry and can be subjected to enquiry. Central to such enquiry is the exploration of ‘models of process’, because it is these that focus the thinking on the purposeful ‘whole’, and the particular set of circumstances, elements, components and conditions that enable the ‘whole’ to fulfil its purpose in ‘transforming’ something (e.g. learning, collaboration, sharing materials, ideas, frustrations, anxieties, etc). The principle characteristics of a teleological process have been formally outlined in ‘Anatomy of Goal Seeking’ (Churchman, 1971). These characteristics can be considered to be a generalized set, which can be used to guide the thinking about many organizational processes, including the KM and the e-learning processes such as those in the MEDFORIST project. As such, the MEDFORIST researchers considered that some of these ideas could guide and help to structure the thinking and the enquiry into the effectiveness of the operation of community e-learning processes and subsequent interventions to improve the processes.

## Teleological enquiry into e-learning processes

Having made the decision to use a form of teleological process modelling, the starting point was to outline some high-level models concerning the design of (i) an ICTmediated learning process that would help the MEDFORIST network members learn to improve knowledge in their own specific operational context and (ii) the ‘monitoring’ or enquiry process for informing how the process was judged to be ‘working’ or otherwise. This is represented in Figure 1.

Each instance of the processes represented in the models can be considered to have transformational qualities, although what they transform and how, in a particular instance, is not defined in the diagrammatic model of Figure 1. Indeed, these types of questions are the starting point for enquiry in a given instance of an e-learning community. Generally speaking, an e-learning process consisted of a number of activities by participants, for example critical reflections, consideration of pre-designed questions, analysing streamed video, undertaking a range of critical analyses, analysing participants’ own actions in practice, reading and analysis of papers, discussion about specific topics, organizing and coordinating activities between members, etc. In MEDFORIST, it was decided that the use of asynchronous discussion areas must be a basis for driving such activities. Each group (or instance) was modelled separately and each was based on a pedagogical approach akin to action learning (e.g. Revans, 1971) and involved bringing together small groups (normally 5–7) of the members focused on topics or issues. Reflexive discussion would be facilitated whose role it was to help the members focus on their purposeful objectives. The use of asynchronous discussion areas required the use of ‘advisers’ who were essentially part of the ‘monitoring system’, whose role it was to assess the purposeful objectives of members and make intervention to assist in the groups’ attempts at attaining their formal or informal objectives.

When the MEDFORIST project started, the purposeful objectives were assumed to be only learning objectives. However, often groups would have a specific task that initially was considered to be classified as a ‘learning objective’. For example, one group developed a case study, which was to be used by all group members. The collection of data for the case involved enquiry and learning, and it also involved other tasks such as ‘writing it up’. In the event what appeared to be ‘non-learning’ tasks often became learning tasks, because when members were undertaking the tasks, the other members were critiquing and making suggestions. There were two significant observations that arose from this. Firstly, a methodological observation that those involved in monitoring were required to have a very clear definition of the purposeful goals of the group process (in Figure 1, termed the ‘e-learning process’). It implied skills in monitoring, which defined the process dynamically (see also Checkland’s (1981) use of CATWOE). The second point was a practical observation that effective monitoring of the e-learning process was commonly a critical element in keeping the process focused on its purposeful objectives. The efficacy of the e-learning process depended on the efficacy of the monitoring process, but this also involved a continual refinement of the purposeful objectives.

Having established the use of teleological models as a basis of enquiry, the first concern was with identifying the ‘function and purpose’ of each group, and group member of each e-learning process. This was in fact a first and primary concern of the monitoring process of an e-learning process. But it was much more difficult to enquire into than at first thought. For example, although the human e-learning process has purposeful characteristics, the ‘purpose’ is often hidden, or assumed, and human behaviour of those who are in some way connected to the ‘process’ may or may not be acting in accordance with the assumed purpose of the ‘process’. For example, the purpose of an e-learning process might be explicitly stated to be to ‘enquire and learn about the opportunities for e-business in the chemical industry in Syria’. But a hidden purpose of an e-learning member might be to ‘develop a new consulting contract in the chemical industry in Syria’. The first of these is an example of an explicitly stated purposeful goal of the e-learning process, and the second is a hidden purposeful goal of a given member of the process. The first might typically be the stated goal of the process and the second might be the hidden purpose of an individual in participating in the e-learning process. Sometimes, the explicitly stated goal and the hidden goal were teleologically incompatible. That is, it was not possible to undertake both simultaneously or that one undermined the value of another in some way.

Given the diversity of the members of the MEDFORIST network, it was found that it was essential within the monitoring process, to encourage the e-learners to engage in expressing explicitly the rather tacit and hidden aspects of the human purpose of the e-learning process. This way, each member involved in any e-learning process could assimilate the language of each other and could indeed help each other in the fulfilment of their purpose in participating in the e-learning process. However, if it was not done, or not done adequately, the researchers noticed a strong tendency towards very swift fragmentation within each elearning group. It was also assumed that this explained why so many e-learning processes have often not fulfilled their promises or expectations in other projects. Further, if a process of expressing explicitly ‘hidden’ purpose was not undertaken and if the groups ‘survived’, the members participated in the learning process out of a sense of duty rather than out of a sense purposefully engaging in a learning process. The researchers surmised that the communication that occurs via the ICT (as opposed to face to face) seems to speed up the ‘fragmentation process’ and thus alterations to the designs of the e-learning process and the monitoring activities were required. The result was to encourage at a very early stage that each member should critically analyse their own purpose in participating and submit to the online group, a synopsis of themselves and their perceptions of their own (‘implicit’) motives. This document was used by the monitoring team and was referred to frequently and amended accordingly during the process of e-learning.

In order to utilize the above effectively, it required that (i) members of the monitoring process could dynamically enquire into the purposeful goals of members of a given e-learning community process and (ii) that they could help members gain the trust required, and familiarity with exploring (and expressing) some of their hidden purpose in participating in the e-learning process. In addition, it was found that other teleological processes were required to be dynamically considered and modelled. For example, the e-learning was of course purposeful but the learning could only be judged as it fulfils another human purpose; that is, it is used in some other teleological process in participants’ practice. The nature of this ‘other process’ was the subject of enquiry and was very different for each member of an e-learning community process and by doing this, the effect was one of enhancing the precision about the nature and function of the knowledge being developed by the e-learning process. This became embedded into the monitoring process and was remarkably useful in a variety of ways. For example, the monitoring of the e-learning process was diversified to consider the function and purpose of the knowledge itself within another human process. This made the research team recognize that knowledge itself could be considered teleological. Furthermore, the function and purpose of the knowledge being developed in an e-learning process may be explicitly stated or it may be hidden. For example, it may be knowledge that serves the function of ‘gaining new consulting contracts to the Syrian chemical industry’; or it might be to ‘gain promotion in the member’s home institution’; or it might be to ‘help prioritize investment decisions in the Syrian chemical industry’; or it might be to ‘get credit for the development of a new course in e-business’; or it might be ‘yto develop a new commercial learning producty’, etc. The point being the e-learning process could not be managed without the enquiry into the function and purpose of the knowledge as it relates to some other process (termed the ‘operational process’ in Figure 2). The examples given are expressed as simple goals, but each can be considered teleological processes and can be subjected to enquiry using teleological enquiring principles in much the same way as any other process. In Figure 2, we use the general term ‘operational process,’ which avoids detailing any particular instance of a process and, as such, simply states that this is in fact an important element in the enquiry and for the management of the e-learning process.

Given that the intended purpose of the e-learning process was perceived to be the improvement of another ‘operational process’, this could only be done by considering how the two processes could be effectively integrated. A given e-learning community group process was required to accommodate each members’ ‘operational process’, and this meant some significant challenges to their design in terms of coordinating activities, actions, learning experiences, materials, readers etc. It also meant that the hidden and implicit purpose was required to be explored (and as far as possible, explicitly expressed) by each community member, and this exploration was designed into the group process. Following Churchman (1971), such enquiry was recognized to have the potential of expanding much further. For example:

How will the e-learning process be judged as to whether it achieves its purpose’, and how will perceptions of this judging process affect its operation? This aspect of enquiry concerns members’ assumptions about what constitutes ‘success’ or ‘failure’ and their perceptions of how performance of the e-learning process is measured. These perceptions will affect behaviour in the community and hence its operation. In the design of physical things, the activity of ‘designing’ implies that criteria are made explicit or are ‘as given’ at the design stage. These are subsequently imposed by monitoring and evaluation activities. However, in e-learning, the purpose and criteria cannot be taken as given, nor easily be made explicit as they may be perceived differently by each of the community members; each member may behave differently according to their perceptions of explicit and implicit purpose and perceptions of performance measures, thus leading to both desirable and undesirable effects.

Who are the perceived clients of the e-learning process, and how does this perception affect its operation? It may be considered to be serving a particular identifiable group (i.e. ‘clients’); the clients themselves can be considered to be the learners, or other groups who use the knowledge within their own sphere of influence (e.g. ‘clients’ of the operational system as depicted as process 2 in Figure 2). If the learning is about medicine, the operational process (2) in which the knowledge is applied is in the operation of the hospital and the clients are the patients. However, often there are multiple ‘client’ groups that are required to be ‘served’, and sometimes this situation can cause difficulties in identifying choices, priorities, decisions and actions.

What should be the component activities, and how can these be organized to achieve ‘success’ for its clients? An instance of a MEDFORIST community process can be considered to have component activities that are purposeful, and these components can be judged as contributing (or otherwise) to the purposeful ‘whole’. In an e-learning process, components can be tasks, activities, researching, evaluating ideas and concepts, abstracting the lessons used from application in practice or a hypothetical practical situation. These will involve a configuration of interactions between people, involving roles (informal or formal), communications, control mechanisms, power relationships, facilitated by resources, for example technology, information, data etc. The particular configuration of such elements determines the outcome of the e-learning community process, and thus it is important that they are subjected to critical analysis. However, the compilation of components must be considered and organized with the objective of maximizing the purposeful learning objectives and the purpose of members’ application of the resultant knowledge in their own particular contexts.

How are internal and external environmental conditions likely to affect the operation of the e-learning process? The e-learning community and process will operate in a context of, for example, social, economic, political and cultural factors. This was very important in MEDFORST because of the diversity of learners involved in the project. But in a given instance of an e-learning community process, there will be identifiable factors, which are beyond immediate influence, but which operate to constrain what is feasible and what is not in terms of the specific configuration of component activities and elements. In the case of MEDFORIST, the diversity of culture was something that determined certain aspects of the designed activities of any one e-learning community process. Cultural diversity could be considered a ‘strength’ or a ‘weakness’ for a given process, or set of purposeful goals of the process, but are outside the influence or control of the designers of the process. Other environmental conditions included existing knowledge, skills, experiences of a given community’s members, the pressures exerted by their own institutions’ time constraints. Such factors affected the way in which the learning processes were operationalized in practice.

How do its decision-making processes impact on the e-learning process? The operation of a given e-learning community process will include decision points and decision making to take action, particularly when its operation deviates from its original purpose. The deviation may or may not be ‘desirable’ from a monitoring perspective, but judgement about whether to take action in response to the deviations is desirable, perhaps either to bring about changes (e.g. to ‘improve’ an e-learning process), to agree to a set of purposefully designed learning objectives, or to instigate purposely designed change to the learning objectives, and hence to re-define and re-structure the process in some way.

What is the ‘design intention’ of a given e-learning process? On designing or re-designing activities of an e-learning community process, there will be a formal or ‘asgiven’ purpose of a given e-learning community. This can be expressed in order to clarify among the members, and to consider how to maximize benefit to an explicit client of an intended e-learning process. The explicit ‘design intention’ may or may not be compatible with an informal or hidden ‘design intention’, and where incompatibility exists, the explicitly stated purpose is unlikely to ‘succeed’. However, this does not mean that the implicit is invalid. However, in order to gain insight into the efficacy of the design intention, it would be considered necessary to subject both the explicit and implicit to the enquiring process and to reconsider the design in the light of the refined perspectives resulting from the enquiry.

How does the e-learning process evolve? The operation of an e-learning community process will include decision points and decision making, which will make the community evolve and change, and which will cause it to deviate from its original formal or informal ‘design intention’. The deviation may or may not be ‘desirable’, and/or may or may not be necessary, but judgement on the deviations is desirable. The decision points will affect whether or not the community members are individually able or willing to continue to participate. Being able to recognize such decision points and to evaluate the desirability, and the likely effect that they may have on the operation of the process was considered an essential aspect of the management of the e-learning community processes.

How stable is it? The design and the operationalization of a given e-leaning process must be sufficiently stable so that its purposeful design intention is realizable, or deviations from it can be judged as having a re-defined ‘design intention’ without having to undertake a complete re-design. In many instances, there was insufficient clarity about the ‘design intention’, and thus community e-learning was impossible or at least haphazard. In these circumstances, the management of the community e-learning was considered impossible. However, it was possible to utilize teleologically based enquiry in order to make judgement about the feasibility and stability of the various instances of e-learning processes.

## Enquiry and intervention

As in all research of this type, there were findings that were directly derived from the enquiry, and there were emergent ideas and principles that resulted in the application of the enquiring process. The ‘design intention’ of the research was ultimately to develop precision in the thinking, in order to effectively make intervention in e-learning processes, and resulted in the development of (i) a set of enquiring principles and (ii) empirical observations derived from the application of the enquiring principles. The enquiry had the function of helping inform the thinking about the feasibility of particular e-learning processes, or how to focus or shape them in some way. Since the research was focused on both developing and evaluating the teleological enquiring activities, it was necessary to find some way of evaluating the enquiry as it contributed to the process of managing the e-learning communities (i.e. ‘monitoring’). This resulted in the requirement for the research team to develop clarity about the process of monitoring and intervention in the e-learning communities, and the role of the enquiry made within it. This is outlined in a broad conceptual sense in a set of teleological process models in Figure 3.

In doing this, it was very important (and difficult) to maintain a conceptual clarity during the undertaking of the different roles (depicted as two teleological processes, 3 and 4). Some may argue that simultaneously undertaking and fulfilling both roles would produce too many inconsistencies and contradictions. However, if a researcher is to move beyond an observational role, managing the ambivalence is sometimes inevitable, particularly in action research or ‘mode 2’ type research (see Gibbons et al., 1994; Starkey and Madan, 2001). Despite the vagaries and difficulties in the research process in operationalizing an evaluation, there were some important conclusions drawn for: (i) the monitoring process; (ii) how enquiry was structured based on the teleological principles; (iii) how interventions were drawn from the enquiring principles; and (iv) the consequences of the interventions. These are summarized in this section.

In man-made mechanical processes, intervention is intended to lead to predictable change often leading to stability within a changing environment. Interventions can be guided by measuring the deviation of performance from criteria established to denote ‘successful’ performance. After Checkland (1981), it is also possible to consider the monitoring as a ‘system’ and having teleological characteristics, which are transformational, involving a specific set of integrated ‘chunks’ of human activities (decisions on the criteria of judgement, application of the criteria and taking action as a result of the application of the criteria), as depicted in Figure 4.

During the research, it was acknowledged that a process of monitoring was itself purposeful. As such, it too could be subjected to analysis, using the teleological enquiring principles as outlined by Churchman (1971). Thus, during the research, the monitoring process was subjected to critique that resulted in (i) the analysis of the basic assumptions, constructs and activities of monitoring and (ii) the findings resulting from their application in context. This resulted in a significant refinement of certain aspects of the monitoring of e-learning activities, as follows.

Beer (1981) outlines monitoring as a ‘technique for organizing control in a system of proliferating variety’ (53).

He describes the use of heuristics and algorithms in which humans work their way towards ‘unspecified and unspecifiable’ goals. His work has been analogous to the situation in the management of MEDFORIST e-learning processes whereby the explicit purposeful objective is to improve an e-learning process that improves the practice and learning of its participants, but there exists no idea what constitutes optimum performance of any given e-learning process. In such circumstances, how can it be possible to organize activities towards achieving such a goal if it cannot be specified clearly? Beer explains this process quite admirably using the example of an individual’s attempt to walk up a hill towards the peak, when the peak cannot be seen and the route is not specified. In this context, we could apply a set of assumptions in a form of heuristic (e.g. ‘keep going up’). Hence, there is a general rule for behaviour that leads

In operationalizing community processes, the enquiry was (to a large extent) focused on the evaluation of the community process in achieving informal or formal goals. Further, the differences between the models as conceived at the beginning of a community process, and those arising during the operation, were seen to be an important aspect of the learning arising from the research. The diversity of culture, language, politics, prior knowledge, etc. was always predicted to be problematic. Achievement of the intended transformation of the MEDFORIST project as a whole was also a difficult challenge because it involved the effective integration of the activities of a number of different sub-projects, countries, institutions and individuals. However, most importantly there were also varying levels of expectations and commitments to learning within the community network, and this was reflected in the models of the operational learning processes. In the initial stages, many community members stated that their involvement in MEDFORIST would facilitate continuous professional development, improving their practice as individuals through networking with others in the community. Indeed, the community members largely considered their diversity as a strength in that it would provide the catalyst for new ideas (and change). However, there was a wide variation in members’ expectations with regard to the nature of a community process with some expecting more opportunity to ‘learn’ existing theory. Translation of these commendable purposeful goals was sometimes not substantiated by subsequent action and resulted in significantly variable participation. Thus, in the operationalization stages, there was much more complexity than anticipated, which stemmed from a number of influential issues that were particularly pertinent in the MEDFORIST project. For example:

\- the teleological incompatibility between such assumed learning objectives and the realities of having to undertake action in the learning process dictated by the project as a whole, and the conditions of the funding;

\- the dominant expectation of learning as a passive pedagogy of ‘communication facts’ more aligned with the ‘knowledge transfer’ perspective, as outlined in section 1 of this paper, not of the more difficult task of evaluating their use in practice, and in a critically reflexive action-based manner. Members were therefore expecting ‘experts’ to structure the learning process and community members to be passive in collecting data (assumed to be ‘knowledge’). The reflective elements of process and the choices available to construct and join groups were rather alien to the community members;

\- the client group were often the EU funders, as perceived in the minds of the community members, and not the members themselves. It meant that some community members sometimes participated prescriptively, ‘going through the motions’ of doing what was considered necessary to fulfil their assumptions about the expectations of the EU funders, or sometimes would assume that learning tasks should be set by the learning set adviser, rather than by themselves.

Hence, the optimism in the design work was dampened somewhat by the messiness in practice: while there were members who expressed interest, purposefulness etc., they were often looking for benefiting from other community member efforts. The initial difficulties in the operationalization of the ICT-mediated learning sets were considered an important raison d’etre for continued research, and in particular, the different management challenges involved in operationalizing them. On the re-modelling and re-designing the operation of the communities, it was felt that there needed to be a stronger human process of ‘monitoring, control and intervention’. For example, one issue identified for improvement was in the role of the learning set adviser. This role had been formulated at design stage as one that was to act like a chair of a meeting, structuring discussions, keeping the process focused, making certain interventions to keep to the agenda etc. It was decided that this was an insufficiently interventionist role, given the particular challenges faced within the MEDFORIST learning process. This role was re-modelled, to assume a greater level of responsibility in the ‘monitoring, control and intervention’ process than it was initially assumed to entail and to use the enquiring principles as outlined.

In the case of MEDFORIST, the purposeful goal of a monitoring process was considered to apply pressure on community members, without being confrontational and without them being aware of it. A range of interventions were considered as a component of the monitoring process. For instance, (i) enquiring activities designed to engage or challenge members; (ii) changing the group membership at specific times and under particular conditions and (iii) changing the structure and order of tasks and activities. The range of interventions are (in part) the subject of new research currently being undertaken, based on the premise that the interactions in ICT-mediated community learning processes are providing challenges for constructing interventions, but are also providing new opportunities and new ways to operationalize and intervene in the community process.

The application of the enquiring principles to the consideration of monitoring as a teleological process with a functional transformation (‘T’), a transitional state and outcomes or effects yielded a number of very important learning points, some specific to the MEDFORIST, and some more generalizable. The most pertinent of these are summarized below.

\- There are often ambiguities in what exactly it is that is being monitored. This was considered in part to be a result of a lack of definition and common vision of the purposefulness, the community process as it was originally defined. Thus, skilful monitoring and intervention activity (e.g. the actions of a learning set adviser) was to be able to both identify common purposeful goals that lay beneath the stated goals and equally help learning set members to define and refine their own purposeful objectives. This implied challenging them and also expecting them to be able to reflect on their own purposeful goals and actions, both in their operational context and/or in their actions, as part of the community. In practice, this meant initiating a range of activities, starting from the notion that humans in general have the capability of ‘thinking about their own thinking’. Encouraging critical reflexivity in the learning process demanded a range of different designs and interventions in their operationalization.

\- For those involved in the monitoring and intervention process, it is important that they are able to analyse what they themselves are selecting as issues and criteria in making judgement. This acknowledges that it is impossible to take any one community process and analyse everything (all events, attitudes, communications, roles, power dimensions, etc.). In other words, anybody involved in monitoring must be selective, and the process of selection could be considered to be a form of attenuation (see also Beer, 1985). If a learning set adviser were to be to be given the role of monitoring (involving attenuation and judgement) then it must be possible to subject the attenuation process to critique (either by the adviser themselves in a form of critical reflexive thinking or by a third party observer/evaluator). The purpose of subjecting it to critique would be to help refine the understanding of the (most commonly implicit) criteria used to inform intervention.

\- When a community process is being monitored via the application of implicit criteria, community members will change their behaviour to fit the monitoring criteria that they think are being applied (which is exactly what the monitoring is intended to do). However, this does not make those actions appropriate. Sometimes, the criteria perceived by the community member is not the same as that perceived by those undertaking the monitoring. Or the behaviours and actions are influenced only at a superficial level (i.e. community members espouse action that panders to the perceived criteria, but behaviour and action do not fundamentally alter). Thus, if a process of monitoring is implemented, then the implicit intended effect is of central importance in an evaluation.

## Conclusion

In practice, there were big challenges in operationalizing the ICT-mediated community processes in the MEDFORIST project. Reflecting on earlier research in other projects, it was realized that the difficulties were not unique to MEDFORIST. The research has used a set of enquiring principles and concepts originating from teleology, which have been refined over the period of research, and the research team now considers them to be invaluable for future work initiating, developing and nurturing ICTsupported learning in communities of practice. The application of the principles and the concepts will always question the underlying purpose and viability of a community process in meeting its purposeful goals, and in practice this may mean taking difficult decisions to change the nature, shape, form, roles, relationships, etc., within a community; it may also result in cutting the investment in a community because it is deemed unviable. In situations where organizations are making investments in such communities, it is essential to be able to make considered judgement about their value, viability, design, monitoring and other process issues. This paper has outlined how questions about value, viability and process can be understood by reference to teleology as a foundational body of existing knowledge and how it might contribute to the challenges of managing such communities. The form of research in this paper, which has been action-based, meant that university researchers were being charged and challenged to bring into the project, their conceptual processes, methods and approaches, and apply them into a particularly challenging context. The generalizable methodological lesson is that the modelling and the underlying enquiring principles of the models, in the case of MEDFORIST, has given significant insight into the designs and the operationalization issues in practice and also given a way of structuring an explanation for the tensions in practice. The vision for MEDFORIST was in part predicated on the assumption that ICT will play a more important role in community-based human affairs in the future. It has been to the credit of the EU funders and project managers that they had such foresight in forming teams across the continent that could both develop communities of practice requiring the support of ICT to function, but more importantly perhaps, to develop a research strand to the project, in order to develop human knowledge about designing, operationalizing and evaluating ICT-mediated community learning processes, which is probably going to be of increasing importance to the human race in the very near future.

## About the authors

Diane Hart joined the University of Sheffield in 2003 as a Research Associate in the Management School, and had been the primary researcher in the MEDFORIST project. Her doctoral studies are concerned with developing a systems approach for the design and management of ICTsupported learning initiatives, which are aimed at ‘improving practice’ in organizations. This work is currently influencing the internal university processes in e-learning, as Diane has taken a role as an educational adviser to provide support, advice and guidance to academic communities in promoting best practice and the effective integration of technology and multi-media learning resources into the learning process. Diane holds an undergraduate degree in Geology and a Masters degree in Internet Computing.

John Paul Kawalek’s professional background is in information systems (IS) and management consulting. He lectures and researches in Systems Theory, Change Management and Management Consultancy at the University of Sheffield. He has authored over 50 academic papers, and has recently published his first book entitled The Art of Organisational Problem Solving: how to think about what we are doing in management (published by the Edwin Mellen Press). He is partner in an international consulting organization in the US, which specializes in rapid organization improvement, using a method called ‘INSPIRE’. John holds three degrees in different disciplinary areas. His undergraduate degree is in History & Economics, his master’s degree is in Computer Science and his Ph.D. is in Management. He is chartered in his professional role an as an IS practitioner.
