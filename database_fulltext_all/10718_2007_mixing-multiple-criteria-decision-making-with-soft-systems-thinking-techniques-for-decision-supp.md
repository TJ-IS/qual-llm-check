# Mixing Multiple Criteria Decision Making with soft systems thinking techniques for decision support in complex situations

D. Petkov <sup>a,⁎</sup>, O. Petkova <sup>b</sup>, T. Andrew <sup>c</sup>, T. Nepal <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, Eastern Connecticut State University, Willimantic, CT 06226-2295, USA

<sup>b</sup> Central Connecticut State University, USA

<sup>c</sup> University of Johannesburg, Johannesburg, South Africa

<sup>d</sup> Durban Institute of Technology, Durban, South Africa

Available online 19 April 2006

## Abstract

This paper shares experiences of the authors with combining Multiple Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) and techniques from soft systems approaches for decision support at particular stages of complex problem solving. These are based on their involvement in three projects within the Information and Communications Technology sector. The justification for the combined use of MCDM and separate techniques from systems thinking is discussed. The contribution of this paper is in the demonstration of how combinations of methods that are parts of soft systems methodologies and MCDM may support multiple perspective representations of complex managerial problems, and in the lessons learned from the three cases. Published by Elsevier B.V.

Keywords: MCDM; Soft systems thinking; Multi-methodology; Multiple perspectives

## 1. Introduction

Simon et al. [43] point that “setting agendas and framing problems are two related but poorly understood processes that require special research attention” (p. 30). This is very important for a particular class of ill structured problems that are known in the Multiple Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) and the systems thinking literature as “messy problems” [1,42]. Messes are ill structured problem situations made up of highly interdependent problems [22]. Such situations are related also to wicked problems which are characterized by being interconnected and complicated further by lack of clarity about purposes, conflict, uncertainty about the environment and societal constraints [22]. The characteristics of the problems in the three cases described later correspond to the above descriptions and served as one of the motivating factors for this research.

Decision Support Systems (DSS) have been a major research area in the Information Systems (IS) field for almost 40 years. Work in the DSS field can be grouped in various ways but for the purposes of this paper we will provide a classification derived from [11,47]:

• computer based automation of problem solving heuristics;

• computer based model development and manipulation;

• problem formulation in organizations.

Most of the publications in DSS have been oriented towards the first type of tasks (e.g., [13] and others).

These were associated with the implementation of various algorithms of Operations Research techniques and the development of Artificial Intelligence based tools [42] and their integration with DSS [23].

The second strand of DSS work is not as widely researched as the first one. Issues of problem definition support systems and the validation of models in them are discussed in [29]. Mental models, interaction and representation aids in group problem structuring and formulation are the subject of [30]. Significant attention was focused on integrated modeling environments [51]. This type of DSS work is still valid today with advances in multimedia and computer interface design. It involves also a growing stream of research in data mining and data warehouses [50].

The complexity of problem situations in organizations is better reflected within the scope of the third strand of development of decision support systems mentioned above. A long term research program on studies in managerial problem formulation systems is presented in [11]. One of their conclusions is that as the use of such systems extends into the most abstract phases of the problem solving process, the interaction between the user and the system becomes more critical (p. 422).

Alter [2] points out, “decision support is not about tools per se, but rather about making better decisions within work systems in organizations” (p. 320). Alter's focus on decision support instead of DSS has certain similarities with the third strand of activities in DSS research above. Our paper deals with issues related to decision support in complex messy problems and the third strand of DSS research.

An elaborate methodology for problem formulation in inquiring organizations based on Churchman's work [10] and Linstone's idea of multiple perspectives [26] is presented in [47]. It uses a mix of soft (cognitive mapping, qualitative modelling and inquiring systems) and hard (systems dynamics) tools to analyse and close the differences between the perspectives of the analysis. That work is an extension of the idea by Mitroff and Linstone [33] for the need to close the gaps between various perspectives, especially when they may be conflicting. The results in [47] are a significant contribution in the third strand of Decision Support Systems research mentioned above.

The authors applied a mix of separate methods from several soft systems thinking methodologies and Multiple Criteria Decision Analysis in three different cases in order to reflect the conflicting nature of the criteria guiding decision makers in complex situations. That is another way of strengthening the Multiple Perspectives view of a complex problem. Another reason for the incorporation of MCDM into our decision framework was the fact that there are various software products implementing such approaches in the tradition of DSS research. Thus our approach aimed also at bridging the past achievements of the DSS field with recent development in soft systems thinking.

The mix of methods in the three interventions described in the paper varied slightly and is different from the methods used in [47]. The contribution of this paper is in the demonstration of how combinations of methods that are parts of soft systems methodologies and MCDM may support multiple perspectives representations of complex managerial problems, and in the reflections on the lessons learned from the three cases. The relevance of our ideas is shown through three cases of complex problem solving in the Information and Communications Technology (ICT) Sector in South Africa. To the best of our knowledge these are the first applications of a mix of individual methods (not whole methodologies) to software development management and rural telecommunications evaluation and planning. Judging from a survey of cases of mixing methods in multimethodology interventions [34], it is the first reported combination of three or more methods involving several soft systems thinking approaches and MCDA in one intervention. For further technical details that are not discussed here, see [3,35,37,38,40].

The research question that we are pursuing in this paper is: How can multiple perspectives be developed through a combination of MCDM and soft systems thinking methods? It is illustrated through the descriptions of the three cases of applications of mixing methods from different methodologies to real life ICT complex problems and the justification for that. Their characteristics are presented in the next section, followed by considerations for the potential applicability of the methods that we applied in the three interventions, details on how they were conducted and some reflection on the lessons learned from them, which is followed by a conclusion.

## 2. The nature of the three problem situations

The first case was at a large South African metal processing plant and was related to the management of IS development and the improvement of the understanding of the factors affecting software development productivity within a particular project. In the late 1990s the plant initiated an expansion. The aim was to increase its product line almost three times and to reduce the lead time in fulfilling customer orders. Achieving such flexibility was impossible without a significant modernization of its information systems. The software project was complex; it required a lot of expertise that was not readily available within the company. The route of outsourcing was chosen. An American company, a leader in shop floor automation software, was one of the outsourcing providers. A local IT company with extensive expertise in development of Oracle based production planning systems was the second vendor. The goal of the project was to integrate the company-wide production planning process with shop floor automation, a unique task that was not addressed before in that industry by anyone else.

The project experienced at a particular point difficulties and delays which were hard to explain. It was at that point that one of the authors, with the assistance of another, got involved in an intervention aiming at identifying what factors drove software development productivity of the providers. Two groups of stakeholders, representing the client and the outsourcing providers, were formed. A series of workshops led to uncovering the differences in their values and to the realization of possible ways to improve the communication between the parties.

The second and the third cases are interrelated as they deal with issues about rural telecommunications infrastructure in developing countries in general and South Africa in particular. The practical needs for improvement of rural telecommunications in developing countries are calling for new approaches. Planning of telecommunications services normally follows standardised technical guidelines, which reflect poorly the recognition of local factors. Most of these countries still have a regulated telecommunications market with a single service provider for land telephone lines. It can be noted that a number of developing countries, including South Africa, recently have changed their political system. A common feature of all these countries is the necessity to address the needs of previously disadvantaged communities by inter alia improving the provision of rural telecommunications. The latter is seen not just as a technical problem but as a development problem as well. A rural telecommunications system is a complex system because it cannot be fully understood and evaluated without understanding the relationships between it and the communities and the dependence of its developmental impact on the availability or lack of basic parallel infrastructures such as, power, water, and transport infrastructure.

The second case study was about evaluation of the existing facilities and needs for telecommunications infrastructure within a particular rural community. It is the first step before any planning for future modernization. It was necessary first to establish means for a dialogue between the local community and the telecommunications service provider. This dialogue needed to involve all stakeholders in order to have legitimacy. It needed to be simple enough to capture the thoughts and feelings of non-specialists from a diverse background and help at the same time to shape the views of the experts in rural telecommunications. Since there is little published research on the assessment of rural telecommunications infrastructure, and most of it focusing on the one sided, technical problem, the challenge in the second investigation was to provide a holistic, systemic evaluation framework for rural telecommunications infrastructure.

The third case was concerned with the planning and provision of the most appropriate telecommunications systems for a particular rural area in a developing country. In general, the successful design and deployment of a telecommunications infrastructure does not depend solely on just technological imperatives. It is affected by socio-economic, political, cultural, market liberalization, legislative and other factors. Hence our third intervention was about the development of a holistic planning framework for rural telecommunications. The need for it was felt to a degree also by the planners at the telecommunications service provider, judging from our conversations with them in the initial stages of the third project. Our work helped them enrich their planning practices.

The three cases were unique with no existing precedents in terms of scope, goals and environmental constraints. That demanded seeking solutions beyond the existing theoretical boundaries of the specific application fields (information systems development and telecommunications planning). The characteristics of the problems of concern involved many criteria, qualitative and quantitative factors. Hence they could be classified as complex and messy problems, following [1,22].

The three situations had unique features due to the human element involved and the unavoidable role of subjectivity in them. The only traditional Operations Research (OR) field that recognizes the role of subjectivity is Multicriteria Decision Making according to [44]. It has been also emphasized in another OR field known also as Problem Structuring Methods or Soft Operations Research [39], part of which are soft systems thinking approaches [22]. That was the starting point for our further work described in the following section.

## 3. An overview of some appropriate methods for messy problems

## 3.1. On the suitability of multiple criteria decision making

With the increase of complexity of problems, addressed within Operations Research/Management Science projects, decision-making goals become increasingly imprecise [44]. According to the same source, “the key philosophical departure point defining Multiple Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) as a formal approach to types of problem solving (or mess reduction), lies in attempting to represent such imprecise goals in terms of a number of individual (relatively precise, but generally conflicting) criteria” (p. 569). Multi-Attribute Decision Making (MADM), known also as Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) [19] is a branch of MCDM. According to [25] it is associated with “problems whose number of alternatives has been predetermined. The decision-maker is to select/prioritize/rank a finite number of courses of action” (p. 399). The situations described in this paper fall under this category.

Lootsma [27] concludes in a note about the MCDA field that besides some signs of formation of schools of thought around the Multi Attribute Utility Theory (MAUT), Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Outranking (French) methods, there is not “a shared view on how human preference and human value judgements should be modelled”. An analysis of the literature of these three schools indicates very little evidence for attempts to discuss in a single publication ideas from more than one of them, with few exceptions like [5,7]. A concise review of the three MCDA approaches can be found in [46]. However the various schools of thought within MCDA continue to be isolated from each other.

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was initially developed as a multicriteria decision support technique [41]. It assumes that a problem can be structured as a hierarchy of an overall goal and a set of conflicting subgoals that may require quantitative and qualitative information. These are used to evaluate a predetermined number of alternatives. All the elements in a particular cluster of the hierarchy are compared in a pairwise fashion with respect to their contribution or importance for the root of the cluster. More details on the AHP procedure can be found in [41]. The resulting local priorities within each cluster of the hierarchy are synthesized into global priorities reflecting the priority of the alternatives with respect to the overall goal, taking into account all subgoals. According to [12], the

Analytic Hierarchy Process focuses on the choice phase of Simon's model of decision-making. It seems that such a statement makes unnecessarily narrow the scope of AHP. As far back as 1982, AHP was applied for the generation of options in decision analysis problems [4]. The results in that source are relevant in our opinion both for the Intelligence and the Design phases in Simon's model of decision making. AHP may support also the Implementation phase of that model through the prioritization of activities/factors involved in a problem.

According to a comparative analysis of MCDM approaches in [36], AHP was considered easy to understand, with the exception of the eigenvalue calculations used to derive the local priorities of the elements in a cluster of the hierarchy and which remain actually hidden from the end-user. The same authors found in their research that Multi Attribute Utility Theory was cognitively more difficult to understand but the Simple Multi Attribute Rating Technique (SMART), which belongs to the same group of methods, was found to be easy [36]. The wide use of AHP by practitioners was the reason we chose that approach as a candidate for mixing with other techniques in complex problem solving.

The support offered by AHP for group decisionmaking (see [16]) and the Team Expert Choice™ software established it as a powerful tool for consolidating opinions in complex situations that usually involve a number of stakeholders. AHP and MCDA in general have however certain limitations since not every problem can be defined as a hierarchical model. It is true that an extension of AHP allows the representation of more complicated relationships through the so-called Analytic Network Process [41]. That enhances the expressive power of this MCDA methodology. Its weak point is still the problem structuring phase which relies for the definition of the model only on brainstorming. This led us to study the applicability of another existing field dealing with solving complex problems in organizations, developed extensively in the United Kingdom and known as soft systems thinking [22] or Problem Structuring Methods [39] as was mentioned earlier. The following is a brief account of those methodologies that we found to be relevant, while further details can be found in [14,22,39].

## 3.2. Soft systems thinking approaches and their relevance to our systemic interventions

Churchman's [10] Social Systems Design is recognised as a major influence on research in complex problem solving and DSS. An element of his approach that we considered in our decision support framework is the insistence that the systems designer's first obligation in carrying out a systems study is not to the decision makers, rather it is to the “clients”, customers, or beneficiaries of the system [10]. The above requirement, together with Churchman's recommendation to strive for “whole systems improvement” influenced the work in the three cases discussed here.

Strategic Assumptions Surfacing and Testing (SAST) [28] is an operationalization of many of the ideas of Churchman. The SAST methodology can be regarded as having four major stages: group formation of stakeholders with similar vested interests, assumption surfacing, dialectical debate, and synthesis. Assumption surfacing is conducted by applying the Nominal Group Technique (NGT). A corner stone of SAST is the assumption that opposing view points can be brought together through dialectical debate. On the basis of the fact that stakeholders may have an overarching common goal associated with the improvement of the current situation, one can employ only the first two stages of the SAST methodology as was the case with our second and third interventions. However, our first case involved very diverse stakeholders and intervention involved using all four stages of SAST.

Interactive Planning (IP) [1] is a broad systemic approach to planning involving the following stages: formulation of the mess, ends planning, means planning, resource planning, design of implementation and control. Phase 2, ends planning, has several steps itself: preparation of a mission statement, development of a list of desired properties stakeholders agree should be built into the system, preparation of idealized design of the organization, formulation of the closest approximation to the design that is believed to be attainable and identifying the gaps between the approximation and the current state of the system [22]. An idealized design is the design for the enterprise that the stakeholders would replace the existing system with today, if they were free to do so [22]. It can be conducted without any considerations for constraints in the process or by taking the features of the internal and external environment into account. Ackoff [1] assumes that one may apply any of the existing approaches that are relevant for a specific stage of IP. The characteristics of our first and second intervention were related just to the first stage in Ackoff's methodology: formulating the “mess” [1]. We found that Ackoff's complete approach was suitable for the third intervention due to its planning nature.

Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) was developed by Checkland in the 1970s as a strategy for handling complex problems, including those involving sociotechnical systems [9]. Instead of being based upon the paradigm of “optimization”, it is rather founded on the paradigm of “learning”. The original methodology can be described as a seven-stage process of analysis which uses the concept of a human activity system as a means of getting from “finding out” about a situation to “taking action” to improve the situation [9]. The essence of stages 1 and 2 is to find out what the problem is. That is summarized in a “rich picture” which expresses the features of the situation. Rich pictures are cartoon-like images that capture the structure of a problem, the processes involved and the relationships between structure and processes. They are better means for recording relationships and connections than is linear prose [9].

In stage 3, the root definitions describing the new system are formulated by identifying six CATWOE analysis elements [9]:

• Customers: the victims/beneficiaries of the purposeful activity.

• Actors: those who are involved in the activities.

• Transformation process: the purposeful activity transforming an input into an output.

• Weltanschauung: the view of the world that makes the root definition meaningful in context.

• Owners: who can stop the activity.

• Environmental constraints, affecting the situation.

In stage 4, the conceptual models for the future solutions are built by drawing out the minimum number of verbs that are necessary to describe the activities that would have to be present to carry out the tasks named in the root definition. In the fifth stage these models are compared with reality. The last stage involves the implementation of changes that are both desirable and feasible.

This formulation of SSM is known these days as “mode 1” SSM [9]. Mode 2 SSM was introduced as a two-stream inquiry in 1990: a logic-based stream of analysis and a stream of cultural analysis, including also social system analysis and political system analysis [9]. We have applied SSM in our work in the sense of mode 2.

Though SSM has been widely used in practice (see [34]) and it has become a major topic in systems research, there are a number of criticisms leveled at it. A detailed evaluation of Soft Systems Methodology is described in [22]. According to [24], an important deficiency of SSM is the lack of support given by it during the Choice and Implementation stages of Simon's model of decision making. This justifies further our idea to combine SSM with AHP.

What unites SAST, Interactive planning and SSM is their focus on the systemic enquiry as a learning process, through which the stakeholders get to understand better the problem situation through the intervention. Another distinctive feature of soft systems thinking is its support for multiple perspectives [26]. The three perspectives for analysing complex problems, as defined by Linstone in [26], are the Technical, Organizational and Personal Perspectives. Multiple perspectives are critical in developing insights on complex socio-technical problems [26]. Habermas [17] has suggested a somewhat similar notion of “three worlds” in his philosophical writings: the personal world, the social world and the technical world. Another possibility for expressing multiple perspectives in systemic problem solving is reflected in mode two of SSM (see [9]). SAST encourages the generation of assumptions by different groups of stakeholders thus generating diverse representations. All of the above considerations reflect Churchman's idea: the systems approach starts when you look at a problem through the eyes of another [10]. How multiple perspectives are developed for better decision support still requires further research and hence this paper.

The above discussion cannot be exhaustive of the entire vast field of soft systems thinking, for space reasons. We used other methods as well where necessary. One of them was Interpretive Structural Modeling by Warfield [48], which is still not widely applied. The need to consider the interests of the disadvantaged stakeholders through seeking ways for improving the problem situation, led us to explore another systems methodology by Ulrich, called Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH) (see [22,31,48]). We do not claim that those methodologies discussed so far are the only possible options. Further justification of why different approaches might be utilized in combination in complex cases continues in the next section.

## 3.3. Why certain techniques can be potentially considered for our type of problems?

As the preceding discussion hints, the needs of the three problem situations were diverse and complex. Therefore we found that they could not be served by a single methodology. Hence we considered mixing methods in the same case. The question that arises inevitably is how do we decide what techniques are suitable for specific tasks. Individual authors suggest different ways of doing this. Jackson [22] justifies the choice of methods using the assumptions of the various systems methodologies as described by him and reflected in his System of Systems Methodologies. Mingers provides guidelines as to how various methods can be mapped to the three worlds of Habermas (see [32,42]). Linstone suggests how multiple perspectives on a problem can be communicated [26]. We have used all of the above guidelines (having in mind that Jackson's ideas were still evolving (see [20]) and not formulated in their present form (see [21,22]) at the time of our interventions). As a result we identified at the planning stages of our interventions potential candidates for inclusion in the problem solving frameworks for them. These are listed below with the justification for their usage in our mix of methods:

• from Soft Systems Methodology we utilised rich pictures and CATWOE analysis [9] as those served well the stage of “formulation of the mess” in each case, however in the second case we found useful to frame the entire intervention within an Action Research framework based on SSM;

• from Strategic Assumptions Surfacing and Testing were applied stakeholder identification, idea generation and ranking through the Nominal Group Technique (NGT) [28] as these provided sufficient input for stakeholder analysis in problems where the stakeholders do not have dialectically opposing views;

• from Critical Systems Heuristics [45] we applied a subset of its Boundary Judgements Questions as they complement well the other techniques from SSM and SAST that we use for the “formulation of the mess” stage;

• from Multi Criteria Decision Analysis we applied the Analytic Hierarchy Process [41] for its wide acceptance by practitioners and because we needed a tool that helps the stakeholders prioritise alternatives and issues leading to action on a problem situation;

• from Interactive Planning [1] we used just the first step, “formulation of the mess” during the first and second interventions (due to their evaluation nature), while in the third intervention we applied its entire process as that was a typical planning problem;

• from Interpretive Structural Modeling (ISM) [48] were used elements in the third intervention as they served well the specific needs of that planning problem.

## 4. How the three systemic interventions in the ict sector were conducted

Each of the three interventions involved important, real, complex and messy problems. They were all formulated as action research projects (see [6]). The work on the three cases was governed implicitly by the need to support multiple viewpoints following Linstone's three types of perspectives and Habermas's three worlds as well as the metaphor ideas of Flood and Jackson [15]. Each of us served as a facilitator, assisted by at least one other author, in designing and applying a mix of methods from different paradigms for decision support in each case. Some of the problems experienced during the three interventions were unique while some were common. The following paragraphs are an account of how the three mixed approaches were applied.

## 4.1. First case: Evaluation of the factors affecting software development productivity within the conditions of a specific project in an organizational environment

The proposed framework for evaluation of the factors affecting software development productivity was a vehicle for improving the understanding of the issues surrounding software project management through assessment of the differences in the values of the stakeholders in the project. Software development productivity research is directly linked to software project cost and effort estimation since productivity is defined as the ratio between the outcomes from a project and the inputs to it in terms of costs. Hence the factors affecting software development costs (see [8]) influence also productivity. Expert judgments about the impact of particular factors on a project are used as inputs in applying cost and effort estimation methodologies like COCOMO II (see [8]). Analyzing the existing literature, it was possible to categorize various software cost factors in four groups as product, project, user and developer's factors. Identifying the strength of interdependence between these factors in a complex project like the one described in our first case is not easy. It requires a systemic framework for investigation that uncovers the stakeholders involved in a project, their assumptions, political and organizational cultural issues affecting a project.

The intervention in this case helped to identify what made this project difficult to manage and how to reduce the gap between the values of the stakeholders involved and improve the results (see [37]). The techniques within the framework for evaluation of factors affecting software development productivity serve different perspectives corresponding to Habermas' [17] three worlds as is shown in Fig. 2.

The framework was applied during three workshops. The first two workshops were conducted separately with the representatives of the client company and from the software outsourcing providers. Essentially their content corresponded to the “Identification of the mess” stage of Interactive Planning since that is a central issue in an evaluation problem. The first workshops started with stakeholder identification and assumption surfacing (these are elements of SAST [28]). The lists of stakeholders identified by both groups were not very different but they played specific roles from the point of view of the two sides in that project. Then the next step was to integrate the rich pictures developed by both teams (clients and software developers) into one. It attempted to show the world views of the various stakeholders in the project. That was further explored through the CATWOE analysis produced by the user company team and the developers. A comparison of the Weltanschauungs (world views) expressed by the two groups showed that there is little difference between their goals. However, exploring the Transformation process showed that the outsourcing providers are more concerned with the technological aspects of the provision of an integrated IT system while the client company looked forward towards the reengineering of the business.

The next method used was stakeholder assumption surfacing through the Nominal Group Technique and their rating and categorization as technical, political and cultural issues. The latter can be viewed as a very simplified version of the three types of analysis of SSM Mode Two [9]. However it provided sufficient insights given the time constraints of the participants.

The existing techniques for software cost estimation [8] require judgments about the influence of given factors on a project. We support that through their suggested prioritization using AHP (see [41]). The improved understanding of the circumstances surrounding the software project achieved through the previous techniques allowed informed judgements by both teams when prioritizing the factors. According to the client team, project management was considered as the most important factor, followed by user management commitment and requirements volatility. On the other hand, the outsourcing providers considered as most important motivation, followed by software reliability, project management quality, team size and the development schedule. A concentration on the most important issues for both sides and attempting to narrow down the differences in their opinions was seen as a way forward towards improvement of the management of the project.

The two separate workshops with the groups were followed by a 2-month period for reflection on the results from them. A third joint session followed. The purpose was to provide a self-reflection on the process of evaluation of productivity factors within the IT project under concern. Following [45], eight Boundary Judgment Questions were formulated in several groups: identifying sources of motivation in the project, sources of power, sources of knowledge, sources of legitimization. It is true that such questions could be used also in the first workshops but we felt that the methods applied in them served well the evaluation process. Hence their use in the second workshop was more as a triangulation tool confirming the results from the first workshop and providing additional reflections on them. Another four questions were dealing with issues of the experimental validation and stakeholder acceptance of the whole process. More details on this case can be found in [37–40].

## 4.2. Second case: Evaluation of rural telecommunications infrastructure

The second intervention used an Action Research framework following SSM as a general sense making vehicle [38]. The intervention was preceded with a lengthy planning stage during which we had to identify a rural area (Wembezi, west of Durban, South Africa) characterised with potential for economic development. We formed a relationship with the management of the telecommunications provider in the area and the principal stakeholders at the level of local government.

The initial step of the framework (see Fig. 3) dealt with identifying the relevant stakeholders and their roles. The purpose of the stakeholder analysis was to address distributional and social impacts of projects, programs and policies; and to identify existing or potential conflicts between various interests.

A rich picture was used to initiate a brainstorming session about the assumptions in the community and the telecommunications provider. An analysis of the findings from the intervention revealed diverse factors of technical, cultural and political nature. The rich picture depicted a long road as a metaphor for rural development. The road goes through a bureaucratic maze. The process is not linear and takes a long time for consensus building and approval.

Statistical data on the provision of telephones in the area of concern helped in understanding the inadequacies that exist. Brainstorming was used to highlight issues associated with the improvement of rural telecommunications infrastructure. The ideas that were generated were ranked according to their importance. The various issues raised were then classified into one or more of the following categories: Technical; Cultural; and Political, in a way similar to the first case.

Multiple perspectives were developed through each stakeholder group performing separately CATWOE analysis from its point of view and answering the Boundary Judgment Questions of CSH [45]. Then these were analyzed by all groups. The participants did not experience any difficulty in determining the sources of motivation, sources of power, sources of knowledge, and the sources of legitimization for the process of evaluation of rural telecommunications.

In order to allocate resources and establish time frames for desired changes in the telecommunications infrastructure, it was necessary to determine those issues that contribute more significantly to the quality of telecommunications infrastructure. This step involved a multi criteria prioritization using AHP of factors that affect the improvement of rural telecommunications infrastructure in the particular area. According to the participants, the planning issues were regarded as most important, with educational issues following closely.

The next steps involved determining whether there was shared understanding among the stakeholders by the degree of consensus achieved. Summary conclusions on the post session questionnaire regarding the opinions of the participants about the framework demonstrated satisfaction with the approach and acceptance of the techniques used.

## 4.3. Third case: Planning of rural telecommunications infrastructure

The mix of methods used in the third case study is significantly different because of the nature and complexity of the problem on hand. The research intervention in this case led to the development of a systemic planning framework for rural telecommunications in developing countries (see Fig. 4).

The framework followed the steps of Interactive Planning (IP) [1]. At various stages of the IP process were used other methods like rich pictures [9], stakeholder analysis [28], Critical Systems Heuristics [45] and a new element in our mix of methods, called Interpretive Structural Modeling, part of the Interactive Management methodology by Warfield [48]. The latter was chosen for its elaborate systematic procedure that was considered appropriate for the rural telecommunications system (RTS) planning process.

Mapumulo, a rural area north of Durban, South Africa was used for the experimental implementation of the planning framework. The preparation for the intervention took more than 18 months of interaction with the relevant stakeholders. Apart from the two preliminary seminars that were given by one of the authors to the Planning Department of the telecommunications company on various aspects of the research, four formal workshops were conducted with the relevant stakeholders.

The initial workshop focused on the “formulation of the mess” phase. We utilized in it similar methods to those applied in the second intervention: stakeholder analysis, rich pictures and Boundary Judgment Questions.

The second workshop focused on “ends planning”. In terms of the systemic requirements of the framework, the community representatives should have been present at this workshop. This did not materialize due to the telecommunications planners' reservation at this stage to interact directly with other stakeholders before assessing the framework. Both the constrained and unconstrained idealized design of the future telecommunications infrastructure was carried out in the second workshop. One possibility was to express it through a SSM type conceptual model. However, due to the profile of the participants, the use of conceptual models was seen as potentially difficult and unnecessary. Rather a set of questions pertaining to the constrained and unconstrained design was posed to the participants. We started first with the constrained idealized design. Two main questions triggered the group discussions:

• Assuming that the external environment to the Rural Telecommunications System at Mapumulo and the internal environment at the service provider company does not change, what new systems would the participants like to see in that area? The external environment included issues such as other types of parallel infrastructure, government policies and regulations, and cultural and political issues that were reflected in the rich picture. The internal environment for the telecommunications provider included issues such as the internal rate of return, the preferred technologies, the current planning process, and the budgetary constraints.

• The second question was to specify what rural telecommunications infrastructure is needed to support and sustain these systems. Due to time constraints the planners were asked to focus on the core infrastructure, rather than on support systems.

It must be mentioned that this task, being a first time experience for all the participants, was not easy for the planners and they required assistance from the facilitator. The four groups produced similar results although the number of systems varied. The soft systems thinking methods used in this telecommunications planning project, amongst other things, led to learning by the participating community stakeholders and the telecommunications engineers about the complexity of providing telecommunications services for a rural area. Soft systems thinking further enabled the engineers to deal with the cultural and political aspects of their design and identify relevant, feasible and acceptable systems for that particular area.

## 5. Concluding reflections

This paper presented the justification and experiences of the authors with mixing certain soft systems thinking methods and MCDM for decision support in three complex problem situations within the Information and Communications Technologies sector. Such work falls in the third strand of DSS research identified in the “Introduction”. While our approach shares some of the intentions of the authors in [47], multiple perspectives in our three complex decision problems were supported by using combinations of methods that varied according to the specific features of the situation. A systemic multimethodology mix of methods was applied for the first time in the applied areas of software development productivity and telecommunications planning. We provided here some reflections on when do we need soft systems approaches or MCDM at particular stages of a complex problem solving intervention and how multiple perspectives on the problem can be supported by different methods.

## 5.1. On the need for using various methods from different methodologies

The cases showed that the answer to this question depends on the complexity of a problem situation, the number of stakeholders involved and the need for multiple perspectives on the problem situation. For example, we needed to use sometimes only part of SAST. Its corner stone is the assumption that opposing viewpoints can be brought together through a dialectical debate. On the basis of the fact that stakeholders may have an overarching common goal associated with the improvement of the current situation, one can employ only the first two stages of the SAST methodology as was the case with our second and third intervention. However, our first case involved a large software project with many users and more than one company providing the software. We needed to strive to unearth potential differences in their values which led to the use of all four stages of SAST.

SSM served as the overall framework of the inquiry in our second intervention since it was not a very complicated evaluation problem. In the first case we needed a different guiding framework in which we mixed specific methods from SSM like rich pictures and CATWOE analysis with other methods. Each of the methods provided results that were used as inputs to the next logically applied technique in that intervention. In the third intervention, rich pictures and CATWOE served only a supporting role in the overall framework based on Interactive Planning. A greater focus to the debate of the stakeholders or in the enquiring cycle of SSM is achieved by using a prioritization technique like AHP.

These interventions provided further thoughts on how to mix methods in a way that is based on their positive features in all three approaches listed above. This places a considerable demand on the skills of the facilitator of the intervention. The methods however are not difficult to understand by the participants in the workshops. We were dealing with diverse stakeholders —some with university degrees and some without much formal education who needed interpreters from English to their native language. We can conclude that when we deal with increased complexity and multiple stakeholders it is useful to explore the possibilities to combine separate techniques from soft systems thinking with multiple criteria decision making to harness their potential to support learning about the problem and more effective decision support.

5.2. Regarding the value of using specific mixed methods in an intervention and their acceptance by the stakeholders

The combined use of various methods helped in addressing the issues in the three cases that characterize them as “messes” and in the formulation of the management priorities for the future. Individual techniques provided outcomes that are used as inputs for other approaches in the intervention. The multiple perspectives pursued in each case and the interconnections between the methods used in an intervention ensured its systemic nature (in the sense advocated in [33]).

ISM was useful in the stage of means planning within the third intervention but was demanding on the level of facilitator knowledge and stakeholder understanding of the required approach. We found also that strictly speaking there is an overlap between some of the aims pursued through the combination of rich pictures, CATWOE and stakeholder analysis on one hand and the Boundary Judgment Questions of CSH. However avoiding this overlap would significantly reduce the amount of understanding generated in the process of the “identification of the mess”, which is contrary to the recommendations of Churchman [10] to sweep as much information into a problem situation as possible and hence we suggest their combined use as in the three cases discussed here.

As a result of the interventions, the stakeholders could focus on building a common appreciation about the most important issues concerning the technical, cultural and political aspects of the problems on hand. The outcome results were used for better informed management decision related to the particular situation. The frameworks that we applied were seen by the stakeholders as promoting learning and participation in a bottom up way and that contributed to their legitimacy.

Possible directions for future research work may involve applying similar or new combinations of methods from various methodologies to diverse complex situations and deriving from those experiences further guidelines for decision support in “messy” problems. One possibility is to compare on a similar problem our recommendation for combining rich pictures, CATWOE, SAST and CSH during the stage of identification of the mess and also our framework for mixing methods with Warfield's [49] statement, that “two group processes were sufficient to move groups along very efficiently from a beginning in which the problematic situation was described, through a design process, and into a detailed plan for implementation of the design: Nominal Group Technique and Interpretive Structural Modeling” (p. 253). Warfield's finding was made at a time when SSM and CSH were not yet in existence or in their early years. Still we think that any debatable questions may be resolved through more field and theoretical work.

The three cases described here demonstrated the importance of assessing the features of a complex situation before deciding on the process for the action research and techniques that could be applied to it. They showed how multiple perspectives can be supported by different means. Further implementations may lead to fine tuning of the mix of approaches that we developed for each of the three types of problems We acknowledge that it is impossible to generalize the results from our single cases to all similar situations due to the difficulty to recreate the features of complex problems involving the human element (see [40]). However the interventions allowed us to generate some conclusions about the characteristics of the process of the analysis. Our experience from these and other applications of mixing methods in the same systemic intervention shows that a similar approach to ours may be transferable and used for improved decision support within other ICT complex problems.
