## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Opportunistic behavior Creditworthiness Online market Crowdsourcing Control

## A B S T R A C T

Information asymmetry and the community-based nature of online marketplaces may lead to opportunistic behaviors by sellers. This study investigates seller opportunism in the online service market from the contro perspective. By analyzing data on online transactions and trade disputes from the largest crowdsourcing platform in China, Zhubajie, this study found that a seller's opportunistic behaviors, such as the non-delivery or late delivery of the final solution, are related to social and formal control in the market. Specifically, social control, i.e., the number of seller fans and members the seller follows, negatively afects opportunistic behaviors by the seller; the seller's reputation score negatively afects opportunistic behaviors, but the efect is weakened when social control is high. The seller's rank in the market is positively related to opportunistic behaviors. This paper contributes to research on online marketplaces by exploring seller creditworthiness from the control perspective. It provides implications for online marketplaces for controlling seller opportunism and enhancing creditworthiness in the market.

## 1. Introduction

Online marketplaces have experienced rapid growth in the last decade. They are characterized by information asymmetry and an impersonal nature, as buyers and sellers trade online without the need for face-to-face communication [1]. In addition, online marketplaces are mostly community-based and are scarcely governed by existing laws or regulations [2]. These facts may give rise to opportunism by participants [1]. Opportunism is defined as a deceit-oriented violation of implicit or explicit promises regarding a predetermined role of behavior [3]. In e-commerce transactions, opportunism may involve acts such as withholding or distorting information or failing to fulfill promises or obligations. Seller opportunistic behaviors may occur in online C2C marketplaces, such as eBay and Taobao [4–6]. In some cases, buyers do not receive products from sellers as promised; thus, seller cred itworthiness is of great concern for online platforms and buyers. In online financial marketplaces, such as peer to peer lending (P2P lending) and crowdfunding platforms, default by borrowers is a major problem that deters investors from making associated funding decisions [7–9]. Crowdsourcing is an Internet-based channel for firms or in dividuals to collect crowd wisdom to solve a problem [10]. Crowdsourcing platforms, such as Innocentive (www.innocentive.com) and

Zhubajie (www.zbj.com), are online service marketplaces in which sponsors, e.g., firms, pay service providers for solutions. As reported by Zhubajie, the largest online crowdsourcing platform in China, seller opportunistic behaviors occur at a relatively high frequency. In some cases, sellers do not deliver a solution on time or do not provide the promised service quality. Thus, enhancing seller creditworthiness and reducing opportunism are essential to the success of online crowdsourcing projects and the service market.

Thus far, the e-commerce research has mainly focused on enhancing buyers' or lenders' trust and reducing their risk perception to boost their purchases or investment. For example, a seller's online reputation may significantly influence buyers' trust and purchase decisions [1,11]. Lin et al. [7] and Liu et al. [12] found that a borrower's online social network significantly afects lender judgment and decisions. Zhang and Liu [13] found that a borrower's online credit rating is an important factor considered by lenders and significantly afects lender investment behaviors. There has been little research on controlling opportunism and reducing credit risk from the seller perspective. In the online crowdsourcing context, researchers have identified the motivations of sellers to participate in crowdsourcing projects, including direct compensation, skill enhancement, and self-marketing [14], as well as intrinsic motivations [16]. Crowdsourcing tasks (ideation ys. expertise-based tasks) [17], rewards [18], task complexity and autonomy [16] are related to seller participation and efort in creating solutions. As in other types of online trading, opportunistic behaviors have also been reported in crowdsourcing contexts [20]. However, there has been no research that focuses on seller opportunistic behaviors in online crowdsourcing projects. To improve creditworthiness in online service markets, it is necessary to control seller opportunistic behaviors.

The goal of control is to motivate people to comply with a desired behavior [21]. Research has found that combining diferent control mechanisms can inspire individuals to perform desired behaviors or actions that result in better outcomes. Formal control focuses on specifications, evaluations, and reward/punishment, and informal control is based on social or people-related aspects [21]. Social control and formal control are efective in mitigating various deviant behaviors and preventing people from conducting illegal or unethical activities (e.g. [23,24]). For example, formal control, such as policy, procedure, re ward and punishment, and informal social pressure from peers, are deterrents to employees' security violation [24] and computer abuse [25]. Lin et al. [7] found that a borrower's online friendship network may control and decrease delinquent activities in P2P lending. As formal control modes in online markets, reputation and ranking mechanisms are adopted to control seller opportunistic behaviors and promote seller quality [5]. However, there have been few empirical studies on how the ranking and reputation score may afect seller opportunism. Furthermore, there are currently few studies on understanding how formal and social control factors act together to restrain the opportunism of sellers in online markets. In this study, we investigate how social control factors, such as a seller's social links, and formal control factors, such as a seller's rank and reputation score on an online crowdsourcing platform, are related to seller opportunistic be haviors to achieve a comprehensive understanding of seller opportunism. The results contribute to online market and creditworthiness re search and provide implications for buyers and crowdsourcing platforms for how to evaluate seller creditworthiness and reduce the potential credit risk of online transactions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review the relevant literature on online creditworthiness, seller opportunism, crowdsourcing, and formal and social controls. Second, the research model and hypotheses are developed. Third, the research method, in cluding data collection and analyses, is described. Finally, we conclude the paper with a discussion of the findings and implications for research and practice.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical background

## 2.1. Online creditworthiness and seller opportunism

Creditworthiness is a dynamic, intrinsic attribute or an internal quality of people or businesses. In business, credit can be defined in both narrow and broad senses. In the narrow sense, credit reflects the debt relationship of the owner and the debtor and encompasses any form of deferred payment [26]. In the broad sense, however, credit is the unity of subjective faith and objective behavior [27]. In this way, creditworthiness can be defined as the probability associated with both the ability and the willingness of a trade participant to fulfill its trade obligations. In e-commerce transactions, opportunism may involve such acts as withholding or distorting information or failing to fulfill promises or obligations [1]. Thus, online creditworthiness is closely related to opportunism.

Credit and creditworthiness are closely linked to several other concepts in the domain of trade and e-commerce, such as trust and trustworthiness, which are often used interchangeably with credit and creditworthiness [28]. The notions of trust and trustworthiness are among the most widely studied constructs in e-commerce (e.g., [1,2]). As defined by Mayer et al. [28], trust is “the willingness of a buyer to be vulnerable to a seller's non-desirable actions while expecting the seller to perform actions that are beneficial to the buyer without the buyer having to monitor these actions.” Thus, trust is developed based on the belief of the ability, benevolence, and integrity of the trade partner [29].

Reputation is another concept that is highly related to creditworthiness. Currently, most major online marketplaces, e.g., eBay, Taobao, and Zhubajie, adopt a feedback-based reputation mechanism. Online traders receive scores or comments from partners regarding the quality of a product or service after trades. The cumulative scores and comments become the trader's online reputation in the marketplace [5]. The reputation mechanism is efective at reducing the adverse selection caused by information asymmetry and credit risk in online market places [30,31]. It has been confirmed that seller reputation significantly afects buyer trust and risk perception and, consequently, their intention to perform online trading [1,2,4,32], while the efects may be moderated by factors such as the familiarity and distance between the seller and buyer [11]. Thus, reputation may act as an indicator of seller creditworthiness and determine buyers' trust and evaluation of credit risk.

Although reputation and trust are closely related to creditworthi ness, most studies have focused on the buyer perspective. There is little research on seller opportunistic activities in online transactions. Reputation may reflect a seller's creditworthiness to some extent. However, to better understand creditworthiness, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive study to identify the factors that afect or control seller opportunistic behaviors in online transactions. Currently, only a few studies have investigated borrower delinquent activities in online financial markets, e.g., P2P lending or crowdfunding [7,33]. There is no research on seller creditworthiness in the online service context. Crowdsourcing is the major type of online service trading. In this study, we investigate the factors that control seller opportunistic behaviors in crowdsourcing platforms.

## 2.2. Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing means to take “…a job traditionally performed by a designated agent (usually an employee) and outsourcing it to an undefined, generally large group of people in the form of an open call” [10]. On a crowdsourcing platform such as Innocentive, demand-side firms seek solutions from external service providers to solve their business problems. For these firms, the main goal is to acquire creative ideas and solutions for business problems from the solvers [34,35].

Broadly speaking, crowdfunding is also one type of crowdsourcing, as this concept evolved from crowdsourcing [10]. In addition to collecting creative business ideas and solutions, crowdsourcing can be used to tap people's excess capacities and resources, such as monetary and financial resources. This type of crowdsourcing in which customers serve as investors is called crowdfunding. According to the reward for investors, crowdfunding can be classified into many types, such as reward-based crowdfunding, lending-based crowdfunding (i.e., P2P lending) and equity-based crowdfunding [36].

We conduct a systematic literature review on crowdsourcing and crowdfunding. The related studies and important findings are summarized in the Appendix A. For crowdsourcing, previous studies have conducted research from either the buyer side or the seller side. Studies on crowdfunding from the buyer side generally focus on crowdsourcing contest design, task allocation algorithms, and idea filtering, with the aim of improving the quality of the solutions and project outcomes (e.g., [19,37,38]). In addition, some scholars have also discussed the development of institutional mechanisms to improve buyer trust in crowdsourcing marketplaces (e.g., [39]).

This study focuses on the seller side. Solvers' motivations, participation and efort have gained much attention. The motivations for solvers to join crowdsourcing projects include reward seeking, learning, developing creative skills, self-marketing, and social motives $[ 1 4 , 1 5 , 4 0 { - } 4 2 ]$ . In addition to extrinsic motivations, such as direct compensation, skill enhancement, and self-marketing [15], intrinsic motivations also play an important role in crowdsourcing [16]. Zheng et al. [16] found that task autonomy, variety, and analyzability are positively associated with solvers' intrinsic motivation, whereas tacitness is negatively associated with their intrinsic motivation.

Some crowdsourcing platforms have adopted diverse models for diferent tasks. For example, contest is mainly used for ideation tasks, e.g., logos, business cards, and slogan designs, while contract is used to match sponsors with optimal solvers for complex, expertise-based tasks, e.g., website development, programming, and translation [17]. The research indicates that projects with greater rewards are able to attract a larger group of solvers and a higher number of solutions. In addition, reward is also a significant determinant of an individual solver's efort and performance [18,43]. Research based on game theory revealed the way in which sponsors set the reward for winner(s) to attract the best solution, while solvers determine the appropriate efort level to max imize their expected utility based on the reward and winning probability [19]. Tasks that are highly autonomous, explicitly specified, less complex, and require a variety of skills are more likely to attract solver efort [16]. Rivalry in contest reduces the solver's motivation to exert efort; thus, it is necessary to limit the number of participants in a project [44].

Some recent studies have discussed ethical problems in crowd sourcing (e.g., [20,45,46]. For example, Schlagwein et al. [45] identi fied several ethical concerns, such as payment fairness and information transparency. They discussed the ethical norms to address those issues. Uncertainties, including task, competition, and buyer behavioral uncertainty in crowdsourcing, are closely related to the interests of sellers and afect their participation [20].

In addition to the research on crowdsourcing, studies in the area of crowdfunding are also closely related to this paper. We listed three streams of the relevant research on crowdfunding in the Appendix A. First, social capital in reward-based crowdfunding plays an important role in improving funding performance [36,47]. Second, soft information such as friendships can act as signals of borrowers' credit quality in lending-based crowdfunding (i.e., peer-to-peer lending markets) (e.g., [7]). Third, electronic word of mouth (a form of opinion-based social interaction), popularity information (a form of action-based social interaction), and even fake Facebook likes (one kind of social information) have significant efects on investor decision-making in reward based crowdfunding [9,48].

An online crowdsourcing platform is an online service marketplace in which solvers or service providers sell solutions to firms or individuals who act as sponsors of projects. Compared to tangible ecommerce goods, services are more dificult to standardize due to their intangibility, simultaneity, intimacy and perishability [49]. In the service market, the information asymmetry issue is more serious compared to that in the traditional e-marketplace. Due to information asymmetry and the community-based nature of online service markets, opportunistic activities by sellers have been reported by many crowdsourcing platforms. Currently, there is no research on seller opportunistic behaviors. To reduce credit risk, it is important to understand the cred itworthiness of service providers and to control opportunism on the platforms.

## 2.3. Formal control and social control

Control in organizations has long been a topic of interest for researchers and practitioners alike, who generally recognize that control mechanisms are needed to help and ensure that organizations achieve their goals. The goal of control is to motivate people to comply with a desired behavior [21]. Whereas formal control focuses on specifica tions, evaluations, and reward/punishment, informal control is based on social or people-related aspects [21,22]. Research has found that combining diferent control mechanisms can inspire individuals to perform desired behaviors or actions and can result in better outcomes.

Researchers in information systems have studied both formal and informal controls used in internal and outsourced software development [22,50–52]. They are complementary to each other and efective in promoting the final outcomes and results [53,54]. The research on information system projects primarily focuses on the antecedents and performance consequences of control modes and amounts and, thus, focuses on control portfolio configurations [55]. Di Tullio and Staples [56] investigated control modes in open-source software projects and identified the unique features of formal and informal control in an open-source context. Venkatesh et al. [57] indicated that managers project-related knowledge facilitates the enactment of the control modes and is important for the outcomes of software development. Maruping et al. [58] found that outcome control and self-control moderate the efects of the agile methodology on software project quality. Kirsch et al. [59] indicated that the choice of control mode in software development depends on behavior observability, outcome measurability, and clients' understanding of the development process. Nidumolu and Subramani [60] proposed the matrix of control and suggested that software project performance is enhanced through the standardization of the performance criteria and the decentralization of the methods.

Control theory has been considered in the study of various forms of deviant behaviors. The research on formal (legal) sanctions posits that the greater the perceived certainty and severity of the sanctions for an illicit act are, the more individuals are deterred from that act [61]. Formal control is found to be efective in deterring IT-related deviant behaviors, such as digital piracy, computer abuse, and security violations (e.g., [23,62,63]). Social control can also play a role in controlling individual behaviors. Social bonds may prevent people from committing a crime or other deviant behaviors. Research has found that social control prevents employees from violating security policy [24] or conducting computer abuse [25]. The research in finance has confirmed the relationship between social links and borrower default; thus, a borrower's social network and relationships can be used as “soft” information for judging creditworthiness [64]. In P2P lending, the borrower's online social network is also a deterrent to loan defaults [7]. In crowdsourcing, seller opportunistic activities are considered deviant since they may breach the contract or violate the policy of the online platform. On a crowdsourcing platform, such as Zhubajie, formal control mechanisms include ranking and reputation. Sellers are ranked based on their trading volume and credit rating; a seller's reputation score is related to buyer feedback from previous transactions. In addition, sellers may develop social links with others on the platform. In this study, we aim to reveal how formal and social control factors may work together to control seller opportunistic behaviors, and how these factors are related to their creditworthiness in online crowdsourcing platforms.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

We develop the research model from the control perspective as shown in Fig. 1. The dependent variable is seller opportunistic behavior in the online service market. In an online crowdsourcing platform, the social control of a seller depends on the seller's social links on the platform, which consist of links with fans and links with others the seller follows. Formal control includes ranking and reputation mechanisms. Seller creditworthiness is rewarded by rank promotion, and opportunism is punished by the decrease in the reputation score. These social control and formal control factors may control opportunism. We propose that there are interaction efects between social and formal control, in which social control factors moderate the efects of formal control.

## 3.1. Social control

Social capital theory proposes that the connections between individuals, or the structural links between individuals in a network, are important predictors of collective action [65]. The more individuals there are in regular contact with one another, the more likely they are to develop a “habit of cooperation” and act collectively [66]. In online environments, social capital is important to actions, such as knowledge sharing and contribution [67]. Individuals with more ties to other members are more likely to cooperate, understand, and comply with group norms and expectations [68]. From the social control perspective, social capital is related to attachment, which refers to internal control based on social links. Individuals may take or avoid taking some actions when they are highly attached to others—including peers, parents, and other people who they consider important. For example, attachment is negatively associated with delinquent behaviors because performing such behaviors can disappoint those to whom the actors are attached [69]. Social pressure significantly mitigates individuals' deviant behaviors and drives them to follow the rules, policies or regulations [24,70]. In P2P lending, Lin et al. [7] also found that borrowers' online social network may help control their ex post default rates.

On crowdsourcing platforms, such as Zhubajie, users may choose to follow others as fans, providing for a more direct interaction. Thus, a seller may develop social links with others on the platform, either by being followed by others or by following others as a fan. We propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. The number of fans is negatively related to the seller's opportunistic behavior.

Hypothesis 2. The number of members a seller follows is negatively related to the seller's opportunistic behavior.

## 3.2. Formal control

The research indicates that deterrents to criminological behaviors include both formal and informal sanctions [61,71]. Formal sanctions refer to the punishment of people committing crimes if they are caught. The certainty and severity of formal sanctions prevent criminal acts. If an individual believes that the cost of sanctions is high. he/she will not commit the crime [61]. Formal sanctions have been demonstrated to be efective in preventing and reducing many deviant behaviors, including employees' computer misuse [23,72], copyright infringement [73], and software piracy [63]. For example, Siponen et al. [74] found that formal sanctions minimize security violation issues in organizations since employees who violate the security policy will be punished by the organization. Crowdsourcing platforms have adopted some of these mechanisms, such as ranking and feedback-based reputation mechan isms, to control opportunistic activities and promote the creditworthiness of transactions. For example, on Zhubajie, rank levels are defined based on seller trade volume and ratings from buyers; thus, sellers that perform well are rewarded by rank promotion, which may lead to more transaction opportunity and privileges on the platform. In addition, negative ratings from buyers may cause a decline in the seller's reputation score; thus, a seller's opportunistic behaviors may result in punishment since the reputation score may significantly afect buyer trust and purchase intention.

According to Kahneman and Tversky [75], value function shows diminishing marginal efects in gains and losses. The principle of di minishing marginal utility explains the negative relation between practices and values (e.g., [76]). If an objective is satiated, the value one attaches to the realization of that objective falls. In contrast, that which is scarce is generally highly valued. For sellers that already have high ranks, the prospect of their rank increasing would have less marginal utility than for sellers with low ranks. For buyers, they might have a higher tolerance for high-ranked sellers. Ranking may have less control efect on high-ranked sellers than on low-ranked sellers. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. Seller rank is positively related to seller opportunistic behavior.

On crowdsourcing platforms, seller opportunistic behaviors are punished by a decrease in their reputation score, calculated based on buyers' ratings of seller service quality. As discussed above, marginal utility diminishes for both gains and losses; in addition, the value function is usually concave for gains and convex for losses and is steeper for losses than for gains [75]. Thus, it is reasonable to believe that sellers who have poor scores after some opportunistic activities may not care much about further decreases in reputation scores. In contrast, sellers that maintain good reputation scores may care more about score decreases and try to avoid punishment by behaving properly. Thus, reputation has more control efect on sellers with high reputations. We propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. Seller reputation score is negatively related to seller opportunistic behavior.

## 3.3. Moderating efects

Informal control may moderate the efects of formal control. In system development, informal control mechanisms have substitutive efects for formal control mechanisms and weaken the influence of formal outcome control [53]. Diferent control factors have interactive efects on deviant behaviors [24]. As discussed above, seller social control is characterized by the number of fans and number of members that the seller follows. A seller that is highly linked to others on the crowdsourcing platform would avoid opportunistic behaviors regardless of its ranking on the platform. Thus, we propose that social links moderate the efect of seller rank on opportunistic behavior.

Hypothesis 5(a). : The number of fans moderates the relationship between seller rank and opportunistic behavior; that is, the efect of seller rank on opportunistic behavior is weak when the number of the seller's fans is high.

Hypothesis 5(b). : The number of members a seller follows moderates the relationship between seller rank and opportunistic behavior; that is, the efect of seller rank on opportunistic behavior is weak when the number of members that the seller follows is high.

Similarly, social control may also interact with reputation. When a seller is highly linked to others on the crowdsourcing platform, he/she would avoid opportunistic behavior regardless of whether he/she has maintained a good reputation. Thus, we propose that social links negatively moderate the efect of seller reputation on opportunistic behavior.

Hypothesis 6(a). : The number of fans moderates the relationship between seller reputation and opportunistic behavior; that is, the efect of seller reputation on opportunistic behavior is weak when the number of the seller's fans is high.

Hypothesis 6(b). : The number of members a seller follows moderates the relationship between seller reputation and opportunistic behavior; that is, the efect of seller reputation on opportunistic behavior is weak when the number of members that the seller follows is high.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Empirical model and variables

To test the research hypotheses, we build a regression model as follows:

$$
y _ {i} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{X} _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{Y} _ {i} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{Z} _ {\mathrm{i}}
$$

here, the dependent variable, $y _ { i } ,$ is the number of opportunistic actions of the service provider, i.e., the number of disputes caused by the provider that were collected in the second step of data collection. Negative binominal regression (NBREG) analysis was employed in the current study, as the dependent variable takes nonnegative integer values and is over-dispersed, which means that its variance is larger than its mean $[ 9 , 7 7 ]$ . The independent variable, ${ \mathrm { X } } _  i , $ includes the main efect terms - fans , follow\_others , rank , and reputation . The term fans refers to the number of fans that follow service provider i, and follow\_others is the number of members that service provider i follows. The rank of the service provider takes positive integer values: level 1, level 2, etc. On Zhubajie, the level number was calculated based on the workpoint of the service provider's transaction money and rating, which is shown as follows.

$$
\mathrm{workpoint} _ {i} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} m o n e y _ {t} * R a t i n g _ {t}
$$

where n refers to the times that service provider i made transactions before the time of our first data collection. Money is the reward that the service provider gained in transaction t. Rating takes a value of 1 if the service provider obtains a positive rating from the service buyer for that service trading, takes a value of 0.5 if he/she receives a medium rating, and takes a value of 0 when the provider obtains a negative evaluation. Rank levels could be obtained based on the workpoint following the rules of Zhubajie. For instance, if the value reaches 50,000, the rank level will be 8. Thus, a service provider's ability and credit are rewarded by the rank level calculated based on the workpoint.

Each service provider has a reputation score equal to 100 when he/ she registers in the marketplace. If the provider receives negative feedback or service disputes, his/her reputation score will be decreased according to the platform rules. We found that approximately 89.9% of user reputation scores are equal to 100. Thus, instead of using the absolute value of the reputation score, we quantify the level of a service provider's reputation (i.e., reputation ) with the position of his/her ratio value in the quantiles. For instance, if the ratio is less than the first quantile point, his/her reputation level is coded as 1. If the ratio falls beyond the four quantile points, the reputation level is coded as 4. In addition to the main efect terms, we also include interaction terms as follows: fans ∗ rank fans ∗ reputation follow\_others ∗ rank , and follow\_others ∗ reputation . To minimize the multicollinearity issue, we standardized the following variables: fans , follow\_others , rank , and re putation<sub>i</sub>.

We also included control variables, $\mathrm { Z _ { i } , }$ describing the characteristics of the service provider. All the variables in the empirical models are listed in Table 1.

## 4.2. Research context and data

Data were collected from Zhubajie (www.zbj.com), which is one of the largest online crowdsourcing service markets in the world. Zhubajie was founded in Chongqing, China, in 2006. There were 12,650,000 members registered as service providers on Zhubajie as of June 20, 2018, and the total transaction volume was > 1.1 billion dollars.<sup>2</sup> In addition to providing service in China, Zhubajie also operates a website (www.witmart.com) in the United States.

There are three modes of service transactions on Zhubajie. The first is crowdsourcing contest, which enables buyers to publish their task and promised rewards for the best solution, and only the provider that submits the best solution wins the reward. The second is auction in which buyers post requirements and are bid on by service providers; then, the buyer selects the optimal provider to commence work. The third is service shopping, where service providers list their service for buyers to search and purchase directly. Service trading is more complex than the shopping of tangible goods, as it is dificult for buyers to articulate requirements in a clear and standard way, and thus, dispute are more likely to occur. In this study, to measure seller opportunism, we focus on disputes involving service providers, such as the nondelivery or late delivery of solutions. As the trading platform, Zhubajie acts as arbitrator, and disputes and resolutions are all published on the website.<sup>3</sup>

We followed two steps to collect data on service providers and trade disputes using a crawler robot designed using the Python language. In the first step, we identified 22,594 service providers listed on Zhubajie. To ensure that the providers were not zombie users, we removed service providers who did not have any transactions during the three months before January 1, 2016, which was the date of the first data collection. Then, 4534 service providers remained as a valid sample for data analysis. The statistics for all 4534 providers are listed in Table 2. We found that the control variables show high levels of skewness. Thus. we conduct the logarithmic transformation of all control variables for data analysis.

As most transactions are completed within three months, we col lected all trade disputes in the following four months after January 1, 2016, in the second step. Among the 2404 disputes collected, 385 were caused by the providers listed in the sample from the first step. The disputes can be classified into categories as shown in Table 3.

Service providers' opportunistic actions can be measured using different proxy variables. Among the disputes, nondelivery and late delivery reflects the violation of sellers' promise in the outcomes of online transactions. In this study, we use the nondelivery and late delivery of a solution to reflect a seller's opportunistic behaviors. ## 6. Robustness checks

We conduct six robustness checks as follows. First, we recalculate the dependent variable as a binary indicator, whether a provider had a dispute or not, and run a logistic regression model. The results of the logistic regression are similar to those of NBREG. Second, as a robustness check for our NBREG regression, we also run a regression model, and the result patterns remain unchanged.

Third, we sort the dataset by “transaction\_num\_latest3month” and use its levels as a relative measure for the number of transactions in the model. We run all the NBREG models once again. The results also show a similar pattern as that of NBREG. In addition, the variable “relative transaction\_num\_latest3month” does not lose significance in all the models, and the directions of its efects in all models do not change.

Fourth, a high-ranked seller may have a high level of service capability, which could diminish the negative efect of rank on opportunistic behavior. We doubt that rank level has a nonlinear relation with opportunistic behavior. Thus, we add a rank-squared term in the model and run the NBREG again. The results show that the squared term of rank does have a significant efect on opportunistic behavior (coeficient = −0.101, p < 0.05). The main efect of rank remains significant (coeficient = 0.942, p < 0.001).

Fifth, we also try to label the sellers as high- and low-reputation groups by recoding reputation as a binary variable (1 if the reputation score is larger than the fourth quantile point and 0 otherwise). The results also follow the patterns of the NBREG model.

Sixth, it seems that the sample size in this study, 4534, is large, and we should check the efect of sample size on the p-values. To determine the sample size at which the key variables become significant [78], we plot the coeficient/p-value/sample size (CPS) chart to display curves of the coeficient and p-value of each key variable for diferent sample sizes, ranging from small to the maximal large-sample size [78]. The results suggest that except for the variable fans, other key terms such as rank, reputation, followothers, fans\*reputation, and followothers\*rank become stable and significant when the sample size is larger than 2000.

## 7. Post hoc analysis

Spam and plagiarism are the two other major trade disputes on Zhubajie. We conducted a post hoc analysis to investigate how social and formal control may afect seller spamming and plagiarism actions. The results are shown in Table 8.

Seller reputation has a negative efect on spamming and plagiarism, which indicates that sellers with high reputation scores are less likely to conduct spamming and plagiarism. However, a seller's social links are positively related to spamming and plagiarism. As shown in Table 8, a seller is more likely to conduct plagiarism when he/she has more fans and is more likely to conduct spamming when he/she follows many others on the platform. In addition, a seller's rank is positively related to plagiarism, but the efect is weakened when he/she has more fans. Meanwhile, the negative efect of reputation on spamming is also weakened when the seller has more fans.

## 8. Discussion and implications

## 8.1. Summary of result

The results show that for formal control, a seller's rank positively afects the nondelivery or late delivery of the final solution. This can be interpreted as, when a service provider achieves a high rank in the market, he/she may not care much about the comments or feedback of buyers; thus, the marginal utility of ranking diminishes for sellers with high ranks. Seller reputation is negatively related to opportunistic behavior, indicating that sellers with good reputation scores are unlikely to conduct opportunism on crowdsourcing platforms. For social control, both the number of seller fans and the number of members followed by the seller, which reflects a seller's links to others on the platform, decreases seller opportunistic behavior. The number of fans of a seller decreases the efect of reputation, which suggests that when a seller has a high number of fans, he/she may not conduct opportunistic activity regardless of high or low reputation. Thus, a seller's social links have control over opportunism in combination with formal control.

## 8.2. Implications for research

This study has implications for research on online marketplaces. Online marketplaces contain various types of markets, including B2C (e.g., Amazon and Tmall) and C2C markets (e.g., eBay and Taobao) for trading goods, crowdsourcing markets (e.g., Innocentive and Zhubajie) for trading services, and online financial markets such as P2P lending (e.g., Prosper) and crowdfunding (e.g., Kickstarter) platforms. The extant research has focused on buyers' purchase or repurchase behaviors in online B2C or C2C markets [79–81], sellers' or service providers motivations and participation in online crowdsourcing [16,17,19], lenders' investment in P2P lending [7,12,13], and sponsors' participa tion in online crowdfunding [36,47,82,83]. There is little research on the outcomes of online transactions. Due to a community-based nature and information asymmetry, credit risk is a major problem in online marketplaces. Lin et al. [7] studied loan defaulting in online P2P lending; Zheng et al. [84] explored the outcome of reward-based crowdfunding projects, which are measured through product quality and delivery timeliness. This study is the first to explore the final delivery of solutions in crowdsourcing, which may reflect the extent to which service providers fulfill their obligations in service trading transactions. Due to the limitation of the extant research, there is great potential to study online marketplaces in this direction to understand the post-purchase results of online transactions.

## 8.3. Implications for practice

The results of this study have important implications for both online marketplace operators and buyers. Currently, on crowdsourcing platforms, sellers' creditworthiness is mainly judged using their online reputation scores. The results of this study show that the likelihood of opportunism is also influenced by a seller's social links on the platform, and the online social network has an interactive efect with the reputation score. Thus, to better evaluate seller creditworthiness and predict credit risk, a seller's social links should also be considered. In addition, to reduce the credit risk and opportunistic actions generated by sellers, online platforms should recognize the importance of social networks. The platforms are suggested to incentivize members to add links with each other to enhance the social network between sellers and buyers. Currently, on crowdsourcing platforms, e.g., Zhubajie, members can communicate using online messages. They can also form online groups based on common interests. More efective communication tools, such as instant messaging and QQ, should be adopted by platforms to promote online interactions among members.

The results show that seller rank is positively related to opportu nism. Seller rank reflects a seller's capability and tenure on the plat form. In many cases, buyers would like to choose sellers of high rank for solutions. It is necessary for buyers to understand that transactions with high-rank sellers may have a higher potential risk of the nondelivery or late delivery of solutions. To reduce this risk, buyers should avoid highrank sellers that follow many others. For platforms with the aim of controlling credit risk, they should pay more attention to high-rank sellers and devote more resources to socializing, training, and monitoring high-rank sellers to reduce their opportunistic actions. Currently, on crowdsourcing websites, e.g., Zhubajie, sellers are listed in descending order of rank. Based on our findings, we suggest that the platforms consider a combination of both rank and credibility in deciding the order, so sellers have more motives to improve the delivery of the solution in transactions.

This study also shed light on the role of soft information in online marketplaces. Currently, in online marketplaces, sellers' credit is mainly calculated based on their “hard” information, including identity, certification, and records of past performance. The findings here imply the importance of including soft information, such as social links and network information, in the calculation of seller credit to provide a more reasonable judgment and accurate prediction of credit risk for online transactions. In addition to the information on fans and followers, online markets should pay attention to and collect other social information, for example, the links among members in communities, to achieve better knowledge of the online social networks on the platforms. Then, they would have more soft information for credit evaluation.

We found that high-ranked sellers who have gained a large amount of trade volume are more likely to face nondelivery/late delivery problems. In addition, the results of the post hoc analysis show that sellers with higher ranks and more fans are more likely to conduct plagiarism, and sellers who follow many others on the platform are more likely to conduct spamming. Thus, to control spamming and reduce plagiarism, online service markets should exert more efort to monitor and control high-rank providers and sellers who have more links with others on the platform.

## 8.4. Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. First, the findings in this paper may be limited to crowdsourcing, which is an online service market. Future studies could extend the research by studying the efects of social and formal control on creditworthiness in other types of marketplaces, such as C2C markets and online financial markets such as crowdfunding markets. Second, the data were collected from Zhubajie, a crowdsourcing platform in China, where social capital is more im portant than in other countries, such as the U.S. Future studies can examine the cultural diferences and whether social and formal control may have diferent efects on creditworthiness in other countries. Third, although nondelivery or late delivery is the maior opportunistic activity and of most concern to buyers, there are still some other delinquent actions on online service markets. For example, inferior solution quality has been reported by some crowdsourcing systems as a serious problem. Future studies may extend the research by investigating the efects of social and formal control on some other opportunistic actions in online marketplaces. Finally, the results of robustness check indicate that the p-values for the variable fans are not stable for diferent sample sizes. The confidence intervals in Table 6 also suggest similar findings for the unstable efect of fans on nondelivery/late delivery. We think that one possible reason for the unstable p-values for the variable of fans is that there are too few samples with opportunistic behavior. Future research could extend and validate our study by collecting more samples or conducting research in a diferent e-commerce context.

## 9. Conclusion

This paper examines the efects of diferent types of control on seller opportunistic actions in the online service market. The results suggest that a seller's online reputation and social links with others have negative efects on the likelihood of opportunism, while the efect of reputation is moderated by social links. A seller's rank on a platform positively influences the likelihood of opportunism. This study contributes to research on online marketplaces, creditworthiness and control. It also provides practical implications for how to control opportunistic actions, enhance creditworthiness, and predict and reduce credit risk in online transactions.
