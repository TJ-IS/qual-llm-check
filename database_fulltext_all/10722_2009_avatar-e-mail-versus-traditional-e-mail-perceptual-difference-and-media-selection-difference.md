# Avatar e-mail versus traditional e-mail: Perceptual difference and media selection difference

Younghwa Lee <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Kenneth A. Kozar <sup>b</sup>, Kai R. Larsen <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Business University of Kansas, Lawrence KS 66045, United States <sup>b</sup> Leeds School of Business University of Colorado at Boulder, United States

Received 25 June 2007; received in revised form 19 November 2007; accepted 28 November 2007

## Abstract

The study investigates how knowledge workers perceive avatar e-mail differently from traditional e-mail, and how they select traditional versus avatar e-mail when different levels of task equivocality and different types of communication direction are present. Three field studies were conducted with knowledge workers who have used avatar and traditional e-mail. This study demonstrates that overall perception toward avatar e-mail is significantly different from traditional e-mail with respect to media richness and social presence characteristics. In addition, this study found individuals used different e-mail selection approaches when conducting tasks with different equivocality (high versus low equivocal tasks) and tasks for different communication direction (lateral versus upward). Avatar e-mail users also sent lengthier messages than traditional e-mail users when conducting a highly equivocal task.

## 1. Introduction

“When you don your avatar and join thousands of other people who are trying out life in virtual worlds you are joining in a great new experiment in human contact. … This contact goes far beyond simple chat to become a whole new way of being with people” [[14], p. 5].

As the business environment has become distributed and fast-paced, as well as information- and communication technology-dependent, companies increasingly consider e-mail an effective communication medium in interpersonal and inter-organizational communication [22,43,52,65]. Previous research has determined that a high proportion of knowledge workers' time is spent on communication-related activities [6,44]. Communication media, including e-mail, continue to significantly change the communication process, structure, and outcomes in organizations [20,60]. New media have capabilities, absent from traditional media, that can improve and modify information gathering and dissemination strategies, affect social interaction, and allow communication partners to express information that otherwise would not have been communicated. Therefore, a better understanding of the effects of new media on business communication is crucial for providing efficient and effective communication among employees.

Despite numerous studies to investigate the factors affecting e-mail selection [19,28,31,46,72,74,78], these studies all used traditional e-mail, not newly emerged email systems with advanced communication features supplementing the leanness (i.e. lack of media richness) of traditional e-mail. Past studies did not investigate whether improved features provided by advanced e-mail systems help overcome traditional e-mail's leanness. Therefore, researchers have requested further studies to examine whether the previous findings are directly applicable to new forms of e-mail [20,39]. For example, El-Shinnawy and Markus [20] pointed out the issue, stating that

Little is known about how people make choices among new communication media. Yet, as new media proliferate and as new combinations of media emerge, people in organizations will increasingly face choices among several new media in addition to, or even in lieu of, one new medium and several traditional ones. This suggests the need for, and value of, studies that examine media choice when multiple electronic media are available (p. 443).

Our study seeks to address this issue in the context of the use of avatar e-mail, one of the advanced types of email. The term “avatar” (or “avatara”) originates from the descent and incarnation of a deity in earthly form, chiefly associated in Hinduism with the incarnation of Vishnu. The term has been adopted from virtual reality and the 3D games arena and represents incarnation or embodiment of human beings in a virtual world. The applicability of avatars to communication systems has been widely recognized [30,34,62]. Companies that have developed and applied avatars as a business communication tool for customers have found the avatars to be useful. For example, LifeFX provides Facemail, an avatar e-mail system for interpersonal communication.<sup>1</sup> First Direct, a web-based bank, has also developed an avatar called Cara that provides financial service information to customers through mobile phones. Yahoo's Jenni, CocaCola's Hank, and Amtrak's Julie are avatars with a face and body–an animated digital image with a voice–that serve as guides or customer service representatives on websites [67]. Simultaneously, research groups at the MIT Media Lab have examined ways to:

make communication, mediated through avatars, more lifelike and natural through appropriate and meaningful animation of the avatar's body and face … an avatar can represent suitably large amounts of information, and can operate as meaningful rhetorical devices [[30], p. 180].

Researchers focusing on computer graphics and virtual reality [24,30,33,41,51,55,59,66,81] have attempted to develop better avatars for communication, e-commerce, virtual reality, and 3D games. For instance, Anderson et al. [2] indicated the importance of avatars in support of communicator identification and perception of telepresence and realism in teleconferencing. Lee et al. [37] and Qiu and Benbasat [55] also found that telepresence was affected by avatar use in an experimental setting simulating online shopping. Avatar e-mail is not yet widespread in the United States and other Western countries, but it does show promise as a next-generation e-mail system, considering its advanced richness features (e.g., a variety of facial expression, voice, gestures, background music)<sup>2</sup> and successful adoptions in several countries (e.g., South Korea and Japan). While some speculations [54] have been made by practitioners about avatar e-mail and its advantageous features, there is a paucity of theorybased research on avatar e-mail. A solid theoretical grounding is necessary to investigate individual perceptual differences when comparing avatar e-mail to traditional e-mail and conditions affecting media choice.

This study has three objectives. First, the media characteristics of avatar e-mail are compared to traditional e-mail with respect to media richness and social presence characteristics. Second, different media choice under different task equivocality (high versus low) and communication direction (lateral versus upward) is examined. Finally, the influence of media type on task performance is investigated. Through the study, a richer understanding of media selection in the presence of avatar e-mail emerges.

## 2. Research background and hypotheses

Developing effective and efficient communication channels between organizational members is critical in organizations [13]. The channels provide a streamlined information flow resulting in timely and accurate decision making. However, as the organizational environment becomes complex, turbulent, and uncertain, organizational structure and business process changes are needed to provide accurate information to knowledge workers [1,65,82]. Providing accurate information becomes more difficult as organizations become decentralized due to globalization, virtual teams, teleworkers, etc., and unstructured due to multi-directional communications between organizational members and other communication partners [47,60,65,79]. Therefore, knowledge workers in organizations have experienced inaccuracy, lack of understanding, and confusion during information exchange.

To resolve these problems, advanced communication media have been developed and introduced into the organizational environment. Since each medium has its strengths and weaknesses and requires different costs and efforts to use, selecting an appropriate medium for resolving a specific business problem becomes an important decision. Researchers have attempted to provide theoretical justification for selecting a pertinent medium for a specific situation [6,19]. Although past findings on media choice conditions provide us with a richer understanding of an individual's media choice, the findings need to be retested as media equipped with new communication features are developed and implemented in the business environment [19,53,60]. In particular, this study reevaluates the findings of previous studies by examining avatar e-mail and investigating whether it has different media characteristics compared to traditional email. In addition, this study examines individuals' avatar and traditional e-mail choice under the two situational factors of task equivocality and communication direction.

## 2.1. Media characteristics

Theories addressing an individual's media choice have been developed and tested. Out of them, rational theories [19] of media choice such as media richness theory and social presence theory [28,46,52,60,72] have received the most attention from researchers of organizational communication [32,80].

Media richness theory [12,13,26,32,60,71] assumes that objective characteristics exist that distinguish rich and lean media. Media richness is defined as “the ability of media to overcome different frames of reference or clarify ambiguous issues to change understanding in a timely manner” [[45], p. 147]. The richness characteristics consist of multiple cues involving the use of multiple information channels, such as voice inflection and body gestures included with the verbal message; immediate feedback, which allows for rapid bidirectional communication and rapid reinterpretation and clarification of messages; personal focus (or personalization), which allows for the infusion of personal feelings and emotions, as well as the tailoring of messages to the needs and current situation of the receiver; and language variety, which refers to the range of meanings that can be conveyed by the available pool of symbols in a language. Previous studies [5,21,26,29], indicate that the media fall in the same richness order, whether they are arranged by media richness with face-to-face as the richest medium and electronic media as a lean medium.

Similarly, social presence theory [4,7,16,42,49,57,64] proposes that people want to perceive other people to be physically present when they interact with others and that each medium has a different capacity to provide such social presence. Social presence theory defines social presence as “the degree of salience of the other person in the interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal relationship” [[64], p.65], a quality inherent in a communication medium. A medium that provides more interaction cues could be judged as being warm, personal, sensitive, and sociable. The more that other persons are perceived to be present in communication, the more participants would be personal in their communication and would engage more frequently in socio-emotional communication [4]. Conversely, when a medium provides a low level of social presence, people with whom one is communicating become less like real people because the low level of social presence slows down the speed of a person's communication task, impairing communication performance [42].

Based on the postulation of rational theories of media choice, we can investigate whether avatar e-mail has distinctive characteristics different from traditional email. Table 1 shows media characteristics, derived from the rational theories, which are used for comparison criteria. They include multiple cues, immediate feedback, personal focus, language variety and social presence. We predict that avatar e-mail has different media richness characteristics compared to traditional e-mail. Avatar e-mail enables multiple cues, taking nonverbal and verbal forms, whereas traditional e-mail enables verbal and pictorial cues but restricts the nonverbal cues that can be transmitted. Verbal cues are cues that can be represented linguistically, whereas pictorial cues are those that are provided by images. Nonverbal cues include general appearance and dress, body movements, facial expressions, smell, paralanguage (vocal cues beyond the spoken words), touch, space, and distance, as well as eye contact and gaze. Avatar e-mail enables natural language, which, together with audio cues, provides language variety and language content. Finally, personalized features make avatar e-mail more amenable to the transmission of feelings and emotions. Avatar e-mail senders can select an avatar that best represents themselves to their communication partners. Furthermore, senders can personalize the avatar by changing its color, clothes, and facial expression based on their preference and emotional status. Avatar e-mail also provides high social presence because it is armed with diverse multimedia features and a variety of cues that provide higher vividness and interactivity.

H2. Avatar e-mail provides more immediate feedback than traditional e-mail.

H3. Avatar e-mail provides higher personal focus than traditional e-mail.

H4. Avatar e-mail provides more language variety than traditional e-mail.

## 2.2. Task equivocality

The influence of task equivocality on media selection has been widely studied [16,25,27,56,58,61,69,75]. Task equivocality refers to “ambiguity, the existence of multiple and conflicting interpretations about an organizational situation. Equivocality often means confusion, disagreement and lack of understanding.” [[13], p. 357]. The effect of task equivocality has been examined based mainly on media richness theory, assuming that high equivocal tasks require a rich medium to facilitate the movement toward shared meaning and consensus, whereas low equivocal tasks are best resolved through the leanest channels because rich channels provide communicators with too much information and superfluous messages [16,25].

Previous studies have confirmed the relationship between high equivocal tasks and rich media as well as low equivocal tasks and lean media. For example, Daft et al. [13] selected five tasks that had different levels of equivocality. They found that managerial media choice was significantly affected by task equivocality. Managers prefer richer mediums (e.g., face-to-face) for highly equivocal tasks and leaner mediums (e.g., e-mail) for tasks of low equivocality. Trevino et al. [73] also demonstrated that for high equivocal tasks, managers are more likely to choose face-to-face communication rather than telephone, written memo, and e-mail. For these reasons, e-mail is more likely to be chosen for low equivocal tasks.

Based on the above studies, we examine whether there is a significantly different pattern of use between avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail when conducting high and low equivocal tasks. First, we propose that for high equivocal tasks, avatar e-mail is more likely used than traditional e-mail. High equivocal tasks bring confusion and lack of understanding [16,28], requiring a medium which provides multiple cues, social presence, and personalization. Avatar e-mail provides these cues through features such as customizable hairstyle, facial expressions, background music, voice, and basic gestures to help users deliver richer expressions to communication partners. Traditional e-mail users cannot express richness in their communication for highly equivocal tasks. Second, we believe that for low equivocal tasks, people prefer using traditional e-mail to avatar e-mail because low equivocal tasks dealing with known and clear information can successfully be performed without rich communication features. That is, people consider the effort to use avatar e-mail (e.g., creating facial expressions, including voice) as redundant and an expensive investment and in turn prefer using traditional email. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H6. For high equivocal tasks, avatar e-mail will be used more than traditional e-mail.

H7. For low equivocal tasks, traditional e-mail will be used more than avatar e-mail.

## 2.3. Communication direction

Previous studies have also found that communication direction influences media choice. For example, Zmud et al. [83] asserted that a perspective shift from the message sender to the message receiver was observed in moving from lateral to downward communication. Based on their findings, they pointed out the inappropriateness of previous media selection studies that ignored directional differences of media selection. Lee et al. [38] found that U.S. employees sent fewer e-mails to superiors than to colleagues to conduct the same tasks. Further, Allen and Griffeth [1] demonstrated that e-mail users were less likely to send e-mail to superiors (upward communication) than to colleagues or team members (lateral communication). This is because lateral communication shares information between persons of the same or similar hierarchical level, whereas upward communication often is filtered and mainly related to complex tasks, such as problem solving, forecasting, and decision making support [48]. Therefore, upward communication requires a relatively richer medium than lateral communication.

Along the same vein, we propose that avatar e-mail is more likely to be used for upward communication than traditional e-mail. This is because avatar e-mail can be used as an alternative rich medium for upward communication. Meanwhile, for lateral communication, traditional e-mail is expected to be preferred to avatar e-mail because the cost to implement richness features is extensive and the tasks conducted through e-mail communication generally are unequivocal. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H8. For upward communication, avatar e-mail will be used more than traditional e-mail.

H9. For lateral communication, traditional e-mail will be used more than avatar e-mail.

## 2.4. Task performance

Based on the postulation of rational theories of media choice, we further examine whether task performance differences between avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail users exist. Previous studies [3,17,18,65,69] have examined performance differences between communication media in the presence of conditional factors. To measure performance differences, researchers have used effectiveness such as quality of outcomes and number of correct answers, and efficiency variables such as speed [69]. Similar to previous studies, we selected two performance variables, length of message and response speed, and examined performance differences between avatar and traditional e-mail users for conducting tasks with different equivocality. For conducting a highly equivocal task, we predict that avatar e-mail users send or receive lengthier e-mail messages than traditional email users. When using its rich communication features, avatar e-mail users can develop detailed and complete information and deliver it to their partners. Diverse cues can be used for creating a responding e-mail. We believe the same result will take place when conducting a low equivocal task due to the characteristics of the medium.

In addition, avatar e-mail users are expected to send a faster response when conducting the same high equivocal task than with traditional e-mail because a richer medium has been characterized as requiring immediate feedback. Avatar e-mail users can quickly compose and send equivocal information using both verbal and nonverbal cues. However, for performing a low equivocal task, traditional e-mail is expected to deliver faster messages than avatar e-mail users because of its task characteristics. Low equivocal tasks are generally simple, concise, and straight forward and do not require diverse cues. By only using text, enough information can be delivered. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H10a. For high equivocal tasks, avatar e-mail users send or receive lengthier messages than traditional email users.

H10b. For high equivocal tasks, avatar e-mail users send or receive faster responses than traditional e-mail users.

H11a. For low equivocal tasks, avatar e-mail users send or receive lengthier messages than traditional e-mail users.

H11b. For low equivocal tasks, traditional e-mail users send or receive faster responses than avatar e-mail users.

## 3. Research methods and results

Three field studies were conducted to examine (1) individuals' perceptual difference between traditional and avatar e-mail, (2) their media choice given different levels of task equivocality and different communication directions, and (3) the relationship between task performance and media choice. A university e-mail system was selected as a traditional e-mail system and a commercial avatar e-mail system was selected as an avatar email system. The traditional e-mail supports text messages only. It includes send/receive, reply, forward, and find features. Meanwhile, in addition to text messages, the avatar e-mail system provides extra features in cluding tens of avatars, customizable options, hairstyle, facial expressions, background music, voice, and basic gestures.

Surveys were conducted with evening MBA students in two South Korean universities who had experienced both avatar and traditional e-mail. Subjects from South Korea were selected because both traditional and avatar e-mail are widely dispersed as interpersonal communication media. Avatar use in South Korea has been well ahead of that in North America and Europe [50]. More than 10 million people in South Korea reportedly use avatar e-mail, and sales in the avatar market were more than US\$100 million. Subjects were recruited through campus advertisements and class announcements. Most of the subjects (94%) were working professionals. For Study 1, 742 subjects were recruited. An invitation letter with a questionnaire was distributed and 505 subjects provided usable responses. Study 2 was conducted with the 505 subjects from Study 1. A quasi-experimental study was performed and a total of 452 subjects completed the survey. Finally another quasi-experimental study (Study 3) was conducted using a total of 285 subjects. On average, they were 32.7 years old, had 4.88 years of traditional e-mail use experience, and 2.01 years of avatar e-mail experience. For all three studies, participation was voluntary. Both class points and a small token (a book gift certificate) were provided as compensation for participation. See Table 2 for detailed demographic information.

Subjects were directed to view the video script before completing a questionnaire. After removing incomplete responses, a total of 505 usable responses were gathered.

Instrument items of media richness constructs were developed by following a scientific instrument development procedure [68]. These instrument items were created based on Daft et al.'s [13] conceptualization and on instruments from follow-up studies [21,80]. As shown in Table 3, at least three instrument items for each construct were then operationalized to fit into the research context for a total of 19 items. As recommended by Churchill [9] and Cook and Campbell [10], pre- and pilot tests were carried out to develop and validate the instrument items. A pre-test was conducted with seven experts, consisting of faculty members, IS doctoral students, and user representatives who had used both avatar and traditional e-mail for at least one year. Wording and questionnaire formats then were modified based on the experts' suggestions in order to verify content validity. Finally, we administrated a pilot test with 36 business major students who had experienced both e-mail systems and found that all constructs had an Eigen value of more than 1, indicating that they satisfied unidimensionality and convergent validity. All constructs also showed high reliability with the lowest Cronbach's α at 0.91. For the pre-test, discriminant validity could not be assessed because of a restriction in sample size [23]. One immediate feedback item that did not group well into the factor was removed. Each item took the form of a declarative statement suggesting whether needs were being met, or not, with respondents agreeing or disagreeing on a 7-point Likert-type scale.<sup>3</sup> 3

## 3.1.2. Study 1: Results

3.1.2.1. Analysis of psychometric properties. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted to examine psychometric properties of the instrument. Psychometric properties were measured by investigating convergent and discriminant validity and reliability. Convergent validity refers to “whether the items comprising a scale behave as if they are measuring a common underlying construct” [[15], p.327]. As shown in the bold numbers in Table 4, the items measuring the same construct are highly correlated with one another and their factor loading scores are all greater than 0.7. Therefore, convergent validity was confirmed.

Discriminant validity is defined as “the ability of a measurement item to differentiate between constructs being measured” [[15], p.327]. Discriminant validity is confirmed if an item is more highly related with items measuring the same factor than with items for measuring different factors. As shown in Table 4, all items correlate more highly with other items intended to measure the same factor than with either the same item used to measure a different factor or with different items used to measure a different construct. Therefore, discriminant validity was confirmed. Finally, reliability was examined using Cronbach α and all constructs exhibited alphas over 0.90, indicating high reliability of items used for each factor.

## 3.2.2. Study 2: Independent variables

3.2.2.1. Task equivocality. To create tasks of varying equivocality, equivocality was defined following Lim and Benbasat's [40] operationalization. Equivocal tasks were characterized as being highly variable, unclear, and difficult to analyze. To identify the tasks, 15 subjects who had at least one year of use experience on both avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail were interviewed. We first explained our purpose of identifying both high and low equivocal tasks. The subjects were then asked to identify five tasks that they encountered during their daily e-mail communication at work and to rank them based on the tasks' equivocality levels. Seventeen total tasks were identified. Seven researchers and practitioners examined the results and selected four tasks, two with high equivocality and two with low equivocality, as shown in Table 7. Subjects were asked to evaluate the degree to which they felt that the provided information was equivocal. Most of the tasks are coincident with those of previous research [e.g., [11]].

3.2.2.2. Communication direction. Communication direction was manipulated in two directions: lateral and upward. We asked the same 15 subjects used for the task equivocality interviews to specify 5 persons to whom they mostly communicated using e-mail at work. Then, we asked the subjects to classify 5 persons with respect to lateral and upward communication partners. Based on the results, we selected team members, colleagues, and employees of other departments who held a similar organizational hierarchy position as communication counterparts in lateral communication, and executives, supervisors, or high-level managers outside of the companies as targets for upward communication. Similar approaches were used to manipulate communication direction in previous studies [e.g., [38]].

3.2.2.3. Media type. Avatar and traditional e-mail were selected as two media types and their difference was measured using perceived media richness scales. As we discussed earlier, a text-based university e-mail system that all subjects have an account with was selected as a traditional e-mail, and a commercial avatar e-mail equipped with tens of avatars, customizable hairstyles, facial expressions, background music, voices, and gestures and with the largest users was selected as the avatar e-mail system.

## 3.2.3. Study 2: Dependent variable

Usage intention was selected as a dependent variable. Previous studies have indicated that usage intention is most frequently used as a surrogate measure of actual usage $[ 6 3 , 7 0 , 7 6 ] . ^ { 4 }$ Two instrument items were adopted from previous studies (e.g., [77] to measure usage intention). Two items are “I intend to use (avatar or traditional) e-mail as my communication medium in the future” and “I will use (avatar or traditional) e-mail as my communication medium in the future.”

## 3.2.4. Study 2 result: Manipulation check

Before conducting a main study, thirty five evening MBA students were selected to conduct a manipulation check examining the perception of task equivocality, communication direction, and media type. We found that subjects perceived significant differences for those independent variables. First, we provided subjects with four randomly ordered tasks, two of which were of low equivocality and two of high equivocality. Each subject was asked to answer each question using 7-point Likerttype scales. We conducted a t-test to verify the success of manipulation for tasks of low and high equivocality. If the manipulation was successful, the subjects were expected to be more likely to indicate that highly equivocal tasks are “high equivocal” and that tasks of low equivocality are “less equivocal.” As anticipated, the mean difference for the low and highly equivocal tasks was significant (t = 15.947, p b 0.001), with the mean scores being 5.70 (S.D. =0.879) for highly equivocal tasks and 2.45 (S.D. = 0.776) for tasks of low equivocality. The Cronbach α for high equivocal tasks was 0.96 and for low equivocal tasks was 0.91.

Then, the test subjects were provided with six randomly ordered scenarios which included different communication partners with different organizational ranks. Three of them were highly ranked persons for upward communication and three of them were equally ranked persons for lateral communication. Given the scenario to send an e-mail to a communication partner, the subjects were asked their perception of the rank of target person using a 7-point Likert-type scale. If the manipulation was successful, the participants would perceive team members, colleagues, and employees of other depart ments at a similar organizational hierarchy as “equal”, while executives, supervisors, or high-level managers outside of the companies would be perceived as “high”. The mean differences for the lateral and upward communication was significant (t = 6.73, p b 0.001), with the mean scores being 6.1 (S.D. = 0.21) for upward communication and 3.89 (S.D. = 0.33) for lateral commu nication. The Cronbach α for upward communication was 0.89, and that of lateral communication was 0.90.

Finally, the same subjects participated in a session held in a computer lab equipped with internet-connected computers. Subjects were provided with two types of email: avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail. Using the same video script from Study 1, covering major features, system requirements, and a demonstration of sending and receiving e-mails, the two e-mail systems used for the experiment were demonstrated. Then, the subjects were asked to perform a hands-on exercise of sending an e-mail to a friend. Finally, they were asked a question about perceived media richness. The subjects were expected to be more likely to indicate that avatar e-mail is a “rich” medium and that traditional e-mail is a “lean” media. As a result, we found a significant mean difference between the avatar e-mail system and the traditional e-mail system $( t { = } 2 1 . 8 7 , p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ with the mean 6.02 (S.D. = 0.37) for avatar e-mail and the mean 2.21 (S.D. = 0.25) for traditional e-mail. In summary, the manipulation of the independent variables appeared to be successful.

## 3.3. Study 3

## 3.3.1. Study 3: Procedure

45 days after Study 2, participants from Study 2 $( n _ { 2 } = 4 5 2 )$ were randomly assigned into one of the four experimental conditions shown in Table 9. The goal of this study was to examine the difference in task performance between avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail users. Subjects were requested to visit their e-mail archival data and examine three e-mails exchanged with either supervisors or colleagues within the past 6 months. In particular, subjects were asked to examine e-mail messages with either a high equivocal task or low equivocal task depending on experimental conditions. Then, they answered questions related to task performance such as quality of message, message length, and speed of response. A total of 284 subjects completed questionnaires<sup>5</sup>.

## 3.3.2. Study 3: Variables

3.3.2.1. Independent variables. The same independent variables from Study 2 were applied to conduct Study 3. However, to measure task equivocality, a single item for a high and low equivocal task was used. The high equivocal task was measured using the item “information on your ideas and plans for a new project you will work on,” while the low equivocal task was measured using the item “information on a list of names and phone numbers of your major customers.”

3.3.2.2. Dependent variables. The experiment used two variables of task performance: message length, and speed of response. Length of message, related to effectiveness, was measured by the extent to which an e-mail includes lengthy contents. Speed of response, related to efficiency, was measured by the elapsed time between sending an e-mail and receiving a reply e-mail. The length of message sent and received was combined to calculate average length of a message. Similarly, the time taken for receiving an e-mail from colleagues after sending an e-mail request and sending an e-mail to colleagues after receiving an e-mail request were examined. All these variables were measured by averaging three e-mails examined by each subject.

## 3.3.3. Study 3: Results

The length of message and the elapsed time to exchange responses were found to be positively correlated at a fairly high magnitude $( r = . 7 9 8 , p < . 0 1 )$ to the expected direction. To test the hypotheses, we ran two separate analysis of covariance<sup>6</sup> for each dependent variable. Table 10 presents the results.

There was a significant length difference in e-mail messages for conducting a high equivocal task between traditional and avatar e-mail users. Avatar e-mail users sent or received on average of 26.81 lines of message, while traditional e-mail users sent or received on average of 22.63 lines of message. There was no length difference for conducting a low equivocal task. Both main effect for media type and task equivocality, and interaction effect between them were found on length of message. Therefore, H10a was supported, although H10b was rejected. There was no significant speed difference in responding to e-mail requests between email users for conducting either high or low equivocal tasks. Only the main effect of task equivocality was found to significant. Therefore, H11a and H11b were rejected.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

Managers dedicate a substantial amount of time communicating to make sense of their environments, coordinate and control internal and external activities, and to make decisions. When communicating, managers have to carefully select the best medium for efficiently and effectively communicating with others in different business tasks and situations. Drawing on the literature from rational theories of media choice, this study proposed perceptual differences between avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail with respect to media richness and social presence characteristics, and hypothetical relationships between communication direction, task equivocality, and media type. As a result, we identified many interesting findings worthy of further discussion.

First, we found people in general perceive more media richness and social presence while using avatar email than when using traditional e-mail. This confirms our speculation that if a traditional e-mail is supplemented with rich communication features, then it can be perceived as a rich medium. The finding indicates that new types of e-mails with different communication features should be considered as different media. However, no significant difference was found for immediate feedback, with people expecting faster feedback from traditional e-mail $( \mu _ { \mathrm { T } } { = } 5 . 7 8 1 4 )$ than avatar e-mail $\mu _ { \mathrm { A } } =$ 5.6906). The fact that they expect fast feedback from both avatar and traditional e-mail is an interesting finding in that traditional e-mail has been considered as a lean medium featuring slow feedback. This may be understood from a social symbolization perspective of e-mail in South Korea. Contrary to Western countries, email systems in South Korea have been symbolized as having an urgency or immediacy of the message, requiring participation, cooperation, involvement, and teamwork in addition to formality and legitimacy. But, because of the fear of showing less respect for others, Korean employees have been known not to send e-mail to their seniors or even colleagues [8,35,36]. Traditionally, they have chosen e-mail instead of face-to-face or telephone for communication when e-mail was the only viable choice.

Second, the significant difference between avatar and traditional e-mail use for upward communication is an interesting finding. Avatar e-mail users had a significantly higher intention to use e-mail for upward communication than did traditional e-mail users. This indicates that if e-mail were equipped with rich communication features, it would be used as a rich communication alternative for upward communication, much like faceto-face interactions versus the telephone. This finding is contradictory to that of previous studies [1] that purported that e-mail is not used for upward communication. Avatar e-mail users did, in fact, express a greater preference for sending e-mail to colleagues over sending e-mail to superiors, just like traditional e-mail users. During a debriefing interview, however, several participants indicated that the main reason for hesitating to send avatar e-mail to superiors was because of the limitation of restricted avatar selections and that the limited functions of current avatar e-mail systems hindered usage. Interviewees pointed out that current avatars are developed based on cartoon characters, and therefore are difficult to use for professional purposes. This study finds it probable that if e-mail were equipped with multiple and appropriate cues, it could be used as a rich medium alternative, especially for upward communication. E-mail developers and providers should note this result.

For lateral communication, there were no significant usage differences between avatar and traditional e-mail. This implies that avatar e-mail can be used selectively either as a lean medium or as a rich medium. Since communication partners of lateral communication share common language and culture, conduct similar tasks, and perceive closeness and relatedness between them, they have high-level mutual understanding when performing tasks. Therefore, there is no strong need for using a rich medium. Avatar e-mail systems are designed to be easily used for sending text-only messages like traditional e-mail, thus avatar e-mail users can choose either text-only messages or avatar-based e-mail messages based on communication direction.

Finally, the finding that there was no significant difference in avatar e-mail use for high and low equivocal tasks partially supports the predictions of media richness theory. Media richness theory posits that rich media would be used more for highly equivocal tasks, whereas lean media would be widely used for low equivocal tasks [12,13]. In alignment with the theory, avatar e-mail users have a strong intention to use avatar e-mail for conducting highly equivocal tasks because they perceive it to be a rich communication alternative.

The reasons for the higher use of avatar e-mail for highly equivocal tasks, however, may be unrelated to rich communication features. Because media use is determined by both media-task fit and social consensus related to media use, it is possible that the social consensus to use avatar e-mail for rich communication was the reason for the use of avatar e-mail for highly equivocal tasks. It also is important to note that the subjects of this study were relatively young, highly educated, and accustomed to adopting cutting-edge technology.

Additionally, as indicated by Suh [69], Koreans prefer to use “state-of-the-art” communication media, such as avatar e-mail, and it is this preference that may have affected the selection of avatar e-mail beyond media richness and social consensus. However, the finding that there was a higher use of avatar e-mail versus traditional e-mail for low equivocal tasks is not consistent with media richness theory's postulation that managers prefer lean media for conducting low equivocal tasks due to the high costs associated with rich media. A possible explanation for this effect is that some users feel that enjoyment (or fun) related to the use of avatar e-mail may be significantly higher than those of traditional email. An avatar e-mail user made the following comment to support the findings:

I love to use avatar e-mail. It's fun, easy and very effective. While it requires more time to complete a message than text-only e-mail, it is always enjoyable to select an avatar and put my emotions into it. I definitely use it more than traditional e-mail for conducting many kinds of tasks.

Finally, we found task performance differences between avatar e-mail and traditional e-mail users for conducting a high equivocal task. That is, avatar e-mail users sent lengthier e-mail for conducting a high equivo cal task than traditional e-mail users, implying that more complete and detailed information will be delivered to communication counterparts while using an avatar email. Although avatar e-mail users sent lengthier messages for performing a low equivocal task than traditional e-mail users, we could not find a significant difference. One potential interpretation is that the information exchanged for conducting a low equivocal task is simple and straight forward, and thus there is no need for sending a longer message to the counterparts.

However, contrary to our expectation, there was no difference in response speed. Even traditional e-mail users respond more quickly than avatar e-mail users in spite of task equivocality, although no statistically significant difference was found. The results can be interpreted in two aspects. First, in general, it takes more time for avatar e-mail users to respond to communication partners because they use multiple cues in their response. Second, as we discussed earlier, traditional email between Koreans is symbolized by urgency or immediacy of the message because of its Confucius tradition. The findings of this study suggest further studies are needed to investigate the situational or contextual factors affecting choices of different types of e-mail systems.

This study does have some limitations. We selected evening MBA students for this study. Although almost all of the students were full-time employees, selecting subjects from an organizational environment may have provided different results. The study also was limited in the examination of avatar e-mail use for downward communication from superiors to subordinates because most of the subjects were middle managers or lowerlevel employees in organizations. The generalizability of the findings is reduced because the subjects were all South Korean with a high level of education. Although we believe that South Korea is an appropriate place to investigate the effect of avatar e-mail since it has implemented considerably more advanced communication technology than any western country, the findings of this study may not be generalizable across nations. Therefore, replication of the study with subjects who have diverse educational levels from other countries is recommended. Finally, the study also was restricted by the exclusion of other communication media. Future research is necessary to compare current findings with a variety of communication media.

This study provides several theoretical and practical implications for researchers as well as practitioners. From a theoretical perspective, this study examined avatar e-mail, an advanced form of e-mail, and found that avatar e-mail could overcome the relative shortage of richness features of traditional e-mail. This indicates that findings of previous media choice studies should be reexamined in future studies of new media. This study also found that media choice in the presence of a new medium is influenced by communication direction and task equivocality, confirming the value of continual investigation of conditional factors affecting media choice. Finally, no significant difference between avatar and traditional e-mail regarding immediate feedback provides researchers a useful insight that media richness may not always be objective, but a subjective and socially constructed characteristic, in particular in different cultures. As with much research, the study findings bring questions as well as answers. Researchers must accept the challenge of conducting further studies to understand how best to use richer mediums such as avatars. There is a need to achieve clarity on just how the avatar characteristics, appearance, emotional stimuli (beyond the classically used emoticons), and presentation affect decision making. Likewise, researchers will need to examine the decision support design process in light of findings of this study. Incorporation of a richer medium such as avatars may require changes or additions to assure the most appropriate use of the medium.

For practitioners, this study demonstrated that avatar e-mail can be used as a rich communication alternative. As organizational structures become more distributed and globalized, and the dominant use of face-to-face communication changes, management may prefer to use cost-effective e-mail instead of traditional high-cost communication media for conducting organizational communication $[ 1 6 , 4 3 ]$ . The findings of this study provide evidence for the use of e-mail when conducting both rich and lean communication. The study results imply that in the future, managers must consider the richer mediums such as avatars when providing decision support in organizations. Avatars could influence the presentation and impact of information by enriching the communication in the same way that nonverbal indicators enhance human interaction. It is good news for management because adding rich features to traditional e-mail provides an inexpensive but effective communication alternative. For major e-mail providers, the study provides guidance about how to best implement avatar enhancements to their existing e-mail offerings. In summary, this study found that avatar e-mail can be used as a rich medium for conducting highly equivocal tasks as well as for upward communication. Avatar e-mail should be seen not as a toy, but as a powerful organizational communication tool.
