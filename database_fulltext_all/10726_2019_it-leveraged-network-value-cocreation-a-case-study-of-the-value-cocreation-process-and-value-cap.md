# IT-leveraged network value cocreation: a case study of the value cocreation process and value capture in the South Korean broadcast advertising industry

Jongwoo Kim <sup>a</sup>, Sunyoung Cho <sup>b</sup> and Balasubramaniam Ramesh <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>MSIS Department, University of Massachusetts Boston, Boston, MA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems Department, University of Texas Rio Grande Valley College of Business and Entrepreneurship, Brownsville, USA; <sup>c</sup>CIS Department, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Organisations operate in increasingly dynamic environments, internetworked in cooperative arrangements that cocreate value. They pursue the cocreation of value through collaborative networks, rather than in isolation. However, there is insu<sup>fi</sup>cient understanding of how networked organisations cocreate value in the network through the innovative use of information systems (IS): existing multi-<sup>fi</sup>rm studies are largely concerned with dyadic relationships. In terms of capturing value from the cocreation process, many studies have reported either enhanced organisational e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies (exploitative capability) or resulting innovations (explorative capability), but rarely both aspects simultaneously. This study attempts to draw a comprehensive picture of IS-based value leverage by reporting both the value cocreation process (network level) and value capture (organisational level) through a case study of innovative IS use in a large business network. This network links a public hub organisation with a large number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the South Korean broadcast advertising industry. Drawing on Grover and Kohli’s framework for value cocreation, the case study investigates the detailed process by which network-level value cocreation occurs in the four layers of relational arrangements. It also highlights, from an organisational ambidexterity perspective, how the cocreated value is appropriated through ambidextrous activities by the networked organisations.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) plays a key role in helping internetworked organisations to seamlessly network and collaborate with one another (Silver, Markus, & Beath, 1995) in order to cocreate value (Grover & Kohli, 2012) and share business processes; for example, by incorporating the viewpoints of customers and partner organisations into the development of products and services (Lusch & Vargo, 2014). Due to rising demand for specialisation and shorter product/service life cycles, <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly seeking other <sup>fi</sup>rms to collaborate with in cocreating technology-enabled products and services. IT is instrumental, as both a tool and an output, in generating this cocreated value and enabling organisations and their partners to interact with one another more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently and e<sup>f</sup>ectively during the value cocreation process (Grover & Kohli, 2012). Over the past decade or so, a fundamental transformation has occurred in business networks’ value creation (Grover & Kohli, 2012). This is because networked organisations collectively leverage information systems to cocreate value that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts many participants and creates synergistic e<sup>f</sup>ects for individual organisations.

However, we still have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant scope for contributing to network-level value cocreation research. First, the value co-creation process in a network remains ambiguous, and few existing studies examine how value cocreation unfolds among multiple stakeholders on the network level (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Reypens, Lievens, & Blazevic, 2016). First, many previous multi-<sup>fi</sup>rm studies concerning IT/IS value have primarily examined dyadic relationships and emphasised transaction costs. Second, developing an understanding of both the value cocreation process and value appropriation in a single study would provide a comprehensive approach, but only a few studies examine both aspects simultaneously. Recent studies suggest that we can build a more comprehensive understanding of network-level value leveraging (which is the overarching process of both value cocreation and value capture by investigating both aspects simultaneously (Morgan, Feller, & Finnegan, 2013; Reypens et al., 2016). Third, existing studies have typically examined value cocreation’s impact on either exploitation or exploration, and very few studies consider explanatory and explorative aspects simultaneously (Reypens et al., 2016). Some studies have focused on how value cocreation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacts and improves the performance of networked organisations (Grönroos, 2011; Möller, Rajala, & Westerlund, 2008). Other studies have examined how value cocreation supports organisations in achieving innovation (Ceccagnoli, Forman, Huang, & Wu, 2012; Romero & Molina, 2011). While some studies examine both aspects in a single study (Im & Rai, 2013;

Rai, Pavlou, Im, & Du, 2012) and make a contribution to understanding ambidexterity at the level of alliances and business networks, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are not easily transferable to other contexts such as networked organisations. Our research is motivated by calls for further research on value cocreation in diverse contexts that focuses on networked organisations, and it examines value capture from the perspective of organisational ambidexterity.

This study attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll the above research gaps by investigating, in the context of a large business network that spans the South Korean broadcast advertising industry, how network-level value cocreation unfolds and a<sup>f</sup>ects participating organisations’ ambidexterity. To understand the network-level process of IT-leveraged value cocreation, we draw on the framework proposed by Grover and Kohli (2012), which provides a conceptual map to understand the value cocreation process through four layers of relational arrangements. In combination with Grover and Kohli’s framework, the study employs organisational ambidexterity theory (March, 1991) to develop a comprehensive view of value capture/appropriation at the stakeholder/organisational level of a network and explain how network-level value cocreation impacts the capabilities of individual organisations. In this paper, we study an IT-based innovation driven by a hub organisation in the context of a large network. We also explore and explain the interrelationship between IT-leveraged value cocreation and its impact on the organisational ambidexterity of the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our speci<sup>fi</sup>c research questions are:

(1) How does the leveraging of innovative IT facilitate value cocreation in the four layers of relational arrangements in a large business network with multiple stakeholders?

(2) How do such network-level cocreation e<sup>f</sup>orts enable networked organisations to capture/ appropriate the cocreated value?

To answer the questions, we conducted a case study to investigate IT-based value cocreation in a large business network in the South Korean broadcast advertising industry. This business network consists of a hub organisation, KOBACO (Korea Broadcast Advertising Corporation), and its business partners. The empirical case analysis contributes to the value cocreation literature by providing a detailed understanding of how ITbased value is cocreated and shared among multiple partners in multi-organisational relationships (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Reypens et al., 2016). It extends the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of current research, which has mostly centred on dyadic analysis and emphasised transaction costs (Grover & Kohli, 2012). The study also adds value to ambidexterity research by showing that the ambidexterity perspective can be used to explain how individual organisations capture value from IT-leveraged network-level value cocreation in both exploitative and explorative ways. The empirical analysis provides qualitative evidence that cocreated value could enhance the capabilities of individual organisations in possibly unforeseen ways. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: The next section presents a literature overview of the chosen theoretical frameworks, namely value cocreation and organisational ambidexterity. The subsequent section discusses the research methods and <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our case study, and the <sup>fi</sup>nal section discusses the contributions, implications, and conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The process of it-leveraged value cocreation

Value cocreation originates in the context of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm exchanges proposing that the organisations create more value, together, by incorporating customers points of view into the development of products and services (Lusch & Vargo, 2014; Vargo & Lusch, 2004). Vargo and Lusch (2008, p.5) later extended their theories and stated that “the venue of value cocreation is the value con<sup>fi</sup>gurations” in which economic and social actors within networks interact with each other and exchange resources across and through networks. In other words, value cocreation takes place within and between systems at various levels of aggregation. When an organisation is involved in a network in which its performance is closely tied to the performance of its partners, its focus may shift from the creation of value via new products and services in isolation to the cocreation of value in close collaboration with its networked partners.

The <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS has witnessed a fundamental transformation in the creation of IT business value (Li, Van Heck, & Vervest, 2009; Schryen, 2013). Value cocreation through IT has become an emerging research topic in the context of inter-organisational settings (Dhar & Sundararajan, 2007; Grover & Kohli, 2012). As organisations increasingly participate in networks to enable knowledge and resource sharing (Lavrac et al., 2007) and take advantage of joint business opportunities, they explore the use of IT to cocreate value with partner organisations. Value cocreation helps participating organisations to improve their business competitiveness and increase the overall revenue of an industry (Dhar & Sundararajan, 2007). Given the changes in the mode of business operations spurred by internetworking technologies, the focus of research on the business value of IT has shifted from examining IT value within a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s boundaries to value cocreation at the network-level.

Despite increasing research on network-level value cocreation, the network value co-creation process is still ambiguous and few studies examine how value cocreation unfolds among multiple stakeholders (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Reypens et al., 2016). Investigating the value cocreation process in a multi-stakeholder network innovation project in the healthcare sector, Reypens et al. identi<sup>fi</sup>ed generic processes for network-level value cocreation: coordination, consultation, and compromise (Reypens et al., 2016). To contextualise value cocreation involving key IT assets, Rai et al. (2012) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a set of key IT functionalities and suggested that such IT capability pro<sup>fi</sup>les facilitate the <sup>fl</sup>ow of physical goods, information, and <sup>fi</sup>nancing across locations during inter-organisational processes, which can lead to increased relational value and loyalty. However, since no single study examines the diverse contexts of value cocreation, the study by Reypens et al. does not fully explain the key role that IT plays and Rai et al.’s study is speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the logistics industry context. Existing studies may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhance our understanding of interdependencies between organisations and related resources, which is critical to understanding ITbased value cocreation processes (Grover & Kohli, 2012).

## 2.2. Four layers of relational arrangement

When identifying divergent views in IT value cocreation research, Grover and Kohli (2012) proposed the four-layer relational-arrangement framework to disentangle complex processes of value cocreation. This framework, based on a relational perspective (Dyer, 2000), provides a comprehensive conceptual map for understanding IT value cocreation. The framework proposes that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s resources can cross the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s boundaries and reside in that <sup>fi</sup>rm’s relationships with other <sup>fi</sup>rms. We adopt this framework of four layers of relational assets because it provides a detailed conceptual map for discovering where and how value cocreation processes unfold in concrete resource layers (Grover & Kohli, 2012). The framework also focuses on IT-based value cocreation and is generic enough to guide investigation in various contexts.

The four layers refer to asset, complementary capability, knowledge-sharing, and governance layers. IT plays a key role in enabling, expanding, or creating value cocreation layers. The asset layer involves two or more <sup>fi</sup>rms, at least one of which contributes specialised IT hardware and/or software or network facilities that create new value in the form of digital (physical) products and services. For the asset layer to be realised, organisations should have established safeguards against opportunism with regard to large volumes of transactions involving networked partners. IT provides transparency for a large volume of such transactions.

The complementary capability layer focuses on identifying and exploiting complementary resources/capabilities among the partners such that, together, they represent a source of value that no single partner could create on its own. To enable the complementary capability layer, organisations should be able to identify and evaluate the potential complementary capability of their partners. Organisations should identify and be able to access complementary resources/capabilities and IT can help organisations to achieve this.

The knowledge sharing layer involves sharing information and expertise to inform decision-making and strategies for cocreating new or better products and services with networked organisations (Dyer & Hatch, 2006). For organisations to have the knowledge sharing layer, they should have the absorptive capability to recognise, assimilate, and exploit partner organisations information. In addition, incentive systems to encourage transparent transactions and information sharing should be in place. IT can provide knowledge repositories via which organisations can share knowledge and thereby cocreate value. These knowledge repositories enhance an organisation’s ability to recognise, incorporate, and use information provided by other organisations, thereby enabling collaborative decisionmaking (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990).

The governance layer refers to control mechanisms that integrate other layers and encourage alliance partners to engage in value cocreation. Research <sup>fi</sup>nds that e<sup>f</sup>ective governance is crucial for creating and capturing value in networked organisations (Morgan et al., 2013). In an alliance network, an e<sup>f</sup>ective governance layer safeguards an organisation from the risk of opportunistic activities by other organisations. Both formal contracts, and informal controls such as trust, enable the governance layer to be e<sup>f</sup>ective.

It is widely accepted that hub <sup>fi</sup>rms reside at the centre of many business networks and play a pivotal role in the formation, growth, and success of business networks, which extends to the value cocreation context. Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) de<sup>fi</sup>ned a hub <sup>fi</sup>rm as one that has gained prominence and power through its individual attributes, as well as its central position in the network structure, and uses that prominence and power to assume a leadership role, pulling together the dispersed resources and capabilities of network members. Recent studies on hub organisations have investigated how they coordinate and manage business ecosystems (Autio & Thomas, 2014). Thomas, Autio, and Gann (2014) argued that factors such as leadership, resource control, and value appropriation are crucial in hub organisations’ ability to orchestrate ecosystems. Gardet and Mothe (2012) found that the size of a hub organisation, and the degree to which other organisations depend on it, in<sup>fl</sup>uence coordination mechanisms in the innovation networks of small and medium enterprises. However, more extensive research is needed to systematically examine how hub <sup>fi</sup>rms create and extract value within their networks (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006). Based on the review and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed gaps in the value cocreation literature, this study employs Grover and Kohli’s framework to investigate the value cocreation process in a business network and the hub organisation’s role in that process. The study sheds light on the interconnection of network value cocreation and organisational ambidexterity through a case study that examines a hub organisation and its business partners in the broadcast advertising industry in South Korea.

## 2.3. Organisational ambidexterity

Ambidexterity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned di<sup>f</sup>erently in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. The literature on organisational ambidexterity provides insights into the di<sup>f</sup>erent con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands imposed on organisations and prior studies have examined ambidexterity along a scale involving exploitation versus exploration (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004), alignment versus adaptability (Tushman & O’Reilly III, 1996), or incremental versus radical innovation (Vinekar, Slinkman, & Nerur, 2006). We subscribe to Gibson and Birkinshaw’s notion of ambidexterity, according to which ambidextrous organisations can manage today’s business a<sup>f</sup>airs in an e<sup>fi</sup>cient way (exploitation), while also being adaptable to changes required by tomorrow’s environment, in order to ensure future success (exploration).

Early research on organisational ambidexterity maintained that organisations cannot pursue both exploitative and explorative activities simultaneously (Hannan & Freeman, 1977; Miller & Friesen, 1986). March (1991) argued that organisations need to be ambidextrous, or both exploitative and explorative, in order to be competitive. Research supporting this perspective noted rhat organisations can be ambidextrous, although there is a trade-o<sup>f</sup> between being exploitative and explorative. IS can help organisations to pursue both exploitative and explorative activities (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). The exploitative use of an IS includes realising short-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by coordinating and streamlining activities. Explorative activities aim to increase the ability to capture and move quickly towards the new opportunities created by an IS. Explorative activities are considered to be an investment for realising future opportunities. Successful organisations should carefully consider the trade-o<sup>f</sup> and <sup>fi</sup>nd an optimal balance between exploration and exploitation (Gupta, Smith, & Shalley, 2006).

Two types of mechanisms for achieving ambidexterity have received considerable attention in the previous literature with respect to structural and contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Structural ambidexterity refers to the creation of dual structures – separating business units or divisions that focus on exploitation from those that are involved in exploration. Contextual ambidexterity helps to balance exploitation and exploration within the same business unit, team, or even at the individual level. We subscribe to contextual ambidexterity since it enables us to richly analyse our case. Contextual ambidexterity encompasses the structural context, organisational culture, and organisational climate (Adler, Goldoftas, & Levine, 1999; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; MacDu<sup>fi</sup>e, Sethuraman, & Fisher, 1996) that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact ambidexterity.

## 2.4. Value cocreation and ambidexterity

Organisations’ value cocreation may facilitate ambidexterity (i.e., exploration and exploitation). Regarding the relationship between value cocreation and explorative innovation, much of this innovation centres on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s focal relationships with customers and suppliers. When pursuing collaboration with customers and partners, organisations can access their clients’ data, improve the quality of their services, and enhance their reputation (Greer & Lei, 2012). By creating value together with their stakeholders, organisations can expedite innova tion in their products and services. Customers can serve as sources of innovation by providing new insights and knowledge (Lee, Olson, & Trimi, 2012). Researchers have found that <sup>fi</sup>rms in the manufacturing and soft ware industries that collaborate closely with their cus tomers to create novel and innovative solutions achieve major commercial gains (Franke & Von Hippel, 2003; Füller, Jawecki, & Mühlbacher, 2007; Lettl, Hienerth, & Gemuenden, 2008). Another stream of research focuses on conditions and factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting the relationship between value cocreation and exploration; for example, antecedent conditions, such as cultural views of colla boration and technological changes, fundamentally a<sup>f</sup>ect value cocreation (Etgar, 2008; Ojanen & Hallikas, 2009). Khanagha et al.’s (2017) empirical study found that managerial engagement was necessary for organisations to achieve innovation via cocreation processes with their customers. Lee et al. (2012) argued that organisations should co-innovate with their networked stakeholders in order to survive in turbulent markets. They found that IS played a role in keeping customers informed, networked, and empowered, thereby engaging them in cocreating value through innovation. Researchers have noted that collaborative online communities are crucial for facilitating clients engagement in innovation (Antikainen, 2011; Romero & Molina, 2011). Virtual customer communities, as strategic networks, can provide access to new resources and integrate complementary competencies, thereby serving as drivers of co-innovation.

Recent research has also established that organisations can make incremental improvements and adaptations via value cocreation; for example, organisations can lower costs and improve services by leveraging the resources or skills of their partners (Grover & Kohli, 2012) rather than developing these in-house. Organisations can improve their services and work processes by collaborating with their customers in various ways and developing capabilities to adapt their products and services based on customer feedback (Romero & Molina, 2011). The adaptations can help organisations to perform according to customers’ expectations and continuously improve their operational e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, as Vargo et al. (2008) noted in the case of Microsoft and FedEx. In addition, controls and interaction mechanisms enforced by participating organisations can reduce transaction costs in the process of value cocreation. As an example, Amazon provides its customers with an online one-stop shopping tool for sourcing, ordering, and paying for products. The resulting cost reduction enables organisations to create advantages that are di<sup>fi</sup>cult for competitors to imitate (Porter, 1989).

The role of a hub organisation in such processes has received insu<sup>fi</sup>cient research attention. A hub organisation that engages with non-substitutable partners may increase its pro<sup>fi</sup>ts because it is embedded in dense networks, allowing the <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain indirect leverage over its partners (Bae & Insead, 2004). Organisations that have superior network structures and bridge structural gaps can better exploit their internal capabilities, thereby enhancing their performance (Zaheer & Bell, 2005). Leifer, O’Connor, and Rice (2001) found that hub organisations play a crucial role in radical innovation projects in their business networks, and radical innovations involving network partners provide long-term growth and renewal. Hub organisations can help in developing ideas, reducing uncertainty, expediting resource acquisition, and smoothing transition processes. Gilsing, Nooteboom, Vanhaverbeke, Duysters, and van Den Oord (2008)

also noted that successful exploration relies on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s network position and network density. Extending the existing research on hub organisations important role in business networks, this study investigates the role a hub organisation plays in cocreating value with its partners and how such e<sup>f</sup>orts, in turn, may lead to individual organisations’ ambidexterity.

## 3. Research context and method

An industry wide IS innovation in the South Korean broadcast advertising industry was chosen for this study. The network consists of a hub organisation, KOBACO (Korea Broadcast Advertising Corporation), and its business partners (advertising agencies, advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, and broadcasters). KOBACO’s hub status was established by law in 1981 as the sole provider of advertising slots for broadcasters. All advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms and agencies are required to go through KOBACO to secure advertising slots. As of 2017, 134 broadcasters (including regional broadcasters of <sup>fl</sup>agship broadcasters) and 990 advertising agencies worked with KOBACO. KOBACO is thus the single hub organisation in a network that covers an entire industry (see Figure 1).

While KOBACO was initially set up as the sole provider of advertisement sales to the broadcast services industry, competition was recently introduced through deregulation legislation. KOBACO, as the established sole provider of services, was in a position to gain a competitive advantage in the industry before deregulation arrives. Against the backdrop of pending deregulation, KOBACO developed and implemented KOBAnet, an industry-wide information system, to facilitate advertisement transactions. KOBAnet supported three main users: KOBACO, advertising agencies, and broadcasters. KOBACO provides sales information and supports <sup>fi</sup>nalisation of orders for advertisement slots through KOBAnet, and advertising agencies use it to submit their purchase requests for advertising slots and materials. Broadcasters use KOBAnet to receive sales information about broadcast advertising materials. Korean Congress passed a law introducing limited competition into the broadcast advertising industry in 2012. As of 2016, KOBACO retained 69% of the industry sales revenue.

We conducted an interpretive case study because it is well suited to answering process-oriented research questions (Yin, 2009) and understanding IT-based innovations in organisational contexts (Darke, Shanks, & Broadbent, 1998) through the theoretical lenses of value cocreation and ambidexterity. Even single cases can provide rich understandings by allowing researchers to perform in-depth analysis (Walsham, 1995). A case study can facilitate the development of richer and more informative conclusions (Alavi & Carlson, 1992; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). Given the paucity of in-depth <sup>fi</sup>eld studies on value cocreation surrounding IS (Sarker, Sarker, Sahaym, & Bjørn-Andersen, 2012), the unique setting of KOBAnet provides us with a rare opportunity to understand value cocreation and ambidexterity in an industry-wide network of organisations o<sup>f</sup>ering a theoretically relevant organisational context. The main data sources are interviews and documents such as company overviews, project development data, and customer satisfaction survey data, which were obtained over several years. Two researchers conducted interviews with managers and executives within KOBACO, broadcasters (small and large), and advertising agencies during the adoption of KOBAnet, the focal system developed by KOBACO. The interviews were conducted with 28 individuals from 21 organisations, primarily between 2008 and 2013 (see Table 1). Follow-up interviews with key informants continued until 2017.

Tailored interview guidelines were developed for the three groups of interviewees, namely from KOBACO (the hub), broadcasters, and advertising agencies. Interviews were semi-structured and typically lasted between 30 to 60 minutes. Follow-up interviews were conducted if needed to seek further information and clari<sup>fi</sup>cations. All interviews were recorded and subsequently transcribed. Additionally, notes were taken during and immediately following each interview. The interviewing researchers also held a brief review session after each interview to summarise the major concepts covered in the interviews. Following recommended practices in qualitative studies, we present evidence herein through quotes from the collected qualitative data (Mason, 2002). Each quote identi<sup>fi</sup>es the organisation and the role of the respondent (see Table 1). Broadcasters and advertising agencies are referred to by letter to preserve anonymity.

From the documents and interviews, the researchers sought a chronological understanding of the development and adoption of KOBAnet. The themes and coding schemes were developed based on the chosen theories of value cocreation and organisational ambidexterity. Events and activities were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as exploitation or exploration oriented. The data were analysed using the four-layer value cocreation framework and impacts on organisational ambidexterity. The overall analysis was re<sup>fi</sup>ned by going back and forth between the theory and the data, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interview notes, transcripts, and documents (Mason, 2002; Miles & Huberman, 1994). We used value cocreation and ambidexterity theories as both an up-front guide to inform this study and an outcome of the study with our research model and theoretical insights (Sarker, Xiao, Beaulieu, & Lee, 2018).

One of the authors had career experiences in the broadcast advertising industry, which provided sound background understanding of the case and a frame of reference for the data collected during our interviews. The analysis focused on processes of value cocreation, beginning with keywords, concepts, and categories guided by our literature review. Based on similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences between concepts, these concepts and the two main constructs (value cocreation and ambidexterity) were revised and merged to better represent the underlying meaning, as shown in Table 2. For example, data on innovation, transformation, and system integration were considered representative of exploration. As Strauss and Corbin (1990) suggest, the theoretical relevance of concepts was indicated by their repeated presence or notable absence when comparing incidents. The data analysis process described above helped us establish the plausibility and consistency of the concepts, sub-categories, and relationships that emerged.

## 5. Value cocreation in the network

## 5.1. Asset layer

The asset layer involves at least one organisation’s commitment of special IT resources that co-create new and valuable services and products. An established relationship and large numbers of interorganisational transactions provide a foundation for developing a key IT asset. In addition, KOBACO and its partners possess shared transaction-related expertise and knowledge in the advertising industry. Trust among these organisations played a key role in developing an asset for them to use. In our case study, KOBAnet, designed and developed by the hub (KOBACO), is at the core of the asset layer that enabled other asset developments and created new value in the form of digital (physical) products and services. It helped KOBACO to digitise most aspects of its sales transactions and facilitate the seamless integration of the various systems used by KOBACO and its partner organisations (advertising agencies and broadcasters).

From a very early stage of KOBAnet, KOBACO meticulously involved its partners in the development so that the system would be e<sup>f</sup>ectively integrated with partners’ own systems. In parallel, KOBACO, in close collaboration with its partners over a long period of time, developed an industry standard for coding advertising transaction data in the broadcasting industry. The creation of this standardised vocabulary, or data dictionary, facilitated interactions between KOBACO’s partners and enabled the integration of advertising data through KOBAnet. Once KOBACO garnered consensus between all partners regarding a digital media standard, it invested in expensive hardware and software platforms that could be used by its partners to convert their advertising materials from traditional to digital media. The new system, called KODEX (which is a standardised digital advertising material system), was integrated as a major component of KOBAnet. This not only facilitated the creation of digital versions of advertisements, but also enabled the digital transmission of advertising materials from advertising agencies to KOBACO and from KOBACO to broadcasters. KOBACO, the hub company, invested signi<sup>fi</sup>cant resources and e<sup>f</sup>ort in building the core IT assets over time and actively engaged network partners in the process. Using the prominence and power gained from its central position in the network structure, KOBACO assumed a leadership role, pulling together the dispersed resources and capabilities of network members in the process of building the core network-level IS assets (Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006). Various informants made the following comments:

From an IS point of view, we developed and opened a nice system . . . attracted others to use the system. It was very efective. In the past, Sony developed Beta independently, losing to VTS badly. We are the opposite case. Standardizing and sharing it created a situation in which other organizations were absorbed into our side. (KOBACO, Manager)

In the beginning, there was some objection as KOBACO took the initiative, making us dependent on data from KOBACO. Then, noticing the speed of transactions, we realized the merit and actively participated in it (development) . . . numerous requests (during development) . . . now most of them reflected on the system. (Broadcaster A, Manager)

Now a system capable of exchanging real-time information 24 hours [a day] is established. With this exchange of information, tasks at KOBACO became quite transparent . . . the advertising purchasing condition for advertisers has improved. (Broadcaster B, Manager)

KOBAnet and its key components are what Dyer and Singh (1998) call “relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets”. These assets create value when they are used as safeguards to limit opportunism and utilised in high volume exchanges. IT supports these safeguards by providing greater transparency of asset usage and lower transaction costs among partners and, through transparent and frequent use of assets, IT enhances the relationship between partners, leading to cocreation of products and services (Grover & Kohli, 2012). KOBAnet provides a prime example of the role of IT in value co-creation. The investment in this platform dramatically reduced the time and e<sup>f</sup>ort taken by both KOBACO and its partners to transmit advertisements, from their creation to broadcasting and archiving. KOBAnet, after being upgraded with KODEX, became a core IS asset, providing networked organisations with a comprehensive view of advertising sales processes and streamlining the sales processes for both KOBACO and its partners. Thus, it provided greater transparency in sales processes and lowered transaction costs among partners.

## 5.2. Complementary capability layer

The complementary capability layer involves identifying and exploiting complementary resources and capabilities among the partners such that, together, they represent a source of value that no partner could create on its own (Grover & Kohli, 2012); for example, network organisations can cocreate a business process by sharing their complementary capabilities of order-taking and delivery logistics in order to lower costs and improve customer service. KOBACO and its partners were able to leverage KOBAnet to share real-time business process information and create integrated business processes between themselves to improve customer service. Using KOBAnet, KOBACO could provide advertising agencies with accurate, real-time sales data and predict audience ratings that had not been available in the past. Advertising agencies, in turn, used this real-time information to provide more cost-e<sup>f</sup>ective sales for their own clients. Using KOBAnet, advertisers, who used to be excluded from the information loop until later in the contract, were also able to check their contract status in real time, which enhanced their service satisfaction.

KOBACO developed a business opportunity via KOBAnet by sharing available advertising slots with its partners in real time. In the past, when advertising slots became available due to sudden changes in broadcasting schedules, they were sold manually, with poor results, or remained unsold. Sharing operational sales information in real time through KOBAnet allowed KOBACO to maximise sales of these last-minute time slots (a perishable commodity), which in turn allowed its partners to purchase advertising slots they were previously unable to obtain due to lack of information.

KOBACO actively developed new sales methods . . . then, our partners positively evaluated them in order to provide their support . . . In the past that was not even possible. Now, as we have a well-developed system (KOBAnet), such collaboration is thinkable and possible on that basis. (KOBACO – Manager)

An empty slot no one knew the existence of may occur when an advertiser suddenly cancels an advertisement. In the past, only KOBACO was aware of such slot: advertisers and advertising agencies had no idea. Such information is (now) available at KOBAnet (Advertising Agency A – Manager)

The analysis shows that KOBAnet and its related IT assets served as a tool to identify and leverage complementary capabilities in cocreating value for all partner <sup>fi</sup>rms, as Grover and Kohli (2012) suggested.

## 5.3. Knowledge-sharing layer

KOBACO and its partners routinely met to negotiate deals on advertising sales and exchange sales-related information and knowledge. KOBAnet made this knowledge exchange fast and transparent, even enabling organisations to create new business knowledge. KOBAnet provided KOBACO and its partners with the necessary IT infrastructure to capture, store, share, and analyse advertising information and distribute knowledge; for example, KOBACO compiled statistical data on broadcast advertising and media consumers and shared this data with its network partners through KOBAnet. This helped KOBACO’s partners, such as advertising agencies and broadcasters, to retrieve historical sales data quickly and easily. Advertising agencies could search for and compile multi-year data about their clients and broadcasters in order to analyse sales trends. Based on this analysis, advertising agencies could choose marketing strategies and develop new services. KOBACO, in turn, could analyse advertising agencies’ sales requests and understand its partners’ preferences and market trends.

We have and share data on the broadcast advertising industry and media consumers . . . Detailed data, such as the sales amount per advertising agency, advertiser, or media, is available . . . They [the advertising agencies] use this data for suggestions or internal evaluations. For example, your competitor spent this amount [with its clients] for advertising on this media, so we should do a similar [marketing campaign]. (KOBACO, Director)

It (KOBAnet) has been useful for our company and KOBACO to manage our sales. Beyond that, information useful to all three stakeholders including KOABCO, ad agencies, and broadcasters, are posted to be shared; what other broadcasters are doing, AD agencies’ suggestions, requests, and interests. That is an interesting part that all three parties read and share. (Broadcaster B, Manager)

KOBAnet not only facilitated rapid exchange of knowledge, but also provided KOBACO and its partners with the necessary IT infrastructure to capture, store, share, and analyse advertising information and distribute knowledge. KOBACO compiled statistical data on broadcast advertising and media consumers and shared this data with its network partners through KOBAnet.

## 5.4. Governance layer

The governance layer incorporates e<sup>f</sup>ective controls for reducing transaction costs and nurturing new value cocreation, which can be either formal or informal controls. O<sup>fi</sup>cial contracts and economic safeguards are examples of formal controls, while social norms and culture may constitute informal controls.

The governance layer regulates and integrates the other three layers. The special regulation that established KOBACO in 1981 represents a formal governance layer. This law made KOBACO the sole provider of advertising slots in the South Korean broadcasting industry for over three decades. Because of this legal protection, despite pending deregulation, KOBACO was able to initiate IS projects targeting industry-wide innovation, engage in value cocreation, and secure funding for expanding its services.

KOBACO skillfully leveraged its informal governance structure. For example, during and after the development of KOBAnet, KOBACO actively engaged its partner organisations in gathering and sharing ideas and suggestions for the development and management of the new system. In the development stage, KOBACO also collected system requirements through informal meetings with its employees and their counterparts in the partner organisations. Such feedback contributed to the successful development and implementation of KOBAnet, as well as to enhanced satisfaction among user organisations.

[KOBACO is] in a suficiently monopolistic position, that [development of KOBAnet] has been possible to control . . . Because KOBACO dominated the industry, it had all the sales systems and relevant information. That circumstance and environment enabled KOBACO to keep engaging with its customers and develop sales systems. By contrast, companies in a cable TV industry could not have a hub company [like KOBACO] due to diferent and conflicting interests. (Advertising Agency B, Manager)

KOBACO regularly collected opinions and comments from advertising agencies as well as broadcasters, then applied them . . . to the system updates from time to time . . . that was necessary to each other. (Advertising Agency C, Director)

With KOBACO e<sup>fi</sup>ciently leveraging the formal and informal governance structure, KOBAnet was e<sup>f</sup>ectively adopted and rolled out across the network. More than 1,100 organisations in total, spanning 134 broadcasters and more than 990 advertising agencies, adopted KOBAnet.

## 6. Value cocreation leading to ambidexterity

The case analysis shows how network-level value cocreation is achieved in the four above-mentioned layers. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support previous research reporting that network-level innovation outcomes enable individual partners to improve their internal processes (e.g., (Reypens et al., 2016). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also shed further light on the nature of the improvements, especially with regard to enhancing explorative and exploitative activities both for KOBACO and its partners. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, value cocreation that is achieved on the four layers enables both the hub and the partner organisations to enhance their organisational ambidexterity through value capture/appropriation. We discuss how this is achieved through process improvements in each layer.

In the asset layer, KOBACO and its partners collaborated to develop and manage KOBAnet and KODEX, two core assets for value cocreation. KOBAnet and its integration with KODEX represented key shared assets that enabled network-level value cocreation via digitisation of advertising materials and related processes. These, in turn, facilitated the value capture/appropriation through exploitative activities, such as the following, at both KOBACO and its partners:

● Speedy and accurate delivery of transaction documents and media <sup>fi</sup>les due to digitisation

● Reduced manpower required to handle advertising sales and resulting cost savings

● Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduced errors in sales data and improved data quality

In addition, value capture/appropriation was facilitated by explorative activities such as innovation in sales practices (e.g., GRPs – guarantee, preemption, and designation of position in break).

In the complementary capability layer, KOBACO and its partners identi<sup>fi</sup>ed what the partners could improve and used this information to engage them in creating greater business value than if they had not shared their capabilities. When KOBACO innovated in its sales methods, advertising agencies were able to provide coste<sup>f</sup>ective sales packages to their clients. This facilitated value capture/appropriation through exploitative activities, such as the development of dynamic and transparent advertisement sales. In addition, value capture/appropriation was also facilitated by explorative activities such as KOBACO’s ability to provide new services to small advertising agencies, and its partners’ ability to o<sup>f</sup>er new products and services to their own clients.

In the knowledge-sharing layer, KOBACO and its partners exchanged data and knowledge via KOBAnet;

for example, value capture/appropriation was facilitated through exploitative activities such as the provision of online access to the status of processed advertisements in real time, supporting a faster and more accurate <sup>fl</sup>ow of information between KOBACO and its partners, and thus permitting the maximum utilisation of timeperishable advertising slots that bene<sup>fi</sup>ted both the hub organisation and its partners. In addition, KOBACO was able to engage in value capture/appropriation through explorative activities, such as the reorganisation of sales functions, to foster innovation and develop patent protection for KOBAnet and RM Partners. Moreover, these activities helped partner organisations to achieve enhanced customer satisfaction though the provision of new, customised products and services.

In the governance layer, both formal and informal controls gave KOBACO the authority and political power necessary to build a foundation for value cocreation. As required by the relevant laws and regulations, KOBACO had the responsibility of leading the broadcast advertising industry. KOBACO’s partners understood this responsibility and cooperated with KOBACO in the development of KOBAnet and, thus, in value cocreation opportunities. The informal and formal governance structures enabled the capture/appropriation of value that resulted from the adoption of KOBAnet by a wide network of partners.

Thus, with the introduction of KOBAnet, which was at the core of network value cocreation, both KOBACO and its partners were able to achieve increased ambidextrous capabilities through their exploitative and explorative activities. Exploration at KOBACO: Value cocreation anchored in KOBAnet helped KOBACO and its partners to capture/appropriate value by enhancing their ability to create new sales opportunities. KOBACO reorganised its sales function by expanding its sales support departments and transforming them into divisions. It also realised that its sales force needed to be supported with detailed and sophisticated data reporting. Newly added functions included not only simple sales support (e.g., adding transaction data and monitoring advertising broadcasts), but also sales management (e.g., planning, market analysis, and the development of new advertisers) and revenue management. This new structure helped KOBACO to pursue new opportunities for developing innovative products and services, attract new clients, and maximise revenue. In turn, its partners were also able to capture value by o<sup>f</sup>ering the new products/services to their own customers.

The case study analysis presented above provides empirical evidence that network-level value cocreation processes in four layers of relational arrangements may result in enhanced ambidexterity in networked organisations. It shows that value cocreation centred on KOBAnet is a complex process that occurs on multiple layers in a business network over time. Such cocreated value is captured/appropriated in participating organisations in the form of organisational ambidexterity. Many exploitative aspects, such as enhanced transparency and quicker transactions, and resulting cost savings were planned and expected, but some exploratory aspects of value capture, such as new products and services for some participating organisations, were not initially foreseen.

## 6.3. Summary

To summarise, not only does the study showcase value cocreation processes in each of the four relational arrangement layers; it also indicates possible interdependencies among the four value cocreation layers. Grover and Kohli (2012) suggest that the creation of one type of value can stimulate the cocreation of another type of value. In the case of KOBAnet, the governance layer played a role in network-wide value cocreation since KOBACO’s market-dominant position, warranted by a special law, gave it the authority and political power to lead the development of KOBAnet and seek collaboration with its partners. KOBACO’s e<sup>f</sup>ective governance capability instilled a sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence and stability in its partners, encouraging them to invest in developing assets and complementary capabilities in the form of modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to their IS so that they could integrate with KOBAnet. Broadcasters invested in their IS so that demand projections for advertising slots could be shared with KOBACO. It has also been observed that KOBACO’s value cocreation e<sup>f</sup>orts to develop relational assets on the asset layer enabled knowledge-sharing, which stimulated new opportunities for asset and capability enhancement in the hub and partner organisations. Value cocreation processes in the complementary and knowledge sharing layers enhanced the analytical capabilities of the partner organisations, which enabled them to o<sup>f</sup>er better services and new product packages to their own customers.

In addition, the study also provides insights into value capture and appropriation at both the hub and partner organisations. By simultaneously enabling both exploitative and exploratory activities, organisations in the network were able to appropriate the value that was cocreated. For example, information exchange supported by KOBAnet led to increased transparency and trust between KOBACO and its partners. This transparency and trust, in turn, led to comprehensive, accurate, and timely reports on sales transactions (an exploitative activity). Accurate and real-time information sharing also led to new dynamic and transparent advertising sales mechanisms for KOBACO and its partners (an explorative activity). In addition, the interorganisational integration of KOBAnet and clients’ IS streamlined the advertisement purchasing and management processes for both KOBACO and its partners. This integration enabled KOBACO to develop new sales opportunities (an explorative activity). Thus, the simultaneous pursuit of both exploitative and explorative activities at both KOBACO and its partner advertising agencies illustrate how network-level cocreation e<sup>f</sup>orts enable networked organisations to capture/ appropriate the cocreated value.

## 7. Discussion on <sup>fi</sup>ndings, contribution, and future research

We have presented a case study regarding the innovative use of IS in a large business network spanning the South Korean broadcast advertising industry. In response to Grover and Kohli’s (2012) call for research on collective leveraging of IT in value cocreation, involving all the participants, this study investigates and describes the mechanisms through which a hub company (like KOBACO) engages other <sup>fi</sup>rms in collaboration and the cocreation of IT-enabled services, as suggested by Barrett, Davidson, Prabhu, and Vargo (2015). The case study is unique in that it highlights detailed processes of how value cocreation, initiated and driven mainly by KOBACO, unfolded across four relational layers in the network: the asset layer, the complementary capability layer, the knowledgesharing layer, and the governance layer. Furthermore, it showcases how such value cocreation processes and e<sup>f</sup>orts resulted in value appropriation/capture in both the hub organisation and its partner organisations by enhancing their organisational ambidexterity. With its detailed analyses of successful IS-leveraged value cocreation and its implications, this study contributes to the IS literature in important ways by addressing the gaps identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature: (1) it provides a comprehensive understanding of how network-level value cocreation unfolds and how it a<sup>f</sup>ects participating organisations’ ambidexterity in the context of a large business network, (2) it addresses the call to develop a comprehensive understanding of value leveraging by simultaneously examining the processes of both value cocreation on the network layer and value capture by members in the business network, and (3) it also examines the appropriation of cocreated value from both exploitative and explorative activities simultaneously, and thus provides a nuanced understanding of how organisational ambidexterity can be achieved by members participating in a network.

Cocreation of IT value has emerged as a critical research theme because the complexity of markets is forcing organisations to transition from creation of o<sup>f</sup>erings in isolation to cocreation of value in collaborative innovation networks (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Reypens et al., 2016). Past research establishes that IT investments that support advanced business strategies create business value (Li et al., 2009). Value can be created when an organisation develops its competencies in integrating and coordinating IT management knowledge that is spread across the organisation (Peppard, 2007). When an organisation’s internal e<sup>f</sup>orts are complemented by the integration of its IT systems with those of key stakeholders such as IT vendors (Ceci, Masini, & Prencipe, 2019), they can generate business value. Our research extends this observation in the context of an alliance network in which IT value is created through co-creation across even organisational boundaries. Researchers have repeatedly emphasised the importance of, and the need to understand, how IT-based value is cocreated and shared among multiple partners in multiorganisational relationships (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Rai et al., 2012; Reypens et al., 2016). This research contributes to the value cocreation literature by examining a detailed value cocreation process in a large business network, which addresses our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question. Our study illustrates how Grover and Kohli’s four layers of relational arrangements (2012) could serve as a framework to conceptually navigate complex processes of network-level value cocreation. The case analysis suggests that the four layers’ framework has explanatory power since the four relational layers are generic enough to be typically observed in many business networks of di<sup>f</sup>erent types. The framework is also speci<sup>fi</sup>c enough to unravel the rather ambiguous network-level value cocreation process.

In the case analysis, it was observed that the four relational layers are interconnected, to which Grover and Kohli have drawn further attention (2012). The governance layer in the form of formal or informal contracts served as a foundation for processes in other layers. It was also observed that IT-based value cocreation in the asset layer enabled processes on the complementary asset and knowledge sharing layers. Value cocreation was found to be a complex, interconnected set of processes across four relational layers involving multiple stakeholders. That, in turn, required signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coordination e<sup>f</sup>orts across the network, which KOBACO showcased in our case analysis. This insight adds value to research on network-level innovations spanning diverse entities and/ or multiple industries, especially as the roles of hub <sup>fi</sup>rms in creating and extracting value from their complex innovation networks become more and more relevant, but little explored (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Levén, Holmström, & Mathiassen, 2014). Our study can help hub organisations develop appropriate use cases to justify and explain their IT investments and their potential impacts on value cocreation with their stakeholders (Nielsen & Persson, 2017). The role of the hub organisation, as reported in detail in the analysis, also extends the <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the orchestration of value cocreation enabled by dominant players in the industry (Eaton, Elaluf-Calderwood, Sorensen, & Yoo, 2015).

We completely concur with the view that value leveraging is the overarching process of both value cocreation (network-level) and value capture (stakeholder level) (Reypens et al., 2016). The value cocreation process is only part of the picture and we need to understand how cocreated value is captured by stakeholders, or organisations that participate in business networks. This study shows that value capture on the organisational level can be understood by using an organisational ambidexterity perspective. It demonstrates that organisations may capture value in both exploitative and explorative ways simultaneously. The case analysis results also show that value appropriation may be either intended or unintended, because IT-enabled cocreated products, services, or capabilities may have been largely unforeseen prior to the cocreation. Our case analysis indicates that unintended value capture may be largely associated with the explora tive capacity of organisations, while the exploitative aspects, such as enhanced transparency and rapid transactions, resulting in cost savings, could have been expected and forecasted. Ambidexterity originated as an organisation-level theory, and it has been extended to include relationship-level and network-level capabilities (e.g., (Im & Rai, 2013). This case study indicates that organisation-level capabilities can be intertwined with, or triggered by, activities in a di<sup>f</sup>erent layer; that is, network-level value cocreation. This is in line with previous research reporting that network-level innovations lead to improvements of internal processes in partner organisations (e.g., (Reypens et al., 2016). Thus, the study adds value to the literature on organisational ambidexterity by describing how organisational ambidexterity may be improved and achieved, with network-level value cocreation activities occurring in the interconnected relational resource layers among organisations. While much of the past research on ambidexterity has focused on examining the role of ambidexterity in improving business performance, and the antecedent factors that enable ambidextrous practices, our study is unique in two ways: (1) it highlights how value cocreation in the four layers may serve as an antecedent of organisational ambidexterity, and (2) it is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the ambidexterity that may be achieved by several organisations (including the hub and its partners) simultaneously. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it identi<sup>fi</sup>es value cocreation in a business network as a unique setting in which this may be achieved.

Value cocreation is interactive and network-based by nature, in the sense that value cocreation takes place within and between systems on various levels of aggregation, which Vargo and Lusch (2008) referred to as value configurations. Value con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, which represent the fundamental nature of exchange (Vargo & Lusch, 2008), range from the individual level to the <sup>fi</sup>rm and national levels and even to global levels. This case study clearly indicates that network-level value cocreation can be organically interconnected with the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level capabilities possessed by both the hub organisation and its partners. This study suggests that organisations may synergistically leverage cocreation if hub organisations take into consideration the speci-<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of four relational asset layers in a business network. For example, when creating a platform that facilitates value cocreation, consideration must be given to how to enable and encourage partners in the network to create complementary investments and provide mechanisms for knowledge exchange among partners. Similarly, while developing governance structures, organisations must be sensitive to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of an IS architecture that can facilitate the leveraging of complementary capabilities.

Like most other research, this study is limited by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c research context. The size of the focal business network is enormous, spanning a major portion of the industry studied. However, although the researchers carefully selected the organisations and interviewed senior managers, the interviewees may not represent all the stakeholders in such a huge network and the qualitative data analysed only represents a small subset. To compensate for such limitations, we strategically selected organisations that represent major stakeholder groups, major and regional broadcasters, and both major advertising agencies and small/medium-sized agencies. Also, we intentionally selected and interviewed senior managers, who had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant experience and a broad view on organisations and the industry. We also acknowledge that this research is a single case study investigating only one business network with a public hub organisation and its partners; hence, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be directly transferable to other contexts.

The insights and limitations of the study suggest future research opportunities. The study illustrates that Grover and Kohli’s framework of the four relational layers could be an e<sup>f</sup>ective conceptual tool for understanding complex value cocreation processes via a case analysis of IT-leveraged value cocreation in the South Korean broadcasting advertisement network. Further research to investigate if the framework would be applic able in analysing value cocreation processes and ambi dexterity in other types of business networks in other industries deserves consideration. For example, would it be applicable to di<sup>f</sup>erently-structured networks such as purely private business networks, in which a hub may not have such enormous power as in this case, or multi sided markets/platforms where a platform provider con nects sellers and buyers? It would be interesting to see how value cocreation processes unfold across the four relational layers in other types of business networks or industries. The role of the governance layer in this case study is unique in that the hub company, on account of its legal status, had great authority and a strong position. How might a weaker governance structure based on relational contracts impact the value cocreation process in the network-level? In this study, inter-dependence among four layers was observed. Our analysis hints that the governance layer and the asset layer could be more fundamental because they enable the other two layers. Might it be possible to prioritise the four layers? For example, it may be argued that, without the govern ance layer, the other three layers may not work well, o without the asset layer, the complementary capability and knowledge sharing layers may not be compatible. Our study suggests a need for further detailed studie regarding the complementarities among the four layers of value cocreation. We suggest that value capture in the organisational level could be explained from an organisational ambidexterity perspective, but our study did not examine how value capture occurs at the network leve or sub-organisational level. In addition, further research is needed to uncover value cocreation processes over an extended period of time, since value cocreation pro cesses and patterns of value capture might change as the business network evolves over time.
