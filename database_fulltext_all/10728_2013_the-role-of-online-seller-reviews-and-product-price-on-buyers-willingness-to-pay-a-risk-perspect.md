## Abstract

Third-party interpersonal communications such as online seller reviews play an important role in buyers’ purchase decisions in online markets. Although it is empirically clear that seller reviews (volume and valence) and product price contribute to buyers’ willingness-to-pay (WTP) differently across various studies, it is theoretically less understood why such effects qualitatively differ (e.g., positive vs negative), rendering unclear managerial implications for online marketers. In this paper, we study the role of online seller reviews and product price in buyers’ WTP. We offer a conceptual framework from a risk perspective in which we argue that the different effects of seller reviews and product price on buyers’ WTP may emerge simultaneously in an online market. We highlight two important drivers for such qualitatively different effects: a difference in buyers’ risk attitudes (averse, neutral, or seeking) and a difference in WTP measures (absolute or relative). We test our hypotheses and find good support for them both internally (an experimental study) and externally (an empirical study). Our research enhances the understanding of the relationship between online user reviews and online price dispersions while shedding light on better management of online user reviews for market makers. ## Introduction

Online word-of-mouth (WOM) often refers to publicly viewable individual communication messages posted in various markets (e.g., Dellarocas, 2003). It includes buyers’ comments and reviews about sellers in reputation systems (e.g., feedbacks at eBay.com, Bizrate.com) and electronic consumer-toconsumer communications involving product opinions and experiences (e.g., postings at epinion.com). Online WOM is an important information source for buyers seeking to make online purchase decisions. It plays a vital role in many consumer-centric online markets, in which sellers rely on online WOM as their primary marketing communication strategy (e.g., eBay, aStore at Amazon.com). Google’s recent purchase of the online user review website Zagat.com, focused on the restaurant industry, boosted Google’s capitalization by 0.6% – or US\$1.04 billion – on the day the news was announced (Kell, 2011). Another user-generated review website Yelp seeks to raise up to \$100 million in initial public offering (Levy, 2012).

The unprecedented scale and precise control of online WOM, accompanied with its better observability and tally than off-line WOM, have attracted much attention in both marketing and information systems literature (e.g., Dellarocas, 2003). Researchers have demonstrated that online WOM significantly affects critical marketing outcomes. For example, online conversation variation such as entropy of posts has an explanatory power of TV ratings for popular TV shows (Godes & Mayzlin, 2004). An improvement in seller reviews, such as the number of reviews and average star ratings, leads to an increase in retail sales volume and sales revenues (e.g., Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Duan et al, 2008).

Studies on online seller reviews, a type of online user review, are prominent in the literature (e.g., Dellarocas, 2003; Godes & Mayzlin, 2004; Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Awad & Ragowsky, 2008; Duan et al, 2008; Dellarocas & Wood, 2008). The basic research question in these studies has been determining what role online seller reviews and product price play in determining buyers’ willingness-to-pay (WTP). In this stream of research, the review system for an online seller allows a buyer to assign a positive rating to the seller if the buyer is satisfied with a transaction with the seller or a negative rating if not (e.g., the pioneering eBay reputation system); buyers’ WTP is approximated using various measures of the prices buyers paid. One group of studies focused on the effects of seller reviews (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002; McDonald & Slawson, 2002; Dewan & Hsu, 2004; Park & Bradlow, 2005; Dewally & Ederington, 2006; Resnick et al, 2006; Bradlow & Park, 2007; Li et al, 2009), resulting in mixed findings. The majority of these empirical findings have suggested that online sellers often strive to accumulate a large number of buyer reviews (or review volume) with high proportions of positive reviews (or review valence) to harness price premiums in return (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002). However, other findings suggest different results. For example, Resnick et al (2006) found that review valence has an insignificant impact, whereas Li et al (2009) found that review volume has a negative impact on buyers’ WTP. The other group of studies focused on the effects of product price (e.g., Gatti & Kattuman, 2003; Lindsey-Mullikin & Grewal, 2006); their findings are also mixed. Researchers have demonstrated that product price contributes to price dispersion positively in absolute measures (e.g., Gatti & Kattuman, 2003; Lindsey-Mullikin & Grewal, 2006), but negatively in relative measures (Gatti & Kattuman, 2003). For clear exposition, we summarize the empirical findings of these two groups of studies in Table 1.

The prolific studies in the literature have made significant progress in empirically examining the effects of seller reviews and product price on buyers’ WTP; however, they have failed to address an important research question: Why do such effects differ across settings? An intuitive explanation of the effect variations is that the settings (e.g., market type or product type) are different. However, this explanation alone does not seem to address the differences evident in Table 1. For example, Kalyanam & McIntyre (2001) identified effect variations of online user reviews within product types (Palm Pilot) and market types (auction). Gatti & Kattuman (2003) found effect variations of product price between absolute measures and relative measures within product types (e.g., CDs) and market types (retailing) as well. Furthermore, in the auction market, Li et al (2009) argued that review volume should decrease a bidder’s willingness to bid because a higher volume may attract more bidders, suggesting that review volume may have a downward pressure on buyers’ WTP. However, it is not clear how this argument may apply in other studies, where review volume has a positive effect (e.g., Kalyanam & McIntyre, 2001) or insignificant effect (e.g., Park & Bradlow, 2005) on bidding prices.

In online retail markets, Lindsey-Mullikin & Grewal (2006) tested two competing theories making opposite suggestions. They argued that product price should affect price dispersion negatively according to search cost theory in economics because a higher product price makes the search more valuable, which encourages a heavier search and ultimately leads to lower price dispersion; yet product price should also affect price dispersion positively according to the psychophysics theory in psychology because the Weber–Fechner law suggests that buyers’ perceptible price change is based on relative measures rather than absolute measures (Monroe, 2003), thereby allowing the sellers to have more leeway in pricing and ultimately leading to higher price dispersion for products with higher value. Although the psychophysics theory supports the positive effect of product price on price dispersion in absolute measures as identified in Lindsey-Mullikin & Grewal’s (2006) empirical analysis, it is not clear how the same psychophysics theory may explain the negative effect of product price on price dispersion in relative measures exhibited in other empirical studies (e.g., Gatti & Kattuman, 2003).

The current competing theories suggest that effects of seller reviews and product price on prices are either one way or another (e.g., the effect is either positive or negative), consequently ignoring the situations in which they can be both (e.g., the effect is positive for some buyers, but negative for other buyers), depending on the buyer characteristics and/or price measures. In the current paper, we use the buyers’ risk perspective to reconcile and explain these seemingly conflicting results. This risk perspective is appropriate for two important reasons. First, risk is central to many online markets. Not only does the risk involved in online purchase decisions matter to buyers (e.g., Bhatnagar et al, 2000; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Verhagen et al, 2006; Dellarocas & Wood, 2008; Li et al, 2009; Quereshi et al, 2009), but buyers’ risk attitude also moderates their shopping behavior (e.g., Gupta et al, 2004). Studies have shown that buyers’ risk concern is the most critical element in their online purchasing decisions and the most sensitive issue in determining prices they are willing to pay (e.g., Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Clemons, 2007; Ghose, 2009). Second, WOM is important in buyer risk reduction. The literature has thoroughly documented that, if the stake of risk is high (e.g., product price is high), buyers often rely on WOM to reduce their purchase risks (e.g., Roselius, 1971; Dowling & Staelin, 1994) as buyers trust personal information sources such as WOM more than commercial information sources such as advertising (Herr et al, 1991). Seller reviews play a similar role in online shopping (e.g., Ha, 2002).

Our research makes three important contributions to the literature. First, our research – to the best of our knowledge – is the first attempt to offer a risk-based conceptual framework to understand the role of seller reviews and product price in buyers’ WTP in online markets. Adding to the recognition of unobserved buyer heterogeneity (e.g., buyer search cost difference) in the effects of seller reviews on buyers’ WTP (e.g., Li et al, 2009; Ratchford, 2009), our framework identifies buyer heterogeneity in risk attitudes as a new source of online price dispersions. Although buyers’ search costs and risk preferences may be related, they have been treated as two separate constructs in the literature. Moorthy et al (1999) listed both search cost and risk aversion as two different drivers on consumers’ amount of search and found different impacts between the two in their experiments in which they can be salient for both or only one but not the other, depending on the search mode. Second, our research suggests that qualitatively different effects of seller reviews and product price on buyers’ WTP naturally emerge because of the difference in buyers’ risk attitudes as well as price measurements. Thus, our framework offers the possibility to reconcile the inconsistent empirical findings reported in the literature (see Table 1). Third, our research suggests that the aggregate empirical findings may be misleading because the effects of seller reviews and product price can differ among various customers (or segments) within a market. As such, our framework may offer marketers a new approach to raising marketing efficiencies in online markets by designing segmented marketing strategies based on buyers’ risk attitudes toward seller reviews.

## Conceptual framework and hypothesis development

## Conceptual framework

To develop a conceptual framework for the role of seller reviews and product price in buyers’ WTP, we adopt the decision theory under uncertainty (see, e.g., Keeney & Raiffa, 1976; Varian, 1992, for a good review), which is a widely accepted framework used to understand decision making under uncertainty in economics, management science, marketing (e.g., Dyer & Sarin, 1982; Bell, 1995; Smidts, 1997; Wu & Rangaswamy, 2003) and information systems (e.g., Verhagen et al, 2006; Quereshi et al, 2009). This theory suggests that, under uncertainty, buyers’ WTP – defined as the maximum amount payment a buyer is willing to pay in exchange for a product – is based on their expected utilities (e.g., Ekstrand & Loomis, 1998). Buyers’ WTP is accessed using various methods in the literature (e.g., Miller et al, 2011), including direct statement, choice-based conjoint, Vickrey auction and incentive compatible/aligned mechanisms.

Buyers’ WTP under uncertainty is jointly determined by the probability of each possible outcome, the payoff of each possible outcome, and the decision makers’ risk attitude. In our context of online shopping, these determinants of buyers’ WTP are (1) the purchase uncertainty of an online seller (e.g., Arndt, 1967, 1968); (2) the market price of the purchased product (e.g., Cunningham, 1967); and (3) buyers’ risk attitude (e.g., Weber & Hsee, 1998). We discuss each of the determinants in our context below.

Purchase uncertainty and seller reviews A fundamental nature of online markets is the spatial and temporal separation between sellers and buyers, which induces greater purchase uncertainty for buyers in online markets than in conventional markets (e.g., Gupta et al, 2009). To some degree, the uncertainty of online shopping is similar to mail-order shopping, in which buyers perceive a higher risk than in-store shopping (e.g., Spence et al, 1970). Online shoppers primarily rely on seller reviews to infer their transaction risks, particularly in consumercentric markets (e.g., the seller reputation system developed by eBay).

Ample studies (e.g., Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Dellarocas & Wood, 2008) have empirically examined the effects of online seller reviews on buyers’ WTP on various types of products (e.g., books, CDs, coins, proactive dialogical agent (PDAs), computers, motorcycles) and in various types of markets (e.g., retail markets, auction markets). These studies focus on the volume and valence of either overall reviews or attribute-specific reviews on branding, reliability, handling and shipping policies and so on (e.g., Kalyanam & McIntyre, 2001; Ba & Pavlou, 2002; McDonald & Slawson, 2002; Dewan & Hsu, 2004; Dewally & Ederington, 2006; Resnick et al, 2006). Although the literature does not specify the role of seller reviews in buyers’ WTP, the empirical findings suggest that online seller reviews (both volume and valence) are an important determinant on buyers’ WTP.

Product price The product price, which refers to the market price of a product, is an indicator of how much a product is worth to buyers; as such, it is an important determinant of buyers’ expected utility. Dowling & Staelin (1994) found that buyers are more willing to adopt risk reduction strategies such as WOM when they purchase bigger ticket items, presumably because they see higher benefits in return for their efforts. Lee & Huddleston (2006) found that buyers perceive purchasing decisions regarding products with higher prices as riskier and, hence, are more likely to use seller reviews in their risk assessment in online shopping.

For a fixed amount of purchase uncertainty, buyers’ expected utility changes as the product price changes; the patterns of the changes could also be qualitatively different for different utility measures. In online price dispersion research, Bhatnagar et al (2000) and Pan et al (2002) reported that online price dispersions are quite different for products with different product prices. More interestingly, Gatti & Kattuman (2003) found that online price dispersions across products systematically change with product prices; such changes are dependent on the measures of price dispersions – namely, absolute price dispersion (e.g., standard deviation) posts a positive correlation with product prices while relative price dispersion (e.g., coefficient of variation) posts a negative correlation with product prices. Again, although the literature does not address the role of product price in buyers’ WTP, it nevertheless suggests that not only is product price an important determinant on buyers’ WTP, but its effect on buyers’ WTP may also depend on the measures of their WTP.

Buyers’ risk attitudes The theory of decision under uncertainty further suggests that buyers’ risk attitudes moderate buyers’ WTP. Such moderations may be qualitatively different for different types of buyers’ risk attitudes: risk-averse, risk-neutral, or risk-seeking (e.g., Bell, 1995; Barsky et al, 1997; Weber & Hsee, 1998; Holt & Laury, 2002). According to the literature, buyers are often characterized as risk-averse at an aggregate level, but they exhibit different risk attitudes at an individual level. In studying buyers’ risk attitudes, Weber & Hsee (1998) found that 76% of Americans are risk-averse, 14% are risk-neutral, and 10% are risk-seeking. Similar results hold true for people from other nationalities, such as the Chinese, Germans, and Poles.

The moderating effects of buyers’ risk attitudes on buyers’ WTP in online markets have also been empirically examined. In online auction markets, Kalyanam & McIntyre (2001) found that seller reviews have a significant positive impact on bidding prices for buyers with high risk-aversion, but an insignificant impact for buyers with low risk-aversion. Although it makes logical sense for the authors to assume that buyers who bid on used items (classified as low risk-aversion) are more risk tolerant than buyers who bid on new items (classified as high risk-aversion), it is not clear whether low riskaversion buyers are risk-averse at all. Li et al (2009) provided empirical evidence demonstrating that buyer experience amplifies the effect of seller reviews on buyers’ WTP, implicitly suggesting that buyers’ risk attitude may alter the effect of seller reviews because buyers’ online experience is a predecessor of their risk attitude in online markets (e.g., Gupta et al, 2004; Lee & Huddleston, 2006).

## Hypothesis development

For clear exposition, we use an analytical operationalization of our conceptual framework to develop hypotheses regarding the role of seller reviews and product price on buyers’ WTP in online markets.

We assume that the online seller is Bernoulli with a probability l for making satisfactory transactions; the basic format of seller reviews is binary. We make this assumption based on the several considerations. First, the reputation system pioneered by eBay is binary: a positive seller review if the transaction is satisfactory (e.g., the buyer gets what he or she wants on time) or a negative seller review if the transaction is unsatisfactory (e.g., buyers do not receive the product on time, receive an incorrect product or a correct product in poor condition, or receive nothing at all). Second, the binary system is a widely adopted format of online user reviews and extensively studied in the literature (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Leszczyc et al, 2009). We assume that the online seller has a review volume $N ,$ n of which are positive. We also assume that a buyer uses the valence of seller reviews $s { = } n / N$ (i.e., the percentage of positive seller reviews among the total seller reviews) to assess the satisfactory transaction probability l of the seller. Thus, $n { \sim } B I N ( \lambda , N )$ is a binomial with $E ( n ) = N \lambda$ and $V a r ( n ) = N \lambda ( 1 - \lambda )$

Let the product price be v. The product price is defined as the market price of a product traded in a competitive market with no seller uncertainty (e.g., an off-line market with many retailers). For an online market with uncertainty, the product price will be adjusted. For an online seller with online user review $s ,$ the product price is sv. This formulation is technically intuitive because $E ( s \nu ) = \lambda \nu ,$ which is consistent with the literature (e.g., Arndt, 1967, 1968; Cunningham, 1967). Furthermore, $\begin{array} { r } { V a r ( s \nu ) = V a r ( s ) \nu ^ { 2 } = \frac { \lambda ( 1 - \lambda ) } { N } \nu ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$

Because s is between 0 and 1, this formulation assumes that online price is less than market price v. This assumption is appealing, based on the well-documented empirical evidences in the literature. Brynjolfsson & Smith (2000) found that prices for books and CDs sold on the Internet (online) average 9–16% less than the identical items sold via conventional channels (off-line). The mean online price was \$2.16 less for books and \$2.58 less for CDs. Degeratu et al (2000) found similar results. Clemons (2007) found that on average, online auction price is lower than online retailing price which is lower than off-line retailing price because of consumers’ risk hedging. In our empirical data set (data collected in 2006 and 2007), we found that online auction price is lower than off-line retail price for both DVD sample (\$27.26 online vs \$59.99 off-line) and iPod sample (\$183.02 online vs \$399.99 off-line).

$$

Therefore:

H3a: If the product price goes up, a buyer’s absolute WTP for an online seller goes up, regardless of the buyer’s risk attitude.

H3b: If the product price goes up, a buyer’s relative WTP for an online seller

(a) goes down if the buyer is risk-averse;

(b) does not change if the buyer is risk-neutral; and

(c) goes up if the buyer is risk-seeking.

Similar to the impact of review valence, the effect of product price (v) on buyers’ perceived risk is dominated by that on buyers’ expected value. However, unlike the impact of review valence, the effect of product price is also dependent on the measurement of buyers’ WTP. For example, a risk-neutral buyer’s WTP is affected only by the buyer’s expected value, not by the buyer’s perceived risk. Therefore, a risk-neutral buyer’s WTP increases proportionally in dollar amount (absolute measure) when the product price increases, but remains unchanged in percentage (relative measure). As such, buyers’ risk attitude moderates the impact of product price on buyers’ WTP differently for different measurements. The moderation exists in the relative measure, but not in the absolute measure (much the same rationale as we see in the impact of review valence).

## An experimental study

## Study design

Our hypotheses involve the effects of three independent variables on buyers’ WTP: review volume N (i.e., the total number of seller reviews), review valence l (i.e., the percentage of positive seller reviews among the total seller reviews), and product price v (i.e., the market price of a product customers pay to a seller with no risk). We designed a 2  2  2 experiment in which each of the three individual variables has two levels: high or low. We selected the levels for each independent variable to make our experimental study compatible with our empirical study (discussed in the next section) – namely, (1) review volume for an online seller is either 50 (low) or 100 (high); (2) review valence for an online seller is either 90% (low) or 99% (high); and (3) product price is either \$39.99 (for a DVD set) or \$199.99 (for an iPod). The product price was presented as the retail price for the products in no-risk offline markets (i.e., consumers can get the product at the store and can always return it if they dislike it).

We conducted our study in a southeastern public university in the United States. The participants were undergraduate students enrolled in a senior business class. We used a within-subject approach so that buyers risk attitudes could be estimated at an individual level. The study included two parts. In part one, we asked participants to answer some general online shopping questions. In part two, we presented the participants with the eight decision scenarios in which seller reviews and product price are the only information available and asked them to write down the perceived risk and the maximum price they would be willing to pay in each of the eight decision scenarios. Although many different approaches exist for measuring buyers’ WTP, we chose the direct approach based on three important considerations: it has been used in the literature (e.g., Weber & Hsee, 1998); it posits a smaller cognitive burden for participants, which is important for our sample (undergraduate students); and it still leads to the right demand curve (e.g., Miller et al, 2011).

Seventy-eight students participated in the experiment; 76 of them completed the study (60% males). The participants were fairly familiar with both products in particular and with the online shopping environment in general; indeed, 85% of the participants have shopped online before, rating their shopping experience at an average of 73 (scale of 0–100). In addition, 59% reported that they had shopped at an online auction market like eBay before. For those who had not shopped at online auction markets, the dominating reason is the risk concern. For those who had shopping experience at online auction markets, 87% used seller reviews as criteria to aid seller selection, and 66% said seller reviews affected their WTP.

## Risk attitude assessment and hypothesis testing

Different approaches have been used to estimate buyers risk attitudes in the literature. We adopted two different approaches to estimate buyers’ risk attitudes in our experimental study.

Subjective assessment We followed Weber & Hsee (1998), specifying

$$
p _ {j} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} E V _ {j} + \alpha_ {R I S K} R I S K _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {j}\tag{7}
$$

to estimate buyer’s risk attitudes, where $p _ { j } , \ E V _ { j } = \lambda _ { j } V _ { j } ,$ and RISK are a participant’s WTP, expected value, and perceived risk for scenario $j ,$ respectively. This approach is straightforward and independent of seller reviews (volume and valence) and product price used in the hypothesis testing.

Objective assessment We estimated buyers’ risk attitudes by directly fitting Eq. (3a). We rewrote Eq. (3a) as ln $( u / \lambda \nu ) = \mathrm { l n } ( c ) + ( \beta - 1 ) [ \mathrm { l n } \lambda \nu + ( 1 - \lambda ) / ( 2 \lambda N ) ]$ based on the approximation and estimated a buyer’s risk attitude using the following specification:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {p _ {j}}{\lambda_ {j} v _ {j}}\right) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {R I S K} \left[ \ln \lambda_ {j} v _ {j} + \left(\frac {1 - \lambda_ {j}}{2 \lambda_ {j} N _ {j}}\right) \right] + \varepsilon_ {j},\tag{7'}
$$

where $p _ { j }$ is the price a buyer is willing to pay in decision scenario $j ( j = 1 , . . . , 8 ) ; N _ { j }$ and $\lambda _ { j }$ are the review volume and valence in scenario $j ; \nu _ { j }$ is the product price in scenario $j ;$ and $\varepsilon _ { j }$ is random errors, with $E [ \varepsilon _ { j } ] = 0$ . The coefficient $\alpha _ { R I S K } = \beta - 1$ assesses the concavity/convexity of the utility Function (2), thereby determining the risk attitude of the buyer consistent with the expected utility framework. Thus, participants were classified as risk-averse $( \mathrm { i f } \alpha _ { R I S K } { < } 0 )$ , risk-neutral (if $\alpha _ { R I S K } = 0 )$ , and risk-seeking (if $\alpha _ { R I S K } { > } 0 )$ , accordingly.

We used the following specification for both absolute WTP and relative WTP to test our hypotheses:

$$
p _ {j} = a _ {0} + a _ {N} N _ {j} + a _ {\lambda} \lambda_ {j} + a _ {V} v _ {j} + \xi_ {j} \text {   for   absolute   WTP }\tag{8a}
$$

and

$$
p _ {j} / v _ {j} = r _ {0} + r _ {N} N _ {j} + r _ {\lambda} \lambda_ {j} + r _ {V} v _ {j} + \zeta_ {j} \text {   for   relative   WTP },\tag{8b}
$$

where $\xi _ { j }$ and $\zeta _ { j }$ are random errors, with $E [ \xi _ { j } ] = E [ \zeta _ { j } ] = 0 $ Other variables were identically defined as in model $( 7 ^ { \prime } )$

Because both estimates of the assessment of buyers’ risk attitudes and the impacts of seller reviews and product prices rely on buyers’ WTP $p _ { j } ,$ the errors $\varepsilon _ { j } , \ \xi _ { j } , \ \mathrm { a n d } \zeta _ { j }$ may be correlated. To accommodate the correlation, we estimated the risk attitude model and the hypothesis testing models jointly, using seemingly unrelated regression model. Furthermore, buyers’ WTP $p _ { j }$ is determined not only by their risk preferences, but also by their intrinsic preferences. To control the intrinsic preferences from risk preferences across participants, we specified a constant dummy for each participant.

We conducted our analyses at an aggregate level and a disaggregate level.

Aggregate analysis We report the results from the aggregate analysis under subiective assessment in Table 2a and those under objective assessment in Table 2b. Both risk assessment models seem to fit the data well, but the subjective assessment has a higher $R ^ { 2 }$ at 0.8980. Both models also suggest the same aggregate risk attitudes for our sample. The participants were risk-neutral as a whole. One reason may be the nature of our participants – namely, college students are young and Internet savvy.

## Drivers of buyers’ risk attitudes

To understand why buyers show different risk attitudes, we compared participants’ average online shopping experience and gender at the segment level under the objective assessment and reported the results in Panel (a) of Table 6. We found no difference in gender among buyers with different risk attitudes (Z-stat ¼ 0.32 and 1.14 for the proportion comparisons, respectively). Although gender may contribute to buyers’ risk perception (e.g., Garbarinoa & Strahilevitz, 2004) or trust (e.g., Awad & Ragowsky, 2008), it seems that gender does not contribute to buyers’ risk attitudes. However, a significant difference was evident in online shopping experience among buyers with different risk attitudes. The participants exhibiting a risk-seeking attitude had significantly higher online shopping experiences than those exhibiting either a risk-averse or a risk-neutral attitude (t-stat ¼ 3.40 for the mean comparison between the risk-neutral segment and risk-seeking segment) – a finding consistent with the literature (e.g., Pennings & Smidts, 2000; Lee & Huddleston, 2006). We also ran a regression analysis of the estimated individual risk attitudes of buyers against their online shopping experience and gender and reported the results in Panel (b) of Table 6. Consistent with our segment-level comparison, we found no impact of gender, but a marginally positive impact of online shopping experience on buyers’ risk attitudes. Such a finding is interesting and consistent with the results identified in other domains. For example, a recent study by All State shows that driving experience still matters in explaining the risky driving behavior, but the gender gap of risk attitude among younger drivers is diminishing even if most auto insurers charge a price premium against young male drivers (Wernau. 2010) (Table 4).

## An empirical study

We also conducted an empirical study in an online market (eBay) to test the external validity of our hypotheses.

## Data

We selected the eBay auction market based on several important considerations. First, eBay is a risky market (e.g., Clemons, 2007). In addition, eBay’s reputation mechanism has been in place for a long time and has been widely studied in the literature (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Leszczyc et al, 2009). It enforces accurate registrations and tallies of the seller reviews. Sellers at eBay do not rely on other means of marketing communication besides seller reviews. Finally, eBay auctions resemble the Vickrey auctions, which access buyers’ WTP.

We chose the DVD box set of the popular Fox TV show ‘Lost’ (second season) as the lower-priced product and a 4 GB Blue Apple Nano iPod as the higher-priced item. We made the product selection for several reasons. First, regarding product identification, the UPC on the DVD set and the model number of the iPod helped us track product identities. Second, regarding transaction frequency, DVD sets and iPods are two popular new products traded via eBay, which ensured a good sample size in a reasonable time window. We restricted our products to new products only to minimize the product differentiation in conditions. Thus, the auctioned products are identical within each of the two samples.

The DVD sample was collected in October 2006 and April 2007 while the iPod sample was collected in November 2006 and January and February 2007. The DVD sample consisted of 415 auctions with an off-line retail price of \$59.99. The average winning price of these auctions was \$27.26 in absolute WTP and 0.45 in relative WTP (winning price as a proportion of off-line retail price). The average price dispersion was \$33.05 and \$5.20 in range and standard deviation (absolute measures) and 0.55 and 0.19 in relative range and coefficient of variation (relative measures). The iPod sample consisted of 147 auctions with an off-line retail price \$399.99. The average final bid price of this product was \$183.02 in absolute WTP and 0.45 in relative WTP. The average price dispersion was \$137.50 and \$19.27 in range and standard deviation and 0.34 and 0.10 in relative range and coefficient of variation. This aggregate observation is consistent with the literature in that the price dispersion was higher for the product with a higher value (i.e., iPod) in absolute measures, but lower for the product with a higher value in relative measures (e.g., Gatti & Kattuman, 2003).

For an auction $j ,$ we measured the review volume $N _ { j }$ by a seller’s total number of reviews. The frequency distribution was asymmetrical, with a long tail to the right, suggesting that few suppliers have a high review volume. For the lower-priced DVD sample, the mean of the distribution was close to 6000 transactions, but its standard deviation was three times larger than the mean.

## Risk attitude assessment and hypothesis testing

We adopted the objective assessment to estimate buyers’ risk attitudes because, in our empirical analysis, the subjective assessment is not applicable as we do not observe the subjective risk evaluation; and also the results of our experimental study favor the objective assessment over the subjective assessment.

In the aggregate analysis, we estimated the risk attitude model (7<sup>0</sup>) and hypothesis testing models (8a) and (8b) jointly as an equation system as the three models were estimated using the same set of variables; thus, endogeneity and correlations may exist among the three models. In the disaggregate analysis, we first used a latent class finite mixture model on the pooled data set to identify buyers with different risk attitudes because we had only one observation per customer and could not estimate the risk attitude model (7<sup>0</sup>) at the individual level (e.g., Wu & Rangaswamy, 2003). We then estimated (7<sup>0</sup>), (8a), and (8b) simultaneously at the segment level for hypothesis testing. Because of the product price change in the time window during which we collected our data, we specified the month dummies as our control variables in models (7<sup>0</sup>), (8a), and (8b).

## Discussion

The risk concern in online shopping often leads to buyers’ adoption of seller reviews as a risk-reduction strategy. In this paper, we examined how buyers may integrate seller reviews and product price to configure the prices they are willing to pay. Adopting a risk perspective, we proposed a conceptual framework on the role of online seller reviews (volume and valence) and product price on buyers’ WTP. Our risk perspective indicated that the role depends on buyers’ risk attitude and the measurement scales of buyers’ WTP – namely, buyers’ risk attitude moderates the effects of review volume in both the absolute measure and the relative measure of buyers’ WTP, does not moderate the effects of review valence at all $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot } )$ , the effects are always positive), and moderates the effects of product price in the relative measure only. We tested these three sets of hypotheses using data from a controlled experiment and real auctions at eBay, finding strong support for the second and third hypotheses, but mixed results for the first one. Our study contributes to our understanding of the role the online user reviews and product price play in buyers’ WTP. We discuss our study’s impacts on theory and practice, as well as limitations in the following sections.

## Implication for theory

Our research has two important theoretical implications. First, our study is the first attempt to formally conceptualize and model the role of seller review (volume and valance) and product price in buyers’ WTP. Our risk perspective addresses buyers’ risk concerns in online shopping decisions and offers a new rationale for online price variations of identical products. This risk perspective is different from existing theories on buyer search cost or seller service quality (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000; Pan et al, 2002; Ba et al, 2007; Quereshi et al, 2009; Ratchford, 2009): Buyers may pay different prices for identical products in online markets because they have different risk return assessment (different risk assessment or different risk attitude or both).

Second, our framework offers theoretical insights into the empirical findings reported in the literature under plausible assumptions. For example, assuming that buyers are risk-averse, our framework suggests that product price has a positive impact on absolute price dispersion (H3a), but a negative impact on relative price dispersion (H3b). H3a offers a theoretical rationale beyond Lindsey-Mullikin & Grewal’s (2006) psychographic theory on the impact of product price on online price dispersion: Buyers’ risk perspective not only explains that such impact is positive in absolute measures as the psychographic theory does, but also explains that such impact may vary in relative measures (e.g., Gatti & Kattuman, 2003), which their psychographic theory does not. Holding retailers’ reputation constant, the increase of buyers’ expected value outpaces the increase of the associated buyers’ perceived risk in the purchase as the product price increases. As a result, for a product of higher price, buyers’ higher WTP leads to higher price dispersion (e.g., price range). Moreover, H3b shows that product price has a negative impact on relative price dispersion if buyers are risk-averse, which may explain the empirical findings reported by Gatti & Kattuman (2003).

## Implication for IS research and practice

Advances in information technology have made organizations more customer-centric than ever before (e.g., Papazoglou, 2000). For example, the development of customer relationship management (CRM) system integrated the information collected at multiple touch points of customers into the database from other functions of the organizations (e.g., ERP). The integration of customer behavior information into the information system improves organizational coordination between the front offices (e.g., sales, marketing, customer service, etc.) and back offices (e.g., operations, logistics, human resources, finance, etc.) and allows for more customized customer service strategies. Because it helps organizations increase customer retention and loyalty, this development has gained popularity as an important decision-support system in customer-centric business processes today (Chen & Popovich, 2003).

The increasing prominence of the Internet has enhanced the development tremendously. In recent years, as organizations have gradually shifted their businesses to online markets, the development of software agents (e.g., recommendation agents/systems, shopping agents/ bots, etc.) to improve customer decision-support using information technology has proliferated (e.g., Grenci & Todd, 2002). These software agents facilitate better customer relationship building by moderating interactions between customers and organizations. Guttman et al (1998) argued that software agents should be combined with traditional marketing models (e.g., product brokering, vendor brokering, etc.) to accommodate online markets. Advances in business analytics and intelligence technologies have re-invigorated this development by incorporating adaptive customer knowledge into the software agent design. At the aggregate level, Allen & Wu (2010) studied how the designs of shopbots incorporated differentiated vendor coverage strategies to pursue their marketing positioning. At the disaggregate level, Montgomery et al (2004) demonstrated that how a shopbot’s design can be improved by taking individual consumer purchasing behavior into consideration.

Two basic issues are addressed in this line of IS research: (1) how to recommend (methods) and (2) what to recommend (substances). To address the first issue, Xiao & Benbasat (2007) suggested that a key factor in recommending agent adoption is whether the agents understand the target customers well and are personalized for those customers. They suggest the application developers adopt explicit preference elicitation methods in agent design to improve decision quality for users. Our research suggests that agent developers may want to incorporate buyers’ risk attitudes into agent design to elicit customers’ risk preferences. In practice, many e-commerce firms (e.g., Amazon.com) have developed product recommendation systems based on customer product preferences forecasted by customer behavior and customer demographics (e.g., product choice, frequency of online shopping, duration, gender, age, etc.).

Our empirical study shows that vendor recommendation systems based on customers’ risk preferences profiled by the same data source can be similarly developed. A recent approach with explicit preference elicitation methods in agent design is the idea of making software agents proactive and adaptive, thereby bringing the online selling process closer to the customer experience in a retail store. Delecroix et al (2012) demonstrate that their PDA incorporates the real-time customer responses of one interaction into the design of the next interaction to profile customers’ preferences before making product recommendations. Our experimental study also sheds light on how application developers can elicit customer risk preferences using seller reviews proactively and adaptively.

To address the second issue in this line of research, our research shows that customers have different risk preferences and thus select sellers with different risk profiles to match their risk appetites. To develop recommendation agents (e.g., vendor brokering) in risky online markets, where seller reviews are the primary source of information for assessing sellers, developers need to understand how customers use seller reviews to assess seller risk at the micro level (e.g., Miao et al, 2007; Lukyanenko & Komiak, 2011). Specifically, a smart agent should recommend vendors with a higher review volume and valence (e.g., established vendors) to novice customers and should recommend vendors with a lower review volume and valence (e.g., new vendors) to expert customers, because novice customers are likely to be risk averse while expert customers are likely to be risk seeking. Furthermore, personalized recommendations to novice customers should also focus on the assurance of purchases from vendors with a higher review volume, whereas those to expert customers should emphasize the better chances of a good bargain from vendors with a lower review volume and valence. The agent or system utilizing such personalization is likely to reduce customers’ costs on search, match and risk assessments, thus leading to higher market efficiencies for market makers (e.g., higher matching rates), more profitable outcomes for vendors (e.g., higher revenues and profits), and better purchasing experiences for customers (e.g., customer satisfactions).

## Limitations and future research

As the first attempt to establish a risk perspective for understanding the effects of seller review and product price on buyers’ WTP, our research has limitations that we invite future research to address. First, we find mixed support of our hypotheses on the review volume in both our experimental study and empirical study. It is not clear whether this is a behavior issue or a data issue. Regarding behavior, one may wonder whether buyers are sensitive to the number of reviews; if so, when? Subjective expected utility theory (e.g., Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Weber et al, 1992) may be used to accommodate such behavioral characteristics. Regarding data, our experiment is a high involvement exercise for students in which participation effort is a common concern. Higher incentives may offset the concern. Our experimental data may also be extended to include different risk attitude measures (e.g., Smidts, 1997). Our empirical data limit us to an assessment of buyers’ risk attitudes based on their responses to seller reviews. A buyer panel data may offset the limitations.

Second, some of the assumptions made for tractability of our analytical representation should be further evaluated. For example, we assume that the positive reviews of a given seller are independent, resulting in a binomial distribution for the number of positive reviews. However, Dellarocas & Wood (2008) demonstrated that the binomial distribution for the number of positive reviews in eBay may suffer reporting biases. Other evidence in the literature indicates that there may be differentiated effects of user reviews between the positive reviews and the negative reviews.

Third, we focused on seller risk and treated the product as risk-free. This treatment may be appropriate for search products, but may need further research for experience products (e.g., Lee & Huddleston, 2006; Huang et al, 2009). Disentangling product uncertainty from seller uncertainty in online markets for used products has attracted some attention recently (e.g., Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Ghose, 2009). An in-depth examination of these important issues can further enhance our understanding for managing the effects of seller reviews on buyers’ WTP in online markets.
