# Proposing the control-reactance compliance model (CRCM) to explain opposing motivations to comply with organisational information security policies

Paul Benjamin Lowry\* & Gregory D. Moody<sup>†</sup>

\*Information Systems Department, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, P7912, Academic Building I, 83 Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong, China, email: Paul.Lowry.PhD@gmail.com, and <sup>†</sup>University of Nevada Las Vegas, Las Vegas, NV 89154, USA, email: Greg.moody@unlv.edu

Abstract. Organisations increasingly rely on information and related systems, which are also a source of risk. Unfortunately, employees represent the greatest risk to organisational information because they are the most frequent source of information security breaches. To address this ‘weak link’ in organisational security, most organisations have strict information security policies (ISPs) designed to thwart employee information abuses. Regrettably, these ISPs are only partially effective because employees often ignore them, circumvent them or even do the opposite of what management desires. Research on attempts to increase ISP compliance has produced similarly mixed results. Lack of compliance with ISPs is a widespread organisational issue that increasingly bears disproportionately large direct and qualitative costs that undermine strategy.

Consequently, the purpose of our study was to contribute to the understanding of both motivations to comply with new ISPs and motivations to react negatively against them. To do so, we proposed an innovative model, the control-reactance compliance model (CRCM), which combines organisational control theory – a model that explains ISP compliance – with reactance theory – a model used to explain ISP noncompliance. To test CRCM, we used a sample of 320 working professionals in a variety of industries to examine the likely organisational outcomes of the delivery of a new ISP to employees in the form of a typical memo sent throughout an organisation. We largely found support for CRCM, and this study concludes with an explanation of the model’s contributions to research and practice related to organisational ISP compliance.

Keywords: organisation security, control theory, reactance theory, reactance, compliance, information security policies

## INTRODUCTION

Organisations increasingly rely on information and related systems for the strategic advantages they provide; however, these systems are an increasing source of organisational risk (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Crossler et al., 2013). This environment has consequently increased the importance of managing information risks within organisations (Lohmeyer et al., 2002; Ransbotham & Mitra, 2009). Information risk management has traditionally relied on technological solutions to improve information security (Straub, 1990; Dhillon, 2001; Cavusoglu et al., 2005; Siponen, 2005). Yet, because employees are serious threats to organisations’ information security (Warkentin & Willison, 2009; Crossler et al., 2013) and cause most information security breaches (D’Arcy et al., 2009), considering socio-organisational elements in addition to mere technical solutions is crucial to assuring the security of information resources (Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001; Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Posey et al., 2013). Accord ingly, fostering employee compliance with information security policies (ISPs) is a key approach used by organisations to attempt to improve this weak link (Boss et al., 2009; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Stahl et al., 2012). ISPs are a set of formalised procedures, guidelines, roles and responsibilities to which employees are required to adhere to safeguard and use properly the information and technology resources of their organisations (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Bulgurcu et al., 2010).

Organisational security and related ISP compliance are critical factors in organisational governance and thus are of strategic concern to organisations because of the strategic value of the information involved (Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001; Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006; Mishra & Dhillon, 2006; Stahl et al., 2012). ISPs help ensure the security of organisational information resources by thwarting employee attempts to bypass information security and by training employees to use information resources appropriately. Recent management research has applied several theories and frameworks to explain ISP compliance and related phenomena; yet, thus far, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been mixed (e.g. Straub, 1990; Kankanhalli et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2004; Pahnila et al., 2007; Herath & Rao, 2009b; Myyry et al., 2009; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Liang & Xue, 2010; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Hu et al., 2011; Vance & Siponen, 2012). ISP compliance is also highly mixed in practice: many employees are apathetic about ISPs and ignore them (Boss et al., 2009) or intentionally try to circumvent them (Dhillon, 2001; Forrester Research, 2010); even worse, some employees purposely do the opposite of the desired behaviour and thus undermine the security of their organisations (e.g. George, 1996; Stanton, 2000; Alge, 2001; Ariss, 2002; Alge et al., 2006; Sewell & Barker, 2006; Posey et al., 2011). Recent extreme-case examples involve Private Manning leaking US military documents to Wikileaks and Eric Snowden leaking NSA documents to the worldwide press.

This literature has produced a strong foundation for organisational ISP compliance research but has left several gaps that provide opportunities for further research. Although some studies have looked at ISP compliance (e.g. Pahnila et al., 2007; Herath & Rao, 2009b; Herath & Rao, 2009a; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Son, 2011) and others have investigated ISP noncompliance (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Myyry et al., 2009; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Hu et al., 2011), we are aware of no study that has directly considered the motivators of both in the same model. Understanding both negative and positive sources of motivation could help explain the most puzzling research results, which show that increased ISP controls sometimes back<sup>fi</sup>re and increase pernicious employee behaviours in organisations (e.g. George, 1996; Stanton, 2000; Alge, 2001; Ariss, 2002; Alge et al., 2006; Sewell & Barker, 2006; Posey et al., 2011). The results could then be used to better deliver and align ISPs in support of the overall governance structure and strategy of organisations.

Given this opportunity, we propose a new model, the control-reactance compliance model (CRCM). CRCM combines control theory, which explains what increases compliance with newly introduced ISPs in organisations, with reactance theory, which explains what motivates a decrease in ISP compliance. After describing these counterpoised theories, we propose CRCM’s details along with its associated hypotheses to predict ISP compliance intentions. To test our model in a pragmatic manner, we used a sample of 320 working professionals in a variety of industries to examine the likely organisational outcomes of the delivery of a new ISP to employees in the form of a typical memo sent throughout an organisation. To conclude, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which largely support CRCM, in terms of their contribution to research and practice.

## PROPOSING CRCM AND HYPOTHESES

## Moving employees towards compliance: control theory

We now provide a detailed presentation of the theoretical foundation of CRCM, starting with control theory, from which we adopt the key constructs of formal control, mandatoriness and security precautions. We use these well-established constructs for the portion of our model tha explains why employees are motivated to comply with new ISPs.

For this portion of CRCM, we build primarily on a model developed by Boss et al. (2009) that incorporates the concept of mandatoriness into control theory to explain effectively that when individuals perceive organisational security policies as mandatory, they are more likely to take security precautions. Control theory was originally developed by Ouchi & Maguire (1975) both to classify the different types of control used by organisations to constrain employee behaviours and to explain the social conditions in which different forms of control are used, depending on the objectives and tasks characteristic of the organisation.

Although this approach to control theory helped explain the environments and contexts in which particular controls are most likely to be used, later research further clari<sup>fi</sup>ed the elements that constitute control itself. Kirsch (2004, p. 375) categorised controls as either formal, that is, ‘formally documented and initiated by management’, or informal, that is, ‘unwritten and often initiated by employees themselves’. A mix of these forms of control can be observed in agile software development (Persson et al., 2012). Formal controls are inherently more discernible because they are more structured, precisely regulated, more recognisable and often have discernible artefacts that can be observed by insiders and outsiders (Kirsch et al., 2002). For theoretical succinctness, we assume formal controls in our study as ISPs are publically available to members of the organisation and are central to security education training and awareness (SETA) campaigns (D’Arcy et al., 2009).

Building on Kirsch’s (2004) work, Boss et al. (2009) applied control theory in the realm of ISPs by observing the general effect of these policies on speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (i.e. measurement), evaluation and reward. Kirsch (2004) de<sup>fi</sup>ned these terms as follows: speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is the mea surability of expected behaviours or outcomes; evaluation is the exchange of information and the process of assessing performance; and reward is the result of the performance of expected behaviours, leading to goal achievement.

Boss et al. (2009) further observed the effect of formal controls (a formative construct com prising evaluation, reward and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation) on compliance with existing ISPs by introducing the innovative concept of mandatoriness, which is ‘the degree to which individuals perceive that compliance with existing security policies and procedures is compulsory or expected by organi zational management’ (p. 151). They found that formal controls associated with existing ISPs do indeed increase the perceived mandatoriness of existing ISPs. The research of Boss et al. (2009) suggested that when policies are implemented, an organisation sends ‘a signal that compliance is expected by individuals in the organization’ (p.154). This signal can vary in the degree of existing mandatoriness by manipulating the dimensions of the formal controls. For further modelling clarity, we use the term existing organisational formal controls to refer to these existing, organisational, formal ISP controls (in general – not associated with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c policy).

The model of Boss et al. (2009) further explained that an individual’s existing perception of the mandatoriness of existing organisational ISPs will in turn affect the general range of ISP security precautions the individual takes within the organisation. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that existing controls increase individual employees’ existing perceived mandatoriness and that this mandatoriness increases the general ISP security precautions taken by the employees (Boss et al., 2009). Formally, we term these existing ISP precautions taken; they are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the degree to which individuals perceive they take measures to secure their computers’ to follow existing formal controls and ISPs (Boss et al., 2009, p. 155).

## Applying control theory to compliance with a new information security policy

A key difference between our model and those of Kirsch (2004) and Boss et al. (2009) is that the latter were proposed and tested in view of predicting how existing organisational conditions impact existing compliance to policies. CRCM adds the nuance of predicting how existing organisationa conditions predict compliance with a new ISP. In our context. the new ISP was introduced through a formal organisational memo so that we could manipulate the formal control and language of the newly introduced ISP as an attempt to encourage organisational compliance. Therefore, we ignored replications and predictions of how existing conditions predict existing compliance because these are established and thus outside the scope of our study. A new ISP might be implemented to account for a new threat or technology that was not previously accounted for (e.g. a new form of phishing, a newly available antispyware software package) or for a change in previous policies (e.g. preventing use of personal email at work when it was allowed before, allowing laptops to be taken home when this was prohibited previously and so on).

Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>rst theoretical proposition is that because past-related behaviour is often a strong predictor of future-related behaviour (Bagozzi, 1981; Ouellette & Wood, 1998; Ajzen, 2002), it follows that if an employee has a pattern of taking ISP precautions, this pattern is likely to continue in terms of new ISPs. One reason for this link is that past ISP compliance suggests a more positive attitude towards ISP compliance than does past ISP noncompliance. Attitude is the degree to which a person likes or dislikes a behaviour (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991). Such positive attitudes tend to be lasting and are strong predictors of future ISP compli ance (Pahnila et al., 2007; Herath & Rao, 2009b; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). Further, individuals tend to maintain consistency between their outward behaviours and the internal motivations that drive such behaviours; thus, their behaviours and motivations are expected to remain consistent over time (Kelley & Michela, 1980). In information systems (IS) security research, links between attitudes and intentions have been demonstrated extensively; whereas some researchers have focused on positive attitudes and positive compliance (e.g. Bulgurcu et al., 2010), others have focused on negative attitudes and noncompliance (e.g. Hu et al., 2011). Assuming that our proposition holds in an ISP organisational compli ance context, we predict the following:

## H1: Existing ISP precautions taken increase intent to comply with a new ISP.

We posit that, in addition to the behaviour of existing ISP precautions taken, the existing organisational formal controls have a direct impact on new ISP compliance. Existing controls help to create a climate and culture where ISP compliance is more normative and where expec tations are clearer than in organisations without formal controls. That is, formal controls from management create normative beliefs regarding ISP compliance, and these norms can evolve into strong social pressure in organisations (Kirsch et al., 2010). In our context, normative beliefs (or norms) are ‘an employee’s perceived social pressure about compliance with the requirements of the ISP caused by behavioural expectations of such important referents as executives, colleagues, and managers’ (Bulgurcu et al., 2010, p. 529). Such norms increase ISP compliance (Bulgurcu et al., 2010).

Such formal policies and controls can also help create a positive information security climate, where compliance is more likely, by reinforcing normative beliefs (Chan et al. 2005). Formally an information security climate involves the organisational mechanisms and practices that de <sup>fi</sup>ne how an organisation treats information security (Hu et al., 2011). In positive climates, it is more likely that employees will perceive that the top management champions ISPs, which is the degree to which employees perceive that the top managers in an organisation <sup>fi</sup>rmly support or advocate for its ISPs (Hu et al., 2011). Such championship emphasises the importance of the ISP in protecting an organisation from harm in its day-to-day work (Schneider, 1987). By eval uating and enforcing the use of ISP controls in an organisation, management again emphasises the importance and the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the ISP to the entire organisation (Rothwell & Baldwin, 2006).

Notably, there are other ways that positive norms associated with formal control increase the likelihood of compliance when new ISPs are introduced. For example, positive security climates with top management championship foster more ISP compliance in employees (Hu et al., 2011), another reason security is an executive-level area of concern. In addition to increasing perceived leadership, top management championship is effective because organisations with such management tend to have more preventative controls (Kankanhalli et al., 2003) and because top management support serves as a form of normative control against noncompliance (Schneider, 1987; Lee & Lee, 2002). Thus, if an organisation lacks strong controls, then the security climate, associ ated expectations and top management championship are more likely to be weaker – as is ISP compliance. Assuming our proposition holds for newly introduced ISPs, we predict the following:

H2: Existing organisational formal controls increase intent to comply with a new ISP.

As a <sup>fi</sup>nal extension from control theory, we propose that the perceived mandatoriness of a new ISP affects ISP compliance directly. We <sup>fi</sup>rst assume the corollary that if existing ISPs have an associated perceived mandatoriness, then new ISPs will have their own associated perceived mandatoriness (assuming that the new ISPs are explicitly communicated within an orga nisation). To increase theoretical clarity, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne new ISP mandatoriness as the degree to which an individual perceives that compliance with a new ISP is compulsory or expected by organisational management. Recall that Boss et al. (2009) found that the acts of specifying policies and evaluating behaviours (i.e. controls) are effective in convincing individuals that secu rity policies are mandatory in general. Because Boss et al. (2009) showed that existing controls are positively correlated with existing mandatoriness, new controls should positively impact new ISP mandatoriness similarly. Assuming that this relationship holds for new ISPs, we predict the following:

H3: New ISP mandatoriness increases intent to comply with a new ISP.

## Threatening employees to the point of noncompliance: Reactance theory

Now that we have explained how formal controls, mandatoriness and taking existing security precautions can in<sup>fl</sup>uence organisational ISP compliance, we now consider reactance theory to explain what discourages ISP compliance. A key assumption of CRCM is that most employees have a tacit limit for the degree of tolerance they will feel towards management policies that are controlling and a similar threshold for how much individual freedom they will give up be fore negative consequences for the organisation will occur. This assumption is supported by a host of deterrence-related and monitoring-related studies that have shown that overly controlling approaches can back<sup>fi</sup>re and result in pernicious employee behaviours (e.g. George, 1996; Stanton, 2000; Alge, 2001; Ariss, 2002; Alge et al., 2006; Sewell & Barker, 2006; Posey et al., 2011). Thus, high levels of new ISP control could lead to negative results for organisations – especially because rewards and punishments are often an integral part of formalised controls. Despite this promising connection, this issue has not been considered in the literature. To address this issue, we leverage psychological reactance theory.

Reactance theory has historically been used in behavioural and psychological studies. For example, Silvia (2005) examined the role of similarity in decreasing reactance. Others have used reactance theory in a variety of behavioural studies: to explain patient noncompliance with doctors’ recommended treatments (Fogarty, 1997), to show why telling college students not to drink alcohol increases drinking (Engs & Hanson, 1989), to study con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and reactance to gender stereotypes (Kray et al., 2001), to propose why warning labels on food products back<sup>fi</sup>re (Bushman, 1998), to study how people react to online recommendation services (Lee & Lee, 2009), to explain several phenomena related to consumer behaviour (Lessne & Venkatesan, 1989), to illuminate the results of various forms of persuasive communication (Burgoon et al., 2002), to ex plain negative reactions to unsolicited recommendations (Fitzsimons & Lehmann, 2004), to expound negative effects related to reactance (Bushman et al., 2003; Wellman & Geers, 2009) and to predict the negative results of forcing users to use a particular computer interface (Murray & Häubl, 2011). Despite its strong behavioural applications, reactance theory has had scant application in management literature although some early studies made promising appli cations in the context of superior–subordinate relations (Organ, 1974; Rosen & Jerdee, 1975). Notably, we have found no application of reactance theory to organisation policy compliance, including ISP compliance.

Psychological reactance theory (which we term reactance theory) predicts that any given person has a set of behavioural freedoms (a realm of personal freedom) that if eliminated. or threatened with elimination, will create an adverse state of arousal called reactance (Brehm, 1966; Brehm, 1972; Brehm & Brehm, 1981). Reactance is a negative emotional response caused by threats to or losses of behavioural freedom (often resulting from a persuasion attempt by another party), and it focuses on restoring the freedom in question (Brehm, 1966; Brehm, 1972; Brehm & Brehm, 1981; Hong & Page, 1989; Witte et al., 1996; Dillard & Peck, 2000; Dillard & Shen, 2005; Maloney et al., 2011). A key condition for the occurrence of reactance is that the person whose behaviour is threatened has an expectation of free choice (Clee & Wicklund, 1980; Lessne & Venkatesan, 1989) and thus is motivationally aroused whenever free choice is threatened (Brehm, 1972). Reactance involves a highly intuitive explanation of basic human nature: ‘People do not appreciate being told how they should behave.… People at different development stages value independence and freedom and tend to reject many, if not most, authority-based appeals’ (Burgoon et al., 2002, p. 215). For these reasons, ‘reactance is motivated by the individual’s basic need for self-determination in effecting his or her own environment’, and thus, performing the threatened behaviour reasserts his or her self-determination (Burgoon et al., 2002, p. 216). Reactance has been shown to occur even when individuals are pressured (e.g. strongly persuaded or manipulated) to choose between two alternatives (Heilman & Tof<sup>fl</sup>er, 1976).

Consequently, in our context, reactance is a negative emotional coping response that is a form of message rejection caused primarily by lost or threatened freedom and thus focuses on re-establishing the threatened or eliminated behavioural freedom. Unlike avoidance, reactance is an active negative response to an external source that tries to in<sup>fl</sup>uence/persuade employees regarding their behaviour. During reactance, an individual systematically processes the sources of the negative emotion, but rather than trying to conceal the emotion, the individual is motivated to challenge the causes of the emotion (i.e. loss of behavioural freedoms), which allows him or her to invalidate the conclusions formed from the causes (Bushman et al., 2003; Silvia, 2005; Lee & Lee, 2009). Hence, reactance is associated with heated emotiona responses that allow individuals to displace the concern over missed opportunities with a sense of anger or frustration directed towards the source of information originally perceived to cause the concern (Dillard & Shen, 2005; Quick & Kim, 2009). Thus, reactance is a particularly perni cious organisational phenomenon because it pits the will of the employee (i.e. the persuadee) against the will of the employer (i.e. the persuader), resulting in negative con<sup>fl</sup>icts arising from undesired employee behaviours and emotions. Hence, employee reactance to new ISPs could be strategically disastrous because of the valuable information involved.

In view of our ISP-compliance context, we propose that new organisational controls are key mechanisms driving reactance in organisations. We posit that newly introduced controls from a new ISP can threaten an employee’s sense of freedom by challenging the existing balance of that employee’s valued behavioural freedoms. Importantly, formal control ‘depends heavily upon monitoring, evaluating and correcting in an explicit manner’ (Ouchi, 1979, p.841; Kirsch, 2004). That is, the manager determines the behaviours, outcomes, procedures, goals and so forth, expected of the employee. An established hierarchy of authority in an organisation provides a context in which the roles and relationships can be understood. For example, an autocratic authority is a formal control because of the clear and strong superior–subordinate relationship.

Notably, Ouchi explained and predicted that higher levels of control are ‘likely to offend people’s sense of autonomy and of self-control… [because] the more obvious and explicit the measurement, the more noxious it is to employees’ (Ouchi, 1979, p. 841). In further research aimed at explaining workplace deviance, Lawrence & Robinson (2007) explained that control can actually encourage deviance when it produces a perceived disparity between employee and employer, a phenomenon that has also been examined in criminological research (Tittle, 2004; Curry, 2005; Piquero & Piquero, 2006). They argued that the disparity and resulting resistance is a consequence of the violation of an individual’s need for autonomy. It is thus not surprising that reactance research has shown that coercion in particular tends to lead to the strongest levels of reactance because it goes beyond mere persuasion (Brehm & Brehm, 1981; Engs & Hanson, 1989). Hence, a strong display of authoritative power – for example, more explicit formal control measurements, whether in the form of speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, evaluation or rewards and sanctions – can threaten an employee’s sense of freedom. That is, when a manager speci<sup>fi</sup>es an employee’s appropriate outcomes or behaviours, the employee’s behaviour alternatives are more limited (Tittle, 2004). Thus, the more speci<sup>fi</sup>c and transparent the controls provided in a new ISP are, the more such controlling approaches are likely to threaten an employee’s sense of freedom as a result of the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, evaluation and rewards associated with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviours.

The key to provoking reactance is thus to threaten or eliminate personal freedoms that em ployees believe organisations should not threaten. In predicting reactance, the issue is not whether it is reasonable or legal to threaten or eliminate an employee’s freedom, but rather how an employee feels about a threatened freedom. A study on underage drinking, which is usually illegal and generally not in keeping with common sense (Engs & Hanson, 1989), illustrated this issue: despite legal and social norms, the more the students felt that drinking alcohol should be a personal freedom, and the more messages they received warning them not to drink, the more they drank. This study was later con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in a more advanced study on collegiate drinking (Allen et al., 1994), and in adolescent smoking research, similar results were shown for dispositional reactance (Wiium et al., 2009).

Consequently, the question in our context is not whether it is reasonable or legal to threaten or eliminate employee freedoms in the workplace regarding ISPs, but rather how employees perceive new ISP controls that potentially threaten their valued freedoms. For example, employers in most countries have the legal right to restrict employee Internet access and to have full access to all employee emails (Morrison & Robinson, 1997). At the same time, many em ployees feel that there is an implicit contract of respecting privacy and personal space in the workplace and are offended by the idea of their Internet access and emails being monitored (Sitkin & Roth, 1993). Further, whether employers actually read employee emails is not the only salient factor. If employers publically reserve the right to read employees’ emails – even if they never engage in reading them – that can cause a threat to personal freedom suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to bring about reactance, according to the concept of violation motivation (similar to reactance) from the control balance theory (Tittle, 2004; Curry, 2005). Hence, these theoretical considerations have a very real, pragmatic effect in organisations.

Moreover, the more important the threatened freedom or the greater the level of threat to freedom is, the greater the magnitude of reactance will be (Brehm, 1966; Brehm & Brehm, 1981; Dillard & Shen, 2005; Miller et al., 2007b; Rains & Turner, 2007). A threat to freedom in a reactance context is the degree to which the actions of or communications from organisational management cause employees to believe their freedom to choose is threatened, manipulated or undermined by management (Dillard & Shen, 2005). In our context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne threat to freedom from a new ISP as the degree to which a new ISP delivered from an organisation’s management causes the employees to believe their freedom to choose is threatened or manipulated by management. The importance of freedom in a reactance context is the degree to which employees value the freedom to engage in speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviours in the workplace that might be controlled or eliminated by management (Brehm, 1966). Likewise, the importance of ISP freedom is the degree to which emplovees value the freedom associated with a specific computer-relateo behaviour that might be controlled or eliminated by management through ISPs. Assuming that these basic constructs and relationships of reactance theory hold in our ISP-compliance context, we predict the following:

H4: A threat to freedom from a new ISP increases reactance to a new ISP.

## H5: The importance of ISP freedom increases reactance to a new ISP.

Moreover, increased behavioural freedom decreases reactance (Brehm, 1966; Brehm & Brehm, 1981). In our reactance context, we are concerned with the behavioural freedom asso ciated with a new ISP. On the basis of Dillard & Shen (2005), we term this freedom perceived freedom from a new ISP, which is the degree to which information regarding a new ISP promotes individual choice and decision making, free of pressure and manipulation from management.

Consider for example the very strict controls many organisations have against personal Inter net sur<sup>fi</sup>ng at work. Now suppose such an organisation provides a new ISP that states employees are free to surf the Internet for personal reasons as long as it does not hinder their productivity. Such a policy should increase employees’ sense of freedom and thus decrease reactance. The opposite would hold if employees were used to freedom to surf, but draconian policies were introduced to hinder sur<sup>fi</sup>ng. Accordingly, we predict the following:

## H6: Perceived freedom from a new ISP decreases reactance to a new ISP.

As reactance theory has been further tested and extended over time, several important addi tions have been included that also drive our CRCM proposal. First, a promising phenomenon described in the reactance literature is that a well-established measure of individuals’ reactance proneness (i.e. trait reactance) has been developed and validated; ironically, however, this mea sure has never been used to predict actual reactance (Dillard & Shen, 2005). We thus newly posit and test this relationship in CRCM. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, reactance proneness (or trait reactance) is one’s disposition or tendency to experience a state of reactance when one’s freedoms are restricted (Dillard & Shen, 2005). Given this conceptualisation, it naturally follows that people who are more prone to reactance to freedom restrictions in general are more likely to manifest reactance when management introduces a new, freedom-restricting ISP. We thus predict the following:

## H7: Reactance proneness increases reactance to a new ISP.

As a second addition to reactance theory, Quick & Kim (2009) demonstrated other negative effects of reactance with a construct they termed boomerang effects. Boomerang effects emerge as a result of reactance and the measurable efforts of individuals to directly restore freedoms and/or undo threats to freedoms that emerge in an organisation. We propose that in an ISP context, an undesired boomerang effect would be lack of compliance with the new ISP as a purposeful act to restore a valued but threatened or eliminated freedom. Therefore, if an ISP is seen by an employee as a direct threat to his or her personal freedom, the boomerang effects would be plausibly manifested as a decreased intention to follow the new ISP in an attempt to regain the lost freedoms and/or decrease the threats to freedoms resulting from the new policy. Similar effects have also been proposed and found in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of criminology (Curry, 2005; Piquero & Piquero, 2006). Assuming that this proposition holds for ISPs in organisations, we predict the following:

H8: Reactance to a new ISP decreases the intent to comply with a new ISP.

## RESEARCH METHODS

We used a scenarios-based method incorporating a 2 × 2 factorial design that provided ISP memos from eight different, randomly selected IS security policies that were manipulated in terms of controlling language (high–low) and formal control (high–low). Eight different forms of IS policies were used with four manipulations each, for a total of 32 conditions. Each policy had four memos, representing the combinations high-language/high-control, high-language/low-control, low language/high-control and low-language/low-control, for a between-groups factorial experiment.

## IS security policy scenarios

We tested CRCM by using a scenario-based method with professionals. The scenario method presents respondents with ‘written descriptions of realistic situations and then request[s] responses on a number of rating scales that measure the dependent variables of interest’ (Trevino, 1992, p. 127–128). This is the method most commonly applied to issues related to ethics (O’Fallon & Butter<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2005). Accordingly, this method is highly useful for studying ISP compliance issues and thus has been increasingly used in the ISP and computer-abuse literature (e.g. Siponen & Vance, 2010; Argelaguet et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2011; Lowry et al., 2013).

Three factors make this scenario method particularly ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious for our study. First, scenarios can incorporate situational details that are salient to decision makers confronted with choices in volving ethics (Klepper & Nagin, 1989). Because ‘the situational context of decision making and the information being handled will vary greatly among offenses’ (Clarke & Felson, 1993, p. 6), scenarios can enhance the realism of decision making by providing contextual details, while ensuring that these details are distributed across respondents (Alexander & Becker, 1978). Second, scenarios provide an indirect way of measuring intentions related to ethical/unethica behaviour, which are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure directly because of respondent bias (Trevino, 1992).

A scenario helps make respondents feel less constrained in reporting an intention to act similarly to the person described in the scenario – particularly when the scenario describes unethical behaviour (Harrington, 1996). Third, scenarios have been used extensively in research involving decision trade-offs because contextual detail helps drive and clarify the associated cost–bene<sup>fi</sup> analysis process (Paternoster & Simpson, 1996; Vance & Siponen, 2012).

The range of IS security policies was chosen to provide a realistic sample of the policies used in industry, some of which may be more restrictive and threatening to freedom than others. To develop these policies, we reviewed the academic and practitioner literature extensively fo user-focused IS security policies. Notably, we focused on policies that end users would notice and that they would understand in terms of the implications for the employees’ daily work. Our review omitted more technical behind-the-scenes security policies that end users may not notice or understand, such as automatic network data encryption, honey pots set up to lure hackers, redundant data backups, and the like. The eight IS policies were built around the following common security issues: end-user software installation, antivirus and antispyware software use with corporate networks, use of non-work related software, inconsistent use of antivirus software, personal use of corporate email systems, lack of centralised data storage, use of USB drives for sensitive data and personal Internet use. These issues have also been thoroughly documented in organisation security practice research (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Hu et al., 2011). Although many of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS security policies are straightforward, they have profound effects on overall IS security because organisational employees represent a majority of all the information security problems (Anderson & Agarwal, 2010).

For strong experimental control, each of the eight unique IS policies had four carefully constructed versions that did not alter the associated ISP. Instead. the ${ \mathsf { I S P s } }$ introductory and con cluding text was changed to represent high–low controlling language<sup>2</sup> and high–low new control. The formal control manipulations were based on the conceptualisations and measurements by Boss et al. (2009) and Kirsch (2004). All three elements of formal control were written into these manipulations for the high-control conditions: speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, evaluation and rewards. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation was introduced by providing more clarity and detail, much of which was achieved through the enhanced description of evaluation and rewards. Evaluation was introduced by explicitly stating how the system and management would monitor the end users’ compliance. Rewards and punishments were also speci<sup>fi</sup>ed for compliance and noncompliance. The lowcontrol conditions lacked this level of detail. To further establish experimental control, these wording changes were exactly the same across all memos, regardless of the ISP itself.

To ensure that the ISP memo scenarios were realistic and effective for manipulating our theoretical constructs, we conducted several rounds of development with experts as well as pilot testing with graduate students who had industry and research expertise on information security. These scenarios were <sup>fi</sup>rst reviewed and built with the assistance of security practitioners and several academics working in security, following guidelines developed by Siponen & Vance (2014). Once the ISP memos were constructed to represent these manipulations, the memos were carefully reviewed in several rounds to ensure consistent language and control. Severa PhD students with security experience and a professor external to the project (a security expert) were also engaged to review the memos and manipulations for consistency with the underlying theories and measurements. After completing the memo manipulations, we conducted a pilot study using 30 volunteer IS graduate students who had extensive educational training and pro fessional experience in organisational security, controls and systems. The focus of this pilo study was to further test our manipulations and underlying measurements. Each student inde pendently evaluated two randomly selected memos and <sup>fi</sup>lled out manipulation checks and all post-experiment measures for each memo. This test con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that all of our manipulations were in the direction intended and provided some minor improvements in wording.

## Participants

To increase the generalisability of our study, we hired a market research <sup>fi</sup>rm to randomly selec and invite industry participants from a pool of nearly 3 million professionals to oversee the stud as an online research panel. External panels have been used to elicit responses to survey instruments in various contexts (e.g. Bennett & Robinson, 2000; Gibney et al., 2009) and are increasingly used in organisational research because they have several established research advantages (e.g. Awad & Ragowsky, 2008; Posey et al., 2010). First, panels allow the respondent’s anonymity to be guaranteed – a design decision that further encourages honest responses to questions about behaviours potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social desirability beliefs (Bennett & Robinson. 2000). Second. respondents from a wide range of industries and positions can be reached for topics requiring the participation of a broad spectrum of individuals that would be almost impossible to attain with traditional methods (Bennett & Robinson, 2000; Lowry et al., 2013). Finally, because of the highly sensitive nature of information security violations, for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organisations are extremely averse to allowing outside researchers to study and publish the security violations of the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ employees (Kotulic & Clark, 2004). Anonymous panels of working professionals effectively allow researchers to work around this roadblock.

The marketing research <sup>fi</sup>rm commissioned 320 working professionals to participate; 160 were male (50%), and 160 were female (50%). Of the participants, 52 (16.3%) were part-time workers, and 268 (83.7%) were full-time workers. The average age was 45.2 years (standard deviation (SD) = 11.9), average years of work experience was 21.3 (SD = 12.3), and average years of formal education was 15.5 (SD = 2.8). The participants represented several key industries (Table 1). Finally, institutional review-board approval was given, and all respondents participated with full consent.

## Procedures

Participants <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>lled out their demographic information and pre-experiment measures. The participants were then given a series of two randomly selected ISP memos in two separate rounds of experimentation and measurement. Participants addressed each memo individually; that is, they answered questions regarding only the memo just viewed. The participants were asked to evaluate how they would feel about these memos in terms of their current organisation and position. They then <sup>fi</sup>lled out the post-experiment measures after responding to each memo, yielding 640 data points. The random presentation of the memos was designed to mit igate any potential ordering effects.

## ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

Prior to testing CRCM, we conducted a pre-analysis and data validation according to the latest standards, for several purposes: (1) to establish the model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, (2) to establish the factorial validity of the instrument through convergent and discriminant validities, (3) to establish that multicollinearity was not a problem for this model, (4) to check for common-method bias using several approaches, and (5) to establish strong construct reliabilities. The results of our validation procedures show that our model data meets or exceeds the latest rigorous validation standards expected for partial least squares (PLS)-based analysis (e.g. Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2006; Petter et al., 2007; Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009; MacKenzie et al., 2011), which we used to analyse our data. We report on these steps in elaborate detail, including our measurement model, in Online Appendix B.

## Reliability of measures

In addition to demonstrating high levels of validity, our measures exhibit high reliability (Table 2). To establish reliability, PLS computes a composite reliability score as part of its integrated model analysis. This score is a more accurate measurement of reliability than Cronbach’s alpha because it does not assume that loadings or error terms of the items are equal (Chin et al., 2003). However, as a conservative check, Cronbach’s alpha can also be used as a basis of comparison (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Kock, 2013). We thus applied the two most conservative criteria to establish the reliability of our re<sup>fl</sup>ective subconstructs: both the composite reliability and the Cronbach’s alpha coef<sup>fi</sup>cients should be ≥0.7 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Kock, 2013).

## DISCUSSION

## Contributions to research, theory and practice

Our key contribution is an innovative model that, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, uses an integrated model to examine two counterpoised forces to predict intent to comply with new ISPs in organisations. CRCM demonstrates both that organisational controls, as predicted by control theory, are shown to be a positive predictor of intent to comply, and that threats to personal freedom from organisational controls result in reactance. as predicted by reactance theory. We also demonstrate the elusive boomerang effects by showing a strong negative connection between reactance and intentions. Although this effect has long been theorised, little empirical evidence for it has been established (Burgoon et al., 2002; Silvia, 2005).

Looking beyond compliance, reactance in the workplace is a particularly troublesome phenom enon because its generation can create negative emotions such as anger that are strongly linked to destructive antisocial behaviour<sup>6</sup> in the workplace. Hence, high levels of control might result in desired ISP compliance, but if reactance is also generated, the situation represents a potentially dangerous Pyrrhic victory for any organisation. Hence, researchers and practitioners should no longer consider controls and deterrence in an organisational vacuum without considering the potential for threats to freedom that can undermine controls and deterrence and result in unintended negative consequences. These results illustrate the danger of the common organisational practice of introducing new ISPs (or other organisational policies) using controlling language in memos distributed widely throughout an organisation. This <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates that managers need to be very cautious in choosing the manner, medium and method of introducing new ISPs and organisationa policies. ISPs are clearly necessary in organisations; however, when managers write or communicate potentially freedom-restricting policies, they need to take into account that employees consider them selves free agents with rights and freedoms that if threatened will cause them to react negatively. Although ISPs require formal written controls, they need to be written and delivered in a respectful manner that softens or eliminates imperatives and provides for a range of options, wherever possible. Most importantly, managers need to balance coercion with care (Sewell & Barker, 2006).

Moreover, although reactance theory has been used for several decades and most reactance constructs in CRCM have been measured in various studies, our extensive review of the theo retical literature reveals that no single study has tested the full reactance theory constructs in any context – let alone with organisational ISPs. In fact, most studies have not directly examined the importance of freedom and threat level. Consequently, portions of the reactance theory model, and sometimes the whole model, are typically treated as a theoretical ‘black box’ where the nomological relationships are assumed but not measured and validated. Moreover, given that, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, formal controls and freedom are diametrically opposed, we considered how newly introduced ISPs might threaten existing freedoms (only those related to the new policies) and thus affect reactance, anger, and intent regarding ISP compliance. Notably, we found that the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential freedom element that drives reactance was threat to freedom – not the importance of freedom. This serves as a particularly important warning for practice, because our study indicates that even threats to a ‘trivial’ freedom can trigger reactance.

## Limitations and future research

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst key limitation, which points to a promising avenue for future research, is that our results, because they derived from a controlled laboratory experiment, have limited generalisability. Although we have every reason to believe that CRCM will hold up in similar matters of organisational compliance – especially any matters involving high levels of control and potential threats to personal freedom (e.g. audits, accounting controls, HR personnel policies, downsizing, mergers and acquisitions, and so on) – further replication of CRCM is needed to establish its generalisability. Moreover, following typical practices in attitudinal research, we considered intent but not actual compliance. Although our scenario/vignette approach to ISP policy compliance is a standard and valid approach for predicting actual compliance (e.g. Siponen & Vance, 2010), testing our new model in actual organisations that are implementing new IS policies would be useful. Likewise, how long reactance endures and the factors that contribute to its weakening are important open issues that could affect generalisability. Thus, longitudinal organisational data that examines reactance strength and compliance over time would be valuable.

Likewise, we tested CRCM in a Western culture – speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with US-based employees. US employees have been shown to be highly individualistic, valuing individual freedom of choice at work more than employees in collectivistic societies, such as the People’s Republic of China (e.g. Zhang et al., 2007; Dinev et al., 2009; Lowry et al., 2010; Avison et al., 2011). Employees in highly collectivistic (e.g. China or France) or highly controlling cultures (e.g. Saudi Arabia or Iran) might demonstrate far less reactance and associated anger than those in highly individualistic cultures such as the USA, simply because those employees do not place the same importance on workplace freedom and instead focus more on the success of the organisation. An important extension of this model would be to consider the differences in the perceptions of the threats to freedom – and subsequent reactance – in collectivistic versus individualistic cultures. Research could also consider the effects within organisations that are increasingly mixed and heterogeneous in terms of the crosscultural propensities of employees, or even in terms of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s organisational culture.

Another promising future research opportunity would be to consider the dual negative and positive effects of CRCM using physiological measures. Again, we showed that reactance constructs decrease intent, whereas control constructs increase it. How is it that the counterpoised forces can work together at the same time in an individual’s cognition? It could be that we are missing a key construct in the policy compliance literature – one of intent to not comply and tha intent to not comply combined with intent to comply is actually what best predicts compliance. This concern is similar to the issue in the trust and distrust literature of whether distrust is simply low trust or actually a separate construct. Recent groundbreaking research involving functiona magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) technology established that distrust and low trust are indeed separate constructs (Dimoka, 2010). The same may be true with intent to comply and intent not to comply. fMRI scans might also be useful in examining the cognitive manifestations of reactance and the resulting anger.

Along these lines, given that reactance, intentions and negative intentions are likely three sepa rate constructs, we believe people can have high reactance, high intentions and high negative intentions and yet comply. How would this be possible? First, reactance is a psychological construct of resulting attitude. Consequently, an individual could have a very high level of reactance and even associated anger and resentment towards his or her organisation, but fully comply. This is a classic example of attitudinal ambivalence, which has been shown extensively in the psychological literature on people being pulled into two diametrically opposed conditions both at high levels (e.g. high trust and high distrust) (Kaplan, 1972; Thompson & Zanna, 1995; Jonas et al., 1997; MacDonald & Zanna, 1998), which can lead to detrimental consequences. In our context, individuals can experience reactance but comply in the face of threats and controls from management. However, an organisation’s success in achieving employee compliance with ISPs does not necessarily mean that employees are happy about it and do not experience reactance. Reactance is toxic and can create a festering organisational brew that gives rise to unintended consequences. Future research in this area should thus further theorise and measure the creation of attitudinal ambivalence.

Finally, for exploratory purposes, we summarised the means for reactance according to ISP topic, which demonstrates that some ISPs naturally create more reactance than others, even when the underlying controls are the same (Table 7). Future research should identify the factors that distinguish ISP target behaviours cherished by users as highly personal freedoms from be haviours not similarly valued. It is possible that some of the drivers could relate to the degree to which a behaviour involves personal information privacy issues (Posey et al., 2011). Likewise, more research should be carried out on ways threats to freedoms can be increased and decreased. Possible factors that we did not examine include how abruptly a policy is delivered; delivering policies face-to-face versus a more impersonal medium, such as email; establishing an environment of threat awareness; treating employees as security partners and allies as opposed to security threats; explaining the wide variety of freedoms that employees have and that they should not assume are restricted by new policies (e.g. the ability to freely surf the Web during lunch breaks); developing policies in conjunction with the employees who will be required to adhere to them; and training that explains the rationale for IS security policies. These alternatives deserve future attention in research and practice.
