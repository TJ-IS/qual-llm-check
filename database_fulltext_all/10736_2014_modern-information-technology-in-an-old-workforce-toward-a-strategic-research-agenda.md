# Modern information technology in an old workforce: Toward a strategic research agenda

Stefan Tams <sup>b,</sup>⇑, Varun Grover <sup>a</sup>, Jason Thatcher <sup>a</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

As the workforce ages rapidly in industrialized countries, a phenomenon known as the graying of the workforce, new challenges arise for firms as they have to juggle this dramatic demographical change (Trend 1) in conjunction with the proliferation of increasingly modern information and communication technologies (ICTs) (Trend 2). Although these two important workplace trends are pervasive, their interdependencies have remained largely unexplored. While Information Systems (IS) research has established the pertinence of age to IS phenomena from an empirical perspective, it has tended to model the concept merely as a control variable with limited understanding of its conceptual nature. In fact, even the few IS studies that used the concept of age as a substantive variable have mostly relied on stereotypical accounts alone to justify their age-related hypotheses. Further, most of these studies have examined the role of age in the same phenomenon (i.e., initial adoption of ICTs), implying a marked lack of diversity with respect to the phenomena under investigation. Overall, IS research has yielded only limited insight into the role of age in phenomena involving ICTs. In this essay, we argue for the importance of studying agerelated impacts more carefully and across various IS phenomena, and we enable such research by providing a research agenda that IS scholars can use. In doing so, we hope that future research will further both our empirical and conceptual understanding of the managerial challenges arising from the interplay of a graying workforce and rapidly evolving ICTs.

## Introduction

Benita Oldfellow, who just turned 68, has to use information and communication technologies (ICTs) in support of her work as an accountant. She has a positive attitude toward the technology and believes it may be useful to her job, but she faces great trouble in using it effectively – in contrast to her grandson Frank who grew up with ICTs. Benita regularly asks Frank for help, and he gladly explains to her how to use such software as Microsoft Excel and how to exactly navigate the menu of her smartphone. In the process, he often notices that these technologies are clearly not designed with older people like his grandma in mind; it seems as if two different worlds were clashing. While these technologies do provide a few accessibility options for such older people as Benita, these options are often hidden in long and ambiguous click paths. As a result, Benita’s interactions with contemporary ICTs are largely ineffective and discomforting; not surprisingly, she considers quitting her job as an accountant.

This vignette illustrates the interdependencies in two workplace trends that are emerging across the world: the ‘‘graying’ of the workforce and the persistent evolution of ICTs in organizations (Ginn and Arber, 1996; OECD, 2010a, 2011, 2013a, 2013b; Panek, 1997, p. 363). According to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), all of its current thirty-four member countries are experiencing rapid population and workforce aging (OECD, 2007, 2011, 2013a, 2013b).<sup>1</sup> More specifically, the number of older workers (i.e., 65 and over) has increased from 15,906,000 in the year 2000 to 24,548,000 in 2012 across the OECD countries, an increase of more than 50% (OECD, 2010b). This OECD trend index is consistent with the trend across the G7 countries<sup>2</sup>, where the number of older people in the workforce has increased from 10,720,000 in the year 2000 to 16,868,000 in 2012, also an increase of more than 50% (OECD, 2010b).

Importantly, the trend toward an older workforce is not merely a phenomenon of the present but also one of the future; the participation of older people in the workforce is expected to increase sharply over the next several decades across the OECD member countries (OECD, 1998, 2011). The main reasons for this trend are pension policy changes (e.g., the reduction of existing incentives for early retirement), legislation against age discrimination, improvements in older peoples’ health, public information campaigns, and greater financial incentives to extend the work life and to work at an older age (OECD, 1998, 2011, 2013a). Another important reason relates to the changing landscape of job demands; people can work longer today due to the shift from physically demanding work in mining, construction, and manufacturing to the less physically demanding computer-based work (OECD, 2013a). To maintain the size of the workforce in OECD countries, the OECD predicts that employment rates for the traditional retirement population have to increase from about 20% today to 40% in 2050 (OECD, 2011).

To provide a concrete and detailed example for the graying of the workforce from a particularly large OECD country, the number of older people (i.e., 65 and over) in the workforce of the United States has increased by more than 100 percent over the last thirty years (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2008). The group of employees aged 75 and over (the older-old) has seen the most dramatic gain; it has risen by almost 175%. At the same time, total employment has seen a much smaller increase of only 59 percent. Thus, the population historically associated with retirement constitutes the fastest growing part of the U.S. workforce. This trend has arisen from legislation that prohibits age discrimination as well as mandatory retirement (enacted by Congress in 1986) and from economic factors that motivate employees to stay in the workforce longer (Panek, 1997; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2008), and it is ‘‘definitely’’ here to stay (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2008, p. 9). While the number of young workers (ages 16–24) is projected to decline between 2006 and 2016 and the number of middle-aged workers (ages 25–54) is expected to rise insignificantly by only 2%, the number of workers aged 55–64 are expected to climb by almost 40%. Still, the most dramatic growth is expected for the traditional retirement population; the number of workers older than 65 is projected to rise by more than 80 percent. Thus, the graying of the workforce ‘‘is only just beginning’ (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2008, p. 9), and organizations are left with a rapidly aging workforce.

In sharp contrast to the graying of the workforce, the technologies used to fulfill the primary tasks and responsibilities associated with most work roles are rapidly becoming ever more modern (Benbasat and Zmud, 1999; OECD, 2010a, 2010b). ICTs have evolved from ‘‘dumb’’ terminals over desktop PCs and more recent personal digital assistants to current smartphones and high quality Voice-over-IP applications (Somogyi and Galliers, 1987). This rapid evolution of ICTs implies a dramatic, continuous increase in technological complexity (Galliers et al., 2012; Ziefle and Bay, 2005) along with a shift of the paradigm according to which technology operates (e.g., the move from mainframe networks over the client–server model to service-oriented architectures, Somogyi and Galliers, 1987).

Since older individuals received their technological educations in former times when technology was far less complex than such current ICTs as smart-phones and when technology operated according to such out-of-date paradigms as mainframe networks, some older peoples’ mental models of how technology works may not suffice to support adequate interactions with modern ICTs<sup>3</sup> (Ziefle and Bay, 2005). This notion is strengthened by the fact that – across OECD countries – older workers are less likely than their younger counterparts to participate in training initiatives to keep their technologyrelated knowledge up to date (because their pay-back period is shorter) (OECD, 2011). The dated mental models that result may often interfere with effective interactions between older people and modern technology. Older workers' difficulties in dealing with the complexity and paradigms of contemporary technologies might be amplified dramatically by cognitive aging (i.e., age-related changes in the allocation of cognitive resources) as well as by changes in personality, sensorimotor abilities, and culture (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006; McCrae et al., 2005; McElroy et al., 2007; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009; Rogers and Fisk, 2001; Walsh et al., 2010)

Yet, despite these problems, ICTs are still designed with little systematic regard for older users (Fisk et al. 2009). This lack of regard for older users may stem from the large age gap between older users and systems designers, who tend to be younger individuals (Hawthorn, 2007). Research suggests that systems designers are often unaware of this age gap (Newell et al., 2006) and design ICTs that generally neither account for older peoples’ dated mental models of how technology works nor for age-related changes in cognition, personality, motor functions, and culture (Hawthorn, 2007). Thus, contemporary ICTs may be more difficult to use for older people than for younger. Indeed, recent research typically reports that older individuals have less computer self-efficacy and internet self-efficacy than younger people and that they experience more computer anxiety (e.g., Czaja et al., 2006; Czaja and Sharit, 1998; Fisk et al., 2009; Mead et al., 2000; Rogers et al., 1996; Rousseau et al., 1998; Sharit and Czaja, 1999). As a result, older people tend to think that the computer age has passed by them: ‘‘It’s not for me, I am too old’’ (Brown, 2002, p. 28B). This conclusion of older people concerning ICTs is surprising given that they generally maintain positive attitudes about ICTs and their benefits (Linton, 2012).

Intrigued by this inconsistency, we examined the most commonly cited literatures and gradually went deeper and farther afield into gerontology, neuropsychology, and other areas. As we read, we noticed important aspects in the reference literatures that seemed not to be fully reflected in the IS work. One is the virtually non-existent use of age as a substantive variable. Indeed, few IS studies focus on the age of ICT users (e.g., Hong et al., 2013; Lam and Lee, 2006), although age is considered a key moderator variable for IS research (i.e., age can help IS scholars qualify for what kinds of users IS research models apply) (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Hence, there is a striking incongruity between the importance of examining the role of age in IS phenomena and the number of studies that actually investigate this role. Another important aspect that our review surfaced is the stereotypical manner in which IS work has tended to treat the concept of age. More specifically, age has often been superficially ‘‘thrown in’’ as a moderator variable with little understanding of the theoretical nature of the concept, and age has regularly been modeled as a direct moderator of IS relationships such that it remains unclear how and why age moderates these relationships. This approach has yielded a limited conceptual understanding of agerelated impacts along with contradictory findings (e.g., Brown et al., 2010; Wattal et al., 2010). Further, IS research has explored the role of age primarily in the domain of initial technology adoption (to a fairly limited extent); other relevant domains such as post-adoption or technostress have been neglected (e.g., Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2007). Hence, there is a need for improved knowledge creation concerning the role of age in ICT usage and for knowledge creation across such domains as initial adoption, post-adoption, as well as such related IS phenomena as technostress (e.g., Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2007).

Our objectives with this viewpoint article are twofold: to alert IS scholars to the emerging importance of studying age-related impacts on IS phenomena and to provide them with a research agenda for doing so. On the basis of recent research indicating that age is a key moderator variable for IS research (Venkatesh et al., 2003), we wrote this article because we believe that it is important for the field to engage in a debate about the nature of the aging-technology relationship (e.g., should age be modeled as a direct or indirect moderator) and about how to stimulate research that tackles this important relationship head on. In view of the graying of the workforce, our focus is on ICT use in the workplace, not on non-work or private use.

Overall, this article contributes to IS research by (1) raising awareness of the importance of examining the moderating effects of age on various IS relationships, (2) providing a review of past age-related IS research on which future IS research can build to advance our understanding of the moderating effects of age, (3) suggesting that future IS research should conceptualize age-related moderating effects in terms of ‘‘touch points of age,’’ which explain how and why age moderates certain IS relationships, and by (4) discussing pertinent frameworks of aging related to the concept of ‘‘touch points of age’ that yield a series of research questions with the potential to further knowledge creation in this area. In proposing this research agenda aimed at explaining not only whether age moderates certain IS relationships (as was done in past research) but also how and why age acts as an important moderator, this viewpoint article enables an important improvement in the modeling and subsequent understanding of the role of age in ICT usage. For example, instead of merely advancing the generic proposition that age moderates a given IS relationship, our research agenda aims at explaining that age moderates this relationship due to specific age-related changes in cognitive abilities, in personality, in sensorimotor abilities, and in culture (please see Fig. 1 in Section ‘Toward a strategic research agenda of ICT-use in a graying workforce’). The improved understanding resulting from this approach is important for managers and systems designers struggling to align the dramatic, age-related demographical change with the rapid technological change. More specifically, consistent with MacKinnon and Luecken (2008), such improved understanding can provide more comprehensive and actionable guidance to managers and IS practitioners by elucidating the mechanisms that connect age to ICT usage.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews prior IS research on aging and discovers that scant IS scholarship has focused on age and that existing work does not advance our understanding of how and why age-related moderating impacts manifest themselves. On this basis, the third section introduces the concept of ‘‘touch points of age,’’ describing a way of studying how and why age-related moderation effects manifest (i.e., touch points of age are relevant mediating mechanisms that explain how and why age moderates IS relationships). The section, then, advances a strategic research agenda based on pertinent conceptual frameworks (cognitive abilities, personality, sensorimotor abilities, and culture) that are related to the concept of touch points of age. These frameworks specify concepts that can serve as mediating mechanisms to explain how and why age moderates IS relationships. IS scholars can use the strategic research agenda put forth here in their future work to advance our understanding of age-related impacts on IS phenomena, with the ultimate goal of helping managers and systems designers shape the future of technology usage in a graying workforce. Our objective is to enable change management that creates a ‘‘fit’’ between technological characteristics and the graying workforce. In doing so, the paper addresses how technological responses to changes in demographics may have strategic implications. Finally, this viewpoint article offers concluding thoughts on the use of the research agenda, and it makes a case for the persistence of age-related impacts on IS phenomena and for the long-term relevance of these impacts to clarify that they are not a simple artifact of time and place.

## What we (do not) know about ICT-use in a graying workforce

To inform our viewpoint on age-related IS research and to form an opinion on the state of such research, we identified a representation of the related literature. Specifically, following recent IS research (Sarker et al., 2013), we investigated the current state of age-related research in the IS field by reviewing age-related studies in the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals: MIS Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), and Journal of Information Technology (JIT). Consistent with recent IS research (Sarker et al., 2013), we selected these journals since they constitute the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals with a global reach and reputation, and they are considered the leading mainstream journals in the IS field. Hence, these journals were likely to include the major contributions relevant to our opinion paper (Webster and Watson, 2002), allowing us to identify an appropriate representation of published, age-related IS research.

We searched for the keyword “age" in the title and abstract of the papers published in those journals since we sought to identify papers in which age played an important role, that is, papers that focused on the role of age in IS phenomena.<sup>4</sup> Using these search criteria, we obtained 67 different search results (on April 22nd, 2014, after the removal of duplicates). We, then, removed all articles that were not relevant because they did not focus on the age of technology users but, for example, on the ‘‘digital age’’ (e.g., Henfridsson et al., 2014), the ‘‘information age’’ (e.g., Mason, 1986), the ‘‘age of the system’’ (not the user) (e.g., Dekleva, 1992), the ‘‘App age’’ (i.e., the time since which an app has been launched) (e.g., Claussen et al., 2013), or ‘‘the age of cyborgism’’ (e.g., Schultze and Mason, 2012). Furthermore, we removed three articles that used the concept of age merely as a control variable (i.e., Hansen and Walden, 2013; Srite and Karahanna, 2006; Truman and Baroudi, 1994). Following these removals, we obtained a set of 12 articles. Using pertinent keywords from these 12 articles, we conducted another search. In this second search, we searched for the keywords ‘‘younger,’’ ‘‘older,’’ and ‘‘elderly’’ in the title and abstract of the papers published in the same journals, and we identified another 2 relevant articles. Thus, we obtained a final set of 14 articles to inform our viewpoint on age-related IS research and to form our opinion on the state of such research (please see Table 1 in Section 2, Subsection ‘‘Concept Analysis: Key Findings,’’ to refer to these articles).

Following the concept-centric approach to reviewing pertinent literature (Webster and Watson, 2002), we reviewed the identified studies with a focus on four concepts that (1) emerged from the objectives of this viewpoint article, that (2) were consistent with Gregor (2006) who emphasized improved knowledge creation in the IS field, and that (3) can help uncover the extent to which age plays a role in IS research, More specifically, given that age is considered a key moderator variable for IS research (Venkatesh et al., 2003), there is a need for numerous studies focusing on age that advance both our empirical and conceptual understanding of the role of age across various IS phenomena. This conclusion is consistent with Gregor (2006), who referred to the twin goals of prediction (empirical) and explanation (conceptual) as well as to the needs for construct centrality and robustness across phenomena. Hence, the following four concepts were deemed pertinent: Age Centrality (i.e., was the concept of age central to the study; did the role of age in an IS phenomenon constitute the focus of the study?), Empirical Advancement (i.e., did the study advance empirical understanding of age-related impacts?), Conceptual Advancement (i.e., did the study advance conceptual understanding of age-related impacts?), and Diversity of Phenomena (i.e., were different phenomena examined across the identified studies?).

## Concept 1: Age centrality

To extend substantially our knowledge of age in IS phenomena, studies are needed in which the concept of age plays a central role, that is, studies that focus their attention on age. To draw a parallel, since computer self-efficacy (CSE) was suggested to be an important concept in IS research (Compeau and Higgins, 1995), many studies have been conducted that focused their attention on the role of CSE in IS phenomena (e.g., Compeau et al., 1999; Compeau and Higgins, 1995; Marakas et al., 1998, 2007; Thatcher et al., 2008). The rational for this approach is simple: papers that focus their attention on a given concept shed more light on the role of that concept in a given phenomenon than papers in which the concept plays merely a minor or secondary role. In our review, each paper was examined to determine what role age played in the paper’s core model. The categories secondary and primary represent a change in the role of age from minor support to a central role in the study. Specifically, we considered the concept of age to be the focus of a paper (i.e. primary or central to that paper) when more than half of the paper’s hypotheses related to age or when age was the core context of the study.

Among the 14 age-related papers we identified, only one study showed a focus on the role of age in the phenomenon investigated: Lam and Lee (2006) examined initial internet adoption decisions with a focus on older individuals. More specifically, using social cognitive theory, the authors explored the effects of internet self-efficacy and outcome expectations on older peoples’ extent of internet usage. Their entire research model, including ten hypotheses, explored technology adoption by older people. By contrast, the other 13 papers in the set did not show such an age focus. For example, only 1 out of the 10 hypotheses in the most recent paper identified (Venkatesh et al., 2014) was related to the role of age in the focal phenomenon.

Concept Takeaway: Few IS studies have been conducted in which the concept of age played a central role. Hence, more studies focusing their attention on the role of age in IS phenomena are needed to extend our knowledge of age-related impacts.

## Concept 2: Empirical advancement

To extend our understanding of the role of age in IS phenomena, it is, furthermore, necessary to obtain empirical evidence showing that the age of users really influences an IS phenomenon and that this influence is positive (or negative). While empirical evidence on age-related impacts, by itself, offers little explanation as to why an age-related impact exists and why this impact has a certain direction, such evidence offers useful insight as to whether age matters in IS phenomena and what happens as technology users grow older (for example, users may be less likely or more likely to adopt a given technology as they grow older).

We consider the advancement of the empirical understanding of age-related impacts to be a function of the conduct of a formal comparison between older and younger user populations using pertinent data; empirical understanding can only be advanced if comparisons between age groups are made on the grounds of observation. In the set of 14 papers we identified, all but one study made such age comparisons and, hence, advanced empirical understanding of age-related impacts on IS phenomena. The study conducted by Lam and Lee (2006) did not formally compare different user populations and, thus, offered limited empirical understanding of the role of age in technology adoption decisions. The other 13 studies, however, formally compared different user populations using pertinent data and, therefore, advanced empirical understanding of the role of age in IS phenomena. For example, the most recent study we identified (Venkatesh et al., 2014) measured age on a continuum that reflected each participant’s chronological age, and the study, then, used this measure in the prediction of technology use.

Concept Takeaway: A number of studies have confirmed empirically the idea that age acts as a moderator in IS research models. Age-related IS research needs to continue down this path to advance further our empirical understanding of age effects.

## Concept 3: Conceptual advancement

While the findings of the 14 identified articles, on the whole, contributed substantially to our empirical understanding of the role of age in IS research, they were derived and interpreted without reliance on relevant concepts of aging, such as fluid or crystallized cognitive abilities, personality, sensorimotor abilities, or culture. In fact, the theoretical frameworks used by the 14 papers for model development made no assumptions about aging and did not specify relevant concepts of aging that could have explained the age-related differences that were hypothesized. Hence, instead of using concepts of aging to justify their age-related hypotheses, these papers merely speculated about the role of age using common stereotypes. In fact, these papers often speculated only post hoc about the potential underlying causal mechanisms for any observed age differences; the underlying causal mechanisms were not explicitly modeled.

For example, the sole study in the area of technostress (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008) framed its research model using the transaction-based model of stress, which is not concerned with age-related differences. The authors speculated post hoc that the possibly longer organizational tenure of older employees could result in more firm-specific experience and an improved understanding of how to assimilate the effects of ICTs in the work environment, weakening experiences of stress. The authors note that this result could be sample-specific. While the correlation identified in this study advanced our empirical understanding of the role of age in technostress, the speculation offered did not refer to any relevant concept of aging (such as cognitive or sensorimotor abilities, personality or culture), and, therefore, offered little to advance our conceptual understanding of the role of age in technology use.

As another example, consider the only study we identified that actually focused on age (Lam and Lee, 2006). As mentioned earlier, this study investigated initial internet adoption decisions by older people using social cognitive theory. The study found that internet self-efficacy and outcome expectations were significant predictors of usage intention for older adults. However, since the authors informed their study by solely relying on social cognitive theory, which does not advance any explanatory concepts related to aging, the study’s important findings contributed only limitedly to our conceptual understanding of internet usage by older adults. For example, it remained unknown how and why age was related to internet self-efficacy. The same holds true for the other studies we identified, which relied on theories such as the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980) that makes no assumptions about aging.

Perhaps as a result of this generic approach, which largely ignores explanatory concepts related to aging, age-related IS studies have produced inconsistent findings. For example, some studies in the area of initial technology adoption found that older people are less likely to adopt than younger (e.g., Brown et al., 2010; Harrison and Rainer, 1992; Venkatesh et al., 2012), while other studies have failed to find these age-related effects (e.g., Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1990; Venkatesh et al., 2014; Wattal et al., 2010). Inconsistent findings generally indicate a lack of knowledge creation, which, in turn, signifies research stream immaturity (Huber, 1983). Overall, our review suggests that it remains unknown how and why age moderates IS relationships.

Concept Takeaway: Few IS studies have used relevant concepts of aging (e.g., declining or improving cognitive abilities, personality, sensorimotor abilities, or culture) as mechanisms that could explain the age effects proposed therein, limiting our conceptual understanding of age-related impacts on IS phenomena. Consequently, more age-related IS research is warranted that applies pertinent concepts of aging as explanatory mechanisms for the proposed age effects to yield more specific explanations of how and why age moderates certain IS relationships.

## Concept 4: Diversity of phenomena

To extend substantially our knowledge of age in IS research, studies are needed that examine the role of age across various IS phenomena. Such diversity would imply that the concept of age has become an integral part of IS research, and it would offer an opportunity to synthesize the age-related findings across phenomena to yield a unified understanding of the role of age in IS research. However, our review finds a marked lack of diversity. The vast majority of studies (64%) treat the same phenomenon: initial technology adoption. Few studies are devoted to the role of age in IT-related moral judgments (14%), browsing strategies (7%), privacy concerns (7%), or technostress (7%). This lack of age-related IS research devoted to phenomena other than initial adoption is surprising, particularly in the domain of technostress, where recent studies have called for an exploration of the role of age (Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2007). Some important, current trends in IS use research, such as post-adoptive use (Jasperson et al., 2005; Ortiz de Guinea and Markus, 2009), have even been left completely unaddressed.

Concept Takeaway: Age-related IS research is largely restricted to the domain of initial technology adoption. Other mainstream domains such as post-adoption or technostress, to which aging is highly relevant (e.g., Riedl, 2013; Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2007), have been neglected. Hence, age-related IS research needs to be more diverse in terms of the phenomena under investigation.

## Concept analysis: Key findings

Given (1) the small number of age-related IS studies, (2) these studies’ lack of age centrality (i.e., few studies focus on age), and (3) these studies’ lack of specificity concerning how and why aging should impact ICT use, age-related IS research appears to be limited from an absolute perspective (i.e., when evaluated against the importance of the topic area as introduced earlier). This limitation implies that a greater number of age-related IS studies is needed, particularly of studies that focus on the role of age, that further our conceptual understanding of age effects, and that examine age-related impacts across diverse phenomena such as technostress and post-adoption (please see Table 1 for a concept matrix evidencing these necessities).

In sharp contrast to the treatment of age in IS research, such other individual differences as gender and cultural values have received far more attention. A search using the keyword ‘‘gender’’ in the title and abstract of the same journals that were used earlier (to identify a representation of age-related IS research) yielded 22 studies that focused on the role of gender in IS. For age, only a single study was identified that focused on the concept (i.e., Lam and Lee, 2006), implying a surprising and marked difference in how IS research treats these two concepts. For culture, the IS community has established such well-regarded journals as the Journal of Global Information Management, among others, that are entirely devoted to the study of cultural impacts on individual interactions with technology. Moreover, a comprehensive theory and review paper has been published in MIS Quarterly that synthesized the vast IS literature on cultural impacts (i.e., Kappos and Rivard, 2008). Furthermore, IS research interested in the role of culture has often applied culture-related conceptual mechanisms, such as Hofstede’s (1980) cultural dimensions, to the study of IS phenomena. In doing so, such research has been able to explain how and why culture impacts these phenomena (Tams, 2013). The difference in how we study the role of age, where we disregard age-related explanatory mechanisms, vis-à-vis gender and culture, further indicates that age is a relatively neglected concept in IS research. Hence, age-related IS research is limited from a relative perspective (i.e., when compared to IS work involving such other individual differences as gender or culture).

Overall, we identified both an absolute and a relative lack of age-related IS research, and these deficiencies are quantitative (i.e., lack of a sufficient number of studies that focus on age) and qualitative (i.e., lack of conceptual understanding of age-related impacts via age-related explanatory mechanisms) in nature (please see Table 2). While a few studies have confirmed empirically the idea that age acts as an important moderator in IS research models, they have not sufficiently explained how and why age matters. Consistent with Bacharach (1989), Gregor (2006), and Whetten (1989), this deficiency implies the need for an improved modeling and understanding of age-related impacts through the use of pertinent concepts of aging (e.g., declining or improving cognitive abilities, personality, sensorimotor abilities, or culture that could serve as explanatory mechanisms for the proposed age effects). Furthermore, it becomes apparent that age-related IS research lacks diversity; the vast majority of such research is concentrated in the domain of initial technology adoption. Other important domains, such as post-adoptive use or technostress, to which aging is particularly pertinent (e.g., Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2007), have been neglected. Therefore, more work is needed to account for the fact that age is a ‘‘key moderator variable’’ for IS research (Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 427).

## Toward a strategic research agenda of ICT-use in a graying workforce

In the paragraphs that follow we outline a strategic research agenda for examining the role of age in IS research, with the ultimate goal of helping managers and systems designers shape the future of technology usage in a graying workforce. The ever-increasing age across all OECD countries, with fewer and fewer births and more and more senior citizens, creates major challenges for society and organizations alike as they are struggling to juggle the changing demographics with related trends (OECD, 2007, 2011, 2013a; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2010). Hence, our objective is to enable strategic change management that creates a ‘‘fit’’ between technological characteristics and the graying workforce, shaping the future of technology usage. As such, our research agenda is strategic in nature since it aims at helping organizations allocate scarce resources so that more effective technology usage is achieved over time (Galliers, 1993; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991).

## Conceptualizing age in IS research

Since conceptual clarity is key for scientific progress, we begin our research agenda by clearly defining the concept of age. Age can be conceptualized in different ways, with the most common conceptualizations being chronological age, subjective or perceived age, and functional or biological age (see Table 3) (Cleveland and Lim. 2007: Neves and Amaro. 2012: Peeters and van Emmerik, 2008). These conceptualizations focus on the number of years a person has been alive. Chronological age reflects the actual number of years that a person has lived. For example, an individual born 65 years ago has a chronological age of 65 years. The group of older adults can be subdivided into the younger-old (60–75 years of chronological age) and the older-old (over 75 years) (Fisk et al., 2009). As such, chronological age constitutes a straightforward and objective measure of life length that has been widely used in the IS literature (Hong et al., 2013). The conceptualization of subjective or perceived age considers age to be more a state of mind than a physiological state. As such, it is also referred to as cognitive age, and it measures how old individuals consider themselves to be. For example, individuals who consider themselves to be 50 years old based on their lively spirits have a subjective or perceived age of 50 years, irrespective of their chronological age (Cleveland and Lim, 2007; Hong et al., 2013; Neves and Amaro, 2012). Finally, functional or biological age measures how an individual’s functioning changes over time. For example, people with poor eyesight, poor hearing abilities, and poor reaction times might have a functional or biological age of 75 years, irrespective of their chronological age (Cleveland and Lim, 2007; Neves and Amaro, 2012).

These conceptualizations of age may be useful for investigations into different IS phenomena. For example, IS research using such theories as social cognitive theory adopted from cognitive psychology may find the conceptualization of age as cognitive age particularly useful. By contrast, research in the area of NeuroIS that uses physiological measures in the study of IS phenomena may find the conceptualization of age as functional or biological age particularly useful (Riedl, 2013; Tams et al., 2014). Hence, aligning the conceptualization of age employed in a study with the phenomenon under investigation or the theory used to inform the study may yield more valid and interesting findings. ## Conceptualizing and testing touch points of age

Past age-related IS research has commonly conceptualized age as a direct moderator of certain use-related relationships, for example, as a direct moderator of the relationship between habit and usage intention (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2012). While this approach was parsimonious and has shed light on whether age moderates certain IS relationships, the approach has also limited what we can learn about the role of age in IS phenomena since explanatory mechanisms for the proposed age effects were not explicitly modeled. For example, it remains unclear which age-related cognitive changes or personality changes explain the moderating impact of age on the relationship between habit and usage intention. Hence, age-related IS research to date has not sufficiently explained how and why age matters (i.e., mediated moderation effects), an important shortcoming since good explanatory research models must answer to the question of why (Bacharach, 1989; Sutton and Staw, 1995; Whetten, 1989). Therefore, to advance knowledge in this area and offer more specific guidance to managers, research needs to examine more carefully the process by which age-related impacts on usage behaviors unfold, implying the inclusion of pertinent mediating mechanisms for the proposed age effects in our research models. We refer to such mediators as ‘‘touch points’’ of age since they constitute points through which age touches on various IS phenomena. As such, touch points of age explain how and why age matters, that is, they are mechanisms that serve to explain exactly how and why age moderates certain IS relationships.

Consistent with MacKinnon and Luecken (2008, p. S99), taking such a touch point-based approach to studying age effects in IS research could yield a ‘‘more sophisticated’’ understanding of the role of age in ICT usage by helping IS research progress from the investigation of the general association between age and the antecedent-use relationship to more detailed and specific explanations of the causal pathways involved. For example, instead of only advancing the generic proposition that age moderates the link between habit and usage, one could explain that age moderates this linkage specifically because of differences in certain cognitive abilities, in personality, in sensorimotor abilities, or in cultural values between different age groups – when using the touch point-based approach (please see Fig. 1).

Following the conceptualization of touch points of age, research could test the age-related impacts on IS phenomena using the generally agreed-upon mediated moderation procedure developed by Baron and Kenny (1986), who also initially coined the term mediated moderation (Mueller et al., 2005; Preacher et al., 2007). This procedure can provide evidence that, for example, the moderating effect of age on the link between habit and usage can be explained with the moderating impact of pertinent cognitive abilities like selective attention (i.e., cognitive abilities like selective attention explain how and why age acts as a moderator). As such, this procedure allows for the inclusion of direct moderators as the explanatory mechanisms for the indirect age effects so that these direct moderators can explain how and why age-related impacts on IS phenomena manifest.

The question remains of what mechanisms IS researchers could leverage as touch points of age to explain how and why age impacts diverse IS phenomena. We propose cognitive abilities, personality, sensorimotor abilities, and culture as relevant explanatory mechanisms since all of these concepts are impacted by age and, in turn, affect technology usage (e.g., Charness et al., 2004; Czaja et al., 2006; Leidner and Kayworth, 2006; McCrae et al., 2005; McElroy et al., 2007; Walsh et al., 2010). We begin with age-related cognitive abilities, which are generally classified into fluid and crystallized abilities (Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009). Whereas fluid abilities decline with age, crystallized abilities improve over time (Pak and McLaughlin. 2010). ## Cognitive abilities

Disadvantages of older adults: Fluid abilities as touch points

The first framework we would like to discuss concerns changes in fluid intelligence that occur as people grow older. Fluid intelligence refers to individuals’ abilities to learn and adapt to new situations; it is important in situations that change rapidly (Czaja et al. 2006: Pak and McLaughlin, 2010: Pak et al. 2009). As such, it is pertinent to human interactions with ICTs since the very nature of technology is continuously evolving and changing at a rapid rate, requiring lifelong learning and adaptation (Benbasat and Zmud, 1999; OECD, 2010a, 2010b). Recent research has shown that fluid intelligence strongly declines as people age (Czaja et al., 2006). Specifically, older adults suffer from large impairments in such fluid abilities as attention, working memory capacity, spatial abilities, reasoning, and perceptual speed (Czaja et al., 2006; McDowd and Shaw, 2000; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009; Rogers and Fisk, 2001; Salthouse et al., 1998).

Attention ensures that stimuli appear clear to the conscious mind and can be processed effectively (McDowd and Shaw, 2000; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Strayer and Drews, 2007). Two forms of attention are relevant to IS research, selective and divided attention. In selective attention situations, individuals attempt to selectively process some information sources while ignoring others. For example, people may be trying to navigate a smart-phone menu while also trying to actively ignore such other stimuli as incoming instant messages (Wickens et al., 2004). In divided attention situations, individuals attempt to attend to multiple stimuli at the same time. For example, people may be talking on the smart-phone while also surfing the Internet (Wickens et al., 2004). In both of these situations, the attentional resource declines with age so that older adults face greater difficulty in selectively attending to target stimuli and in processing multiple pieces of information at the same time than do their younger counterparts (Kane et al., 1994; McDowd and Shaw, 2000; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009; Park, 2000; Rogers and Fisk, 2001; Whiting et al., 2007). IS research should advance knowledge on how this impairment affects technology usage, and it needs to advance recommendations on how to accommodate the age-related limitations in the attentional resource, for example, by designing interfaces so that attention is easier to allocate. Therefore:

RQ3: Do differences in selective and divided attention between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the attentional deficit of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

Attention closely relates to the concept of working memory, which is a temporary storage and processing component in the human brain that holds the information necessary to complete an active task (Boduroglu et al., 2007; Strayer and Drews, 2007; Wickens et al., 2004). For example, phone numbers are held in working memory until they are fully dialed. Yet, the storage capacity of working memory for information related to an active task is strictly limited (Strayer and Drews, 2007; Wickens et al., 2004). This limitation is particularly severe for older adults because of their reduced selective attention ability, which implies that their working memory is often overloaded with task-irrelevant information (Boduroglu et al. 2007; Darowski et al., 2008; Smith, 1996; Stoltzfus et al., 1996). As a result, there is a ‘‘dramatic’’ difference in the capacity of working memory between younger and older adults in terms of holding task-related information (Pak et al., 2009, p. 68; Stoltzfus et al., 1996). This age-related difference in working memory capacity strongly impacts the ability to comprehend textual and spoken material in, for example, ICT interfaces (Boduroglu et al., 2007; Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Thus, IS research should advance knowledge on how working memory capacity impacts the antecedent-usage relationship for different age groups, and it needs to advance recommendations on how to accommodate the age-related limitations in working memory capacity, for example, by designing interfaces so that working memory demands are reduced (e.g., in a voice menu system, callers could be presented with relatively short series of items to help older adults remember their task objective at the same time as they need to remember all of the number/option pairings). Hence:

RQ4: Do differences in working memory capacity between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in working memory capacity of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

The accommodation of working memory limitations in older adults should be supplemented by ICT-related design characteristics that support age-related differences in spatial abilities. Indeed, spatial ability, that is, the ability to create a mental representation of a physical system, declines rapidly as people age (Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009). This decline implies that older people face more difficulty navigating computerized interfaces and browsing the Internet than do younger ones (Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Wickens et al., 2004). This problem holds particularly true for the deep information hierarchies found on such websites as Amazon.com, which require a complex mental map of the site for effective site navigation (Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Hence, IS research should advance knowledge on how the lack of spatial abilities in older adults affects ICT-usage, and it should explore opportunities for accommodating the age-related limitations in spatial abilities, for example, by replacing deep information hierarchies with keyword or tag organizations that may impose fewer demands on spatial abilities (Radvansky et al., 1990, 1996; Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009). Therefore:

RQ5: Do differences in spatial abilities between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in the spatial abilities of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

Another important aspect of fluid intelligence to consider is reasoning ability. Research has suggested that older individuals perform less well than younger on tests of inductive reasoning (e.g., Salthouse et al., 1998). These tests require the subjects to interpret geometric patterns and to choose the best one of a number of alternatives provided to complete the patterns. These reasoning tests are usually conducted under time pressure so that valid observations of reasoning ability can be obtained (Salthouse et al., 1998). The age-related decline in reasoning ability implies that older people may face more difficulty using technologies than do younger ones (Czaja et al., 2006; Salthouse et al., 1998). Hence, IS research should advance knowledge on how the decline in reasoning abilities with age affects ICT-usage, and it should explore opportunities for accommodating this age-related limitation. Therefore:

RQ6: Do differences in reasoning abilities between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in the reasoning abilities of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

A final aspect to consider is perceptual speed. Research has shown that older individuals have lower processing speeds than do younger individuals (e.g., Czaja et al., 2006; Salthouse et al., 1998). This pattern is robust; younger adults generally perform better on perceptual speed tests than do middle-aged adults, who, in turn, perform better than older adults (Czaja et al., 2006). At the same time as processing speed varies as a function of age, processing speed, in turn, is an important determinant of successful performance on technology-based tasks (Czaja et al., 2006). Hence, processing speed may mediate the moderating impacts of age on various aspects of technology usage. IS research should advance knowledge on how the decline in perceptual speed with age affects ICT-usage, and it should explore opportunities for accommodating this age-related limitation. Therefore:

RQ7: Do differences in perceptual speed between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in the perceptual speed of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

## Advantages of older adults: Crystallized abilities as touch points

While fluid intelligence decreases with age, crystallized intelligence increases (Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009). Crystallized intelligence refers to the general knowledge that people gain from a lifetime of formal education and life experience (Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Such knowledge is pertinent to human interactions with ICTs, for example, in terms of interface navigation (Pak et al., 2009). More specifically, older adults benefit from strong verbal ability, knowledge and experience transfer abilities, and discrete knowledge structures (Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010; Pak et al., 2009; Radvansky et al., 1990, 1996; Rogers and Fisk, 2001).

Verbal ability is reflected in vocabulary knowledge (Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Older adults tend to be more familiar with the various nuances of word meaning than younger ones, implying stronger verbal ability (Czaja et al., 2006; Pak and McLaughlin. 2010: Pak et al. 2009). Older adults tend to have more knowledge and experience to draw from than younger people, implying an advantage for them in terms of transferring such knowledge and experience to new tasks (Radvansky et al., 1990, 1996; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Finally, older adults have an advantage in terms of a greater number of discrete knowledge structures, which can be conceptualized as bundles of related pieces of knowledge that people develop over time (Radvansky et al., 1990; Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). Because of more life experience and knowledge, older adults tend to be better able than younger ones to link related pieces of information, creating a specific knowledge structure that can aid in a specific context (Pak and McLaughlin, 2010). ## Personality

The impact of age-related changes in personality on ICT-usage

IS researchers have recognized the importance of personal factors in technology usage for decades (e.g., Lucas, 1981; Zmud, 1979). Recent research has underscored the importance of personality in predicting technology usage, especially in terms of the ‘‘Big Five’’ personality traits: neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness (Thatcher and Perrewe, 2002; McElroy et al., 2007). For example, individuals with agreeable personalities tend not to use the Internet for email to the same extent as other people do (Swickert et al., 2002), suggesting that the Big Five personality traits are useful for predicting technology usage. Consistent with this notion, a recent study by McElroy et al. (2007) found some of the Big Five personality traits to explain significant incremental variance in internet usage, after controlling for computer anxiety, self-efficacy, and gender.

While the Big Five are important predictors of technology usage, they, in turn, have been shown to vary as a function of age (McCrae et al., 2005). More specifically, while neuroticism, extraversion, and openness decrease with age, agreeableness, and conscientiousness increase (Chan et al., 2012; McCrae et al., 2005). These age-related changes in personality reflect that older people compared to younger are less impulsive, lower in activity, more agreeable, and more likely to prefer routine, whereas younger people emphasize competence, achievement striving, assertiveness, and invulnerability to stress (Chan et al., 2012).

Hence, age is an important driver of the Big Five personality traits, and both concepts age and the Big Five are important factors in ICT usage (Chan et al., 2012; McElroy et al., 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012), indicating that the Big Five may mediate the moderating impact of age on technology usage. Moreover, recent IS research (McElroy et al., 2007) suggested that age may dominate personality in the prediction of ICT usage, further indicating that the Big Five personality traits may mediate the moderating impact of age on technology usage. ## Sensorimotor abilities

The impact of age-related changes in motor functions on ICT-usage

In addition to cognitive abilities and personality, age-related decrements in sensorimotor abilities are important factors to consider in the context of technology usage across age groups. More specifically, older individuals suffer from declining motor functions that impact movement duration, movement variability, as well as force control and regulation (Ketcham and Stelmach, 2004). Movement duration refers to the time that elapses between the initiation and the termination of a movement (Salthouse, 1985). Due to decrements in motor abilities, older adults require substantially longer durations for various movement tasks (up to 60% longer) (Ketcham and Stelmach, 2004). These tasks include technology-related work. For example, older compared to younger adults tend to need longer pointing times when using the computer mouse as a pointing device (Jochems et al., 2013). This problem holds particularly true for complex mouse control tasks such as double-clicking operations (Charness et al., 2004; Murata, 2006). Since the mouse remains the most widely-used input device in organizations and since alternative input devices such as touch screens also require movement (Jochems et al., 2013), older workers may experience substantial difficulties in using ICTs effectively. Thus:

RQ18: Do differences in movement duration between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in movement duration of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

Movement variability refers to a person’s consistency in executing a task (Ketcham and Stelmach, 2004). Older individuals have, generally, more difficulty in producing the same movement repeatedly due to decrements in the motor system (Ketcham and Stelmach, 2004). This deficiency implies for technology-related movements that older people often show a marked lack of precision and accuracy when operating the mouse or other input devices (Jochems et al., 2013). To give an example for motor system variability, when the motor system is quite variable, it is difficult to know whether one may knock over an iPhone in its socket when reaching for it. Since technology-related work requires people to repeatedly perform the same movements in a precise and accurate fashion (e.g., a double-clicking operations using a mouse or dragging and dropping operations), older workers may experience substantially more difficulties than their younger counterparts do in using organizational ICTs effectively. Therefore:

RQ19: Do differences in movement variability between younger and older users explain how and why age negatively impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, how can the deficit in movement variability of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

Force control and regulation are basic elements in movement production since smooth and accurate movements require efficient adjustments of force (Ketcham and Stelmach, 2004). Deficiencies in the regulation of force imply problems in the control of movements, and older adults have less efficient force regulation than younger. For example, studies have demonstrated that older people produce multiple bursts of force, in contrast to young adults who produce a single burst to the targeted force level (Kinoshita and Francis, 1996). This inefficient force regulation of older people makes it more difficult for them to execute movements quickly and accurately, and it has implications for such functional tasks as executing a mouse click. Thus:

If so, how can the deficit in force control and regulation of older users be accommodated through managerial interventions or systems design decisions?

## IT culture

Through a glass, darkly – The role of age in a globalized world

Since the preceding research questions all focus on the immediate need of studying the interdependencies in two workplace trends that are emerging across the world, the graying of the workforce and the persistent evolution of ICTs in organizations, an additional research question must be specified to account for a third trend that is less visible but potentially very important in this context: international migration (OECD, 2013b). As the workforce is aging and modern technologies are becoming ubiquitous, there is a strong need for international migration to maintain and increase productivity levels (OECD, 2012). International migration implies that cultural differences and cultural values gain importance, and these cultural differences and values, which are comprehensive in nature and encompass a broad spectrum of external factors, may interact with age in impacting IS phenomena. For example, there are major dissimilarities across OECD countries in terms of how age differences are perceived, implying that age-related impacts on IS phenomena may be even more pronounced in some countries than in others. To illustrate, whereas the United States celebrates youth and negatively stereotypes older people so that age becomes a salient dimension for negative self-evaluation (Bandura, 1986), Japan shows a high appreciation of senior citizens so that age becomes a salient dimension for positive self-evaluation. Thus, age-related impacts on IS constructs that vary on the basis of self-evaluation may differ across these countries. Consider computer self-efficacy (CSE), which may be one such construct (Compeau and Higgins, 1995; Marakas et al., 1998). Since people adjust their self-efficacy beliefs on the basis of their awareness and subiective experience of their age-related cognitive decline (Bandura. 1994). CSE may decline particularly rapidly with age in cultures that negatively stereotype older people and where people are likely to evaluate themselves ever more negatively as they grow older. This analysis suggests that CSE may decline more rapidly with age in the United States than in Japan, implying that the impact of age on CSE may depend on the cultural context under examination. More generally:

RQ21: Does age interact with cultural values in impacting initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress? If so, which cultural values interact positively as opposed to negatively with age and what managerial interven tions and systems design decisions can be recommended to account for or leverage such interactions?

Additionally, the specific concept of IT culture (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006; Walsh et al., 2010) might be an important mechanism that could explain age-related impacts on IS phenomena. IT culture refers to the values attributed to IT by a group (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006). According to a recent study conducted by Walsh et al. (2010), one prior paper advanced a typology that interrelates age and the values attributed to IT by a group. Specifically, Prensky (2001) differentiated digital natives from digital immigrants. Digital natives have spent their lives surrounded by and using technologies, and they generally use all technologies of the digital era. By contrast, digital immigrants have embraced and adopted many technologies later in their lives. This contrast is based on the premise that, much like personality, culture evolves with age.

Yet, the literature on IT culture is fast evolving such that the validity of the differentiation between digital natives and digital immigrants has recently been called into question (e.g., Kennedy et al., 2010; Prensky, 2009; White and Le Cornu, 2011). Some suggest that age might not be a significant differentiating variable with respect to IT usage when IT culture is taken into account (Walsh et al., 2010). On the one hand, this suggestion implies that researchers examining age, IT culture, and IT usage have to be cautious and that ongoing research on IT culture is needed to confirm or falsify the preliminary results that were obtained from exploratory research. On the other hand, this suggestion implies – consistent with Baron and Kenny (1986) – that the effect of age on IT usage might be indirect and that this effect might, in fact, be fully mediated by IT culture. This interpretation indicates that IT culture could well be an additional touch point of age. Accordingly, we specify the following, additional research question:

RQ22: Do differences in IT culture (e.g., digital natives versus digital immigrants) between younger and older users explain how and why age impacts initial IT adoption, post-adoption, and such related IS phenomena as technostress?

Macro-level outlook: ICTs, aging workforces, and firm performance

The impact of an aging workforce on digital capabilities and practices

There is reason to believe that the study of ICT-enabled (digital) organizational capabilities and practices on the part of an aging workforce could provide interesting insights into the firm performance effects of ICTs. A digital capability is an organization’s focused deployment of ICT-enabled abilities to develop, mobilize, and use organizational resources effectively (e.g., customer relationship management, new product development, and knowledge collaboration) (Daniel et al., 2014; Peppard and Ward, 2004). A digital practice is the effective use of certain affordances that are inscribed in ICTs (Whittington, 2014). Digital capabilities and practices have become increasingly important for organizations to improve organizational agility and responsiveness (Lavie, 2006; Peppard et al., 2014). As a result of the improvements in agility and responsiveness, firms can achieve greater performance and competitive advantage, even sustainable competitive advantage (Lavie, 2006; Peppard and Ward, 2004; Teece, 2007).

In this context, a firm’s digital capabilities and practices are embedded within the firm’s social fabric so that their effectiveness depends on peoples’ interactions with ICTs (Peppard et al., 2014; Peppard and Ward, 2004). More specifically, ICTs, by themselves, have no inherent value; the value of ICTs has to be unlocked manually. This value extraction can only be achieved by employees who use organizational technologies (Peppard and Ward, 2004). Accordingly, for organizations to reap expected performance benefits from their digital capabilities and practices, their ICTs have to be used effectively by their employees (i.e., an effective use process is required) (Peppard and Ward, 2004). This effective use process necessitates that employees have knowledge and skills vis-à-vis organizational ICTs. At the same time, this effective use process entails that employees show behaviors and possess values that foster the effective use and sharing of information (Peppard and Ward, 2004). Only then can business processes be supported effectively and agility as well as responsiveness achieved.

In the case of an aging workforce, the effectiveness of the ICT use process might be reduced given that ICTs are still designed with little systematic regard for older users (Fisk et al., 2009). This less effective ICT use process might result in less effective digital capabilities and practices and, thus, in reduced firm performance impacts of ICTs. To provide an example, knowledge collaboration capabilities and practices have been shown to depend on ICTs for communication purposes (Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak, 2008). Employees collaborate via such technologies as e-mail, instant messages, and video con ferencing. In this case, ICT enables a firm’s knowledge collaboration capabilities and practices, leading to sustained competitive advantage if these capabilities and practices are valuable, heterogeneously distributed across competing firms, and causally ambiguous (Wade and Hulland, 2004). However, consistent with Peppard and Ward (2004), when employees are using ICTs to collaborate, they must engage in an effective use process so as to collaborate effectively. Such effective use involves knowledge and skills requiring various cognitive abilities, personality traits such as neuroticism, extraversion, as well as openness, good sensorimotor abilities, and supportive cultural values (Davenport and Prusak, 2000; Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak, 2008). Since ICTs to date do not systematically account for older employees’ disadvantages regarding the majority of these traits, an aging workforce could counter the sustained competitive advantage arising from the collaborative aspects of the technologies.

Overall, the relationship between ICTs and aging might yield important implications for firm performance at a higher unit of analysis. As long as ICTs are not designed with an aging workforce in mind, digital capabilities and practices might be less effective than desired in terms of yielding organizational agility, responsiveness, and performance improvements. This idea suggests that firms have to institute structures, mechanisms, and programs to counter the potential impacts of an aging workforce on the effectiveness of digital capabilities and practices. To help explore these ideas, we specify the following research questions:

RQ23: How does a firm’s aging workforce impact the firm’s digital capabilities and practices?

RQ24: What structures, mechanisms, and programs can be instituted to counter the potential impacts of an aging workforce on the effectiveness of digital capabilities and practices?

Leveraging ICTs to offset country-wide competitive disadvantages due to cross-country differences in workforce aging

Country-wide studies on ICT usage and aging constitute another promising avenue for IS research. Specifically, if ICTs were systematically designed with older users in mind, they could help offset the potential disadvantages that a country’s aging workforce might entail for the competitive positions of the country’s firms in the global economy. While OECD countries are experiencing rapid population and workforce aging (OECD, 2013a), certain emerging economies such as India or China are aging much less rapidly (Lazear, 2013; Velkoff et al., 2014). These cross-country differences in workforce aging could disadvantage economies with rapidly aging workforces (Bloom et al., 2011). Potential disadvantages include diminished innovation, entrepreneurship, and economic growth (Bloom et al., 2011; Lazear, 2013). Yet, if countries with older demographics managed their aging populations so that older workers remained highly productive members of the workforce, any competitive disadvantage due to aging could be alleviated. It is in this respect that systems design and specialized training initiatives for older users have a crucial role to play in mitigating potential disadvantages of more developed econ omies vis-à-vis emerging ones (Bloom et al., 2011).

For example, the government of the Canadian province Alberta has issued guidelines for the firms under its administration to help them manage their aging workforces more effectively (Government of Alberta, 2006). ## A side note on systems design decisions

We wish to emphasize that our research agenda should not be used to encourage any kind of negative or positive discrimination against older employees but to facilitate interventions that can increase the well-being and productivity of a graying workforce. While the age of employees cannot be changed, design science research could help systems designers create technology artifacts with older workers in mind, particularly if our research agenda were to be supported by future behavioral work. In other words, consistent with Hevner et al. (2004), we encourage a complementary research cycle between behavioral and design science IS research to prepare organizations for effective technology usage in times of a graying workforce For example, technology artifacts could be designed such that they take advantage of older employees’ strong crystallized abilities if our research agenda were to be supported by future behavioral work. Additionally, older workers could be matched with positions that see less rapid technological change and that, hence, require less learning and adaptation with respect to technology (implying that those positions place lower demands on fluid intelligence).

Furthermore, to improve ease of system use for older individuals, systems designers may need to involve them often and early in the design process since there tends to be a large age gap between younger designers and older users (Leikas et al., 2012; Newell et al., 2006). Younger designers often assume a similarity with the user, an assumption that is not accurate for older users. Involving older people early in the design process could help bridge this age gap, ensuring that older people are not overwhelmed by the demands of modern technologies for, for instance, fluid intelligence. For example, systems designers could more often test their systems on populations reflective of the actual users rather than on convenience samples of young adults as commonly done (Leikas et al., 2012; Jeng, 2005). Since older adults tend to travel a lot (Bai et al., 1999), accessing them for usability testing is relatively inconvenient. Hence, older people are underrepresented in software usability testing, implying that software is not designed with them in mind. To improve this situation, systems designers may need to refrain from convenience sampling and involve older adults from early on in the design process (Leikas et al., 2012). Moreover, design science research could propose software engineering methodologies that account for the age gap between younger designers and older users.

## Conclusion

In light of two emerging workplace trends, the graying of the workforce and the proliferation of increasingly modern ICTs in organizations, it is critical to develop a deeper understanding of the role of age in individuals’ interactions with technology. Such knowledge will help managers design more effective intervention strategies, and it could help systems designers in designing systems that are more accessible to diverse user populations. From a theoretical point of view, the study of agerelated impacts on IS phenomena is important because the concept of age can contextualize and bound individual-level IS theories. As such, the concept can help IS research progress toward more detailed and specific explanations of for whom IS theories are applicable. This progress is particularly important since age-related impacts on IS phenomena can be expected to remain relevant in the future, for three main reasons.

First, the workforce will continue to age; the OECD (2011, 2013a) expects the number of young workers to decline over the next several decades, whereas it projects the traditional retirement population to experience a dramatic growth within the same time period, Second, the nature of ICTs will continue to evolve and change at a rapid rate (Benbasat and Zmud 1999) so that older peoples’ mental models of how technology works will continuously be grounded in the past and, therefore, be dated and incongruent with later technological developments. This conclusion holds particularly true since ICT-related training is usually received at a younger age (OECD, 2011), when technologies are very different from those present at an older age. Hence, the dynamic nature of ICT is an important reason for age differences to persist.

Finally, the large age-gap that exists between older users and younger systems designers (Hawthorn, 2007) may continue to exist in the future given that this gap is substantial and will, therefore, likely exist for systematic reasons. For example, systems designers in the United States tend to be very young compared to the rest of the economy, with about 85% of all designers being between 25 and 44 years of age (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2010). By contrast, less than 2% are older than 65, perhaps because older adults’ dated mental models of how technology works make it more difficult for them to design contemporary systems. Together, these trends in technological developments and society indicate that ICTs may continuously be more difficult to use for older than for younger people, with dramatic consequences for the workforce.

Consistent with this notion, recent research indicates that the age difference in technology usage is not a transitory phenomenon but will continue into the future (Hill et al., 2008). Hence, age-related IS research will be relevant for decades to come.

This paper contributes to the literature by making a case for the importance of conducting age-related IS research, by providing a review of age-related IS research on which future work can build, by suggesting that future IS research conceptualizes age-related impacts in terms of ‘‘touch points of age’’ that explain how and why age moderates certain IS relationships, and by discussing pertinent mediating mechanisms related to the concept of ‘‘touch points of age’’ that yield a series of research questions with the potential to further knowledge creation in this area. Thus, we have taken an important first step toward advancing knowledge creation on ICT-use in a graying workforce, and we hope that future IS research will respond to our research agenda.

Responding to the research questions that we identified can help managers understand how and why age matters in the context of an ever more modern organizational ICT infrastructure, and it can help systems designers design systems with pertinent age-related differences in mind. Hence, we hope to enable strategic change management, creating a ‘‘fit’’ between technological characteristics and the graying workforce (Galliers et al., 2012; Walsh, 2014). Overall, our paper addresses how technological responses to changes in demographics may have strategic implications. It deserves mentioning that the ideas advanced here should not be used to encourage discrimination against older employees or to encourage any kind of stereotypes but, rather, to facilitate interventions that can increase the well-being and productivity of a graying workforce. Ultimately, both organizations and their employees such as Benita Oldfellow (our opening vignette) would benefit strongly from managerial interventions and technological developments that offset the weaknesses and leverage the strengths of older employees to improve employee well-being and increase organizational productivity.
