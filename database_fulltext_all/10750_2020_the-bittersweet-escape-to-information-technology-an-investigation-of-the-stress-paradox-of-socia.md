# The bittersweet escape to information technology: An investigation of the stress paradox of social network sites

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technostress Coping with stress IT duality Social network sites IT paradox

## A B S T R A C T

It seems counterintuitive for people to continue using Social network sites (SNSs) despite the stress they cause. This study addresses this issue by suggesting that a stress paradox exists on SNSs, and by developing process and variance theoretical models to explain this new type of IT-duality. It argues that SNS use occurs as a coping strategy that people adopt to deal with life-stress. It also argues that SNSs are stressful, due to more than excessive demands, since SNS use also involves privacy threats. An online survey of 633 Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat users provide support for the study’s main premise.

## 1. Introduction

The last decade has seen a surge in research focusing on the dark side of technology [1]. This signals an interesting turn in information systems (IS) research. IS are no longer solely viewed as helpful tools that in dividuals and organizations can use to reap benefits (e.g., [2]). They are now also viewed as possible causes of numerous harmful outcomes to individuals and societies (e.g. [3–6],).

Stress related to IT use is among these negative outcomes and has emerged as an important issue that needs to be addressed by researchers. IT is now recognized as an important source of stress in people’s lives [7]; it is even considered a stress symbol [8]. Although traditionally linked to work-related IT, stress has more recently been associated with hedonic IT [9]. It appears that everyday IT, such as information and communication technologies, email systems, virtual worlds, and social network sites (SNSs), can also be stressful. Given the rise of computing in everyday life [10,11] and the central role that IT currently plays in people’s personal and social lives [12], research on stress in these emerging settings is more important than ever.

Social network sites are especially interesting in this regard. As venues for information sharing that are generally well integrated into the lives of their users [13], SNSs have usually been viewed as enjoyable platforms that people are fond of using [14–17]. However, it is also clear that SNSs can be stressful because of the constant flow of information that they generate and the social pressure that they exert on users [18]. Yet despite the overwhelming evidence of their stressful effects, people continue using SNSs, a behavior that seems counterintuitive, given the voluntary nature of this use. Indeed, it seems logical to assume that people will stop using a voluntarily adopted IT if their experience with it is disappointing [19].

So far, the only explanation for this somewhat surprising behavior is that many users may see switching from a stressful SNS to an alternative one as more stressful than continuing to use the same platform. That is, users of a stressful SNS may choose not to switch to an alternative SNS if they believe that the strain associated with switching outweighs the strain of continuing to use the incumbent SNS [20]. Although appealing, this explanation fails to account for the continued use of a stressful SNS when it is possible to abandon it without switching to an alternative. Since people tend to seek pleasure and avoid pain in their voluntary activities [21], one would expect them simply to stop using an SNS that they perceive as stressful. Abandoning the SNS should always be the expected outcome, except in cases where the SNS causes more pleasure than pain.

Most research on SNS use has focused on the potential impact of this behavior on the individual. As a result, it has examined SNS use either as a cause of productivity and pleasure, or as a source of discomfort, and it has paid less attention to the non-technological conditions that can cause discomfort in their own right irrespective of the technology, nontechnological conditions that can alter the decisions that individuals make about using SNSs. In today’s highly digitized world, the bound aries between people’s online and offline lives continue to be blurred [11]. This means that the failure to account for the interplay between digital and non-digital user experiences may conceal a significant part of the individual–IT relationship (exp. [22]). Since most research on stress in SNS contexts has focused on technology as the sole source of stress, it has not considered the possibility that non-IT-related stressors may offset the effects of technostress. Yet it is plausible to assume that people use SNSs because of a need to escape and/or reduce everyday stress related to issues such as job security, family welfare, and academic performance. No doubt, using SNSs can be similar to using traditional media in that it can help people relax and recuperate [23], and it may be the case that the interplay between life-stress and technostress involves a new form of IT duality that has so far been under-examined. Therefore, accounting for this IT duality may shed light on the mechanisms un derlying the decision to persist in using an SNS even when this use causes strain.

Following this line of thinking, the present study identifies a stress paradox in SNS contexts. Under the assumption that SNSs are venues where there is both stress and stress relief, the study argues that life stress can lead to SNS use since individuals suffering from it often seek alternative environments that support their well-being or offer a worthwhile escape. It also argues that although SNS use is undoubtedly stressful, social pressure is not the only explanation for this, since SNSs are also plagued by privacy threats. In other words, the case is made that SNSs are paradoxical because they both cause and relieve stress—even if this stress can have different sources. The main objective of the study is to show that SNSs are complex venues where the bright and the dark sides of IT collide, and where users can have a relaxing experience that causes strain at the very same time.

To explain the SNS stress paradox, it is necessary to develop both a process and a variance theoretical model. Some researchers have argued that process and variance approaches must be used separately (e.g., [24, 25]), whereas others have argued that they should be combined (e.g. [26,27]). Be that as it may, almost all researchers agree that each of these approaches provides unique ontological and conceptual insights that add to the theoretical understanding of social phenomena [26–29]. Social phenomena are generally complex and intertwined so that using variance and process theories to clarify them should make it possible to develop a more adequate explanation of them [30–32]. Accordingly, this study begins by presenting a theoretical framework for explaining the Stress–Release–Stress (SRS) process, the goal being to describe how the manifestation of stress and the effort to cope with it unfold over time in the context of SNS use. A variance model is then derived from this theoretical framework to assess an important part of the stress paradox. The model focuses on beliefs about pleasure and pain that coexist in relation to the same SNS and that codetermine the SNS user experience, and it becomes clear that the enjoyment perceived during SNS use because of stress relief tends to diminish over time due to the exhausting effects of this use. This suggests that it is the balance between enjoyment and exhaustion that determines the decision of users to adopt or to continue using an SNS.

As a theoretical underpinning for the explanation of the multifaceted stress experience on SNSs, the present study draws on regulatory focus theory (RFT) and conservation of resources theory (CORT). Both RFT and CORT focus on individual motivation and can be used to explain human attitudes and behavior. Moreover, they are conceptually consistent because they are both anchored in the hedonic principle that people tend to seek pleasure and avoid pain in their voluntary activities, which is a central premise in the present study. The two theories are different to the extent that they focus on different sides of the pleasur e-pain spectrum. but this means that they are also complementary. for combining them in the same theoretical frame can help explain the duality of SNS environments. More specifically. RFT is a theory about the strategies and the appraisals that people use in adverse circum stances to regain and maintain the desirable state referred to as regu latory fit [21]. As such, it is well suited for explaining the enjoyable side of the SNS duality. Conversely, CORT is a theory about the motivation behind people’s efforts to protect their resources and about the reaction of people when their valued resources are depleted or when they perceive that their valued resources are in danger of being depleted [33]. As such, it is well suited for explaining the side of SNS duality involving strains. Together, RFT and CORT can be used to shed light on the paradoxical stress experience. The first can be used to show that digital environments offer a refuge where people can escape from life stress, and the second can be used to highlight the emergence of new stress associated with these environments of refuge.

The present study makes several contributions to IS research. First, it contributes to the literature on IT duality (e.g., R. T [3,34–36]) by exploring the paradoxical duality of a simultaneously positive and negative experience of stress in the same digital environment. Second, it contributes to the study of emotions and coping (e.g., [37,38]) by theorizing IT use as a coping strategy that users adopt when they suffer from life stress and by developing a theoretical framework that in tegrates both the positive and the negative emotional impacts of social technology use. Third, it contributes to the literature on technostress by exploring additional stressors that can drain SNS users. Up until now, research has mainly examined the stressful effects of SNSs in relation to social issues. Using the theoretical framework provided by CORT, the present study extends this work by suggesting that privacy threats are notable stressors in SNS contexts. Finally, by developing both a process model and a variance model to theorize the SNS stress paradox, it im proves understanding of the decision that people make to continue using a given SNS even though their experience with it is less than ideal.

The study is organized as follows. It begins by explaining the concept of stress and by briefly reviewing the IS literature on the topic. Drawing on existing research, it then discusses the duality of IT. Next, it uses RFT and CORT to develop a theoretical framework that integrates life-stress and technostress mechanisms. A research model and its main hypothesis are then presented. This is followed by an examination of the results of an empirical study of Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat users. Finally, the theoretical and practical implications of these results are explored.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The concept of stress

Stress occurs when an imbalance between environmental re quirements and available resources impacts a person’s well-being [39, 40]. It also occurs when environmental factors threaten a person’s valued resources [41], i.e., when a person senses that losing valuable resources is possible in a given environment. As indicated in Fig. 1, stress is generally a transactional process composed of multiple interconnected layers [42]. It starts with stimuli or events, generally referred to as stressors, which are experienced as threatening or over-demanding [43]. Stressors can be related to numerous issues, including family instability, job or financial insecurity, an inadequate social life, and the pressure to perform academically.

When human beings encounter stressors, the latter cause tension, and this leads to upsetting emotional and/or psychological reactions often referred to as strains [44]. Strains vary in type and severity and include harmful responses such as burnout, depression, and exhaustion [45,46]. Strains shape all the outcomes of the stress process and can manifest themselves in numerous ways. Among other things, they can be related to behavior, to job or life satisfaction, and to overall well-being. When they are under stress, individuals often attempt to alter their sit uation by behaving in ways that can help them avoid undesired feelings [47]. Sometimes they seek comfort in alternative environments that they see as nourishing or in ones that allow them to disconnect and escape from the unpleasantness of life.

## 2.1.1. Technostress

Because of the inherent uncertainty and complexity of information technologies, they are often viewed as stress inducers [43,48]. Indeed, the omnipresence of IT in everyday life and the tendency of individuals to assess their IT self-efficacy—particularly when it is associated with workplace demands—are recognized as central causes of stress. More over, IT is often responsible for the frequent interruptions in employees workdays that reduce their productivity and increase their stress levels in the process [49]. Although IT is useful because it can enhance indi vidual performance, it is becoming more and more apparent that IT can invade personal and social life outside of the workplace, and that its ubiquity at the workplace can spark a fear of being replaced that can lead to exhaustion [50].

The concept of technostress tends to be applied to work-related settings; however, its applicability is no longer limited to organiza tional contexts, for it has been suggested that stress also emerges in everyday settings where IT is mostly used voluntarily to perform tasks that are not work-related [9]. For instance, smartphones can induce stress because their use often blurs the line between professional and private life [51,52]. Email systems are another example of stressful IT. Although they offer people flexibility by affording uninterrupted access to an easy means of communicating, they are also cultural symbols of stress because of the constant and often overwhelming flow of infor mation that they facilitate [8].

## 2.1.2. Stress and SNS use

SNSs are typical examples of stress-inducing hedonic IT. Because of their widespread use in contemporary culture, there has been an increasing amount of research on stress in SNS settings in recent years. As indicated in Table 1, this research can be grouped under three main themes related to the way that stress in SNS settings is conceptualized and/or operationalized.

In research focusing on the first theme, stress is conceptualized in terms of stress creators, and the effects of one or multiple stressors (simply referred to as technostress) on relevant dependent variables are examined. Studies using this conceptualization of technostress have examined workplace SNS and found that stress induced by the use of personal SNSs like Facebook reduces work performance [53,57], that this type of stress is associated with addiction [54,56], and that it is negatively associated with happiness [55] and self-esteem [56]. Exces sive use [58], personality traits [57], and the distraction afforded by SNSs [54] are all believed to cause technostress. More recently, research using this conceptualization of technostress has found that stressors lead to addiction and that users sometimes decide to cope with technostress by focusing on less stressful features on the same SNS [59].

In research focusing on the second theme, technostress is conceptu alized in terms of its components: stressors and strains. Although studies using this conceptualization do not focus on organizational contexts, the negative effects of technostress on performance such as academic results are sometimes examined (e.g., [62]). As a behavioral response to SNS stressors, exhaustion is a frequently discussed strain (e.g., [62,18,20]). Sometimes simply referred to as strain (e.g. [61]), exhaustion is gener ally considered to lead to dissatisfaction with SNSs and to the intention to stop using them [18,60]. Yet the thought of switching to an alterna tive SNS has also been found to be stressful, so that it may be the case that it can counterbalance the effects of technostress [20].

Research focusing on the third theme does not center on stress, and it does not examine its components. Instead, stress is studied as one of several indicators of individual well-being. As an emotional response to SNS use (other emotional responses include mood, perceived social support, life satisfaction, depression, and loneliness), stress is viewed as a general perception influenced by factors related to the structure and the size of social networks. For example, it is suggested that the type of communication (composed/targeted), existing ties (weak/strong), and the number of SNS connections (few/many) lead to stronger perceptions of social support [63,64] that then enhance physical and mental welfare, including the perceived level of stress [63,179].

Two key inferences can be drawn from the existing research on stress related to SNS use: 1) Most research on stress in SNS settings has iden tified the misfit between environmental demand and individual capa bilities (i.e., lack of person-environment fit) as the best theoretical lens for uncovering the causes of stress. The main premise is that stress occurs because of an imbalance between environmental requirements and available resources [39,40,50]. This seems to entail that an individual’s appraisal of the social demands on an SNS and of his or her available resources to meet these demands is the unique cause of stress. However, stress can also occur even when valuable resources are unused and when their potential depletion is perceived to be possible [41]. Here, valuable resources can be cherished possessions that people dread losing as well as the cognitive effort and the time that they need to complete a task. For example, the fear of losing personal information—a valuable resource that IT users often strive to protect [65]—is a potentially important stressor (and one that has not been examined in the literature on stress). 2) Prior research has reached mixed conclusions regarding the associa tion between stress and SNS use. Some studies have emphasized that SNSs are stressful because of the continuous requests that they allow (e. g. [18]), whereas others have emphasized that they provide comfort by affording social support that helps people deal with life demands (e.g. [63]). These divergent interpretations underscore the value of the view advanced in the present study that SNS use involves a fundamental paradox. Indeed, it is essential to theorize the association between stress and SNS use as concurrently positive and negative for the same indi vidual and in relation to the same SNS.

## 2.2. The duality of IT

The duality of IT—the fact that IT has both favorable and adverse consequences that can simultaneously enable and hinder its adoption and use—is a long-standing issue in the IS literature (see [34]). There is general agreement that people face trade-offs between the rewards and the drawbacks of using IT. The personalization-privacy calculus, which determines the level of self-disclosure that users should tolerate by referring to their assessments of the costs and benefits of personaliza tion, reflects one such trade-off [66,67]. Although personalization often makes IT use more gratifying, privacy concerns frequently arise as the price of this benefit [68]. Personalization relies on the collection and analysis of personal information, for some users a disconcerting process involving potential breaches of confidentiality [69–71].

Researchers have explored duality in SNS settings, and some have suggested that SNS use leads to both positive and negative experiences. For example, it has been found to enhance life satisfaction and self esteem, but it has also been found to cause addiction and depression [35]. A similar result has been reported for SNS enjoyment. Although the enjoyment experienced by SNS users is a desirable effect because it can lead them to become highly engaged in SNS use, it is also dangerous since it can create problematic dependencies [36]. Information con sumption on SNSs is another important issue, for the desire to learn more can have both positive and negative effects on users. Although this desire can lead users to engage in personal self-enhancement activities, it can also negatively impact their affective and cognitive well-being because of social comparison mechanisms involving envy [3].

Moreover, the duality of IT is sometimes paradoxical. Polar opposi tion being the essence of paradoxical duality, this occurs when the concurrent positive and negative user experiences are related to one and the same idea. The enablers and inhibitors identified in IT duality studies typically reflect two or more conflicting ideas that are qualita tively distinct, such as high engagement and pathological addiction [36], and that coexist in relation to the same IT. However, in the case of a paradoxical duality, the same IT is experienced positively and nega tively in relation to the same issue. In other words, one and the same IT is experienced as “both X and not-X at the same time” (Mick & Fournier [185], p. 125). In line with this notion of paradoxical duality, the present study theorizes SNSs as paradoxical venues that are potentially both stressful and stress relieving at the same time. This comes to light in the interplay between the stress in the digital lives of users and the stress in their real lives.

## 3. Theoretical framework: the stress paradox of SNSs

This section builds on RFT and CORT to investigate the paradoxical duality of the stress experienced in SNS contexts. These two theories provide a conceptual lens for explaining the dynamics of stress and stress relief as experienced on an SNS. First, RFT is used to examine how people determine whether an SNS is an adequate environment for dealing with life stress. The premise is that individuals who are unable to reach regulatory fit in their lives will shift their attention to an alter native environment, where desired states are perceived to be possible. In other words, individuals suffering from stress will tend to seek refuge on an SNS if the latter is believed to be conducive to self-regulation. CORT is then used to examine the causes of the stress that users experience on SNSs. CORT differs from most of the other stress theories used in tech nostress research by the way that it conceptualizes stressors. In addition to the actual depletion of valued resources (the main type of stressor identified in technostress research), CORT suggests that in a given environment, perceived potential threats to cherished possessions can also be key stressors [33]. This unique aspect of CORT is used to conceptualize information privacy threats as additional stressors that affect SNS users.

## 3.1. Regulatory focus theory

RFT is a theory of human motivation that builds on the hedonic principle that people tend to seek pleasure and to avoid pain [21]. RFT distinguishes between two self-regulatory strategies for attaining these objectives: promotion focus and prevention focus. The first strategy has to do with accomplishments and aspirations, and the second one, with safety and protection [72]. When individuals have a promotion focus, they are concerned with higher-level gains such as advancement and well-being, and with whether or not these gains are obtainable. When individuals have a prevention focus, they tend to avoid negative out comes and experiences such as failure or disapproval from others [73, 74].

According to RFT, it is plausible to assume that individuals will decide to use an SNS to cope with life stress if they believe that using it will reinforce their promotion-focus and/or prevention-focus self-regu lation strategies. Beliefs about the promotion-focus benefits of an SNC are related to its capacity to offer fulfilling experiences that offset negative feelings associated with life experiences, whereas beliefs about the prevention-focus benefits of an SNC are related to its capacity to offer an environment worth escaping to. Both types of beliefs can shape the SNS experience of users and help determine their commitment to continue using an SNS.

## 3.2. Conservation of resources theory

CORT states that resources and their potential gain or loss are the main inducers of stress [33]. This resource-oriented view of stress is based on the notion that human beings generally try to obtain new re sources, protect the ones that they have, and increase their quantity. According to CORT, human beings experience stress when their re sources are depleted to the point of becoming insufficient to meet de mands [41]. This is consistent with the person-environment-fit perspective on stress that has been dominant in technostress research for many years. However, CORT also suggests that stress occurs even when resources have not been depleted or completely used up, if their depletion is perceived as at least possible. In other words, the potential loss of valued resources such as time, money, and cherished belongings causes stress even if this loss never materializes. Hobfoll [33] refers to stress that is related to potential (not yet actualized) loss as anticipatory stress.

In line with CORT, it is plausible to assume that SNS users who find their continuous interactions on an SNS taxing will experience the latter as stressful. Moreover, since any perceived resource vulnerabilities will result in stress, the potential loss of valued online possessions, such as personal data and a digital identity, are also likely to result in stress even if this anticipated loss never actually occurs. Accordingly, the persis tence of actual and anticipatory stressors should be exhausting for SNS users [33,41], and it should shape their SNS experience.

The phenomenon of SNS user stress is complex and even paradoxical (see [75]) because SNSs are stress inducers and stress relievers at the very same time. The SRS process presented in Fig. 2 clarifies this central issue by providing a theoretical framework for explaining both life stress and technostress in terms of two coexisting mechanisms. The first mechanism underlies SNS use to the extent that it is an outcome of everyday stress. The second one underlies strain to the extent that is an outcome of SNS use. RFT will be used to examine the first mechanism, and CORT will be used to examine the second one.

## 3.3. The stress–Release–Stress proces

The SRS process is related to life imbalances that have been caused by stressors. It is triggered when individuals are strained by everyday life stimuli or events that they perceive as threatening or overdemanding [39,40]. The strain can be connected to numerous issues, including job constraints, financial insecurity, social burdens, and the pressure to perform academically. Under strain, individuals usually try to alter their situation by behaving in ways that will help them deal with the undesired feelings associated with it [47,76]. One way that they can do so is to attempt to come to terms with the source of the strain by accepting or modifying it through planning, strategizing, and/or seeking support [77]. Alternatively, they can attempt to avoid the source of the strain altogether. In the present study, the focus is on avoidance behavior, for it is a question of strained individuals who choose to engage in activities to distract themselves instead of addressing stressors and the imbalances that they cause in their lives [78]. This behavior typically involves seeking the comfort of alternative environments that the strained individuals consider nourishing or that allow them to disconnect and unwind.

It is clear that SNSs can provide this type of alternative environment. When they do, their users move into the second stage of the SRS process, a stage that highlights the bright side of SNS use (Fig. 2). This use be comes a coping strategy adopted to deal with life stress. Stressed in dividuals come to believe that contrary to their offline environment, the SNS is a social space that fits with their promotion-focus or preventionfocus self-regulation strategy. Beliefs about the fit between an SNS and a prevention-focus self-regulation strategy emphasize the capacity of the SNS to offer an environment worth escaping to; beliefs about the fit between an SNS and a promotion-focus self-regulation strategy emphasize the capacity of an SNS to promote fulfilling experiences.

Thus, as strained individuals adopt and use an SNS, they continuously assess (either consciously or unconsciously) its potential to help them achieve self-regulation. They make a first assessment after an initial series of interactions, and they continue to make assessments when they are under stress and considering alternative environments. The beliefs that they form about the possibility of satisfying the conditions for promotion-focus and prevention-focus self-regulation explain the psy chologically satisfying state of flow [79] that they experience while using an SNS. It is these beliefs that shape their decision to use or to continue using the SNS.

Believing that an SNS is capable of offsetting the negative feelings that they experience in their non-digital lives, users sometimes later discover that this alternative digital environment is stressful in its own right (the dark side of SNS use shown in Fig. 2). This involves resource conservation appraisals such as the assessment of the demands created by SNS use and the assessment of potential threats that SNS use poses to valued possessions. This means that the stress paradox manifests itself after individuals adopt an SNS and start making sense of its features and affordances, and after they become aware of the values promoted by these features and affordances [80]. This is because people often attempt to “re/create” themselves while they are using social technologies [81] and because, when they are under stress, their psychological ownership of a personal digital space such as an SNS profile seems to provide them with a soothing environment in which to seek respite [82]. However, an enjoyable SNS that seems to provide stress relief can slowly become stressful itself because of the human need to protect possessions, to in crease their value, and to acquire new ones.

In a word, SNSs are stress relievers and stress inducers at the very same time. They relieve stress to the extent that using them is a coping strategy for people suffering from stress, one that puts them in a desired state of well-being and/or simply allows them to escape from a difficult reality. SNSs are stress inducers to the extent that they create excessive social demands and to the extent that they expose their users to threats to valued resources. Users’ beliefs about the potential of SNSs to relieve stress, or to cause strain, shape the pleasure that they derive from using them and determine their decision to continue or to stop doing so.

## 4. Theoretical model

The SRS process described earlier underlies the dynamic perfor mance of self-regulation by strained individuals who use SNSs in a coping strategy that involves resource protection appraisals. Earlier, we saw that this self-regulation coping behavior determines the paradoxical SNS experience of these individuals. However, the examination of the SRS process did not explicitly identify the key factors that influence this determination of their paradoxical SNS experience. To develop a more adequate explanation of the stress paradox on SNSs—one that pinpoints these key factors and establishes their interrelationships—it is necessary to create a variance model using RFT and CORT (see Fig. 3). Integrating the coping strategies for life stress and for technostress, this model will provide a cross-sectional snapshot of the SRS process.

This variance model consists of a group of relevant constructs that represent the two self-regulation appraisals (prevention and promotion) identified by RFT, the two conservation of resources appraisals (actual and anticipated) identified by CORT, and the outcomes of both sets of appraisals in terms of the strain and flow that are generated. Table 2 defines the constructs in this variance model and shows their link to the SRS process. These constructs and the interrelationships between them offer a sufficient explanatory framework for the SRS process as well as for the desired and undesired outcomes of the mechanisms that it en tails. More specifically, the model describes SNS use as a strategy for coping with life stress. This corresponds to the idea that the degree to which users believe an SNS supports their self-regulation determines the degree to which they enjoy the platform. This includes their believing that the SNS is a viable venue that they can visit in order to escape from life stress as well as their believing that it is a venue that favors their well-being. In line with RFT, escaping from life stress and enhancing well-being by using SNSs are seen as typical representations of prevention-focus and promotion-focus beliefs. However, the model also describes SNSs as sources of stress. It is clear that technostress stems from the depletion of a user’s valuable resources, but CORT goes further, suggesting that it is also likely to occur even when the depletion of these resources is merely perceived as possible. This translates into two possible sources of stress: SNS overload and privacy threats, both of which can lead to exhaustion. In line with CORT, the model represents SNS overload and privacy threats as typical representations of resource depletion and anticipated resource loss.

Although the SRS framework describes a process, the derived theo retical model is a variance model. There are three important reasons for this: 1) Contrary to the SRS process model, which describes entities and events that unfold over time [27,86], the variance model presented in Fig. 3 describes the properties of entities at a cross-sectional point in time. These properties have fixed meanings that do not change over time, and only their values are expected to vary (Mohr, 1982; [26]). 2) The relationships described in the model are constant and unidirectional (e.g., if perceptions of privacy threats increase, then SNS exhaustion increases). 3) The temporal order of the different variables in the model is immaterial ([26], p. 667; [87], pp. 32–33) because a continuity of effects in the underlying relationships is assumed (e.g., at any point in time, the perceived levels of enjoyment and exhaustion determine the intention to continue using an SNS). The model and the details of the relationships that it entails are explained in the following sections.

## 4.1. SNSs as stress relievers

When people are under stress, they seek temporary refuge in envi ronments that offer significant opportunities for mental escapism and/or in ones that offer significant opportunities to obtain comfort and a sense of well-being. People tend to enjoy being in these two types of envi ronments because they are generally conducive to self-regulation. As a result, people are intrinsically motivated to visit them frequently. These two types of environments provide essential stress relief connected to the two forms of self-regulation that RFT identifies as the fundamental determinants of human behavior: prevention focus and promotion focus.

## 4.1.1. Prevention focus self-regulation: escape

The Oxford Dictionary defines escapism as the “tendency to seek distraction and relief from unpleasant realities, especially by seeking entertainment or engaging in fantasy.” Escapism is one of the possible ways for people to deal with the strains in their daily lives and the negative feelings that they cause. It is a typical form of prevention-focus self-regulation, for it involves attempting to avoid the unpleasant as pects of daily life [21]. To protect themselves from the pain and discomfort of a stressful life, people turn to activities that help them escape from reality and/or to ones that help them engage in fantasy.

Escapism is a coping mechanism that people use to relieve emotional distress. A typical response of individuals suffering from life stress, it involves repressing negative thoughts [76,88] by shifting the focus away from oneself and directing attention to the execution of concrete actions [74]. This shift in focus is referred to as cognitive narrowing, a psy chological state characterized by a decrease in self-evaluation [89,90]. There are many ways to achieve this state, including the use of IT. Indeed, many people find that engaging in IT-based activities such as playing video games is an enjoyable way to escape from life problems [91,92]. SNSs offer an environment where people can avoid everyday tensions and negative self-evaluations by temporarily directing their attention away from life stressors [9], and this makes SNSs fun to use. Escaping from unfavorable realities sometimes leads individuals to engage in harmful activities (e.g. [89]), but this is not always the case. Health-promoting activities such as sports and reading are fulfilling pursuits that can help people reduce excessive self-awareness and find positive escape routes [93,94].

Escape is conceptualized in the present study as the perceived po tential of an SNS to offer a sheltering environment. In this conceptual ization, escape is directly related to the need to “avoid thinking about real life problems” (Z. [92] p. 324) and to the need to engage in fantasy by activating the ability of the human imagination to build new worlds [95]. This conceptualization highlights the perception of SNSs as envi ronments where it is possible to escape from an undesirable reality (“From Reality” in Fig. 3) or as ones where it is possible to escape to fantasy (“To Fantasy” in Fig. 3). Because escapist activities are generally absorbing, they tend to reduce the level of self-evaluation, and this helps individuals enjoy a psychologically satisfying state of flow [79]. This means that an SNS perceived as a viable environment for escape is more likely to be perceived as enjoyable.

## H1a. Escape positively influences SNS Enjoyment.

## 4.1.2. Promotion focus self-regulation: SNS well-being

Prevention-focus self-regulation centers on evasion, whereas promotion-focus self-regulation centers on accomplishments [21], which means that it is based on the anticipation that an activity will offer a favorable experience involving an enhanced version of oneself [74]. This is particularly relevant to people suffering from stress. When their environments create pressure and impose excessive demands, people seek alternative ones that are more conducive to self-realization. Selfrealization and growth are innate psychological needs and the basis of intrinsic motivation and well-being [96]. When satisfied, the three following needs are the key elements in intrinsic motivation that facil itate psychological well-being: 1) competence, the need to feel capable and in control: 2) autonomy. the need to exercise free will and volition: and 3) relatedness, the need to feel connected to and accepted by others [83,84].

It follows that people whose daily lives are stressful will seek refuge in environments where they can experience competence, autonomy, and connectedness. Moreover, the belief that these three psychological needs will be satisfied in an environment has an impact on the overall wellbeing of people in such an environment. This means that an SNS will become an enjoyable environment and a viable means of dealing with life stress for users who believe that it satisfies these three needs. Since there is inherent enjoyment in any activity associated with the satis faction of these three needs and in the positive experiences connected to this satisfaction [97,98], the latter is a major intrinsic driving force that shapes the SNS user experience. The sense of well-being and the asso ciated feelings of self-enhancement (i.e., the impression that one is competent, autonomous, and connected while using a given SNS and the satisfaction that this entails) create a state of flow [79] in users engaged in SNS activities, and this makes their SNS experience more enjovable.

## H1b. SNS Well-being positively influences SNS Enjoyment.

## 4.2. SNSs as stress inducers

Stress occurs when environmental stimuli (stressors) induce tension that culminates in strains [42]. Exhaustion is a particularly relevant strain in SNS contexts. The idea that stressors can lead to exhaustion is not new. Indeed, past research has shown that exhaustion is an impor tant issue for professionals in organizational contexts (e.g. [99–101],). Moreover, hedonic IT in general—and SNSs in particular—have been identified as sources of stress that can lead to exhaustion ([8] [18];). Conceptualized using general exhaustion constructs (e.g. [50,100],), SNS exhaustion refers to the feeling of being tired and mentally drained as a result of engaging in SNS activities [18]. More specifically, it refer to a “psychological reaction to stressors caused by using [an] SNS” ([20], p. 282). CORT suggests that there are two types of stressors that can cause SNS exhaustion: the actual depletion of resources and the anticipated loss of resources.

## 4.2.1. Depletion of resources: SNS overload

Creating and maintaining relationships with others [102] and disclosing personal information [67] are a central part of SNS use. The private and public messages that SNS users share with their contacts vary in content, in feedback requirements, and in response expectation [67]. Some messages are shared with a direct expectation of response, while others are not. SNS users have to monitor and assess transmitted messages continuously because the latter contain social requests that are difficult to ignore and that require immediate attention. Anticipated benefits and anticipated reciprocity are significant aspects of this experience, for they provide important incentives to remain constantly engaged with a social network. Anticipating reciprocity means believing that if you are active in your social network, then other users are likely to behave toward you in a similar fashion when an opportunity to do so presents itself. This aligns with Social Exchange Theory, which posits that social exchanges are voluntary transfers of resources between two or more actors [103]. In such exchanges, individuals offer reciprocal benefits to those from whom they have previously received similar benefits [104]. In line with this theory, SNS users invest significant portions of their personal resources reacting to the SNS posts and mes sages of other users because they expect these users to behave similarly with respect to their own messages. However, these interactions can become too demanding if they continuously consume users’ online and offline time and resources. In other words, they can lead to SNS overload.

While social contact is a significant component of SNS use, contin uously engaging with other SNS users and constantly providing them with feedback can require a great deal of time and effort (e.g., [105]), and it can lead to stressful overload (e.g. [18],). SNS overload is conceptualized in the present study as a user’s perception that there are an excessive number of social requests on a given SNS. The concept refers to situations where users believe that they are receiving and answering too many social support requests and that this is taking up too much of their SNS time. It is important to note that the stress associated with this overload can extend beyond the SNS and impact the personal life of users. The overload brings about a depletion of the time and en ergy of SNS users, a depletion that they assess both with respect to the excessive number of SNS requests that they receive and answer (“Excessive Demand” in Fig. 3) (e.g., [18]) and with respect to the intrusion of the SNS into their personal lives (“Invasion” in Fig. 3) (e.g., [106]). Both of these assessments are related to the overload experi enced by SNS users, but the first one focuses on the way that this overload depletes their online SNS time, whereas the second one focuses on the way that it impacts their offline life beyond the boundaries of the SNS environment.

SNSs are now accessible not only on computers and laptops but also on mobile devices and tablets. The ubiquity of these social platforms has enabled users to remain continually connected to them. As a result of this hyper-connectivity, SNS users can become trapped in an environ ment where they are always expected to be available to respond to incoming messages and where their personal space is constantly under surveillance [106]. Given the amount of time required to manage the endless streams of communication afforded by SNSs, users can have the impression that omnipresent technology is invading their lives, and this can be a significant source of stress (e.g. [107],). Moreover, SNS over load is likely to increase over time because users’ social networks tend to grow in size [108] and requests for support from other users tend to become more numerous [67,109]. As SNS overload increases, it depletes the resources that users have available for online social interaction and brings users closer and closer to exhaustion.

SNSs are useful because they facilitate human interaction and connection, but they are also stressful because they exert social pressure on users through their ubiquity. SNS users receive incessant requests for social support from other network users [110], and they often come to see both the technology itself and the requests from other users as excessive and invasive. This negative experience occurs because user develop the belief that they have a duty to respond to social support requests, a belief that forces them to be continually prepared, not only to receive requests, but also to respond to them promptly [18]. The per petual exposure to these requests causes users to feel that their personal time and space have been depleted by the SNS [43], and their energy is drained in the constant battle to cope with the SNS and its ubiquitous communications demands. In other words, SNS overload—a situation where a depletion of online and offline resources makes it difficult or impossible to meet requests from other users—induces a significant amount of stress and can lead to exhaustion [41].

## 4.2.2. Anticipated loss of resources: privacy threats

In addition to the straining effects of the resource depletion that occurs with SNS overload, CORT also suggests that stress can occur in situations where resources are not yet depleted but where their deple tion is perceived as possible. That is, the risk associated with the potential loss of valued resources can lead to what is known as antici patory stress, a form of stress that does not depend on the actual occurrence of resource depletion [33]. Instead the emphasis is on the potential threat to valued resources and on the related negative out comes that may be possible if this threat materializes. While people are using SNSs, they continuously generate significant amounts of infor mation either intentionally or unintentionally, information that they perceive as a digital self-extension [111] and that they claim ownership over (e.g. [112].). This digital self-extension is intimately linked to the identity of SNS users as individuals and constitutes a set of valuable resources that they strive to protect. Users find any potential loss of informational resources extremely disconcerting because they are very sensitive to the need to control access to what they consider private [113]. Accordingly, SNS privacy threats that put users’ personal infor mation at risk can lead to high levels of stress. This type of stress is related to perceptions of potential threats to valued possessions. In other words, although these threats usually represent real possibilities of loss, they do not have to materialize in order to cause stress.

Protecting privacy means protecting against intrusions into one’s life and against the unwanted gathering of information [114]. Privacy threats are a key issue in contemporary society because of the omni presence of social technologies with the capability to track numerous aspects of people’s lives ([11,65]; [115] [71,116];). When social tech nology users believe their online activities expose them to sophisticated data collection, processing, and dissemination strategies and techniques that threaten their personal information [117], this can have a signifi cant impact on their attitudes and behavior [118,119]. It is important to note that SNS users almost always expect access to their personal in formation to be allowed to some and denied to others. That is, they expect to be able to choose, not only what personal information can be shared, but also with whom it can be shared [120]. This means that users’ perceptions of privacy threats are related to their fear that ex pected configurations are either not fully respected or completely breached [65,121].

Privacy threats are conceptualized in the present study as the perceived potential loss associated with the disclosure of personal in formation on an SNS. In this conceptualization, privacy threats refer to situations where informational resources are perceived to be endan gered during SNS use. These threats usually have to do with concerns that SNS users have about the possibility that there may be unlawful access to their personal information (“Privacy Concern” in Fig. 3), and with concerns that they have about the possibility that they run the risk of exposing their personal information to unauthorized exploitation (“Privacy Risk” in Fig. 3). Privacy concerns and privacy risks are perceived threats that have been shown to have a negative impact on user engagement with IT (e.g., [66,85]). Users clearly perceive these two types of privacy threats on SNSs. They feel apprehension about unau thorized access to their personal information, and they fear potential harm related to the disclosure of their personal information [120]. If users perceive that there are privacy risks associated with disclosing their personal information on SNSs, and if they are concerned that their SNS use may make it possible for others to exploit their personal in formation, then they necessarily believe that privacy threats exist on SNSs. Moreover, it seems clear that over time, these constant worries and apprehensions about privacy threats will become draining.

According to CORT, the threat of losing resources can act as a stressor even if such loss never actually occurs [33]. In other words, if users perceive that their privacy expectations may not be met, that their personal information may be stolen, or that there may be unauthorized access to it, this perception can cause them to feel a significant amount of stress even if these privacy threats never materialize. In addition, it is likely that the persistence of nagging doubts about whether they are sufficiently protected from these threats will have a draining effect on SNS users and that this will deplete their emotional resources and lead to exhaustion.

H2b. Privacy Threats positively influence SNS Exhaustion.

## 4.3. Strain versus flow

The hedonic principle states that people tend to pursue pleasure and to avoid pain [21]. Accordingly, it is normal for individuals to adopt and use an SNS that they perceive as enjoyable and to avoid an SNS that they perceive as exhausting. As discussed earlier, the construct Exhaustion refers to a psychological reaction characterized by acute fatigue and mental tiredness, and the construct Enjoyment refers to “the extent to which using an IS is perceived to be enjoyable, apart from any perfor mance consequences” [122,123]. This second construct conceptualizes the pleasure that users feel during an IT-interaction, a pleasure that is a positive emotional state that can become an anticipated psychological reward motivating subsequent IT-use decisions [97,124–128].

From a conceptual standpoint, enjoyment and exhaustion are not mere polar opposites of the same idea. Enjoyment is not the flip side of exhaustion, and these two emotional states can coexist in the same theoretical frame. Enjoyment and exhaustion are similar to other con flicting constructs, such as adoption and avoidance [31] and acceptance and resistance [129], that have been examined in the IS literature. They have different sets of predictors and potentially different outcomes, so that they do not necessarily stand in a fully negative correlation. For example, playing basketball can be a fun activity, particularly in a competitive yet nourishing environment. However, after playing basketball for several hours under the hot sun, exhaustion can begin to set in, rendering the game less enjoyable without necessarily completely eliminating the fun involved. The basketball players may continue to enjoy the game even though their fatigue diminishes the pleasure that they derive from this sporting activity. It is the balance between enjoyment and exhaustion that determines whether they continue playing or stop and go home.

Similarly, SNS exhaustion and SNS enjoyment lie at the opposite ends of the pleasure–pain spectrum, but each of them is a unique state that does not have to stand in a fully negative correlation to the other. As a taxing psychological reaction to stressors, SNS exhaustion can be the end result of the accrued fatigue of SNS users [18]. It occurs when users are completely drained because of experiences that make an SNS diffi cult to enjoy. Since exhaustion often leads people to change their behavior and their environment (e.g. [101]) in an attempt to recover their energy [47], it is likely that to cope with the exhausting stress caused by an SNS, people will choose to stop using it [37,20,43]. This means that users who experience an SNS as enjoyable will frequently seek to actualize this hedonic reward and will be likely to continue using the SNS [130], but that those who experience an SNS as exhausting will not find it very enjoyable and will be likely to attempt to stop using it.

## 5. Research method

## 5.1. Data collection and study sample

Because of their enormous market share, Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat were chosen as the main SNSs to assess the model. Three surveys for Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat users were designed in accordance with guidelines recommended in the literature [131], and distributed to undergraduate students at a large North American busi ness school. This sampling choice was made because all three SNSs have a vast diffusion among university students in North America, and because this user group constitutes a significant portion of their user populations [108,132]. Furthermore, students constitute appropriate sample subjects for social media studies in general because they repre sent the typical user population of SNSs based on age and gender [108]. As digital natives their behavior in regard to technology is known to differ significantly from that of digital immigrants [133–136], so that prior studies have repeatedly used student samples to test their hy pothesis in SNS contexts (e.g. [67,132,137]). They are suitable subjects for this study in particular since students are known to sometimes suffer from life-stress, because of financial, academic, or even self-imposed stressors [138]. As such, the decision to use a student sample is sup ported by proposed guidelines [139], suggesting generalization based on student samples to be possible when the intended population is clearly identified and when the rationale for the use of students as re spondents is provided.

The effects of several relevant control variables were assessed in this study. Consequently, the influence of habit on continuous use intention [141,142] was controlled for. ## 5.3. Data analysis

Partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using SmartPLS version 3.2.4 [144]. SEM is the method of choice to analyze path diagrams involving latent variables with multiple indicators [145]. such as the one examined in this study. PLS-SEM and covariance-based SEM (CBSEM) are the two most widely used SEM approaches in MIS research (Chin et al., 2008; [146]). Although PLS-SEM has been sometimes criticized, this criticism has not been related to the rigor nor to the accuracy of the method [147]. It has rather been related to method misuse, lack of justification, and/or inadequate reporting [148]. In fact, if appropriately used, and if used when it is appropriate, PLS-SEM can be the better choice for SEM analysis (Joe F [147–149].). Hence, the choice of SEM approach should always be explicitly discussed and key indices should be reported on and explained [145].

In this study PLS-SEM was chosen for two reasons: 1) model complexity and 2) use of formative constructs. More specifically, as the research model presented in Fig. 3 contains 12 first-order constructs, 4 sorder constructs, and 5 control variables, it is considered to be a highly complex model [147]. CBSEM is known to be ineffective in such contexts because of model identification issues ([150,151]; Reinartz et al., 2009). Similarly, formative constructs under CBSEM are subject to identifica tion problems (see [152],) and to consistency problems (see [153]) that together negate the advantages of measurement error modeling [154, 155]. However, since PLS-SEM relies on weighted composites rather than on factors, it is more suitable for analyzing formative measurement even if it does not allow for measurement error modeling [145,156]. For these reasons, PLS-SEM was deemed most appropriate to analyze the model in this study. To do so, the two-step approach recommended by Hair et al. [157], and the reporting guidelines suggested by Gefen et al. [145] were followed.

## 5.3.1. Measurement model

Three models were assessed, each related to one of the three SNS examined. All Cronbach’s alpha values were superior to the recom mended 0.7 minimum, which suggests that all constructs were reliably measured [158]. As recommended by Fornell & Larcker [159], convergent validity of the reflective constructs was assessed using three criteria: 1) indicator loadings; 2) construct reliabilities; and 3) average variance extracted. As can be seen in Table 4 (and in Tables 8–10 for the second-order reflective constructs), all the items had loading values higher than the 0.70 benchmark. Moreover, the composite reliability of all the reflective constructs was between 0.81 and 0.97, and the AVE values for each construct ranged between 0.59 and 0.90. This exceeds the variance of the measurement error and suggests that the measures satisfy the convergent validity criterion. In order to further assess val idity, a cross-loadings table was also assessed. As shown in Table A1 in the Appendix A, each item loading is much higher on its assigned construct than on the other constructs, supporting adequate convergent and discriminant validity.

Discriminant validity was assessed using the square root of the AVE of each construct and the correlation between the constructs [150]. In Table 5–7, all the AVE values are greater than the off-diagonal elements, which demonstrate discriminant validity. However, the sign of the correlation between the two dimensions of SNS overload and SNS enjoyment was found to be intriguing. Although this correlation was small (<.25), it was nevertheless positive, whereas one would expect excessive demand and invasion to negatively correlate with enjoyment. This issue is further analyzed in the post hoc analysis section of this manuscript.

Before estimating the model, a multicollinearity test was conducted. It yielded VIF (variance inflation factor) scores that were all lower than 1.62. This is significantly lower than the generally used threshold of 10 [160] and also lower than the more conservative threshold of 3.3 [161], suggesting that multicollinearity was not an issue. Common method bias was assessed by adding a first-order latent common method factor to the measurement model with the items of all first-order constructs used as indicators of the method factor [131]. The model was then assessed using CBSEM via AMOS (V.23). The fit indices of the model with the method factor did not significantly change from those of the model not including a method factor. Moreover, factor loadings of all constructs remained strong and significant despite the inclusion of the common method effects (Table A2 in the Appendix A), suggesting that method bias is unlikely to have affected the study results [162,163].

To further evaluate the formative second-order SNS well-being construct, multicollinearity among the three dimensions and the sig nificance level of each dimension were assessed [164]. As can be seen in Tables 8–10, all VIF scores were below the recommended upper limit of 3.3, and all the weight values were significant, suggesting that the three dimensions contribute significantly to the formation of the construct.

These results support the premise of the present study that SNSs are venues where a stress paradox exists. On the one hand, they show that SNSs are stress-relieving technologies that users experience as venues supportive of their well-being and/or as spaces worth escaping to, and that the more users experience SNSs as conducive to their self regulations, the more likely they are to find them enjoyable. On the other hand, they show that SNSs are straining, and that the more users experience SNSs as stressful, the more likely they are to become exhausted by using them. This suggests that it is the balance between enjoyment and exhaustion that determines whether or not people continue to use an SNS. However, these results also suggest that not all SNSs are experienced similarly, and that pleasure and strain associated with SNS use may differ across SNSs. As such, the next section reports on the results of a post hoc comparative analysis conducted on the three SNSs in order to gain a deeper understanding of users’ dual experience with SNS use.

## 5.3.3. Post hoc analysis

Two post hoc analyses were conducted. The first aimed at investi gating the sign of the correlation between the two dimensions of SNS overload, i.e., excessive demand and invasion, and SNS enjoyment. As can be seen in Tables 5–7, although the correlation values between the two dimensions and SNS enjoyment were small (<.25), the sign of these correlations were surprisingly positive. This appears to be counterintuitive, as one would expect constructs that reflect stressors to be negatively associated with enjoyment. For this reason, the direct effects of SNS overload, excessive demand, and invasion on SNS enjoyment were assessed. In addition, a mediation analysis was conducted to assess the indirect effect of SNS overload on SNS enjoyment through SNS exhaustion. To do so, a bootstrapping test was conducted with a boot strap subsample of 1000. The results, presented in Table 12, show that the direct effects of SNS overload, excessive demand, and invasion on SNS enjoyment were all not statistically significant. Furthermore, the indirect effect of SNS overload on SNS enjoyment through SNS exhaustion was negative and statistically significant (Facebook: -0.111, $\mathbf { p } < . 0 0 1 ;$ ; Instagram: -0.107, p < .001; Snapchat: -0.134, p < .001). This suggests that SNS exhaustion had a full mediating role in the relation ship between SNS overload and enjoyment, and that SNS overload (and its two dimensions) is negatively associated (yet indirectly) with SNS enjoyment as one would expect.

A second post hoc analysis was conducted to investigate the differ ences between the three SNSs. Although this study does not theorize these potential differences, it is nevertheless expected that the stress paradox might not be experienced similarly on the three SNSs, since they each have unique features and affordances, and may project different values to users [80]. For this reason, a multi-group analysis was con ducted to determine the differences in the path coefficients across SNSs for the hypothesized relationships. Three pairwise comparisons of strength of path coefficients between SNSs (Facebook vs. Instagram, Facebook vs. Snapchat, and Instagram vs. Snapchat) were conducted using PLS-MGA procedure [151,166]. As can be seen in Table 13, the differences in path coefficients between Facebook and Instagram models were all not significant. However, one path differed significantly when Snapchat was compared with Facebook or with Instagram. More spe cifically, the effect of Escape on SNS Enjoyment was significantly different (stronger) in the Facebook and Instagram models compared to that of Snapchat. That is, although Escape plays a significant role on SNS Enjoyment for the three SNSs, this role is less influential in the case of Snapchat. This is not surprising giving the nature of use of the SNS. In fact, Snapchat is mainly used as a communication platform where messages can be exchanged easily across an existing network. Although Snapchat can still afford its users the possibility to escape and to unwind, this characteristic may not be as strong for this SNS as it is for the other two.

To further investigate the differences between the three SNSs, average enjoyment and exhaustion levels for Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat were compared using ANOVA (Tukey’s HSD and Scheffe). As can be seen in Figs. A1 and $\mathrm { A 2 } ,$ as well as in Table A3 of the Appendix A, Facebook was found to be significantly less enjoyable than both Insta gram and Snapchat (no significant difference in enjoyment was found between Instagram and Snapchat). The fact that Facebook was the least enjoyable of the SNSs is due to its capacity (or lack thereof) to provide an escape worthy environment or an environment that is conducive to users’ well-being. Indeed, we have recently witnessed a surge in fake news, cyber warfare, and online extremist propaganda taking place on Facebook, an issue that has been widely publicized [167]. Among other things, this new reality surely impacts the perceived escape potential of this SNS in the eyes of its users, and it decreases the chances of Facebook being perceived as an environment where one can find well-being.

Moreover, Instagram was found to be significantly less exhausting than each of Facebook and Snapchat (no significant difference in exhaustion existed between Facebook and Snapchat). This is due to the fact that Instagram exerts the least strain on its users, in terms of overload and privacy threats. Instagram is primarily a photo and video sharing SNS that allows users to add artistic filters to their posts and to organize them using tags and location information. Given the artistic inclination of Instagram posts, users are less likely to perceive threats to their privacy to be as significant of an issue on this SNS as they may do with Facebook and Snapchat. Also, given the goals of this SNS and the type of infor mation that is shared by its users, Instagram is less likely to be perceived as over-demanding since the urgency embedded in the exchanged posts is less likely to be elevated.

## 6. Discussion

During the last few decades, the way that people experience and interact with IT has radically changed [133,136]. As a result, traditional research perspectives focusing on users’ apprehensive predispositions toward technology may no longer be up to date. Today people live in and through IT [11], and this makes the individual–IT relationship much more complex than it used to be (e.g. [15,168],). For instance, when people use and appropriate everyday technologies, they create a digital version of themselves [111] that leads to feelings of digital ownership [112] that can be both comforting and disturbing. In particular, the digital footprint that people generate while they are using an SNS slowly develops into a set of virtual spaces that reflect who they are and what they perceive as their own. Over time, caring for these virtual posses sions becomes a source of joy and also of stress.

SNSs are widely used technologies that are well integrated into people’s lives [13]. However, although their use is mostly voluntary, they are frequently seen as sources and symbols of stress because of the constantly increasing flow of information that they generate and because of the social pressure that they exert on users. Previous research on stress in SNS contexts (e.g. [20],) has shed light on the stress expe rienced by users when they consider switching SNSs, but it has not provided a comprehensive picture of stress on these platforms, nor has it explained why individuals continue using an SNS that they perceive as stressful instead of simply abandoning it. This is the main reason for closely examining the SRS process highlighted in the present study. This process is an emerging phenomenon that deserves much more attention from researchers. The present study takes into account the contradictory experience of stress on SNSs and argues that users are likely to abandon an SNS because of its stress-inducing effects, unless these effects are outweighed by the stress-relieving potential of the SNS in their lives. In other words, the present study shows, in line with the hedonic principle that people tend to seek pleasure and to avoid pain, that SNS use is likely to continue if the stress relief that an SNS offers is greater than the stress that it causes.

Another key point emphasized in the present study is that people often choose to use an SNS in order to forget and/or escape life stress and in order to relax and recuperate. To support this position, RFT and the concepts of promotion-focus self-regulation and prevention-focus self regulation [21,72] have been used to highlight relevant beliefs that people have about SNSs. As a form of promotion-focus self-regulation, SNS use revolves around viewing an SNS as offering a viable means of improving one's well-being. As a form of prevention-focus self-- regulation, SNS use revolves around viewing an SNS as a safe escape if one wants to avoid the stresses of life. Together, these two beliefs about SNSs shape the hedonic experience of using them. Highlighting these relevant beliefs contributes to the IS literature by pointing to a new way to investigate the hedonic experience of IT use and by indicating plau sible antecedents of perceived enjoyment in SNS contexts. In a word, the present study makes clear that a digital platform that supports people’s hopes and aspirations and/or provides them with a safe escape from life stress is likely to be experienced by them as enjoyable.

The present study contributes to research on the duality of IT (e.g., [3,35,36]) by conceptualizing the stress process in SNS contexts as a paradox. This conceptualization depends on the plausible assumption that an SNS can be a venue where stress and stress relief are both pre sent, an assumption that has so far received limited attention from re searchers. The analysis of this type of double-edged user experience goes beyond the standard analysis of two qualitatively distinct and radically opposed user experiences because it highlights how, in a digital envi ronment, a phenomenon like stress can be experienced positively and negatively at the very same time. Although it does not explicitly discuss the idea of paradox, a recent study by Tarafdar et al. [59] identifies a comparable experience of IT duality. Theorizing the relationship be tween SNS-induced technostress and SNS addiction, Tarafdar et al. [59] point to a positive relationship that may exist between these two con structs in situations where users attempt to cope with the stressful fea tures of an SNS by focusing on other less stressful features or activities that are available on the same platform. This is possible because SNSs are feature-rich IT that can be interpreted using different frames [169, 170]. The present study contributes to this line of recent research by uncovering a paradoxical duality that is rooted not only in the IT but also in the non-digital environment of users. This shows that the non-technological conditions of users’ lives can impact their decision about SNS and that the effects of non-IT-related stressors can sometimes offset the effects of technostress. The analysis of this type of IT duality provides a more accurate picture of the individual–IT relationship in an increasingly digitalized world (exp. [22],) and sheds much-needed light on the mechanisms underlying the decision to continue using an SNS even when using the digital platform causes significant strain. In other words, instead of focusing on the interaction between the multiple digital characteristics of an SNS [59], the present study focuses on the interaction between the online digital experience of the SNS and the off-line non-digital life of users.

The analysis of the SRS process indicates that SNS use is eniovable and stressful at the same time, and that people determine whether or not they should continue using an SNS by weighing the experience of enjoyment against the experience of stress. However, the simultaneity of these two SNS experiences brings into question the directionality of the relationship between enjoyment and exhaustion, for it may be relevant to ask whether it is the flow experienced by SNS users that reduces already existing SNS strain and not the other way around. Here it is essential not to lose sight of the fact that the SRS process underlies the directionality of this relationship. In other words, given that SNSs are hedonic IS and that people use them in order to cope with life stress, SNS enjoyment must be considered the key driver of SNS use. This is consistent with most research on hedonic IT use [98,127]. The SNS user’s enjoyable experience of pleasure precedes the onset of SNS exhaustion. Since the main motivation for SNS use is the relief from life stress, it is only at later stages of SNS use that exhaustion builds up. It is only when people become overwhelmed by requests on SNSs and when they become attached to the digital possessions they have created that SNS exhaustion becomes an issue for them. The emergence of these concerns over time slowly begins making the SNS and its use les enjoyable. Therefore, it is the strain experienced by SNS users that de creases the flow that they experience and not the other way around. This conception of the directionality of the relationship between enjoyment and exhaustion is consistent with research on stress and fatigue that has found that strain decreases overall contentment and satisfaction in SNS environments [60,171]. Of course, it is possible to imagine particular cases where the directionality of the relationship would be reversed, cases where flow would decrease strain. For example, if technostress became a dominant norm on an SNS (although this would likely lead users to abandon it), the sporadic SNS enjoyment derived from certain social or IT-related activities on the platform might temporarily decrease the level of exhaustion experienced by the people who persisted in using it. Future research should attempt to shed light on the possibility that the flow experienced by SNS users may sometimes reduce their SNS strain

By conceptualizing IT use as a coping strategy that people adopt when they suffer from life stress, and by providing a theoretical framework that integrates both the positive and the negative emotional impacts of social technology use, the present study also contributes to the IS literature on emotions and coping. IS coping theories currently focus on the threats and the control levels that users assess after the occurrence of IT events and on the emotional reactions to these events that guide their adaptation behaviors (e.g., [37,38]). Thus, these the ories identify IS and their use as the main triggers for coping behaviors. By examining IS use as a coping behavior that people adopt when they suffer from life stress, the present study goes further. Indeed, the results of the study show that this aspect of their IS use can orient the online emotional reactions of people and help determine their appropriation behaviors.

Moreover, the present study contributes to the literature on tech nostress by exploring an additional category of stressors that drain SNS users. Researchers studying the stressful effects of SNS use have identi fied social issues as the core stressors on these platforms. The research presented here extends this work by identifying privacy issues as stressors that also shape the experience of SNS users. According to CORT, stress is caused not only by the actual depletion of resources but also by the perception of potential threats to resources [33]. In line with CORT, the results presented here provide strong empirical evidence that potential threats to information privacy are important stressors affecting SNS users.

The present study does not examine other additional stressors, ones such as patterns, disclosure, and uncertainty, which have been discussed in the literature (e.g., [20]). Because CORT is used as the overarching theoretical framework for identifying relevant stressors, the depletion of resources and the anticipated loss of resources are the two main cate gories of stressors under consideration. It is important to emphasize that these two categories of stressors are conceptually and empirically distinct. Moreover, given that the omitted stressors primarily belong to one category of stressors (i.e., social demands), and given that they are often conceptualized as reflective dimensions of a second-order SNS stress-creator construct (which is similar to the SNS overload construct used in the present study), this omission should not put into question the validity of the empirical results presented here. Unlike aggregate con structs (i.e., formative constructs) that combine specific dimensions into a general concept. a reflective construct embodies different manifestations of a higher-order construct and their shared variance is what represents the higher-order construct [140]. This means that adding additional reflective dimensions to the construct should not modify empirical results that have already been obtained.

The empirical results of the present study suggest that the experience of the SNS stress paradox is not the same on different SNSs. The three SNSs examined here, Facebook, Instagram, and Snapshot, each have some unique sets of features and allow for different arrays of affordan ces. It is quite possible that these three SNSs even project different values to users. Thus the user experience of pleasure and strain on these SNSs is likely to be different on each one. For example, although there seems to be no significant difference between the levels of exhaustion experi enced by Facebook and Snapchat users, the technostress experienced by the two groups of users is different because the causes of exhaustion are not the same on the two SNSs. In fact, privacy threats do not appear to be a significant issue on Snapchat. This may be because of the spirit of Snapchat and the values that this IT spirit projects to users (see [80]). On Snapchat, published messages are only available to users for a short period of time before they become inaccessible. This is one of the oldest features of Snapshot, and it may reflect an IT spirit that favors the values of safety and security. If it does reflect such a spirit, this may explain why privacy threats do not lead to exhaustion on this SNS. Of course, this is not to say that there are no privacy threats on Snapchat; it simply means that their effects on users may not be strong enough to cause strain.

Although the findings presented here indicate that SNSs offer stress relief at the same time as they cause stress, this should not be interpreted as suggesting that technostress is no longer a significant issue. On the contrary, by investigating the stress dynamics of SNS use, the present study has shown that the stress process is much more complex than previously thought. In fact, it may be the case that this complexity makes SNS use even more harmful than prior research has suggested. It is important to emphasize that the present study examines on-line well being, which should not be confused with overall well-being. There is no doubt that SNS well-being does not necessarily translate into overall well-being. Indeed, given the substantial evidence for the potentially negative effects of constant SNS use, it is likely to be the case that online well-being has a negative impact on overall well-being. For instance, research suggests that SNS enjoyment facilitates the development of IT addiction [36], a psychological state of maladaptive dependency that can produce typical symptoms of addiction such as the loss of self-control and the neglect of personal relationships [5,172,173]. This maladaptive dependency may be more likely to develop if an SNS is experienced as a reasonable escape venue [89], which means that the stress-relieving potential of SNS use may be an additional factor in the development of IT addiction.

The findings presented here can be useful for practitioners since they point to several recommendations for SNS providers. First, it is impor tant for these providers to focus more on stress relief and enjoyment. Although SNSs follow a variety of different business models and cater to numerous user objectives, they have to rely on the contributions and the continuous interactions of users to be successful [165,174]. Conse quently, SNS providers should draw more attention to the stress-relieving potential of SNSs (the bright side of the theoretical model) and find more ways to address the issue of user stress (the dark side of the theoretical model). Doing so would enhance the SNS expe rience of users and reinforce their intention to continue spending time on these platforms. For example, if SNS providers introduced gamified design elements to help users create fantasies and disconnect from worries, it would make SNSs more enjoyable. The second recommen dation has to do with privacy issues. SNS providers should implement privacy mechanisms that offer users greater control of the secondary use of their personal information and that rigorously restrict unauthorized access to it. However, ensuring that a platform is secure does not suffice to make it enjoyable. Indeed, the present study has shown that any perceived threat of losing personal information on an SNS, whether founded or unfounded, can be just as stressful for users as the actual loss of personal information. To help alleviate users’ concerns about privacy issues, SNS providers should communicate their privacy measures with greater clarity and accuracy. This would entail addressing any possible privacy threats that users believe to exist, even ones that appear un founded. Finally, to help protect users from the stressful effects of excessive social requests, SNS providers should implement filtering mechanisms that allow users to manage SNS feeds in accordance with their own tolerance levels.

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

Like all research results, the findings of the present study have several limitations. First. given that different SNSs create value for their users in different ways, caution needs to be exercised when generalizing these findings. It is possible that they are not valid for all SNSs. That being said, as well-established SNSs that are thoroughly integrated into the lives of millions of users, Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat should provide a legitimate starting point and an appropriate context for studying the SRS process. Still, further research is needed to investigate how the stress paradox is experienced by users of other SNSs. Second, because the present study focuses on respondents who are young adults, research on other SNS user segments should be conducted to examine the boundaries of the theoretical model and its generalizability more thoroughly. It is possible that other age groups experience stress differently on SNSs. However, given the large proportion of young adults on these platforms, the findings of the present study remain highly relevant. A further limitation of these findings has to do with the se lection of respondents. All of the respondents in the present study are current Facebook, Instagram, or Snapchat users, which raises the pos sibility that the study sample may be biased. Indeed, researchers have suggested that the underlying motivation for hedonic practices may not be the same for users and non-users [127,175]. In other words, in dividuals who consider SNSs stressful and/or do not consider them enjoyable are less likely to be current SNS users. Future research should look into this survival bias by surveying respondents who are non-users.

In addition, the link between user motivation based on selfregulation and user motivation based on potentially maladaptive addiction deserves to be thoroughly examined in future research. To be more precise, the cognitive narrowing involved in self-regulation stra tegies can make some SNS users so dependent on an SNS that their constant use of it leads them to neglect other important activities in their lives. That is, the enjoyable distraction afforded by SNS can slowly lead to addiction, which often manifests itself as an “obsessive pattern of ITseeking and IT-use behaviors that take place at the expense of other important activities and infringe normal functioning” [5]. It may be the case that the development of these maladaptive behaviors can alter the way that beliefs about pleasure and pain affect decisions about continuing to use an SNS. This is more of an issue now that researchers have identified a theoretical relationship between SNS stressors and addiction [59]. Future research should explore the potential trans formation of SNS escapism and well-being into maladaptive behaviors and the moderating effects that emerging states or behaviors such as SNS addition can have on decisions about continuing to use an SNS.

Life stressors, life strains, and offline well-being are beyond the scope of the present study. Although this does not undermine the validity of the research results presented here, additional insights could be gained by investigating the SRS process depicted in Fig. 2 more fully. The effect of technostress on overall well-being is a particularly significant avenue for future research. Moreover, overall well-being is a dependent variable that it would be worthwhile to study among users of everyday IT. The search for relevant dependent variables in organizational IS research has mostly focused on performance-enhancing outcomes [2], but the latter may not be as relevant in everyday hedonic contexts. Well-being is a more suitable dependent variable for this purpose, particularly in research on SNS use (e.g. [3],). IS scholars should attempt to integrate this construct into their research models, especially when they investi gate the two-sided stress relationship in everyday IT settings.

## 7. Conclusion

SNSs are helpful and enjoyable tools, but they are also complex and even paradoxical venues where beneficial and harmful outcomes coexist. In the context of the widespread use of these technologies in the everyday lives of millions of human beings, the present study has examined two opposite relations to SNS stress whose interconnection helps determine the success of these online platforms. The study has conceptualized SNS use as a coping strategy for dealing with life stress, one that can provide comfort but create additional stress at the same time. This conceptualization offers an extremely interesting starting point for further research on technostress in everyday contexts. It is hoped that future research will develop the theoretical framework pre sented here by elaborating it with specific emphasis on the paradoxical interrelationship of the stress mechanisms that it conceptualizes.
