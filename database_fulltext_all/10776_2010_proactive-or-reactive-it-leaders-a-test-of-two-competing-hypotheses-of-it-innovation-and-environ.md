# Proactive or reactive IT leaders? A test of two competing hypotheses of IT innovation and environment alignment

Ying Lu<sup>1</sup> and Keshavamurthy Ramamurthy<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Information Systems and Operations Management, The Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina-Charlotte, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Ying Lu, Department of Business Information Systems and Operations Management, The Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina-Charlotte, Charlotte, NC 28223, US.A. Tel: 704 687-7644; Fax: 704 687-6330; E-mail: ylu5@uncc.edu

## Abstract

When does it benefit a firm to take a lead in innovation with information technology (IT)? How should a firm align its IT innovation with a dynamic industry? In addressing these questions we present a typology of IT Innovation and Environment Alignment (ITIEA) based on a firm’s IT innovation strategic orientation and the industry environment. The traditional ‘fit’ perspective predicts that a firm that matches its IT innovation with a dynamic environment would obtain performance benefits. In contrast, the ‘options’ perspective suggests that a firm that takes advantage of a stable environment to proactively pursue IT innovation and develop IT capability as real options would obtain performance advantage. We employ a quasi-experiment design to test these two competing hypotheses using archival data of IT leaders over a time frame of 6 years. We perform a longitudinal analysis of the performance change trajectories of proactive and reactive IT leaders over time. The results indicate a general support for the ‘options’ perspective that proactive IT leaders that lead in innovation with IT in relatively stable environments are found to consistently outperform reactive IT leaders in overall performance, allocative efficiency, and cost efficiency in management process. However, interestingly, the results also show a partial support for the ‘fit’ perspective that reactive IT leaders that emphasize intensive deployment of IT innovation in dynamic environments are found to obtain a cost advantage in production and operation process over time. The findings offer practical implications for managers to better engage in IT innovations to create and sustain competitive advantage. Synthesizing options thinking and dynamic capability perspective, we provide a framework to better understand IT innovation, dynamic environment, and performance outcomes. The findings also shed useful light on whether, when, and how to innovate with IT for sustained competitive advantage. ## Introduction

Information technology (IT) leader firms that lead in innovation with IT to enable business strategies and transformation have gained much attention in business press. At the same time, the business environments have become increasingly volatile and unpredictable prompting questions such as: How should a firm align its IT innovation with the industry environment? Under what circumstances does it benefit a firm to take a lead in innovation with IT?

The answers to these questions are of value not only to managers, but also to researchers. IT alignment is a topic of fundamental interest to IS scholars and a top concern for IT managers and executives (Luftman et al., 2006; Chan & Reich, 2007). Much of prior IT alignment research has focused on IS strategic alignment or internal consistency among IT, business, and structure (c.f., Chan & Reich, 2007). While researchers recognize the importance of the industry environment, research to date has been scarce and has largely focused on the role of industry as a contextual or antecedent variable in determining or facilitating strategic alignment (e.g., Tan, 1995; Chan et al., 2006). For instance, environmental uncertainty is shown to have an impact on alignment between IT and business and closer IT alignment is found to be desirable in uncertain conditions (Sabherwal & Kirs, 1994; Karimi et al., 1996). Yet, volatile business environments and rapidly changing information technologies impose significant challenges (Fichman, 2004a). Firms that place too much emphasis on internal IT strategic alignment may actually end up with tightly coupled contexts and experience undesirable, negative outcomes in volatile, unpredictable environments (Vitale et al., 1986; Tallon, 2003). Thus, there is a need to better understand the alignment of IT with the external industry environment.

Meanwhile, understanding whether, when, and how to innovate with IT and how innovating with IT can lead to organizational capabilities that provide competitive advantage remains a crucial challenge to firms (Fichman, 2004b; Lucas et al., 2007). IT innovation is ‘broadly defined as innovation in the organizational application of digital computer and communication technologies’ (Swanson, 1994, p. 1072). For many organizations, IT innovation plays a critical role in creating and sustaining superior competitive performance (Stratopoulos et al., 2008). It is generally accepted that IT innovation has the potential to provide firms with easier access to markets, to differentiate themselves through product/service offerings, or to practice cost leadership due to enhanced efficiencies. All of these could ultimately result in improved economic benefits such as profitability and market share (McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2008). However, IT innovation is also associated with risks stemming from incorrectly identifying and developing the innovation, uncertain benefits from using the innovation, and irreversible deployment costs thus presenting a challenge for managers trying to make strategic decisions about IT innovation (Fichman, 2004a). This challenge is further amplified by the simultaneity of the difficult-to-predict changing business environment. A key issue then is how a firm should align its IT innovation in a dynamic environment.

Two opposing explanations exist in aligning IT innovation with the environmental condition for competitive performance. The traditional ‘fit’ perspective suggests that high degree of uncertainty and unpredictability prevailing in a dynamic environment increases the need for information processing and the firm that emphasizes IT innovation in such an environment would obtain performance benefits (Galbraith, 1977; Jaworski & Kohli, 1993; Lee & Grover, 2000). A competing and opposite position, synthesizing options-thinking and dynamic capability perspective, suggests that the firm that actively seeks out new opportunity to innovate with IT would take advantage of a relatively stable setting to proactively pursue IT innovation and develop IT capability as real options to obtain performance benefits (Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Fichman, 2004a).

The presence of these competing explanations limits our abilities to understand when and how to innovate with IT to realize sustained competitive advantage. To address the limitation, this study develops and tests these two competing hypotheses to better understand the relationship among IT innovation, dynamic environment, and performance benefits. We employ a quasiexperiment design using archival data on a sample of IT leaders. We perform a longitudinal analysis of the performance change trajectories of proactive and reactive IT leaders over time. Our research provides a useful framework for the firm to achieve a favorable alignment with the industry environment for sustained competitive advantage. We proceed as follows. We first briefly discuss the key constructs and present the IT innovation and environment alignment (ITIEA) typologies that set the conceptual foundations. Next we develop the two competing hypotheses derived from two theoretical perspectives, present the research methodology, and the results. These are followed by discussions and implications. Finally, we close with conclusions and directions for future research.

## Conceptual foundations

IT innovation is broadly defined as innovation in the organizational application of IT including digital computer and communication technologies (Swanson, 1994). Prior research has distinguished different types of IT innovation, extending a dual-core model (product/ process innovation) to a tri-core model of IS innovation (cf. Zmud, 1982; Swanson, 1994; Grover, 1997; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). We focus on the user organizations and their organizational innovation with IT – either the adoption of an emerging IT base innovation (e.g., new information/ communication technology innovations); or the deployment of service innovations – Type II and Type III (e.g., the use and application of IT to support the administrative core, functional processes, and business core of the organization). We also consider IT innovation as general purpose technologies that are characterized by pervasiveness, inherent potential for technical improvements, and ‘innovational complementarities’ (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, 1995). As such, IT innovation is not an isolated artifact but a combination of the enabling technological component and complementary organizational process and structure (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003).

Extending the notion of IS strategic orientation as realized IS strategies (Chan et al., 1997), we define IT innovation strategic orientation as realized or executed IT innovation strategies reflected in the actual deployment and use of IT innovation in the organization (Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003). We focus on a firm’s innovative behavior in the actual adoption, implementation, and deployment in the IT innovation-engagement process (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). Similar to the concepts of strategic orientation of business enterprises (STROBE) or strategic orientation of IS (STROEPIS) (Venkatraman, 1989b; Chan et al., 1997), IT innovation strategic orientation is conceptualized as a relatively stable and enduring characteristic of a firm than any specific IT innovative initiatives and can be used generally cross-industry.

On the basis of this concept of IT innovation strategic orientation, we classify firms into two groups: leaders vs followers. IT leaders are usually technology pioneers in their industries who are more innovative in their deployment and use of IT than their competitors. In contrast, IT followers are those who lack such orientation and do not emphasize innovation with IT as a core element of their strategy. This concept of IT leader and follower extends the traditional notion of innovator and laggard in classical innovation adoption theory to understand a firm’s innovative behavior within its industry context. For example, an IT leader is likely a pioneer in innovation with IT in its industry but may not necessarily be an innovator or early adopter of IT innovation across the course of innovation diffusion life cycle (Rogers, 1995). In addition, industry dynamism refers to changes that are difficult to predict in the operating environment (Dess & Beard, 1984). It highlights unpredictability of environmental change and uncertainty in the productmarket space (Duncan, 1972; Miller & Friesen, 1983). Given that firms can have different orientations toward IT innovation in response to industry environments, a question of interest is: when should a firm take a lead in innovation with IT to obtain competitive performance?

Previous IT business value research has proposed and shown that IT (innovations) may contribute to improved organizational performance (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Kohli & Devaraj, 2003; Melville et al., 2004). However, it is not very clear if this relationship holds in all types of environmental conditions triggering interest in IT literature to examine how IT affects firm performance under different environmental conditions (Melville et al., 2004, 2007; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Firms in volatile and rapidly changing environments face greater information processing requirements and the need to change (Eisenhardt, 1989). IT innovation could be a key weapon in such environments and can be expected to confer differential returns across alternative competitive landscapes (Melville et al., 2007). However, the findings of the few studies that have examined the role of industry environment in the relationship between IT and performance have been inconclusive. For instance, Zaheer &

Zaheer (1997) found that banks employing innovative IT applications that enhanced alertness and market responsiveness were able to exert a greater influence on their markets. Choe (2003) found that IT contributed more to performance improvements in unstable environments than in stable ones. Li & Ye (1999) also found evidence that IT investments (not necessarily IT innovation) had a stronger positive association with performance for firms facing high levels of dynamism. On the other hand, Flynn & Flynn (1999) did not find information systems investment to moderate the relationship between uncertainty and performance in their study of manufacturing environments. Other recent research also finds that IT innovation did not contribute to enhanced value generating capability in dynamic industries but that it neutralized productivity loss in such contexts (Melville et al., 2007).

The above findings suggest that innovation with IT is not always successful in dynamic environments. The relationship among industry dynamism, IT innovation, and organizational performance is likely to be quite complex and paradoxical and not well understood. Thus, extending the concept of IS strategic alignment that focuses on the fit between business and IS strategic orientation (Chan et al., 1997), we define IT innovationenvironment alignment as external IS alignment between IT innovation strategic orientation and the industry environment. Instead of the usually assumed sequential alignment that IS strategy supports business strategy which, in turn, addresses the environmental requirement, we consider IT innovation strategy as a core component of business strategy that needs to be well aligned externally with the industry context. The question, then, is how should a firm align its IT innovation in a dynamic industry?

The matrix is formed along two dimensions: IT innovation strategic orientation (leader and follower) and industry environment (stable and dynamic). These two dimensions illustrate four different types of IT innovation-environment alignment.

The key distinction of this typology from the one in Table 1(a) lies primarily in the behavior of the two subgroups of IT leaders in quadrants 3 and 4. Quadrant 4, reactive IT leader, represents those IT leaders that end up reactively pursuing IT innovation in response to dynamic environments. These firms may recognize the importance of innovation with IT but, generally, tend to lead in IT innovations as a result of rushed response to high level of uncertainty in the industry environments. Since they do not have adequate time for experimentation or learning they are more likely to imitate and follow ‘best practices’ or fads/norm and jump on the innovation bandwagon in unpredictable, volatile industries to gain legitimacy (Abrahamson, 1996; O’Neill et al., 1998). Due to pressures to respond they may engage in implementation/ deployment of IT innovation without contextually differentiated reasoning grounded in their own organizational facts and specifics (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004).

Meanwhile, these firms are pressed to rapidly develop and simultaneously exercise IT capability in successfully undertaking innovative IT initiatives (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Further, the short response time available coupled with the challenge of identifying and deploying IT innovation at short notice is also fraught with high risk of failure. As such, there may be a perpetual cycle of continuously trying to catch up with the volatility latent in the industry and the time lag they face in their IT-based innovative actions bearing fruit (Eisenhardt, 1989; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). The mindset of a reactive IT leader is also analogous to that of opportunistic innovators (Stratopoulos et al., 2008) where the IT innovation actions are episodic based on the dynamics of their industry; when the pressure becomes excessive firms are required to respond with IT innovations regardless of their suitability.

This is in sharp contrast to proactive IT leaders in quadrant 3, who proactively seek out new opportunities to innovate with IT and actively undertake IT innovation to improve their business processes and strengthen their competitive position while operating in relatively stable environments. These IT leader firms are prepared to purposefully explore IT innovation and exploit their existing IT by taking advantage of a relatively stable environment that is conducive for experimentation with IT innovation. For instance, these IT leaders are likely to engage in mindful IT innovation implementation or deployment to fully extract and realize value with a more nuanced appreciation of context, continuous refinement, and ongoing identification of new dimensions of context to improve their managerial foresight (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). Knowing that it takes considerable time for acceptance and effective use of IT innovation they desire to prepare their organizations for the consequential changes resulting from IT and deploy these innovations during business periods that are less traumatic and more favorable. These IT leaders also actively manage the long learning process that typically accompanies successful deployment (Kohli & Devaraj, 2003). These firms are able to engage in the ‘learning-byusing’ that can result in positive self-reinforced organizational learning, which, in turn, create path dependency through interconnected asset stocks, organizational routines and procedures, and complementary resources (Piccoli & Ives, 2005). Over time the effective learning is likely to lead to building up superior IT capability – the ability to acquire, deploy, combine, and reconfigure IT resources in support and enhancement of business strategies and work processes, and sustain competitive advantage (Mata et al., 1995; Ross et al., 1996; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1997; Bharadwaj, 2000; Bhatt & Grover, 2005).

Given the foregoing discussion, the interesting question is which of the alignment strategies will lead to superior performance. We specifically focus on IT leaders. The following sections develop two competing hypotheses derived from two theoretical perspectives.

## The traditional ‘Fit’ perspective (Table 1(a)) and hypothesis

Both contingency and institutional theories emphasize the importance of the environment and the need for a fit between an organization’s internal practices and the external environment to realize superior performance (Melville et al., 2007). The underlying rationale of contingency theory is that organizations are market-driven and adaptive to changes in the external environment and that organizations and their sub-systems (IT being one) are most effective when designed to ‘fit’ the competitive environment in meeting the needs and demands of that environment (Chakravarthy, 1982). Institutional theory also suggests that external environment sets the boundary for organizations and the complex interaction between IT and industry context shapes the role of IT in organizations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Chiasson & Davidson, 2005).

Organizational information processing theory views an organization as an information- processing system and explains organizational behavior by examining information flows occurring within and around organizations (Tushman & Nadler, 1978). Uncertainty caused by dynamic environments in terms of unpredictability in the operating industry increases the need for information processing (Galbraith, 1977). Firms seek to achieve a fit between their information processing capabilities and information needs emerging from the demands and pressures of the environment to obtain optimal performance (Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1995). Organizations in such environments tend to emphasize IT innovations to increase their information processing capabilities and to create electronic linkages with customers and suppliers (Lee & Grover, 2000).

In dynamic environments, lack of relevant and timely information about the industry can cause uncertainty in the decision processes. Innovative IT systems can greatly assist a firm in such environments to detect and anticipate changes via competitive intelligence gathering and interpretation ( Jaworski & Kohli, 1993; Prieto & Easterby-Smith, 2006) and to respond rapidly with flexible product offerings or to reconfigure the operational processes required to produce and deliver the products/services thereby differentiating itself from its peers (Feeny & Willcocks, 1998; Weill et al., 2002). Thus, it is desirable to take a lead in innovation with IT when the industry is volatile and unpredictable. Similarly, in relatively stable environments in which the changes are more predictable, a firm would maintain a minimal sufficient level (not more or less) of IT innovation that is in sync with low level of uncertainty to generate performance gains; being an IT follower is desirable in such a stable setting.

Conversely, firms with a misalignment between their IT innovation and the industry condition would face poor performance. First, in relatively stable environments with lower uncertainty level, firms taking a lead in IT innovation (quadrant 3 – IT proactive misfit) may actually oversee a suboptimal allocation of their valuable organizational resources by misdirecting it to IT and consequently realize suboptimum performance. This is similar to the arguments made in the case of BPO alignment (Mani et al., 2006). Hence, one would expect that firms that match IT innovation with industry environments would garner greater performance benefits than those firms that have mismatch of their IT innovation with industry conditions. On the basis of the foregoing, we expect that the ‘aligned’ IT leaders (quadrant 4) should perform better than the ‘misaligned’ IT leaders (quadrant 3).

H1 IT leaders that match their IT innovation with dynamic environments (quadrant 4) are likely to have performance advantage.

## The ‘Options’ perspective (Table 1(b)) and hypothesis

In contrast to the fit perspective, the options perspective proposes a different approach to IT innovation-environment alignment and performance outcome. Optionsthinking is posited as an integrative strategic tool to guide decisions on when and how to innovate with IT especially in fast-moving, unpredictable environments (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Fichman, 2004a; Raman & Grover, 2005). The key premise in options-thinking is that innovation with IT involves both upside benefits and downside risks that requires the firm to actively manage downside risks while maximizing upside potentials and flexibility.

First, innovation with IT in a dynamic environment generally involves two types of uncertainty: endogenous uncertainty (the inherent technical uncertainty) and exogenous uncertainty about unpredictable market (Benaroch & Kauffman, 1999; Fichman et al., 2005). Usually, firms need to find ways to resolve the technical uncertainty while waiting and staying alert to the market in the face of market uncertainty (Fichman et al., 2005). The presence and potential interaction of these two types of uncertainty complicates IT innovation decision. On the one hand, greater uncertainty generally increases the variance of potential benefits and thus the value of the options and will therefore direct firms to pursue IT innovations in a dynamic environment (Fichman, 2004a). On the other hand, increased market volatility can usually shift the decision to a deferred deployment/ implementation while waiting until the industry condition continues to unfold and the direction becomes clearer over time (Luehrman, 1998; Fichman et al., 2005). Studies that apply real options theory to examine optimal investment timing generally support the ‘deferring’ decision in the face of high uncertainty (e.g., Campbell, 2002; Kauffman & Li, 2005). This is well illustrated in the analogy of tomato gardening in uncertain weather (Luehrman, 1998, p. 93); the author demonstrates how increasing level of uncertainty (holding constant the modified net present value) is likely to shift the decision from region 2 (maybe now) to region 3 (probably later) because the harvest is shifting from ‘the imperfect but edible tomatoes’ to ‘inedible but very promising tomatoes’.

Second, institutional theory suggests that firms that seek change are more likely to imitate others to gain legitimacy in their response to uncertainty in volatile environments to be seen as being rational (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Oliver, 1991; O’Neill et al., 1998). This mimetic institutional force may make a firm susceptible to following fads and jumping on innovation bandwagons without local attention to organizational particulars (Abrahamson, 1996). For example, attention lapse/deferral in volatile and uncertain contexts often results in rushed decision making and urgency of bandwagon pressures in the firm (March & Simon, 1993). Because prevailing assumptions and routines are not much applicable in changing circumstances, the firm may make bad choices on the technology or its complementary resources and result in implementations that are not guided by attention to organizational specifics (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). Further, the assimilation process of IT innovation into the firm’s work systems can be painful and lead to failure in delivering on its potential value because of time pressure as well as inattention and insensitivity to nuanced contexts (Alter, 2002).

Third, firms engaging in innovation with IT face a paradox that is inherent in IT and its use in organizations especially in a dynamic setting. IT innovation can enable business agility but it can also constrain a firm’s repertoire of actions because the enabling technology may become outdated and inflexible over time and thereby result in lock-in rigidity (Lucas & Olson, 1994; Weill & Broadbent, 1998). The structuration model of technology also posits that the duality of technology (i.e. technologies as human artifacts simultaneously enable and constrain human actions) can frequently lead to unintended consequences constraining organizations when change is necessary (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Orlikowski, 1992). Further, there is also a need for a balance between financial risk (of investing) and competitive risk (of not investing) during challenging times (Ghemawat, 2009). These simultaneous contradictions are pertinent in the process of IT innovation implementation and can intensify the dilemma in IT innovation decision making.

Finally, volatile industry environments require that firms develop dynamic capabilities (via various forms of IT-enabled digital options) if they are to be successful, for instance, by improving market intelligence capture and dissemination, speeding up the decision-making process, making better decisions using real-time information, being agile and responsive to markets, rapidly generating new product designs, and quickly reconfiguring/redeploying resources (Jaworski & Kohli, 1993; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). IT innovation can help a firm to dynamically adjust its competitive position in the face of unpredictable environmental changes (Volberda, 1996; Ghemawat & Del Sol, 1998) or even trigger more dynamism and difficulties in the external environment for competitors (D’Aveni, 1994).

Hence, a relatively stable environment offers the proactive firm a favorable condition to pursue IT innovation and build up IT capability. First, although a stable industry may generally provide fewer opportunities for competitive advantage, it offers an advantageous environment that allows the proactive firm to actively search for and create opportunity to innovate with IT while protecting it from ferocious imitation. Competitor firms in a stable environment tend to emphasize efficiency and focus their scanning efforts around the general external environment (social, economic, and regulatory sectors) (Garg et al., 2003) and thus are less likely to monitor the proactive IT leader’s actions. Moreover, the institutiona force promotes stability and persistence and firms tend to avoid change and are less likely to imitate others in such setting (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Oliver, 1991; O’Neill et al., 1998). As such, the IT leader in a stable environment may be able to stay out of the radar and take advantage of such a favorable setting to pursue IT innovation and engage in organizational learning to position itself well in the industry. Second, a more predictable industry provides adequate time and cushion for the firm to engage in careful attention to work processes and organizationalspecific learning as well as ways to reinvent the innova: tion prior to implementation (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). The effective deployment and implementation of IT innovation can enable the firm to engage in learningby-using and lead to robust assimilation and positive selfreinforcement in organizational learning over time. The continued learning would create path dependency that differentiates the firm from its competitors (Piccoli & Ives, 2005). The firm could further sustain competitive advantage through creating causal ambiguity and path dependencies from learning experiences that form the firm’s unique historic trajectory and socially complex links embedded in its unique resources and contextual conditions (Kettinger et al., 1994; Mata et al., 1995; Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003). Finally, the effective learning over time is likely to lead to the development of superior IT capability. For instance, prior research has shown that the intensity of organizational learning is an important antecedent to IT capability building (Bhatt & Grover, 2005).

On the other hand, in a dynamic setting, firms actively seek out changes and are more likely to imitate others or follow fads and thus strategies will diffuse and spread quickly and dissipate any advantage that accrue to the leader firm (O’Neill et al., 1998). Further, firms have to rapidly develop and simultaneously exercise IT capability to effectively and successfully deploy and implement IT innovation. However, the organizational learning process that can create path dependency and reinforce barriers to erosion require experience and time; rushed efforts to learning, for example, will subject the innovator to time compression diseconomies, or diminishing returns (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Piccoli & Ives, 2005).

Therefore, IT leaders that take advantage of a relatively stable industry context to proactively explore IT innovations in assembling an optimal mix of IT resources, integrating information infrastructure, and exploiting and deploying IT-enabled resources (quadrant 3: proactive IT leaders), can generate greater business benefits. Conversely, IT leaders that match their IT innovation with environmental conditions in a dynamic setting (quadrant 4) in a reactive stance would only realize suboptimal performance. Hence, the foregoing discussion leads to the following hypothesis:

H2 IT leaders that emphasize IT innovation in relatively stable environments (quadrant 3) are likely to have performance advantage.

## Research method

## Sample and research design

Testing our two competing hypotheses requires a sample of firms with similar IT innovation strategic orientation (IT leader) but varying in the levels of dynamism in their operating industries and thus their differing IT innovation-environment alignment. We used the setting of IT leaders identified as technology pioneers in their industries during the year of 1991–1994 and announced in the four special issues of InformationWeek – 21 September 1992; 27 September 1993; and 10 October 1994; and 18 September 1995. In each year of 1991–1994, the panel of experts, including industry analysts, IT executives, IT academic experts, and other practitioners, commissioned by InformationWeek selected about 40–50 leaders in technology use in each industry group (Dreyfus, 1994). As in prior research, a list of IT leaders was compiled and selected if a firm appeared on the list at least twice (cf. Bharadwaj, 2000). The final sample in this study contained 48 IT leaders from a wide range of industries with varying levels of industry dynamism (see Appendix A for details).

Although firms were not ranked on specific IT resources or skills, those ranked as IT leaders were known to have successfully launched innovative or strategic applications or have a strong reputation for being a technology leader. Firm-specific case studies described in the above noted four issues of InformationWeek also showed that IT leaders were technology pioneers with more innovative deployment of business technologies than their competitors in the industry (Bharadwaj, 2000). As such, the perceptual ranking of a firm as an IT leader reflected a firm’s realized or manifested IT innovation strategies and can be used as a good proxy for a firm’s IT innovation strategic orientation. Firms identified as IT leaders can, therefore, be expected to be representative of technology pioneers leading in innovation with IT in their respective industries. These IT leaders were also the biggest and best users of IT with comparable organizational contexts such as firm size and slack resources, etc. Thus, this sample of IT leaders provides a good setting to test our research model. We collected data for the 48 IT leaders over the period of 1991–1996 about their performance outcomes and firm context variables such as firm size, IT spending, and industry concentration etc., from COMPUSTAT database and InformationWeek500 (IW500 listing), which resulted in a sample of 277 firm-year observations.

We employed a quasi-experiment design and multilevel modeling for change data analysis to test the two competing hypotheses. The data structure was longitudinal data of repeated measurements of individual IT leaders over time nested within groups. Consistent with our conceptualization, the time-invariant dichotomy group variable is a between-subject ‘treatment’ factor while time and other firm variables are within-subject factors. This longitudinal quasi-experiment design allows us to study the relationship between the ‘treatment’ (group status) and patterns of change in ‘response’ (performance outcome).

Singer, 1998; Singer & Willett, 2003). We specify the composite multilevel growth model as in Eq. (1):

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i, j, t + 1} = b _ {0 i} \text { Group } _ {i, j} + b _ {1 i} \text { Group } _ {i, j} \times \text { Time } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {2} \text { Past   Performance } _ {i, j, t - 1} \\ \quad + b _ {3} \text { Firm   Size } _ {i, j, t} + b _ {4} \text { Rank   Index } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {5} \text { IT   Spending } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {6} \text { Industry   Dynamism } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {7} \text { Industry   Munificence } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {8} \text { Industry   Concentration } _ {i, j, t} \\ \quad + b _ {9} \text { Industry   Sector } _ {i, j} + r _ {i, j, t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $r _ { i , j , t } \sim N ( 0 , \Sigma _ { \mathrm { r } } )$

Notice that the subscript i represents group status (0 for reactive IT leaders and 1 for proactive IT leaders); j represents the jth firm in the sample; and t represents the particular time point in the study period of 1991–1996. For example, $Y _ { i , j , t + 1 }$ is the industry average adjusted performance score of the jth firm in group i at time $t + 1$

This model has two major components: (1) the structural component containing the fixed effects of group on performance changes and the fixed effects of firm variables and (2) the stochastic component modeling the composite residual $\mathit { \Theta } - r _ { i , j , t } ,$ or random error associated with the jth firm in group i at time t. We explain next several model features. First, considering the fact that most of the firm variables are time-varying, we use a 1 year lag in performance outcomes – $Y _ { i , j , t + 1 }$ to diminish the possibility of problems of reciprocal causation or endogeneity (Singer & Willett, 2003). Second, we fix the effects of group on change trajectory, or constraining the intercept and slope to vary randomly across firms within each group. Basically, we assume that the group effects are constant across firms within groups. This is a reasonable assumption to simplify the model because we have modeled the within-firm performance variation in the fixed effects of firm variables and the composite residual containing remaining random error. Third, we center time variable around the first wave of data in the sample and firm variables on the sample means. RankIndex is centered on the benchmark (ranking of 250) in IW500 listing; and industry sector is a dummy variable with 0 for manufacturing. This centering practice is recommended in multilevel modeling for change to ensure easy interpretations of the parameters (Singer & Willett, 2003). Finally, we invoke several assumptions to specify the stochastic component $r _ { i , j , t }$ in Eq. 1 (Singer & Willett, 2003). We assume that the composite residual has a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector 0 and a block-diagonal error covariance matrix constituted from submatrices, $\textstyle \sum _ { \mathbf { r } }$ . In other words, residuals are assumed to be homogenous and independent across firms but heteroscedastic and correlated over time within firms. We model the residual component as block diagonal with each block $\displaystyle ( \sum \mathbf { r } )$ corresponding to a firm in each group with all other matrix elements as zero. We correct for autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity in residuals within firms by specifying the error covariance matrix $\textstyle \sum _ { \mathbf { r } }$ in the form of first-order autoregressive structure.

We performed additional test on alternative models to ensure the robustness of our model specification. The random effect model produced nearly identical parameter estimates but penalized on fit statistics of Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) and Schwarz’s Bayesian criterion (BIC) because of the increased model complexity. Further, the fit statistics of models with alternative error covariance specifications showed that our model is appropriate and parsimonious.

We briefly explain the coefficients in the model. First, our research interest mapped into testing the intercept and slope coefficients $\textsuperscript { - } \ : b _ { 0 i }$ and $b _ { 1 i }$ . Because time is centered around the initial time point, the intercept $b _ { 0 i }$ and the slope $b _ { 1 i }$ respectively, estimate the average initial performance and the average annual rate of performance change of IT leaders in group i after controlling for firm variables. A significant coefficient, especially the slope, would best indicate the performance advantage of proactive IT leaders. Second, the coefficients $b _ { 2 } { - } b _ { 9 }$ estimate the fixed effects for firm variables, representing the relationship between each control variable and the average initial performance. Finally, the $r _ { i , j , t }$ estimates residuals within firms over time that remain unexplained by the variables in the model.

## Operationalization and measures of variables

Grou $\varPsi _ { i , j }$ is the jth firm’s group status i and is operationalized as a dichotomy with 1 for proactive and 0 for reactive IT leaders.

Performance outcome $Y _ { i , j , t + 1 }$ is the industry average adjusted performance score of the jth firm in group i at time t þ 1. Because IT leaders were selected as technology pioneers in their respective industries, it is desirable to use industry average as the benchmark (Santhanam & Hartono, 2003; Radhakrishnan et al., 2008). We adjusted firm performance scores by subtracting the average industry performance each year; thus, a positive value would indicate a better than industry average performance. Specifically, we used two profit ratios: return on assets (ROA) and operating income to assets (OI/A) and two cost ratios: cost of goods sold to sales (COGS/S) and cost of sales and general administration to sales (SGA/S). These financial ratios reflect a firm’s overall performance (ROA) as well as process performance in both operational process – allocative efficiency in product and service enhancement process (OI/A), or cost efficiency in production and operations process (COGS/S) and management process – cost efficiency in administration and coordination process (SGA/S) (Radhakrishnan et al., 2008).

Tim $\mathcal { P } _ { i , t } \dot { . }$ We created a time variable by centering around the starting time point and coded the six time periods from 1991–1996 as 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

The following firm contextual variables were measured for the 6 years over the study period of 1991–1996. We included the fixed effect of these variables in the model to control for any potential confounding effect and to improve internal validity and strength of inference in the quasi-experiment design.

Past $p e r f o r m a n c e _ { i , j , t - 1 }$ is the performance score of the jth firm in group i at time t1, corresponding to each performance variable ROA, OI/A, COGS/S, or SGA/S. It was necessary to control for a firm’s prior financial performance because past performance may influence reputation ratings by industry experts and introduce a ‘halo effect’ (Banker & Kauffman, 2004). After controlling for prior performance, we can more accurately estimate the group effect on performance change trajectories over time.

Firm $s i z e _ { i , j , t }$ is the logarithm of number of full time employees for the jth firm in group i at time t.

RankInde $\ell _ { i , j , t }$ is the RankIndex for the jth firm in group i at time t. InformationWeek also publishes an annual ranking of 500 largest public traded U.S. companies since 1989, known as the IW500 listing. The ranking criteria of IW500 during the study period had some changes; it was based on IT budget in 1990–1992, company revenue in 1993 and 1994, and an extensive asset survey in 1995 and 1996 (InformationWeek 500, 16 September 2008). Thus, we controlled the specific ranking of each IT leader on the IW500 listing in a particular year $( { \mathrm { R a n k i n g } } _ { i , j , t } ) .$ . We constructed a variable RankInde $\tau _ { i , j , t } = 1 \mathrm { - R a n k i n g } _ { i , j , t } / 5 0 0 ,$ with value ranging between 0 and 0.998. The sample ranges of RankIndex is between 0.016 and 0.998 $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 0 . 7 6 , \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 2 1 )$ ). The wide range of values of IT leaders’ RankIndex suggest that the operationalization of IT innovation strategic orientation as leader vs follower based on the perceptual ranking of IT leader is valid and unlikely to be contaminated by the changes in the ranking criteria of IW500 listing. We centered RankIndex around the average ranking benchmark on the IW500 listing (ranking ¼ 250, RankIndex ¼ 0.50).

IT spending<sub>i,j,t</sub>: We measured IT spending as an organization’s annual IT budget divided by its annual sales revenue in a particular year. The data of IT budget and revenue were retrieved from IW500 listing and COMPU-STAT, respectively.

Industry dynamis $n _ { i , j , t } .$ Industry dynamism was operationalized as industry volatility in a four-digit SIC industry segment during the year of 1991–1996. Industry volatility was computed by regressing industry sales on a 5-year time period, and standardizing the resulting standard error of the regression coefficient by the average industry sale for each four-digit SIC code (Melville et $a l . _ { \ast }$ , 2007). Larger values indicated greater volatility in the industry.

Industry munificence $\cdot _ { i , j , t }$ is the industry munificence for the jth firm in group i at time t. Industry munificence was operationalized as the slope coefficient from the above noted regression equation for computing industry volatility divided by the average industry sales. Larger values indicated greater munificence or growth opportunity in the industry.

Industry concentratio $\iota _ { i , j , t }$ is the industry concentration for the jth firm in group i at time t. Industry concentration was measured by computing concentration ratios for the top four firms in an industry (Dess & Beard, 1984; Keats & Hitt, 1988). Higher values suggested greater level of concentration in the industry.

Industry sector is a dummy variable with 0 for manufacturing and 1 for service.

## Data analysis and results

We first conducted manipulation check to assess the validity of our ‘treatment’ – proactive and reactive

IT leader groups. Following a matching perspective (Venkatraman, 1989a), we constructed an alignment index as the ‘difference scores’ between a firm’s IT spending and the concurrent industry dynamism for a particular year, reflecting how a firm aligned its IT spending with industry conditions. A close-to-zero align ment index would indicate a nearly perfect match with environmental requirements. Table 2 presents the results of t-test for the mean difference between the two groups in terms of their IT spending, dynamism, and alignment index. First, the two groups’ IT spending was statistically different, with proactive IT leaders engaging in on average higher level of IT spending. Second, the two groups were found to be significantly different in their industry dynamism, with reactive IT leaders operating in more dynamic environments. Finally, the alignment index for the two groups was also significantly different, with, on average, reactive IT leaders having a negative alignment index and proactive IT leaders having a positive alignment index. This result indicates the validity of the manipulation of the ‘treatment’ – IT innovationenvironment alignment as proactive and reactive IT leaders.

## Discussions

We conducted a longitudinal quasi-experiment to test two competing hypotheses derived from the ‘fit’ and ‘options’ perspectives to better understand the relationship among IT innovation, industry dynamism, and performance outcomes. The results from the longitudinal comparison of the performance change trajectories of the two groups of IT leaders show a general support of real options expectation that proactive IT leaders outperform reactive IT leaders in most performance outcomes. Although proactive IT leaders initially have comparable, industry average performance (inferior in OI/A) over time they are able to garner significant performance benefits as a result of their much faster annual performance growth in ROA and OI/A or cost reduction in SGA/S. However, interestingly, reactive IT leaders that match their IT innovation with dynamic environments exhibit initial superior cost efficiency in production and operation cost (COGS/S) and over time they sustain this cost advantage although neither group is able to significantly further reduce their cost. These findings indicate that IT leaders that take a proactive stance appear to initially have no performance advantage or even suffer temporary inefficiency (lower OI/A) but over time they are able to extract and realize better performance growth and process improvement in allocative efficiency or cost efficiency in management process. However, these performance gains of proactive IT leaders may be obtained at the expenses of suboptimal cost efficiency in production and operation process over time possibly as a result of their proactive IT stance.

The findings in partial support of the ‘options’ and ‘fit’ model provide some interesting implications. These findings suggest that when faced with a volatile industry environment, the logic of options thinking better predicts the performance gains of proactive IT leaders in overall performance, allocative efficiency, or cost efficiency in management process. However, the traditional fit perspective seems to better predict the cost advantage of reactive IT leaders in production and operation process. In addition, these findings suggest that industry environment is an important context that shapes IT practice and couples with IT to generate business value. Firms need to strike a delicate external ‘fit’ with the industry context in planning and deploying IT innovation. However, the conventional approach to merely matching or adapting to the industry condition may not be an optimal strategy (Chakravarthy, 1982) in achieving superior performance; rather, a firm should proactively build their IT innovation base even in relatively stable environments to create and sustain performance benefits.

## Conclusions

IT innovation can provide competitive advantages to a firm but at the same time involves risks. The challenge to manage the duality of IT innovation is further intensified by the uncertainty and unpredictability in a dynamic environment. This creates dilemmas for managers about how to align IT innovation in a volatile environment. To help better understand these problems, we raised the question: Under what circumstances should a firm take a lead in IT innovation for performance advantage? Our study was designed to answer this question.

We conceptualized IT innovation strategic orientation and presented the ITIEA matrix that differentiated two types of IT leaders: proactive IT leaders who take a lead in innovation with IT in a relatively stable environment and reactive IT leaders who emphasize IT innovation in a dynamic environment. The key finding is a general support of options thinking that proactive IT leaders outperform reactive IT leaders in overall performance, allocative efficiency, and cost efficiency in management process. However, interestingly, the finding also partially supports the fit perspective that reactive IT leaders that match their IT innovation with dynamic environments achieve cost advantage in production and operation process.

By extending options thinking with the concept of mindfulness in IT innovation, we gain a dynamic capability perspective that provides a useful framework to better understand IT innovation, dynamic environment, and performance outcomes. By introducing the concept of IT innovation strategic orientation, we have developed a typology of IT innovation-environment alignment. We have also gained insights into the timing and underlying processes a firm can take to successfully pursue IT innovation. This helps the firm achieve a favorable alignment with the industry environment and realize superior performance. It also generally shows whether, when, and how IT innovation may be related to performance outcomes. Further, by testing competing hypotheses derived from two opposing theoretical views, we provide a more rigorous explanation and better understanding of the boundaries of both theoretical perspectives.

The foregoing results and implications should be considered in light of several limitations and extensions. First, the data set published in and obtained from InformationWeek is about the largest U.S. organizations; thus, one should be cautious in generalizing the findings to small and medium enterprises. Future research needs to examine the effects in other populations of firms. Second, the practice of using perceptual ranking of IT leaders as a proxy for IT innovation strategic orientation may not be adequate in completely capturing IT innovation practices in a firm. It would be desirable to look at different measures that characterize IT innovators in future research such as the types and the attributes of IT innovations. For example, much additional research is needed to better understand whether and how the dynamics of aligning IT innovation with the external industry would be any different for radical vs incremental innovation, architectural vs component innovation, or product vs process innovation with IT. Third, industry dynamism was measured as the volatility or unpredictability of changes in the product-market space. There are other dimensions of environmental dynamism and the study findings may not directly apply to how firms react to changes in other, more macro-level sources of uncertainty. Further exploration and validation of the findings in other settings is needed in future research. However, coincidently, the study period of 1991–1996 was the last economic recession/recovery during which there was a negative percentage change in real GDP in 1991 and a positive percentage change in real GDP in 1992 through 1996 (Coy, 2009). Our findings may shed some limited light on managing IT innovation during economic recession/ recovery, but it is an interesting avenue for future research (Ghemawat, 2009). Finally, the study time period is fairly old (about 15 years) and primarily dominated by process-oriented innovations such as enterprise systems (ERP, CRM etc.). Future studies need to extend to more recent settings with Internet computing and other subsequent generations of technology innovations.

Furthermore, the findings of this study may suggest several additional avenues for further inquiry. First, more research into the process of capability development in innovation with IT is needed as called for recently (Lucas et al., 2007). For example, how do firms manage the process of organizational learning and capability building in innovating with IT and what particular factors affect success or failure? Second, this study only focuses on external alignment with industry dynamism. Future research should consider both internal and external alignments and examine how these are addressed by optimally aligning with IT practices. Third, although some dynamics of alignment is explored in this study, future research needs to further investigate the dynamic process of alignment as called for in recent research (Sabherwal et al., 2001; Chan & Reich, 2007). Finally, the competing hypotheses model of our study offers some general implications for future research. Further research is needed to explain why the options perspective might be more applicable than the fit perspective and under what circumstances.

In conclusion, the study highlights the importance of attending to the industry condition when managing innovation with IT. IT innovation can be a double-edged sword, both creating and destroying value when it is not well managed. The high uncertainty in a dynamic environment can intensify the dilemmas inherent in the duality of IT innovation. The study results suggest that the timing and industry context should be an important part of the innovation process. Our study offers implications to practitioners to plan and manage their IT innovation. The study results also offer limited insights into why many IT innovative initiatives fail or fail to deliver expected benefits. It is conceivable that a firm should pursue IT innovation in coping with uncertainty in a dynamic environment. However, innovation with IT in a dynamic environment involves high level of uncertainty that may adversely make downside risks exceeding potential benefits and requires simultaneous developing and exercising IT capability for successful deployment and implementation. Thus, it would seem logical to track the industry condition as it continues to unfold and the direction of change becomes clearer over time. IT innovation could be better managed as real options and a relatively stable environment offers a favorable setting for the firm to proactively pursue innovation with IT to create and sustain competitive advantage.
