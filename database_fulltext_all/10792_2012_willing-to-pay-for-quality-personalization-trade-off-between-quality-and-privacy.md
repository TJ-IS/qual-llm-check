RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Willing to pay for quality personalization? Trade-off between quality and privacy

Ting Li<sup>1</sup> and Till Unger<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Decision and Information Science, Erasmus University, Rotterdam, The Netherlands; <sup>2</sup>Heßstr. 53, 80798 Munich, Germany

## Abstract

Online personalization presents recommendations of products and services based on customers’ past online purchases or browsing behavior. Personalization applications reduce information overload and provide value-added services. However, their adoption is hindered by customers’ concerns about information privacy. This paper reports on research undertaken to determine whether a high-quality recommendation service will encourage customers to use online personalization. We collected data through a series of online experiments to examine the impacts of privacy and quality on personalization usage and on users’ willingness to pay and to disclose information when using news and financial services. Our findings suggest that under certain circumstances, perceived personalization quality can outweigh the impact of privacy concerns. This implies that service providers can improve the perceived quality of personalization services being offered in order to offset customer privacy concerns. Nevertheless, the impact of perceived quality on personalization usage is weaker for customers who have experienced privacy invasion in the past. The results show that customers who are likely to use online personalization are also likely to pay for the service. This finding suggests that, despite privacy concerns, there is an opportunity for businesses to monetize high-quality personalization.

Keywords: e-commerce; information disclosure; personalization; privacy; quality; willingness to pay

## Introduction

Personalization technologies allow customers to find information efficiently and to leverage the opportunity for increased information availability. With different degrees of interactions, these technologies present recommendations to customers based on their preferences or usage behavior. In an online environment, personalization technologies offer a broader range of services based on customers’ recent browsing behaviors or purchase histories. These services include, for example, book recommendations by Amazon (http://www.amazon.com), selected news services by Google News (http://www.google.com/news), and travel recommendations by Expedia (http://www.expedia.com) and its European counterpart Opodo (http://www.opodo.com). According to MyBuys Inc., a personalization technology provider with 300 e-retailer clients, its total sales grew 3.4% in April 2011 compared with April 2010. Its monthly E-Commerce Wellness Index examines how the use of personalization practices such as product recommendations can affect online sales. The index showed that e-retailers realized a 12% increase in average order value for personalized transactions compared with sales without personalization (Siwicki, 2011).

Nearly 80% of marketers in a recent Bloomberg Business-Week study named personalized marketing as their top priority, but less than half of them use any form of personalization. One reason is that personalization is not easy to accomplish using manual techniques and ad hoc efforts. When asked how much of a priority it was for their organization to use different kinds of online marketing tools in the future, 79% of marketers indicated that they would use personalized e-mail based on individual online behavior, 76% indicated that they would use technology-driven personalized onsite crosssell product recommendations based on individual behavior, and 76% indicated that they would use manual onsite personalized content and recommendations (Coremetrics, 2011).

Leveraging personalization technologies such as collaborative filtering, data mining, and click-stream analysis, online personalization provides the right content in the right format to the right person at the right time (Tam & Ho, 2005). Online personalization can be used as a tool for customer relationship management, allowing online companies to present product and service offerings tailored to customers and to create a relationship of trust and customer loyalty. Thus, the quality of personalization and its ability to meet individuals’ preferences accurately are critical to its success (Jahng et al, 2002). Recommendations that do not meet customers’ interests or requirements will result in customers avoiding the service in future. Service providers can improve the quality of their online personalization through acquiring detailed customer information.

Privacy can be a major concern in customers’ use of online personalization. Prior research has shown that customers are generally interested in using personalized services, but are concerned about how online companies use their private data (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Tam & Ho, 2005; Kobsa, 2007; Junglas et al, 2008). Even though the services themselves are valuable, a customer may still choose not to use them if the privacy concerns that arise from the associated information sharing outweigh any benefits of using the services (Chellappa & Sin, 2005). Thus, customers, on the one hand, desire high-quality personalization that may require more data, and, on the other hand, have strong concerns about the privacy of their anonymous, personally unidentifiable, and personally identifiable information.

If customers have concerns about their privacy, why would they use online personalization? Customers may be doing a privacy calculus, considering the trade-offs between benefits and costs of providing private information when they make the decision to reveal it (Dinev & Hart, 2006). It may be that the perceived value of personalization is high enough to overcome privacy concerns (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Chellappa & Shivendu, 2010; Xu et al, 2011). It could also be that customers are offered financial incentives so that their opportunism may overcome their privacy concerns (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Tsai et al, 2011). Alternatively, consumers may be largely unaware of the privacy implications of disclosing their information, and so may not see the privacy downside. For example, many people disclose their Facebook profiles to play quizzes, not realizing that the quiz app retains their complete profile information, which can be used by marketers. Nevertheless, it remains unclear which aspects of quality are important and how quality plays a role in explaining the usage of personalization. Are customers less concerned about their online privacy if they receive satisfying recommendations? Can high quality recommendations overcome privacy concerns? Does the ability to use privacy protection tools alleviate the problem?

Companies are willing to spend a large amount of money to improve their recommendations. For example, Netflix, an online DVD rental company, announced that a reward of US\$1 million would be conferred on the person who could improve the accuracy of the company’s film recommendations by the greatest amount over a 10% threshold. The contest was such a great success in 2009 that Netflix planned a new contest (Lohr, 2009). However, it remains a challenge to find the extent to which customers are willing to pay a premium for personalized results.

In this paper, we examine the impacts of privacy concerns, privacy protection tools, and recommendation quality on the use of online personalization. To be more specific, we discuss how privacy concerns, privacy protections, and recommendation quality will affect customer contribution – a term we use to describe customers’ willingness to pay a premium and to become profiled (i.e., store information in service providers’ customer profiles) – in order to receive personalized services. Previous research has provided insight into the conditions under which privacy concerns have a negative influence on customers’ willingness to become profiled (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Kim & Son, 2009). However, the impacts of privacy concerns, privacy protections, and recommendation quality on usage and customer contribution are not clear.

The objective of the paper is to explore the relationships between personalization quality and privacy concerns through the theoretical framework of a privacy calculus (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Sheng et al, 2008; Xu et al, 2011), considering differences in industry context. Specifically, we seek to examine the impacts of privacy concerns, privacy protection tools, and recommendation quality on the use of online personalization, and to determine how these factors affect customers’ willingness to pay a premium and to become profiled. In addition, acknowledging that personalization in different industry contexts yields different consumer responses, we seek to understand whether these effects vary across different personalization contexts. To achieve this, first, we will investigate the impact of personalization quality, privacy concerns, and privacy protection on the extent of personalization usage. We will further examine how these factors affect customers’ willingness to pay a premium and to become profiled. Second, we will investigate how customers’ past experience (e.g., familiarity with personalization, privacy invasion experience) affects their usage. Third, we will investigate to what extent a high standard of personalization quality can outweigh the customer’s concerns about privacy and lead to a higher willingness to disclose information.

We report findings from a series of experiments in which participants are assigned to different experimental scenarios to assess the impact of privacy concerns, privacy protection tools, and recommendation quality on usage of personalization, willingness to pay, and willingness to become profiled. The contribution of this study lies in its focus on examining the personalizationand-privacy paradox through a privacy calculus lens. This study furthers the theoretical contribution by examining the valuable information that personalization quality provides about the conditions under which the relationship between privacy concerns and intention to use exists, and incorporating these constraints into the understanding of the personalization-and-privacy paradox (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Dinev & Hart, 2006; Sheng et al, 2008; Xu et al, 2011). Furthermore, it provides empirical evidence about the roles of privacy and quality in customers’ usage of personalization. We believe the results will help service providers to understand the feasibility of charging for online personalization and the conditions under which customers are willing to provide more personal information. This knowledge can help further enhance personalization experiences for both customers and companies.

## Theoretical background and hypotheses development

In this section, we will first give an overview of the personalization literature. Then, we will present the research model and discuss hypothesis development. We will explain how concerns related to information privacy and personalization quality impact people’s use of online personalization. In addition, we will discuss the extent to which these two factors influence customers’ willingness to become profiled and to pay a premium for the use of online personalization.

## Personalization

In the past few years, the academic disciplines of Information Systems (IS) and Marketing have begun to generate significant research on personalization and customization (Arora et al, 2008; Kim & Son, 2009; Ho et al, 2011). Personalization is initiated by a firm, which decides what product or service is suitable for the customer based on previously collected customer data (Tam & Ho, 2005, 2006; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Kumar & Benbasat, 2006; Liang et al, 2006; Vesanen & Raulas, 2006). Customization occurs when the customer proactively specifies her products or services (Wind & Rangaswamy, 2001; Arora et al, 2008).

The idea of personalized products or service offerings already existed long before the Internet, but advances in information technology and the Internet have made it possible to furnish consumers with efficient and valuable product and service recommendations. This proliferation of online personalization is also attributed to the fact that customers increasingly make their product orders online instead of in traditional brick-and-mortar shops. The many forms of online personalized product offerings and services range from personalized YouTube (http:// www.youtube.com) features to customized music playlists (e.g., http://www.musicchoice.com). For example, Google is now offering personalized results based on individuals’ search queries. Companies such as Amazon and Netflix are offering product recommendation services to their customers. Research has found that it is more effective to use personalization techniques in an online than in an off-line environment, because of the ease of collecting a large volume of customer data and offering recommendations in real time online (Adomavicius & Tuzhilin, 2005).

Providing online personalization to their customers makes companies better able to enforce product differ entiation, and thus achieve higher profit margins. Personalization is a ‘new mode’ in the relationship between firms and customers (Murthi & Sarkar, 2003; Tam & Ho, 2005). Learning about customer preferences and consequently improving the quality of product and service offerings leads to ‘substantial dividends’ for service providers (Ansari & Mela, 2003). Businesses and academics are busy figuring out how to optimize personalization. They are considering both what to offer – determining the impact of different types of online personalization – and when to offer it – calculating the timing of presenting personalized content. For example, Senecal & Nantel (2004) find that recommendation systems affect online consumers’ choices in different ways depending on the nature of the product. Recommendations for experience goods are more influential than for search products. One recent article has studied when to present personalized content to consumers (Ho et al, 2011). The authors find that a website’s recommendation quality improves over the course of an online session but that the probability of a consumer’s considering and accepting a given recommendation diminishes over the course of the session.

Using online personalization also has a number of benefits for customers. First, online personalization offers them accurate and timely information (Senecal & Nantel, 2004) and decreases information overload by providing customers what they are looking for (Tam & Ho, 2005; Liang et al, 2006). As a result, it improves user information processing and decision outcomes (Tam & Ho, 2006). Second, online personalization improves the degree of customer loyalty (Senecal & Nantel, 2004). Previous

Personalization and customization techniques are useful for building one-to-one relationships by understanding and fulfilling customer needs and consequently fostering loyalty (Adomavicius & Tuzhilin, 2005).

However, contrary to common expectations, personalized marketing has not yet lived up to its promise (Vesanen, 2007). The benefits of personalization do not come without costs, and, for customers, personalization’s benefits must outweigh its costs to encourage more usage. We identify four types of costs that may hinder personalization’s more widespread use. First, customers have privacy concerns that may prevent them from using online personalized services. The value of personalization needs to outweigh these concerns to improve consumers’ likelihood of using online personalization (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Awad & Krishnan, 2006). Second, lack of trust in the web environment can also deter customers from using personalized services. However, firms can alleviate this problem by improving website quality and reputation in order to build trust and overcome customers’ doubts (McKnight et al, 2002). Third, certain customers lack expertise and are uninvolved with personalization systems. Because they can become ambivalent toward or even dissatisfied with these systems, it will be ineffective and wasteful of resources to devote capital toward creating a personalization experience for these customers. Others argue that customers’ familiarity with online personalization can increase the intention to adopt as their cognitive and emotional trust improves (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). Finally, lock-in effects, as supported by the customer’s willingness to provide information (Murthi & Sarkar, 2003), may prevent customers from experimenting with alternative online personalization methods. This is because customers need to spend time and effort to learn how to use and navigate a website, as well as how to use its specific features, in order to receive personalized services from a service provider. Once the customer decides to switch to another service provider, she has to repeat a similar process to receive the same level of service from the new service provider. Thus, the greater the extent of personalization performed on an online service, the higher the costs of switching for the consumer (Kim & Son. 2009).

## Research model and the personalization-and-privacy paradox

Our research model (see Figure 1) includes the perceived quality of online personalization, customers’ privacy concerns, and privacy protection offered by service providers. Together, these factors predict the likelihood that customers will use online personalization and their willingness to pay and to become profiled. The model also includes industry domains and customers’ past experiences. Before we discuss the relationships among these factors and the development of hypotheses, we will introduce the personalization-and-privacy paradox, which provides the theoretical foundation of the research (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Dinev & Hart, 2006; Sheng et al, 2008; Xu et al, 2011).

As a psychological state, privacy can be defined as ‘the ability of the individual to control the terms under which personal information is acquired and used’ (Westin, 1967, p. 7). It can also be seen as a form of power because individuals have control over information about themselves. In this paper, we refer to information privacy as an individual’s ability to personally control information about herself.

The personalization-and-privacy paradox refers to a situation where consumers give out their private information with subjective expectations that the service provider will provide personalized services based on their profiles and trust that the provider will not indiscriminately share their personal information (Chellappa & Sin, 2005). In this research, we examine this central paradox and attempt to determine whether the extent of online personalization use depends on the extent to which a customer’s concerns about privacy are outweighed by her perceptions of the personalization service’s quality. We argue that the negative effect of customers’ privacy concerns on the intention to use online personalization will be weakened if the personalization’s perceived quality is high.

## Privacy concerns and privacy protection

Prior research has argued that privacy concerns play a crucial role in customers’ online purchasing behavior (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Kobsa, 2007; Junglas et al, 2008). Kobsa (2007) has argued that the existence of unsolicited marketing is one of the major reasons for privacy concerns. Privacy concerns can occur in various situations. For example, customers may be concerned that their personal information may be used for unwanted targeted advertising or sold to other companies. They may fear price discrimination, unauthorized access to accounts, or government surveillance. They may be afraid that their habits and interests can be predicted by a technological system. It is also possible that customers are afraid of the risk of harassment rather than about how their data will be used. The asymmetric nature of the relationship between companies as data collectors and customers as data providers worsens the problem of privacy. This concern for privacy is also influenced by personality traits, which could have a greater influence in highly personalized settings than in less personalized settings (Junglas et al, 2008). We expect that the extent of online personalization use likely depends on the difference between privacy concerns associated with online personalization and its perceived benefits. Thus, we hypothesize that a customer’s intention to use online personalization is negatively affected by her privacy concerns.

## H1a: (Privacy Concerns and Intention to Use Online Personalization) A customer’s intention to use online personalization is negatively influenced by the degree of her privacy concerns.

Companies are aware of the fact that trust-creating actions can lead customers to a more frequent use of websites and to a higher acceptance of online personalization. Moorman et al (1992) define trust as the willingness to rely on an exchange partner in whom one has confidence. Trust is an important determinant for building and maintaining long-term relationships (Sirdeshmukh et al, 2002), and can be regarded as an antecedent to loyalty (Ribbink et al, 2004). In an online setting, different kinds of actions and instruments can be used to create trust. Four primary privacy protection tools mirror the degree of customers’ self-responsibility and the actions a company or governmental institution can take to create trust (Ishitani et al, 2003; Malhotra et al, 2004; Kobsa, 2007).

The first privacy protection tool is anonymity, which can be ensured through the use of pseudonyms (Ishitani et al, 2003). Personalization service providers need to design techniques to guarantee customers’ anonymity and pseudonymity (Ishitani et al, 2003). These techniques focus on protecting customers who are not willing to reveal their personal information, such as their name, age, and profession. Every customer can be identified by a pseudonym during the interaction with a certain website (Ishitani et al, 2003). Prior research has found that if individuals believe that they cannot be identified individually, the likelihood is greater that they will provide personal information (Kobsa, 2007). These techniques can be used to reduce, although not to completely eliminate, the risk of unauthorized access to customers data.

The second privacy protection tool is made up of privacy statements and privacy policies (Kobsa, 2007). E-retailers rely mainly on the use of privacy policies. Companies can use a number of ways to assure customers that their privacy concerns are being addressed, but the most common is to highlight privacy statements and policies. Several studies have shown that privacy certifications can lead to trust and disclosure (Kobsa, 2007). However, research has also shown that only a few people – between 0.5 and 1% of users – make the actual effort to read published privacy statements (Kobsa, 2007).

The third privacy protection tool is the security seal (Kobsa, 2007). Several third-party providers offer security seals to show that interested online companies process customer data in a coherent and secure way. Well-known examples are TRUSTe and VeriSign, both of which offer digital trust certificates, which aim to establish a relationship of trust by guaranteeing that participating online companies respect their customers’ personal identities and information. These seals assess a company’s privacy standards based on accessible privacy statements.

The fourth privacy protection tool is related to information transparency. Information transparency is a measure of customer awareness of how companies deal with the data they collect from customers (Malhotra et al, 2004). Awareness refers to a passive dimension of information privacy, where customers articulate their concerns about an online company’s information privacy practices. Awareness becomes very important when customers are confronted with a request to reveal more private information. If they lack sufficient information about the company’s privacy policies, they will be reluctant to provide more personal data. Online companies are responsible for highlighting how private data is processed in order to increase customers’ perception of fairness (Malhotra et al, 2004). In addition, better control over a website’s privacy settings can increase the degree of customer trust. Thus, we hypothesize that the use of privacy tools will increase the use of online personalization.

H1b: (Privacy Protection and Intention to Use Online Personalization) A customer’s intention to use online personalization is positively influenced by service providers’ privacy protection measures.

## Perceived quality of online personalization

The quality of online personalization is commonly judged by the degree by which it decreases information overload, and thus increases customer satisfaction. Importantly, however, a reduction in information overload is not the single critical success factor that encourages customers to accept personalized results. In the literature, the term e-quality is used to describe different dimensions of an online service’s quality. Ribbink et al (2004) define e-quality based on five dimensions: ease of use, website design, customization, responsiveness, and assurance. Madu & Madu (2002) give a more detailed analysis of e-quality by adding indicators such as performance, reliability, and the scope of features. However, not all dimensions can be used to describe the quality of online personalization. On the basis of previous literature (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Chau et al, 2000; Madu & Madu, 2002; McKinney & Yoon, 2002; Nanou et al, 2010), we consider three quality dimensions as relevant for online personalization: usefulness, presentation, and information quality.

The first quality dimension is made up of usefulness (Madu & Madu, 2002; McKinney & Yoon, 2002). One of the major indicators for quality of personalized results is the usefulness of the recommendations generated through online personalization. Perceived usefulness is defined by how readily someone can use an application to perform her job better. The degree of perceived usefulness can be measured by the degree to which the performance benefits of using a given application outweigh the effort required in order to use it (Davis, 1989). Perceived usefulness is closely related to the personalized content’s understandability and reliability (McKinney & Yoon, 2002). Understandability – the extent to which customers understand the personalized results presented by the service provider – is regarded as a salient dimension of information quality. Reliability can be determined by the degree of consistency of a product or service’s performance during a certain time period (Madu & Madu, 2002). In the case of personalized services, reliability covers issues such as accuracy, trustworthiness, and consistency of information.

The second quality dimension, presentation (Chau et al, 2000; Jahng et al, 2002; Blanco et al, 2010; Nanou et al, 2010), concerns the extent to which a site can attract customers by visual appearance and layout consistency. The way in which graphical design displays recommendations should be incorporated into quality. Nanou et al (2010) argue that the presentation of information and the selection of appropriate recommendation content are equally important in creating successful recommender applications. They found that the most efficient presentation methods regarding user persuasion and satisfaction proved to be the ‘structured overview’ and the ‘text and video’ interfaces.

The third quality dimension is information quality (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Chau et al, 2000; McKinney & Yoon, 2002). It includes factors such as relevance, timeliness, reliability, and scope (McKinney & Yoon, 2002), and is distinct from system quality, which stresses the importance of access, usability, navigation, and interactivity.

Recommendation systems are designed to ‘find the wanted and filter out the unwanted’. Customer satisfaction of the recommended services is influenced by the quality of information content (Liang et al, 2006). The higher the recommendation quality, the more likely it is that customers will use personalization services. We propose:

H2a: (Perceived Quality of Personalization and Intention to Use Online Personalization) A customer’s intention to use online personalization is positively influenced by the degree of perceived quality.

Although privacy plays a crucial role in an environment such as the Internet, where customers want high security and low risk, these concerns seem to diminish if the services’ perceived value is higher than the perceived cost of obtaining them. This is because customers make trade-offs between benefits and costs when they decide whether to use online personalization. Undoubtedly, privacy concerns fall into the category of costs (Awad & Krishnan, 2006).

When the quality of an online personalization implementation is high, a customer who recognizes its high quality may be engaged in a privacy calculus (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Sheng et al, 2008) to see whether the benefits they receive can overcome their potential privacy concerns. Culnan & Bies (2003) introduced the concept of second exchange to explain the privacy calculus as a utilitarian exchange whereby personal information is given in return for value received, such as higher quality of personalization services. In the context of online personalization, customers disclose their personal information in return for added value, such as personalized services that will be delivered based on their context. The customer will be more likely to trust the service provider to an increasing degree if the perceived quality of the personalization is high. In such a case, privacy concerns’ negative effect on the likelihood of usage will diminish.

In contrast, when the quality of a personalization service is low, customers may be more concerned about the services they receive, resulting in their distrusting the service provider to a greater degree. Personalization depends on consumers’ willingness to share their personal information, but consumers have an interest in obtaining these services in exchange for giving as little information as possible (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Sheng et al, 2008). Finding personalization services unsatisfactory will worsen this situation, because if the consumers cannot perceive the benefits in a personalization service, their privacy concerns will not be allayed. Thus, privacy concerns will have a stronger negative impact on the likelihood of usage when personalization’s perceived quality level is lower.

The extent of online personalization use, therefore, likely depends on the extent to which a customer’s concerns about privacy are outweighed by her perceptions of the personalization service’s quality. That is, personalization quality provides valuable information about the conditions under which the relationship between privacy concerns and intention to use exists. To some extent, recommendations’ high perceived quality can overcome customers’ privacy concerns about personalization usage. Thus, the effect hypothesized in H1a will be weaker with higher quality.

H2b: (Perceived Quality of Personalization and Privacy Concerns) The negative effect of customers’ privacy concerns on the intention to use online personalization will be weakened if the personalization’s perceived quality is high.

## Industry domain and customer past experience

Increasingly, companies establish price differentiation practices through personalization. Personalization provides companies an opportunity to offer differentiated products at differentiated prices to obtain higher profits (Murthi & Sarkar, 2003). Businesses that provide personalization services may be able to achieve better estimations of customers’ valuations, and thus to establish finer price discriminations. The likelihood of using online personalization depends not only on products or services’ general economic value – the monetary value of the personalized products or services – but also on the specific individual economic value customers perceive in certain products or services. This may vary across different industry domains that have different transaction values. Prior research has assumed that the industry domain in which the personalization technologies are used can affect customers’ use of the services (Chellappa & Sin, 2005). However, this has never been empirically studied.

The question remains: What application areas are suitable for personalization? Using personalization techniques, companies process a large amount of data through distinct IS. The challenge is to use the collected data for a better understanding of customer needs and to transform them into knowledge to further improve customized product and service offerings (Liang et al, 2006, 2008). This task is particularly critical for contentbased applications such as news services and knowledge management (Liang et al, 2006). News recommendations can help customers focus on news that interests them the most. For example, news providers such as Bloomberg and Reuters provide customized news offerings to meet their customers’ needs. Financial services are another area that is appropriate for personalized recommendation services. For example, customers may feel overwhelmed by the amount of financial content offered by different banks and financial institutions. They will be more likely to use companies that can provide personalized advice.

The nature of the business providing such services also may affect the services’ perceived value. For example, we can imagine that customers may perceive a higher economic value for personalized financial services compared with personalized news services. It is more likely that users would investigate more carefully recommendations about investment items than about news items. As a result, customers would have increased privacy concerns with respect to financial recommendations, especially during the transaction phase. Therefore, we argue that the costlier a personalized product or service is, the more acute a consumer’s privacy concerns will be, and consequently the less likely she will be to use online personalization. To alleviate this issue, firms can provide stronger privacy protections and higher-quality recommendations. We expect customers’ intentions to use online personalization that involves higher economic value and requires higher customer involvement will increase under these conditions.

H3a: (Industry Domain and Privacy Concerns) Customers’ privacy concerns have a greater impact on a customer’s intention to use online personalization for transactions of higher economic value.

H3b: (Industry Domain and Privacy Protection) Providing privacy protection has a greater impact on a customer’s intention to use online personalization for transactions of higher economic value.

H3c: (Industry Domain and Personalization’s Perceived Quality) Online personalization’s perceived quality has a greater impact on a customer’s intention to use services for transactions of higher economic value.

Customers’ past experience plays an important role in their perception of privacy concerns and quality expectations for online personalization. Thus, the roles of past experience with online recommendations and general online expertise need to be considered when explaining the role of personalization (Senecal & Nantel, 2004). Privacy depends on a person’s experience with using the Internet. The role of experience in relation to Internet usage and of past experience in online transactions influences the relationship between privacy concerns and personalization usage. We argue that customers who are familiar with online personalization perceive privacy concerns as less important.

H4a: (Familiarity and Privacy Concerns) Privacy concerns are less important for customers who are more familiar with online personalization.

In addition, the role of experience can also be determined by examining pre-existing relationships between a customer and the company that provides online personalization; these relationships might affect customers’ willingness to reveal personal data. Privacy invasion can negatively affect a customer’s willingness to be profiled (Awad & Krishnan, 2006). If a customer’s privacy has been invaded in the past, she will be more likely to be concerned about privacy when using online personalization. Thus:

H4b: (Privacy Victim and Privacy Concerns) Customers who have experienced privacy invasion in the past are more concerned about privacy when using online personalization.

Similarly, if a customer has had her privacy compromised in the past, she will be less likely to use online personalization of the same quality, compared with someone who has not.

H4c: (Privacy Victim and Perceived Quality of Personalization) The impact of online personalization’s perceived quality on personalization usage is weaker for customers who have experienced privacy invasion in the past.

## Customer contribution

We discuss the importance of online personalization for customers in terms of customer contribution, which represents customers’ willingness to pay a premium and to become profiled. A previous study has found that customers are willing to pay a premium for mass customization products and services (Bardakci & Whitelock, 2004). However, the study focuses on explicit and customer-initiated customization, and therefore it is doubtful that it wholly applies to personalized recommendations. Similarly, Tsai et al (2011) have discussed the importance of privacy protection on customers’ purchasing behavior, indicating a positive relationship. They find that customers are willing to pay a premium to purchase from privacy-protective websites if privacy information is made more accessible. However, their study focuses on a context of personalization-free e-commerce retailers. An exploration of whether people are willing to pay for online personalization remains an unmet challenge. In online personalization, carefully executed personalization techniques require knowledge about customer needs. When customers have high confidence in the use of online personalization due to low privacy concerns and fulfilled quality expectations, they will be more willing to pay a premium for the services. Thus, we propose:

H5a: (Likelihood of Usage and Willingness to Pay) Likelihood of usage positively influences customers’ willingness to pay a premium for online personalization.

A greater degree of customer participation could fundamentally improve online personalization. For this reason, prior research has proposed different approaches to profiling customers’ web browsing behavior for user identification (Yang, 2010). Thus, assuming that customers possess a high degree of confidence in personalization because of their low privacy concerns and fulfilled quality expectations, the dilemma is to what extent they will be willing to provide further personal information to improve services. We hypothesize that a customer’s likelihood of using a particular service will positively affect her willingness to become profiled.

H5b: (Likelihood of Usage and Willingness to Become Profiled) Likelihood of usage positively influences a customer’s willingness to become profiled for online personalization.

## Research methods

We used an experimental approach to test the hypotheses. We will describe the experimental scenarios, experimental procedure, sampling and participants, and measurement of constructs.

## Study design

Experimental scenarios A quasi-experimental (Field & Hole, 2003) 2 - 2 - 2 between-subjects factorial design was chosen as most appropriate because it allowed us to partially control the independent variables of privacy and personalization quality, while at the same time permitting the distribution of an online survey within users’ natural Internet environment. The between-subjects design helped avoid any carryover effects, which are common for many within-subjects designs (Field & Hole, 2003). Carryover effects, which are based on acquired knowledge from a previous treatment, have the potential to cause altered behavior. In addition, the subjects might experience fatigue effects if they were assigned to more than one treatment.

We manipulated three dimensions in the experimental conditions: privacy (high or low), quality of personalization (high or low), and industry domain (news or finance). (1) Two levels of privacy were studied: low and high. Participants using the high privacy protection website could see a number of privacy seals and a link to the sites’ privacy statements. For example, TRUSTe, a privacy certification service, reviews the consistency of the participating website’s privacy statement and investigates the type of information gathered and how it will be used, conferring a seal on sites meeting its criteria. By showing the TRUSTe trust mark and a mark indicating security features (e.g., McAfee), the service website gives customers a clear signal that it is agreeing to disclose ‘their information gathering and dissemination practices’ (Benassi, 1999). In addition, different privacy options were displayed to assure participants that they had a high degree of control over how their privacy was treated. Participants using the low privacy protection website could not find privacy seals or a privacy policy. (2) Two levels of personalization quality were studied: low and high. Quality of online personalization was determined by using customers’ ratings of the relevance of the recommended news or financial offer. Higher ratings indicated a higher quality, whereas lower ratings indicated a lower-quality recommendation. (3) We compared two different industry domains: news services and financial services. For news personalization services, users were given a list of recommended news items. For financial personalization services, users were given a list of recommended lending and insurance products. In our definition of the news domain, we presented customers with recommendations on news items such as Google or Yahoo! News, including international events and weather, but also real estate, mortgage rates, and finance. The news services provided customers more information about what was going on in a particular sector of their interest. In contrast, in the finance domain, we presented customers with financial lending and insurance recommendations, such as the HomeLoan and PetInsurance examples illustrated in Figure 3. These services are different from financial news per se, as they are actual financial product recommendations, not news of a particular stock or company’s performance, and, as such, have a higher economic value compared with the news services.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the eight scenarios. We compared the likelihood of their using personalization in the following between-subjects design to gauge the impact of privacy, quality, and industry domain. Table 2 presents an overview of the experimental scenarios. As an example, the online personalization shown in Figure 2 represents greater privacy protections and higher-quality recommendations than the service in Figure 3.

Experimental procedure At the beginning of the experiment, we provided a general description of online personalization. The participants then were randomly assigned to one of the eight scenarios. In a betweensubject design, participants across different experimental conditions received different scenarios. They were asked to examine the presented scenario carefully before moving to the rest of the survey. In the next few steps, participants first responded to questions related to their likelihood of using such a service. They then were presented with questions related to privacy concerns, privacy protections, personalization quality, and customer contribution. Manipulation checks were conducted to ensure that the respondents perceived the scenarios in the same way as we intended.

## Analysis and results

## Measurement model

We tested the hypothesized relationships among the constructs using partial least square (PLS) with SmartPLS. We performed the following procedures.

We first assessed the measurement model by checking convergent validity and discriminant validity. The former tests the degree to which all the items are significantly related to their respective constructs, whereas discriminant validity refers to the degree to which the scales are differentiable to each other (Dinev & Hart, 2006). Second, we used the bootstrapping method to measure the significance level of the relationships between the independent and dependent variables. We then determined the structure model’s path coefficients and model fit.

We examined convergent validity and discriminant validity to check the measurement model for both news (see Tables 5 and 6) and finance (see Tables 7 and 8) scenarios. Convergent validity was assessed using four indicators: reliability of items, composite reliability of constructs, average variance extracted (AVE), and factor analysis (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Bollen, 1989). Items’ reliability was assessed by examining each item’s loading on its corresponding construct. All measurement items had loadings higher than 0.7, which is a good indicator for unidimensionality and convergent validity (Bollen, 1989). In addition, Cronbach’s a values were all above the suggested benchmark of 0.7 for acceptable reliability (Gefen et al, 2000), suggesting good scale reliability and internal consistency. Moreover, all AVE values were above the recommended value of 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981), indicating a good convergent validity. Finally, factor analysis results showed that all measurement items loaded highly, above 0.7 in our case, on their respective constructs.

Two analyses were used to test discriminant validity. First, we examined cross-factor loading and found that each measurement item’s loading on its own construct was larger than its loadings on any other construct, which is an indicator of good discriminant validity (Gefen et al, 2000). Second, we computed the square root of the AVEs and compared them with the correlations between the constructs, indicating that more variance was shared between the construct and its indicators than with other constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The results show that all the AVEs’ square roots were greater than the correlations among constructs, suggesting that all the constructs had satisfactory discriminant validity.

## Structural model

We first assessed the structural model’s explanatory power by examining the $R ^ { 2 }$ scores of endogenous variables and structural paths. For news, the model accounted for 17–31% of the variances, which is moderate according to Chin (1998). In addition, all of the paths were significant at a level of 0.05. Thus, the overall model’s fit is moderate. Similarly, for finance, the model accounted for 3–32% of variances. All paths were significant at a level of 0.05, indicating that the overall model had a moderate fit.

Next, we examined all moderating effects in the model. We first tested whether high perceived quality decreases privacy concerns about likelihood of usage. To estimate this moderating effect, we used the product term approach, which is a straightforward modeling of a moderating effect and a suitable approach no matter whether the independent and moderator variable are continuous or categorical (Henseler & Fassott, 2010). We conducted the test in two stages. First, we ran the main effect PLS path model to obtain estimates for privacy and quality. Second, we built the interaction term between privacy and quality as the element-wise product of the two variables. We then used the interaction term and the two variables as independent variables in a multiple linear regression on the likelihood of usage. A moderating effect exists if the direct relation between the interaction term and the exogenous variable is significant (Henseler & Fassott, 2010). The results showed that the interaction term (b ¼ 0.651, P 0.05) can nearly offset the impact of privacy concerns $( \beta = - 0 . 6 5 9 , P { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ on likelihood of usage, insignificantly for news but significantly for finance $( t _ { \mathrm { n e w s } } = 1 . 5 1 4 ; ~ t _ { \mathrm { f i n a n c e } } = 2 . 7 7 4 )$ . Thus, H2b is partially supported (Henseler & Fassottt, 2010), suggesting that quality may overshadow privacy concerns.

## Discussion

In this section, we will discuss our findings with respect to the following aspects: privacy concerns and information disclosure, the role of privacy protection, perceived quality of personalization, and the potential willingness to pay.

## Privacy concerns and information disclosure

This paper examines the impact of privacy concerns on people’s likelihood of using online personalization and the effect of these concerns on their willingness to provide information. Our findings suggest that privacy concerns have a negative impact on the likelihood of using online personalization, consistent with previous research. In addition, we studied two moderating effects, industry domain and past experience, on the relationship between privacy concerns and likelihood of usage. For industry domain, the results do not support our hypothesis that financial services customers are more likely to be deterred by privacy concerns and will be less likely to use personalization services than customers of news services. This may be because financial services customers may be more concerned about their privacy with respect to financial recommendations during the transaction phase, when the services are purchased, but not in the information search phase. The scenarios presented do not allow us to examine customers’ behavior involving actual transactions. Thus, the scenarios cannot explain the role of industry domain in privacy concerns about personalization usage. Another alternative explanation is that respondents may assume, in the context of the experiment, that their own banks offer online recommendations. As a result, they may trust the financial services provider and may therefore have fewer privacy concerns about its services.

Further, we introduced two aspects of past experience: familiarity and privacy invasion. We found that privacy concerns’ negative impact on likelihood of usage is less for customers who are familiar with online personalization. Our results suggest that for people who are familiar with online personalization, both for news and financial content, privacy concerns play a smaller role. Stated differently, customers with experiences or expertise regarding online personalization regard privacy threats as less important in terms of how likely it is that they will use online personalization compared to customers with less experience concerning personalization. We further tested the influence of privacy invasion and found that privacy concerns have a stronger impact on personalization usage for customers who have previously experienced privacy invasions. This test is different from that of Awad & Krishnan (2006), who studied privacy invasion as a predictor variable instead of a moderating variable to explain likelihood of usage.

## The role of privacy protection

We studied privacy protection, which has received limited attention in the academic literature, particularly for online personalization. We included different aspects of privacy protection, including privacy statements and policies, privacy signs, security features, and seals. The results suggest that providing privacy signs, security features, and seals positively impacts customers’ likelihood of using online personalization, especially in the financial setting. This is because privacy features and seals can create trust for customers who are willing to disclose personal data, even though previous research has found that customers perceive that sites which integrate privacy seals often use more privacy-invasive practices than websites without seals (Kobsa, 2007).

Are customers aware of privacy signs, features, and seals? According to Moores (2005), customers often do not fully understand the function or form of certain privacy seals. Some customers are unaware of the existence of independent third-party providers who assess websites’ privacy statements. This does not imply that customers are unaware of the fact that privacy seals can promote trust. However, most know only vaguely which criteria a site has to fulfill to acquire a seal. The presence of privacy seal logos is important for customers with relatively high perceptions of online shopping risk but has decreasing importance for those with low risk perceptions (Miyazaki & Krishnamurthy, 2002). Therefore, we can ascertain that privacy seals have a positive impact for online personalization when customers are actively looking for trust-indicating features. To foster users’ disclosure behavior, website may consider displaying privacy seals (Kobsa, 2007).

Previous research has found that customers value security features (Belanger et al, 2002). With respect to news services, our study did not support the idea that privacy seals and security features such as SET and SSL have an impact on a customer’s propensity to use online personalization. These standards are designed for the transfer of sensitive data, and in particular, for financial information such as credit card details. Thus, it is logical that customers may find these standards irrelevant for news services. Overall, it is important for service providers to combine different security and privacy features in order to get customers’ attention and establish a sense of trustworthiness.

## Perceived quality of personalization

To our knowledge, this paper is the first to examine the role of quality in online personalization. We introduced the construct of perceived quality of online personalization and related it to usefulness, presentation, and information quality provided by recommendations. In our definition, personalization quality includes usefulness, understandability, reliability, presentation, relevance, timeliness, reliability, and scope of the information delivered. This differs from prior studies, which have developed quality measurements based on nonpurchase-related customer attributes, product browsing and purchasing experience, and products themselves (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Awad & Krishnan, 2006).

Our results show that perceived personalization quality positively affects customers’ likelihood of using online personalization, and this effect is stronger for services of high transaction value, such as financial services. In addition, this impact is weaker for customers whose privacy was invaded in the past. This means that a customer who once was a victim of privacy invasion does not value quality online personalization as much as a non-victim does.

In addition, the results also support the assertion that personalization quality can overcome customers’ privacy concerns. We can think of two reasons. First, higherquality personalization can foster higher trust. Customers have different levels of trust; higher trust may mitigate and even outweigh privacy concerns customers may have about providing personal information (Dinev & Hart, 2006). Trust enables customers to suspend worries about privacy, making them willing to provide personal information to obtain personalized services. Second, the impact of privacy concerns on willingness to provide information may be related to a company’s reputation or to its website’s perceived size. This is because online companies’ magnitude of reputation can be seen as a trust indicator and consequently as a reason for customers to disclose private information (Andrade et al, 2002). Moreover, assessing the website’s size (in terms of sales volume and products and services offered) can assist customers in forming an opinion about the site’s trustworthiness (Jarvenpaa et al, 2000). A company that has a higher reputation and rich content will be more likely to develop higher-quality personalization content. This will overshadow customers’ privacy concerns and encourage higher customer usage. Steve Johnson, CEO of Choicestream, a firm that makes personalization software, commented that customers ‘like it when they can see that data can be opted out of and when the watching is done by a trusted source like Amazon or iTunes’ (Knowledge@Wharton, 2006).

Furthermore, we have intriguing findings suggesting that likelihood of usage partially mediates perceived personalization quality’s impacts on customer contribution. These findings further support our belief that perceived quality can improve customers’ likelihood of usage. Moreover, they indicate that customers will be more willing to pay for services and to disclose their private information in proportion to the quality of personalization services they receive, and may subsequently receive even higher-quality services.

## The potential of willingness to pay

To obtain insight about customers’ responses toward paying for online personalization, we examined the notion of willingness to pay. The results show that customers’ likelihood of usage positively influences their willingness to pay. This suggests that customers who are likely to use online personalization are also likely to pay for the service. We further tested the direct relationships between privacy concerns, privacy protection, quality, and willingness to pay, but did not find strong connections. The general question arises: Is it viable for service providers to ask customers for monetary compensation for online personalization? The answer to this question may depend on the types of products or services being offered. We offer some explanations for why we do not observe a direct relationship and why customers in each industry domain we studied showed a potential willingness to pay.

In the domain of news, the news content and the added value of online personalization is probably not sufficient to convince customers to pay because alternative professional services exist. For example, news providers such as The Wall Street Journal (http:// www.wsj.com) and The New York Times (http://www.nytimes.com) offer paid personalized news content and provide value-added services online. Moreover, many other news sources are freely available online (Google News, Yahoo News, MSN News). Customers may see less value in paying for another news service.

In the domain of financial services, our study showed that people may think they are assessing their own bank’s online account, and thus may have difficulty justifying additional charges when they have paid an annual fee. Customers may be willing to pay for personalized financial services if a financial service provider acts as a financial adviser and provides services such as account management, in-depth research and analysis, real-time account status, and access to advanced analysis tools (Verma et al, 2004).

Online personalization benefits both the customers and the companies providing the service. It is useful for companies to practice product and price differentiation, which provides them with the opportunity to gain additional profits (Murthi & Sarkar, 2003). If service providers can provide greater value-added services, customers may be more inclined to pay a premium for these services. For example, customers may pay for portals that can generate a greater value for them by providing access to a greater number of different sources (Kim & Son, 2009). Prior research has found a significant relationship between customers’ trust and their willingness to pay for an e-commerce site that provides legal advice (McKnight et al, 2002). The findings support the idea that news or financial services providers can charge fees for online personalization to their customers – in particular, if the customers perceive higher added value through personalized recommendations that cannot easily be found on competitors’ sites.

## Conclusion

This paper provides a better understanding of online personalization. Through a consideration of privacy concerns, privacy protection, and quality, this paper provides a new perspective on customers’ intentions to use online personalization. The findings show that online personalization reveals distinct characteristics depending on the industry domain. In this section, we will discuss this study’s theoretical and managerial implications, its limitations, and directions for future research.

## Theoretical implications

The results of this paper give valuable insights toward a better understanding of online personalization and its antecedents. First, while prior research in IS has examined the relationship between privacy concerns and usage (Chellappa & Sin, 2005; Awad & Krishnan, 2006), this paper is the first to include the variable of personalization quality and to examine its impact on likelihood of usage and willingness to pay and to disclose information. Through the theoretical framework of privacy, this paper furthers the theoretical contribution by examining the relationships between personalization quality and privacy concerns, considering differences in industry context. The results show that quality has a greater effect on customers’ intention to use online personalization than their concerns about privacy. This means that, under certain circumstances, perceived personalization quality can overcome the impact of privacy concerns. In turn, this implies that service providers can improve the perceived quality of personalization services being offered in order to offset privacy concerns, though the effectiveness of doing so may vary across industry domains. In practice, this means that in order to build customers’ trust, websites offering online personalization should focus not only on their customers’ potential privacy concerns, but also on increasing their own quality standards.

Second, this paper found support for the idea that customers are willing to pay for online personalization on their favorite news sites and financial services sites. This has two implications. First, past studies have argued that when privacy policy information is made available, consumers tend to purchase from online retailers that better protect their privacy. In fact, some customers are willing to pay a premium to purchase from privacyprotective websites when privacy information is made more salient and accessible (Tsai et al, 2011). This suggests that businesses may be able to leverage privacy protection as a selling point. Second, our findings imply that online personalization generally can be made to pay through providers’ ability to engage customers to use these services by means of providing higher-quality recommendations. Service providers who offer recommendations by providing a greater access to different sources and better value-added services will have a greater chance to monetize their services.

## Managerial implications

This paper also has implications for business practice – in particular, for how businesses can better create trust and customer involvement to increase the usage of online personalization. First, the findings suggest that, in order to make it more likely that customers will use their online personalization services, service providers need to address their information privacy concerns, create trust, and improve recommendation quality. Our research shows that using privacy signs, seals, and security features can ease concerns about privacy, particularly for personalized services involving higher transaction values. In addition, a website’s size and perceived reputation may influence whether it is appropriate to implement online personalization. Such services can be best offered by websites that have already achieved a high rate of customer satisfaction, and thus established a good relationship with customers willing to try out new recommendation services. Second, to successfully offer online personalization, customers must be willing to feed personalization systems with personal information. Our findings suggest a number of relevant factors influencing customers’ willingness to provide personal information. Service providers should exercise caution about offering financial incentives in the form of vouchers or discounts to engage customer usage. Even though this approach may be successful in the beginning, in the long term it may undermine the website’s perceived reputation.

## Limitations and future research

We will discuss some limitations of this paper and some avenues for future research. The discussion here has focused on the impacts of privacy concerns, privacy protection, and quality on personalization usage and users’ contribution. To offer a richer explanation of personalization usage and customers’ willingness to pay and to disclose information, future research can study other determinants leading to a better trust relationship and increased usage. For example, it would be interesting to study how online personalization providers can use peer ratings and reviews to generate a higher degree of trustworthiness and intention to use online personalization. It may also be worthwhile to examine the importance of site reputation and its potential impact on trust and intention to use. In terms of information disclosure, further research can study the extent to which privacy protection features can create trustworthiness in online personalization. It would also be worthwhile to know how the content of privacy statements and better control over privacy settings can be better communicated, and thus increase customer confidence in using online personalization.

Our paper has focused only on information quality, presentation, and the usefulness of the personalization quality dimension. Future research can also consider interface design (e.g., text or Adobe Flash design). Prior research has argued that recommendations’ appearance and design strongly influence subsequent usage. For example, recommendations in text format can generate a high degree of trustworthiness (Gefen et al, 2008). It would be useful to determine the relative impact of recommendations displayed in different styles and designs. In the present study, we did not implement different interface designs to manipulate the presentation of the online personalization quality. Future research can experiment with different personalization designs and formats, and adapt valid theoretical measures to further test the quality dimension. Furthermore, when it comes to content quality, some customers may find it intrusive to receive extremely accurate predictions of what they will like. Another fruitful question would be: How far should service providers go in terms of providing quality content to customers in a personalization setting?

Given the limited availability of empirical data, we collected this paper’s data set through a series of online experiments. As such, we were limited to validating our ideas via experimental survey methods using the online environment. The challenge lies in developing good online personalization designs that convey different levels of privacy and personalization quality information.
